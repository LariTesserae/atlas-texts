Preparing to visit this high basin is less like packing for a wilderness expedition and more like preparing to enter a vast, inhabited instrument that has already been tuned. The traveler’s chief task is not to survive the place, but to avoid becoming a disturbance within it. The basin is not hostile, yet it is exacting. Its beauty lies in balance, and the ordinary human visitor must therefore prepare by becoming cleaner, quieter, more self-sufficient, and less eager to change anything.

The ideal preparation is a mixture of mountaineering discipline, monastic restraint, field-biology caution, and diplomatic humility. You are going to a town-sized landscape where water is sacred, silence is structural, and every stone, plant, animal, and shadow seems to have been assigned a role. The safest visitor is one who arrives almost as if they had not arrived at all.

---

## 1. Understand What Kind of Place You Are Entering

Before choosing gear, choose the right mindset.

This is not a ruin to be explored. It is not a wild valley to be conquered. It is not a city to be toured casually. It is a maintained equilibrium. The mountains are young but not weathering. The water is present but not flowing wildly. The plants are lush but do not advance. The buildings are cared for but do not grow. The animals are numerous but do not overrun. The people, if present, are quiet and measured. The place behaves like a held breath.

That means the main risks are not dramatic. You are unlikely to face monsters, storms, or obvious violence. The real risks are:

- contaminating a carefully kept water system;
- introducing foreign seeds, spores, microbes, insects, or animal scents;
- damaging moss, soil pockets, or fragile new stone;
- making unnecessary noise;
- creating a new shadow, marker, trail, stain, scent, or object;
- taking water where you should not;
- touching, moving, “fixing,” or improving something that is already correct;
- overstaying because the steady light and calm make time feel irrelevant;
- becoming psychologically unsettled by the absence of change.

So the first preparation is ethical: you must be willing to visit without leaving evidence of your visit.

If you cannot accept that, you are not ready.

## 2. Pre-Arrival Preparation

### Arrive healthy

Do not enter the basin if you are ill. Even a mild cold matters here. The ecosystem is balanced, hushed, and possibly vulnerable to outside biology. A cough, sneeze, runny nose, or feverish breath could introduce pathogens to people, animals, plants, or water systems that have no context for them. In a multiverse setting, this risk is greater, not lesser.

Before arrival, you should be:

- fever-free;
- free of respiratory infection;
- free of gastrointestinal illness;
- not carrying an open wound that cannot be fully sealed;
- not under the influence of drugs or alcohol that impair balance, judgment, or quiet movement.

If you have been traveling through another biome, another world, or another ecological zone, treat your body and gear as potentially contaminated. Shower, change clothes, clean your nasal passages, rinse your mouth, and inspect your hair, nails, cuffs, boots, and pack seams. You do not want to carry another universe’s dust into this one.

### Acclimatize to thin air

The air is thin, clean, and cool. Breath becomes faintly visible. That suggests altitude, even if the temperature is not extreme. An ordinary human coming from sea level should not rush into this basin fully exerting themselves.

If possible, spend time beforehand at a comparable elevation. If your multiverse transit allows gradual staging, use it. Arrive slowly. Avoid sudden exertion. Do not sprint, shout, climb, or carry heavy loads immediately.

Know the signs of altitude trouble:

- headache;
- nausea;
- dizziness;
- shortness of breath beyond normal exertion;
- poor coordination;
- unusual fatigue;
- disturbed judgment.

If symptoms become more than mild, leave. Descend or return to a lower-pressure staging area. Do not try to “push through” in a place where rescue would itself become a disturbance.

Carry any personal altitude medication only if you know how to use it. Do not rely on stimulants. Avoid alcohol. Hydrate carefully, but remember: hydration here must be managed without contaminating the basin.

### Decontaminate your gear

Your equipment should be as clean as your body. The basin is not merely damp; it is kept. Every droplet seems accounted for. You should assume that even a few foreign seeds, fungal spores, insect eggs, pollen grains, or chemical residues could matter.

Before entering:

- brush boots thoroughly;
- check tread for mud, seeds, or organic fragments;
- empty pockets;
- inspect cuffs, hoods, seams, and pack straps;
- remove lint, hair, and fibers if they come from another ecosystem;
- clean any gear that has touched soil, water, animals, or plants elsewhere;
- use unscented cleaning agents only, and ensure nothing remains wet or fragrant.

Do not bring anything that has recently been in contact with another living system unless it has been sealed, sterilized, or thoroughly cleaned.

### Plan your entry point

If you can choose where you arrive, choose carefully. Do not materialize, step out, or descend directly onto moss, a cistern roof, a dew-collecting thread, a planted terrace, or a damp hollow. Arrive, if possible, near an existing path, stone apron, causeway, or edge district where the surface is already durable.

Enter from the margin. Give yourself time to adjust to the light, the thin air, and the silence before moving far.

Do not arrive loudly. If your transit method produces noise, flash, pressure change, wind, or heat, arrange for it to occur as far outside the basin as possible. The basin should not have to absorb your entrance.

### Decide how long you will stay

The light does not seem to move toward evening. There may be no natural day-night rhythm. That is dangerous in a subtle way. You may feel alert long after your body needs rest, or you may underestimate fatigue because the environment remains unchanged.

Before entering, decide:

- how long you intend to stay;
- when you will rest;
- when you will leave;
- what signs will make you leave early;
- how you will measure time without relying on the sky.

Carry a silent timepiece. Do not rely on a ticking watch, beeping device, or audible alarm. A simple mechanical timer, a silent digital watch, or an internal counting discipline is better. If you use a device, mute it completely.

## 3. Clothing: Dress for Quiet, Damp, Thin-Air Stillness

Your clothing should protect you without announcing you. It should be warm enough for cool damp air, breathable enough for exertion, soft enough not to rustle, and visually modest enough not to become a glaring presence in the basin.

### Colors and appearance

Choose muted colors:

- gray;
- moss green;
- slate blue;
- dull brown;
- charcoal;
- pale stone tones.

Avoid:

- bright orange, red, yellow, or white;
- reflective strips;
- shiny waterproof shells;
- glittering jewelry;
- polished metal that catches the light;
- anything that flutters loosely.

You do not want to become a new visual event. The basin already has exactly the reflections it needs.

### Fabrics

The air is cool and damp, but not necessarily freezing. You need insulation that works when moist. Avoid cotton as a primary layer because it holds dampness and chills the body.

Good choices:

- thin wool base layers;
- fleece or brushed synthetic insulation;
- soft-shell outer layers;
- breathable waterproof shell, matte-faced;
- wool or synthetic socks;
- light gloves;
- a close-fitting hood or cap.

Avoid fabrics that crackle or rasp loudly. Many hard waterproof shells make noise when you move. If you must wear one, choose a quiet, matte, soft-finished material. If your shell rustles, consider a softer outer layer and accept some dampness rather than becoming a walking noise source.

### Layering

The basin is cool enough to make breath visible, but likely not brutally cold. Still, dampness can steal body heat, especially if you sit still for observation.

A sensible system:

1. thin moisture-wicking base layer;
2. light insulating layer;
3. wind/damp shell;
4. spare dry base layer sealed in a waterproof bag;
5. light hat and gloves for rest periods.

Do not overdress to the point of sweating. Sweat is moisture you carry with you, and in a place this carefully water-balanced, you should not be shedding excess dampness onto moss or stone.

### Footwear

Footwear is critical. The basin has wet stone, moss, damp hollows, smooth causeways, and crisp steps. You need grip, sensitivity, and quietness.

Ideal boots or shoes:

- broken-in;
- soft rubber soles;
- non-marking;
- good grip on wet stone;
- no metal cleats, spikes, or aggressive lugs that scar surfaces;
- stable enough for slick stone;
- quiet when walking.

Do not wear new boots. Do not wear hard-soled boots that clack. Do not wear anything that might chip stone or tear moss. If you need traction, use careful foot placement rather than aggressive tread.

Carry spare socks sealed in a dry bag. If your feet become wet, change only in a place where you will not contaminate the ground. Do not leave wet socks behind. Do not wring them over channels, pools, moss, or planted areas.

### Hands, head, and face

The air is cool against lips and nostrils. A light cap, thin gloves, and perhaps a soft neck covering may help. If you breathe cold air easily, a thin scarf or buff can warm the air slightly, but avoid anything that sheds lint or smells of detergent.

If your lips dry easily, carry unscented lip balm. Do not use strongly scented balms, medicated ointments, or anything that might transfer to water cups or stone.

### Personal scent

Avoid all strong scents:

- perfume;
- cologne;
- scented soap;
- scented sunscreen;
- insect repellent;
- fragrant food;
- tobacco;
- herbal incense;
- medicated lotions.

Animals may be sensitive to smell. The inhabitants may be sensitive to it too. The basin’s air is clean; do not become an odor event.

If you must use sunscreen or skin protection, choose unscented, non-greasy, minimal amounts, and cover skin with clothing instead where possible. Do not touch water surfaces after applying anything to your skin.

## 4. Pack and Equipment: Carry Less, Carry Quietly

Your pack should be soft, matte, compact, and silent. It should not swing, clink, scrape, or shed. You are not carrying a camp. You are carrying a small, self-contained life-support system that will leave no trace.

### Pack design

Use:

- soft-sided pack;
- muted color;
- minimal external straps;
- no dangling carabiners;
- no metal water bottles knocking against stone;
- no loud buckles;
- no crinkly rain cover unless necessary;
- no external sleeping pad, tent, or gear that suggests settlement.

Wrap any hard objects in cloth. Secure everything so nothing can fall. A dropped object is not merely litter; it is an intrusion.

### Essential gear

A reasonable personal kit might include:

- sealed water supply;
- sealed food supply;
- sealed waste bags;
- small unscented hygiene kit;
- spare socks;
- light insulation layer;
- waterproof dry bag;
- silent timepiece;
- simple navigation aids;
- small first-aid kit;
- soft cloth or towel;
- emergency shelter only for life-threatening need;
- observation tools, if any;
- personal medication;
- multiverse recall or emergency exit device, if you have one, kept silent and concealed.

### What not to bring

Do not bring:

- engines;
- drones;
- motorized pumps;
- radios playing sound;
- loud communication devices;
- musical instruments;
- bells;
- fire-making kit unless for extreme emergency;
- stoves;
- fuel;
- flares;
- tents and stakes for casual use;
- hammocks requiring anchors;
- climbing hardware unless absolutely necessary;
- chalk, paint, markers, nails, screws, adhesives;
- sampling jars unless authorized;
- specimen bags unless authorized;
- souvenirs;
- pet animals;
- pack animals;
- seeds, fresh fruit, loose grains, or food that can shed plant matter;
- scented chemicals;
- soaps, detergents, or disinfectants intended for use in the basin;
- fishing gear;
- traps;
- nets;
- weapons;
- anything intended to alter, collect, mark, or improve the landscape.

If you are an ordinary traveler, the simplest rule is: bring nothing that wants to change the place.

## 5. Water: Do Not Drink from the Landscape Unless Invited

Water is the central art of the basin. It is gathered drop by drop, guided through channels, held in cisterns, encouraged on stone slabs, and balanced through overflow paths. You must treat every water feature as functional, calibrated, and possibly essential.

### Carry your own water

Bring enough water for the entire visit, plus a safety margin. Thin, cool air can deceive you; you may not feel thirsty, but altitude and dry respiration increase fluid loss.

Use quiet containers. A metal flask can clink; wrap it or use a soft bladder. Ensure containers do not leak, sweat, or drip. If condensation forms on the outside, wipe it with a cloth and keep the moisture contained. Do not shake droplets onto the ground.

### Do not take water casually

The local rules are clear, even if unspoken:

> Take water only from the prepared cups.

That means you should not:

- drink from pools;
- drink from cisterns;
- drink from channels;
- collect dew from threads;
- scrape moisture from stone;
- scoop water from basins;
- fill your bottle from a cistern roof;
- drink from a hollow just because it looks clear;
- use dew cups as washing stations;
- add your own water to channels or basins.

Even clear water may be biologically delicate, chemically unusual, or carefully allocated. Even your mouth touching a cup rim could introduce oils, microbes, or foreign residue. If you must drink from a prepared cup, do so as simply as possible. If inhabitants offer water, accept according to their custom. If no one offers, assume you may not take.

### If invited to drink

If you are offered water:

- do not touch the collecting threads;
- do not insert your own container unless invited;
- do not pour water back;
- do not spill;
- do not wash your face or hands in the cup;
- do not leave moisture on the rim unless you can do so without contaminating it;
- drink only what is offered.

If you need to fill a container for later, ask first through gesture or local custom. If permitted, let the water fall into your container without touching the source. Do not immerse anything.

### Do not add water

Adding water can be as disruptive as taking it. Do not pour leftover water into a channel, pool, moss bed, or soil pocket. Do not empty a flask to “return” water to the place. The system already knows its amounts. Your extra water is not generosity; it is imbalance.

## 6. Food: Eat Without Leaving Evidence

You should bring food that can be eaten with almost no residue.

Good choices:

- compressed ration bars;
- sealed nut pastes;
- dense breads that do not crumble;
- smooth energy gels;
- sealed dried foods without seeds or loose particles;
- simple, low-odor, low-crumb rations.

Avoid:

- fresh fruit;
- seeded breads;
- granola that sheds;
- nuts with skins or fragments;
- strong-smelling foods;
- salty foods that make you thirsty beyond your supply;
- anything requiring cooking;
- anything requiring utensils that clink;
- anything that leaves wrappers, peels, pits, shells, or crumbs.

Eat only when necessary. Eat in one place, preferably on durable stone away from moss, channels, cisterns, and planted pockets. Catch crumbs. Chew carefully. Do not let food scent linger. Pack out every scrap, including tiny fragments.

Do not feed animals. Do not leave food for birds, voles, hares, insects, or amphibians. Feeding is one of the fastest ways to break an ecological balance. Even a few crumbs could encourage one species to become numerous enough to disrupt another.

Do not cook. No fire, no stove, no heat tablets, no smoking fuel. The basin has no signs of industrial force, and smoke or heat would be a serious intrusion.

## 7. Waste: Carry Everything Out

This is one of the most important preparations. In a place where moisture is gathered, channels are fine-tuned, and animals have measured roles, your bodily waste is not natural. It is a foreign nutrient load and a biological contamination.

You must be able to manage:

- urine;
- feces;
- vomit, if necessary;
- blood;
- saliva, as much as possible;
- used tissues;
- hygiene waste;
- menstrual products;
- wound dressings;
- food waste, even tiny crumbs.

Do not bury waste. Burying disturbs soil, introduces nutrients, and may attract animals. Do not urinate on stone, moss, soil, or into channels. Do not use pools or hollows as toilets. Do not wash with waste water.

Carry sealed waste bags. Use absorbent material if needed. Bring a small amount of unscented sanitizer or wipes, but do not let their chemicals enter the environment. Rub sanitizer fully into your skin away from water features, and do not touch pools afterward.

If you need to relieve yourself, move to a place as far as possible from channels, moss, cisterns, and buildings, and use a sealed portable system. If you cannot do that without risk of contamination, shorten your visit.

If you have a wound, keep it sealed. If it bleeds, contain it immediately. Do not let blood touch stone, moss, or water.

The rule is simple: your metabolism should not become part of the basin’s cycles.

## 8. Movement: Walk as If the Ground Is Already Correct

The basin has paths, causeways, steps, terraces, and stone aprons. Use them. Do not create new lines of travel.

### Where to walk

Prefer:

- existing paths;
- stone steps;
- causeways;
- broad terraces;
- rock aprons;
- already compressed surfaces.

Avoid:

- moss beds;
- soil pockets;
- planted cushions;
- damp mossy flats;
- cistern roofs;
- channel edges;
- dew-collection threads;
- retaining wall tops;
- freshly beaded stone lips;
- narrow irrigation channels;
- shallow basins;
- overhang shadows where moisture gathers.

If you must cross a damp area, use the built route. Do not lay stones to make a path. Do not stack rocks. Do not widen a trail. Do not step around a wet patch by trampling new vegetation. It is better to wait, turn back, or accept a longer built route.

### How to walk

Move slowly. Take short, quiet steps. Keep your weight centered. Watch for slick stone. The moss absorbs footfalls, but moss is also living and easily compressed. Do not treat it as carpet.

If you slip, try not to scrape stone with boots or hands. If you dislodge a stone, stop and assess. If you can replace it exactly, do so. If you cannot, do not improvise a new arrangement. If inhabitants are nearby, make it known quietly, or leave the area carefully and seek guidance if possible.

Do not kick stones. Do not test loose rocks. Do not lean on walls unless necessary for safety. Do not sit on retaining walls if they seem functional or delicate. If you need to rest, choose broad, bare stone where your weight will not compress plants.

### Climbing

Do not climb the young mountains unless absolutely necessary. They are sharp, angular, and freshly made. Their talus fields and slab planes may be unstable, even if they appear crisp. The basin is not asking to be ascended.

If you must climb for safety, avoid leaving marks, scraping boots, dislodging stones, or creating new scuffs. Do not use pitons, cams, chalk, or bolts. Do not anchor rope around living plants. Do not build cairns.

### Group movement

If traveling with others, keep the group small. One or two companions are better than many. Establish signals before entering:

- stop;
- wait;
- look;
- listen;
- water check;
- rest;
- leave;
- danger;
- contamination incident.

Walk single file where paths are narrow. Do not talk to coordinate movement if gestures will do. Do not spread out. Do not let anyone wander to “see something” without agreement.

## 9. Sound: Make No Sound That Requires a Second Echo

The basin’s silence is not absence; it is part of the architecture. Moss softens footfalls. Damp air softens echoes. Walls scatter noise. Sounds stop sooner than expected. You must match that.

### Voice discipline

Speak only when necessary. Speak softly. Do not call across distances. Do not shout, even in surprise, unless safety demands it. If you must warn someone, use a hand signal or a short, low word.

Avoid:

- loud laughter;
- whistling;
- humming;
- singing;
- repeated calling;
- clapping;
- snapping fingers;
- testing echoes;
- speaking to yourself at length.

If you cough or sneeze, muffle it into your sleeve or a cloth. If you have a tickle in your throat, swallow or sip water carefully rather than clearing your throat loudly.

### Gear discipline

Silence your equipment before entering.

- Wrap metal items in cloth.
- Remove bells or chimes.
- Replace loud buckles with quiet toggles if possible.
- Avoid Velcro if you can; it can be startlingly loud.
- Secure zippers so they do not jingle.
- Pad containers.
- Keep tools sheathed.
- Do not let straps flap against stone.
- Do not drop items.
- Do not let a water bottle knock against a wall.

Even a small tap can feel large here. A single bead falling into a cup is part of the basin’s music. Your gear should not add a second music.

### Device discipline

Avoid powered devices that make noise, light, vibration, or electromagnetic disturbance. If you must carry a multiverse emergency device, keep it silent, shielded, and concealed. Do not use it unless there is a true emergency.

No drones. No speakers. No fans. No pumps. No motors. No recorders with audible feedback. No alarms. If you carry a camera, use a silent shutter, no flash, and no bright screen. If possible, cover the lens when not in use to avoid glints.

## 10. Shadows, Objects, and the Rule Against New Things

One of the felt rules of the basin is:

> Build nothing that casts a new shadow at the same hour.

Your body will cast a shadow; that is unavoidable. But you should not create persistent new shadows with objects.

Do not:

- set up tents;
- erect tarps;
- place bags in open courtyards for long periods;
- leave gear standing where it creates a new dark shape;
- hang clothing to dry;
- lean boards or packs against walls;
- place equipment on cistern lids;
- spread maps on the ground in open light for long;
- lay out tools as if preparing a workshop;
- build shelters;
- arrange stones as seats, markers, or windbreaks.

If you must set something down, do it briefly, preferably in an existing shadow or on durable stone, and remove it as soon as possible. Keep your pack close to your body or in a place where it does not become a new feature.

Do not leave anything behind. Not a thread, not a wrapper, not a dropped stitch, not a flake of graphite, not a fiber, not a hair tie, not a coin, not a “gift.”

Gifts are especially dangerous. In a place of sufficiency, an unwanted object is not kindness; it is clutter. Do not bring offerings unless you understand the local custom extremely well. The safest offering is usually restraint.

## 11. Stone, Plants, and the Discipline of Not Fixing

The basin is maintained with endless care. That care is not yours to improve unless invited.

### Stone

Do not move stone unless you must replace a stone you displaced. Do not:

- stack stones;
- make cairns;
- prop stones upright;
- rearrange wall faces;
- test the stability of steps;
- chip edges;
- scratch symbols;
- carve;
- rub moss away to “clean” a surface;
- reposition slabs;
- wedge loose rocks.

If a stone is loose underfoot, step around it if possible. If you accidentally move it, try to return it to the same position with the same angle and weight. If you cannot, do not guess proudly. Seek guidance if available.

### Plants

The vegetation is lush but bounded. Do not let any plant advance because of you.

Do not:

- pick flowers;
- collect seeds;
- press leaves;
- take moss samples;
- dig soil;
- divide plants;
- prune growth;
- remove weeds;
- transplant anything;
- drop organic matter;
- leave fruit skins or food particles;
- shake clothing over planted areas;
- brush pollen from your clothes into the basin.

If a plant seems to be growing beyond a border, do not fix it. Maintenance may be precise and ritualized; your intuition may be wrong.

### Moss

Moss is especially important. It pads shelves, absorbs sound, holds moisture, and contributes to the basin’s hush. It may look resilient, but it can be damaged by compression, oils, salts, chemicals, or foreign spores.

Avoid walking on moss. If you must touch it, do so lightly and briefly. Do not sit on it unless there is no alternative. Do not press it flat to make a seat. Do not remove it from stone. Do not let food, sweat, urine, blood, or chemicals touch it.

### Water features

Do not touch dew threads. Do not adjust channels. Do not clear what looks like debris. Do not dry a damp wall because it seems unhealthy. Do not scrape algae. Do not rinse your hands in basins. Do not blow dust from a cistern lid. Do not step on overflow paths.

Everything that looks like a small imperfection may be intentional.

## 12. Animals: Observe Without Entering Their Economy

Fauna is abundant but hushed. Birds, amphibians, insects, small mammals, fish-like shapes, beetles, and moths all have roles. Your role is not to manage them.

### General rules

Do not:

- feed animals;
- touch animals;
- chase animals;
- corner animals;
- imitate calls;
- make mating sounds;
- lure them with food or water;
- photograph them with flash;
- disturb nests;
- collect eggs;
- collect insects;
- catch fish;
- tap pools to see movement;
- introduce any animal or animal scent;
- leave food scraps;
- block their routes.

If animals approach, remain still. Do not interpret their lack of fear as invitation. They may simply be unaccustomed to disruption. Let them pass.

### Birds

Birds live among eaves and cliff shelves. They drink from dew-cups. Do not stand between a bird and its water. Do not linger near nesting ledges. Do not look directly into nests for long. If a bird gives a warning call, withdraw slowly.

### Amphibians

Amphibians are often sensitive to skin oils, salts, chemicals, and pathogens. Do not touch newts, frogs, salamanders, or pale water creatures. Do not put hands into pools. If your skin has sunscreen, repellent, sweat, or sanitizer residue, keep it away from water entirely.

### Small mammals

Voles, shrew-like creatures, hares, and dense-coated deer-like animals may move through terraces. They may seem calm. Do not approach them. Do not attempt to touch or feed them. If they are on a path, wait or go around without cutting into moss.

### Insects

Insects are present but low-noise. Do not swat unless necessary for immediate safety. Do not use repellent sprays. Do not crush insects on stone where scavengers might be disrupted. If an insect lands on you, let it leave.

### If you see a dead animal

Do not bury it, move it, or clean it away unless you are specifically instructed. Scavengers and decomposition may be part of the balance. If it is in a place where it contaminates water or creates hazard, seek local guidance rather than acting on your own.

## 13. Inhabitants: Be a Guest, Not a Project Manager

The basin feels inhabited even when people are not visible. Worn thresholds, fresh repairs, tools left exactly where needed, and low voices suggest a settled population. If you encounter them, treat them with respect and restraint.

### First contact

If you see inhabitants:

- stop;
- lower your gaze slightly;
- keep hands visible and relaxed;
- do not approach quickly;
- do not raise your voice;
- do not smile in an overly broad or demanding way;
- wait to see whether they acknowledge you.

If they ignore you, that may be permission to remain quiet and observant, or it may be a signal to keep distance. Watch their movement. Do not follow them. Do not enter buildings. Do not cross into private courts unless invited.

### Communication

Speak softly. Use few words. If you do not share language, rely on simple gestures: open hands, slight bow or inclination of the head, pointing to yourself, then making a question with your face rather than your voice.

Do not pantomime taking water unless you are asking carefully. A gesture toward water could be misunderstood as demand. If thirsty, indicate your own sealed supply first, showing that you are not trying to take theirs.

### Tools and work

Do not touch tools. The basin’s tools are left where they will be needed. Moving a chisel, lever, rope, measuring cord, or polished stone level could disrupt work you cannot see.

If you see someone repairing a wall, channel, stair, or roof, do not offer advice. Do not pick up a fallen stone to “help.” Do not hand them a tool unless they ask. Do not stand in their light. Do not cast your shadow across the work if you can avoid it.

If invited to assist, follow exactly. Use local materials. Do not innovate. Do not tighten, straighten, or improve unless shown how. Your competence may be less useful than your obedience to the pattern.

### Gifts and exchange

Avoid giving objects unless asked. If you must offer something, the least harmful offerings are usually:

- your labor, if requested;
- your departure, if requested;
- silence;
- attention.

Do not give food. Do not give water containers. Do not give metal, cloth, seeds, ornaments, or technology. Even a beautiful object can become a contaminating variable.

### Photography and observation

If inhabitants are present, do not photograph them without clear permission. Even if you cannot speak the language, make the request visible. If uncertain, do not record.

Avoid long staring. The basin is not a zoo. The people are not exhibits. If you observe, do so as one observes a ceremony: with patience, humility, and a willingness to not understand everything.

## 14. Time, Light, and Psychological Preparation

The light is steady. It remains what it is. This may feel peaceful at first, then strange, then disorienting. Without sunset, your body may lose its sense of when to stop.

### Manage your own time

Use a silent watch or internal schedule. Decide rest intervals in advance. For example:

- move for a set period;
- rest for a set period;
- check hydration;
- check fatigue;
- check for contamination incidents;
- leave before exhaustion.

Do not let the unchanging light persuade you that there is always more time.

### Prepare for stillness

The basin may hold a traveler for a long time without offering obvious event. You may sit beside a pool and watch a bead form, swell, and fall. You may notice the same mist neither thickening nor thinning. You may feel as if the world is waiting for you to become quiet enough to understand it.

That can be beautiful. It can also be unsettling.

Prepare by practicing:

- slow breathing;
- silent walking;
- not needing to comment on what you see;
- not needing to touch, collect, or document;
- tolerating absence of novelty;
- noticing small changes without demanding large ones.

If you begin to feel restless, anxious, unreal, or overly fascinated by trivial repetition, check your body first. Altitude, dehydration, fatigue, and low blood sugar can feel like strangeness. If physical needs are met and the unease remains, leave.

### Do not become “held”

The valley behaves like a held breath. A traveler may begin to feel held too. This is not necessarily dangerous, but it should be noticed. If you find yourself unable to decide to leave, or if the idea of departure feels rude or impossible, that is a warning sign.

Set a departure cue before entering:

- “I leave after four hours.”
- “I leave if I feel dizzy.”
- “I leave if my water drops below half.”
- “I leave if I have not seen a safe exit route.”
- “I leave if I begin to feel the place deciding for me.”

Then honor the cue.

## 15. Shelter, Rest, and Sleeping

Ideally, do not sleep in the basin unless invited or forced by emergency. The steady light and quiet may make rest tempting, but sleeping introduces long-duration presence, body heat, shed skin, breath moisture, waste risk, and pressure on surfaces.

### If invited to rest

If inhabitants offer a place to rest, accept gratefully and follow their arrangement exactly. Use only the space given. Do not rearrange bedding, walls, mats, or tools. Do not explore while they are away. Keep your gear contained. Do not sleep with food exposed.

### If you must rest without invitation

Choose durable stone or an already bare surface, away from moss, channels, planted pockets, cisterns, and paths. Sit rather than lie down if possible. Keep your pack on your lap or beside you, not spread out. Do not rig shelter. Do not remove clothing and leave it drying. Do not create a nest.

If you are forced by injury or exhaustion to lie down, protect the ground with your own carried cloth if necessary, but do not leave it. Keep your body from touching living plants. Keep waste sealed. Leave as soon as you can.

### Sleep and darkness

Because the light may not darken, carry a sleep mask if you expect to rest. Do not build a dark enclosure unless emergency. A soft mask is less intrusive. If you use one, make sure it does not shed fibers.

Do not use lamps. If you must have light for medical emergency, use the dimmest possible shielded source, briefly, and conceal it. Do not let light spill across walls, water, or sleeping areas.

## 16. Health and Safety Hazards

The basin is not dramatic in its dangers, but ordinary human limitations still apply.

### Altitude

Thin air can cause headache, nausea, poor judgment, and fatigue. Move slowly. Hydrate from your own supply. If symptoms worsen, leave. Do not wait for rescue.

### Damp chill

Cool dampness can lead to hypothermia if you become wet and inactive. Wear layers. Keep a dry spare layer sealed. If you become soaked, do not try to dry yourself by spreading clothes through the basin. Leave to a staging area or shelter outside the maintained landscape.

### Slips and falls

Wet stone is dangerous. The basin’s surfaces are beautiful but slick. Do not rush. Use handholds where available. Do not slide. Do not leap between steps. If you fall, check for injury and for damage to the basin. Treat any bleeding as a contamination event.

### Sharp stone

The young mountains and broken ridgeline have angular features. Edges may be sharp. Do not lean on slabs, scramble over talus, or place hands blindly on rock lips. Wear gloves if you expect to touch stone, but avoid gloves that shed.

### Biological exposure

Do not drink untreated water. Do not touch amphibians. Do not wade in pools. If you accidentally contact basin water, do not assume it is safe. Monitor for irritation or illness after leaving. If you develop symptoms, seek medical care and mention possible multiverse ecological exposure.

### Psychological effects

Stillness, silence, and lack of change can cause:

- drowsiness;
- dissociation;
- anxiety;
- compulsive observation;
- reluctance to leave;
- feeling watched;
- emotional overwhelm.

If these occur, do not interpret them as mystical summons. Check oxygen, hydration, food, warmth, and time. If still unsettled, leave.

## 17. Emergency Conduct

Emergencies are difficult here because the normal solutions—fire, loud calls, markers, shelters, rescue signals—are themselves disturbances.

### If someone is injured

First, prevent further disturbance. Move only if necessary for safety. If moving, use existing durable surfaces. Contain blood and waste. Keep the injured person warm with carried layers, not landscape materials. Do not build fire. Do not cut plants. Do not break stone.

If you have a silent emergency exit method, use it. If you must call for help, use the lowest effective volume. If inhabitants are nearby, seek them with gestures rather than shouting.

### If you are lost

Stop. Do not blaze a trail. Do not stack stones. Do not mark walls. Use memory, map, compass, or landmarks. Follow existing paths downhill or toward built areas only if you can do so without damage. If you cannot find the way, stay on durable ground and wait rather than trampling new routes.

### If you contaminate something

If you spill food, blood, waste, chemicals, or foreign water, contain it immediately. Pick up all visible matter. Do not wash it into channels. Seal the waste. If the contamination reaches a cistern, channel, pool, or dew-collection system, inform inhabitants immediately if possible. Do not try to clean it with unknown methods unless they guide you.

### If you damage something

Do not hide it. Do not over-correct. If you displaced a stone, replace it if you can. If you tore moss, do not peel it further. If you cracked a step, do not attempt makeshift repair unless invited. Report it quietly if inhabitants are present.

## 18. Scientific Observation and Documentation

If you are a researcher, prepare for severe restraint.

The best methods are:

- visual observation;
- memory;
- silent sketching;
- non-flash photography;
- passive listening;
- counting without marking;
- mental mapping.

Avoid:

- sampling water;
- collecting soil;
- swabbing surfaces without permission;
- trapping insects;
- recording animal calls loudly;
- placing sensors;
- attaching tags;
- leaving data loggers;
- marking transects;
- taking measurements that require stakes or lines.

If you must measure, use non-contact methods. If you must write, keep materials sealed. Do not let pencil shavings, paper fragments, or eraser dust fall. If possible, use a stylus on a reusable surface that sheds nothing.

If you are authorized to sample, follow the principle of replacement and minimal intrusion. Take less than you think you need. Use sterile tools. Do not sample near dew cups or cisterns unless explicitly permitted. Replace volumes or materials only under local guidance.

## 19. If You Stay Longer

A longer stay requires deeper conformity. You should not remain merely because the place is beautiful. You should remain only if you can live within its limits.

For an extended visit:

- seek explicit permission;
- accept assigned space;
- use assigned water sources;
- eat only what is approved or what you brought;
- follow local maintenance rhythms;
- do not start personal projects;
- do not rearrange anything;
- do not create private storage;
- do not befriend animals;
- do not establish routines that require the landscape to adapt to you.

If you are allowed to work, begin by watching. Then imitate. Then do only the smallest task. The basin does not need your creativity. It needs your carefulness.

---

## 20. Departure: Leave Before You Are Missed

Prepare to leave while you still have clarity. Do not wait until exhaustion, dehydration, or fascination pulls you beyond good judgment.

Before departing:

1. Count your gear.
2. Check for trash, fibers, hair, food fragments, or dropped items.
3. Inspect your clothing for seeds, spores, insects, or plant matter.
4. Ensure no waste bags have leaked.
5. Ensure you have not left any shadow-casting object.
6. Look at the place where you rested and ask whether it appears unchanged.
7. If you displaced anything, make sure it is restored or reported.
8. Leave quietly.

Do not take souvenirs. Do not take stones, moss, water, feathers, leaves, shells, bones, or “small harmless” samples. If an object attaches itself to you accidentally, remove it carefully and seek guidance. If no guidance is available, seal it and do not release it into another environment.

After leaving, decontaminate again. The basin’s equilibrium should not travel out through you any more than another world’s contamination should travel in.

If you want a simple code, carry this:

- Arrive clean.
- Enter slowly.
- Breathe quietly.
- Carry your own water.
- Drink only from prepared cups if offered.
- Eat without crumbs.
- Keep all waste.
- Stay on stone and established paths.
- Do not touch dew threads, channels, pools, tools, or plants.

- Do not leave objects.
- Do not build shelters.

## 23. The Deeper Preparation

The basin will test a traveler’s need to matter. Most travelers are used to places responding to them: paths bending underfoot, fires warming air, voices returning echoes, tools changing materials, journeys leaving marks. Here, the world may not need your mark at all.

That can feel like rejection, but it is not. It is completeness.

The proper preparation, therefore, is not only physical. It is an education in restraint. You must learn to look without grasping, to admire without collecting, to rest without settling, to notice without naming, and to leave without grieving that you changed nothing.

If you prepare well, your visit may consist of very little: sitting on a low wall, watching a bead of water form and fall, hearing the soft tick of moisture, seeing a bird drink and depart without sound, realizing the light has not changed, feeling your breath cool in your throat, and understanding that the place does not require you to add anything.

That is enough.

In fact, that is the point.

The best traveler in this basin is the one who leaves it exactly as it was found—because it was already found rightly.
