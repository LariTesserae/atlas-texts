Prepare as if you are entering three places at once:

1. a high-altitude mountaineering environment with thin air, cold, and exposure;
2. a saturated wetland where water is always near, under, or on the surface;
3. an active industrial site with heat exchange, pressure release, moving water, mineral deposition, and occasional electrical charge — but with no machines, walls, rails, controls, or rescue infrastructure.

The most important mental adjustment is this: **the basin is not hostile, but it is not arranged for you.** It has no paths, no shelters, no dry routes, no landmarks, no built meaning. It is a landscape still deciding where it will put itself. An ordinary human multiverse traveler should therefore prepare to be a temporary, removable, self-contained presence. Bring everything needed to survive, observe carefully, and leave no enduring trace.

---

## 1. Understand What the Place Will Ask of You

Before choosing gear, understand the character of the visit.

### The air is thin and the space is open

The basin is high enough that the sky feels “scrubbed of depth.” Even if the atmosphere is breathable, treat it as a serious altitude environment. Ordinary humans degrade quickly in thin air: poor judgment, headache, nausea, breathlessness, exhaustion, altitude sickness. If the insertion point is far above comfortable air, do not assume you can simply acclimatize after arrival. You may need staged arrival, supplemental oxygen, or both.

### Wetness is constant

This is not merely a cold place. It is a cold place where everything is damp, beaded, seeping, misted, or thinly submerged. Wet cold steals heat faster than dry cold. Your clothing, shelter, and sleep system must remain functional while surrounded by moisture that never fully goes away.

### The ground is provisional

The terrain is young. Shelves, channels, crusts, hollows, and sediment fans may not have settled. What looks like a firm patch may be saturated. What looks like a thin mineral floor may be a crust over water. New gullies may fill suddenly. Thin ice may be deceptive. Footing must be tested constantly.

### There are no artifacts

No walls, paths, foundations, ruins, wires, or shelters. If something breaks, there is no replacement. If you drop something, it becomes the first artifact. If you need shade, windbreak, seat, bed, heat, light, or protection, you must bring it.

### The landscape is processual

Water moves, condenses, freezes, thaws, deposits minerals, cuts channels, and redistributes sediment. Heat rises in local spots. Gas or steam may pulse. Electrical charge may accumulate. The place behaves like machinery without being mechanical. You are entering an operating system, not a static scenery.

### Life is sparse but concentrated

Vegetation is thin and fragile. Animals gather at warm points, pools, seeps, runnels, and food-rich margins. This makes the ecology both visible and vulnerable. You must avoid disturbing the exact knots where life concentrates.

### The emotional effect matters

The basin’s openness, dimness, lack of vertical features, and unresolved forms can produce a strange psychological strain. The eye seeks objects and finds few. The mind may feel exposed, under-stimulated, or unsettled. Prepare for that as seriously as for cold or altitude.

## 2. Decide Whether You Are Ready

An ordinary human should not treat this as a casual multiverse excursion. The correct standard is:

> If you cannot survive a night, a storm, a navigation failure, a wet-out, or a minor injury without local help, you are not ready.

### Minimum experience before visiting

Ideally, the traveler should already be comfortable with:

- high-altitude hiking or mountaineering;
- cold, wet-weather camping;
- fog navigation;
- moving over unstable or slippery terrain;
- strict Leave No Trace ethics;
- managing hydration and calories under physical strain;
- recognizing early hypothermia, altitude sickness, and exhaustion;
- using a stove safely in wet wind;
- keeping critical gear dry.

If the traveler lacks these, the first visit should be brief, supervised, and near a reliable exit point.

### Solo travel is not recommended

The basin is not technically extreme in every way, but it is cumulatively demanding. A solo traveler who slips, gets lost, wets out their sleep system, or develops altitude symptoms may have no margin. A pair or small team is much safer.

A good team has:

- one person focused on navigation and timing;
- one focused on terrain and hazard reading;
- shared responsibility for weather, health, and equipment;
- agreed turn-back rules.

## 3. Pre-Visit Planning

### Confirm the local physical parameters

Even if the multiverse route is stable, verify:

- oxygen partial pressure;
- atmospheric pressure;
- gravity;
- temperature range;
- wind patterns;
- humidity and fog behavior;
- UV and ionizing radiation;
- electrical storm probability;
- geothermal gas risk;
- presence of toxic minerals or volcanic aerosols.

If oxygen is uncertain, carry supplemental oxygen. Do not gamble on “thin but breathable.”

### Choose a conservative weather window

The basin seems perpetually late-afternoon, dim, and damp, but it still has cycles: fog advances and withdraws, frost forms, melt releases, pressure shifts, charge builds. Choose a window with:

- lower predicted electrical activity;
- less fog;
- calmer wind;
- stable pressure;
- no unusual melt or rain pulse.

Do not assume that because the landscape looks quiet, it is safe. Much of its operation is slow and low-amplitude.

### Plan the insertion and extraction carefully

Because there are no landmarks, your arrival and departure points must be recorded precisely. Use:

- multiverse anchor coordinates;
- local GPS or equivalent;
- inertial reference;
- altimeter reading;
- barometric baseline;
- visual panorama if visibility allows;
- a written description of the immediate terrain.

If your return method depends on a fixed point, mark it mentally and electronically, not with permanent artifacts. If you must place a temporary marker, make it fully retrievable and impossible to leave behind by accident.

### Establish communication and check-in protocols

Assume ordinary local infrastructure does not exist. Carry:

- a multiverse emergency beacon;
- a local communicator if compatible;
- scheduled check-in times;
- a missed-check-in response plan;
- a written route card left with someone outside the basin.

Keep communication devices in waterproof, shockproof, electrically shielded containers.

## 4. Physical Preparation

### Train for wet cold, not just cold

Many people prepare for cold but forget wet. Train by carrying a loaded pack in damp, cool conditions. Practice:

- changing layers with cold hands;
- operating zippers and buckles while wearing gloves;
- pitching shelter in wind and drizzle;
- cooking with a stove in wet conditions;
- walking slowly to avoid sweating;
- managing wet boots and socks.

### Acclimatize if possible

If the basin is genuinely high, arrive gradually if your multiverse method allows. If not, consider:

- pre-acclimatization elsewhere;
- conservative exertion on arrival;
- hydration;
- avoiding alcohol and sedatives;
- medications for altitude prophylaxis if appropriate and prescribed;
- carrying emergency oxygen.

Know the symptoms of altitude illness:

- persistent headache;
- nausea;
- dizziness;
- unusual fatigue;
- poor coordination;
- shortness of breath at rest;
- confusion;
- coughing or rattling breathing;
- inability to walk straight.

Any worsening neurological or respiratory symptom means descent, oxygen, or emergency treatment. Do not rationalize it away.

### Strengthen balance and foot stability

The terrain is abrasive, wet, and uncertain. Training should include:

- balance work;
- ankle stability;
- stepping over uneven rock;
- slow controlled descent;
- using trekking poles;
- getting up safely from wet ground;
- moving while looking down without losing route awareness.

## 5. Clothing Doctrine

The clothing system should be built around one principle:

> You will be wet. Your task is not to remain perfectly dry while moving; it is to protect your core, preserve a dry sleep system, and avoid hypothermia.

### Avoid cotton entirely

Cotton holds water, loses insulation, and dries slowly. It is dangerous here.

### Use a layered system

#### Base layer

Wicking synthetic or fine wool. Avoid heavy saturated cotton. Bring at least one spare base layer. For multi-day visits, bring more.

#### Mid layer

Synthetic insulation or fleece. It should retain warmth when damp. Down is risky as primary active insulation in constant moisture unless it is high-quality hydrophobic down and protected carefully.

#### Shell layer

A waterproof-breathable jacket and overtrousers. The jacket needs:

- a good hood;
- wrist closures;
- pit zips or ventilation;
- a high collar;
- enough room for layers underneath.

The shell will not keep you dry forever in continuous mist and wet rock, but it must block wind and shed water.

### Carry sacred dry clothing

You need at least one complete set of clothing that is reserved for shelter/sleep and protected in a dry bag:

- dry base layer;
- dry insulation;
- dry socks;
- dry gloves or liner gloves;
- dry hat if needed.

This set is not used for travel, cooking in bad weather, or “just a quick task.” It is your emergency thermal reserve.

### Manage sweat aggressively

Overheating leads to sweat, and sweat leads to chilling. Adjust layers before you become sweaty. In a dim, cold, wet basin, ventilation is as important as insulation.

### Head, neck, hands

Heat loss from head and neck matters, especially in wind and mist. Bring:

- warm hat;
- hood;
- neck gaiter or balaclava;
- thin liner gloves;
- waterproof gloves;
- overmitts if conditions are severe;
- spare gloves in dry bags.

Wet hands lose dexterity quickly. Small tasks — adjusting buckles, using a device, treating water — can become difficult. Practice operating gear with gloves.

### Eye and face protection

The light is dim, but altitude can still carry UV, and wind, mist, and mineral grit can irritate eyes. Bring:

- clear or low-light lenses;
- UV protection;
- anti-fog treatment or spare glasses;
- a soft face covering for wind and cold.

Do not assume gray light means safe light. The sky may feel soft while UV exposure remains significant.

## 6. Footwear and Foot Care

Feet are a major failure point in wet cold terrain.

### Boots

Use waterproof, stiff-soled, well-broken-in mountaineering or approach boots with good grip. They should:

- protect against sharp rock;
- support ankles on uneven ground;
- shed mud and sediment;
- accept crampons or microspikes if needed;
- have enough room for warm socks without crushing circulation.

Do not bring new boots.

### Gaiters

Gaiters are essential. They keep grit, water, and sediment out of boots and protect lower legs from abrasion.

### Socks

Use wool or synthetic socks. Avoid cotton. Bring:

- thin liner socks if they work for your feet;
- mid-weight hiking socks;
- several spare pairs;
- one dedicated dry sleep pair.

Change socks at the first sign of persistent dampness, hot spots, or skin wrinkling.

### Traction

Depending on ice conditions, carry:

- microspikes for thin ice and slick wet rock;
- light crampons if firmer ice is possible;
- trekking poles with baskets;
- a probe or staff for testing ground.

Thin ice and wet mineral crust can be treacherous. The place is not necessarily steep, but falling on wet rock or plunging a foot through a crust can cause injury, wet-out, or rapid heat loss.

### Foot care routine

At least once per day:

- inspect feet;
- dry skin;
- treat hot spots immediately;
- change socks;
- air feet inside shelter if conditions allow;
- keep toenails trimmed before the trip.

Immersion foot can develop even without freezing. If feet stay cold and wet for many hours, they become vulnerable.

## 7. Shelter and Sleep System

There is no natural shelter here worth relying on. You must bring your own.

### Tent

Use a stormworthy, double-wall tent with:

- full fly;
- strong pole structure;
- good ventilation;
- a vestibule large enough for wet gear and cooking in emergencies;
- low profile for wind;
- high-visibility details for fog.

A mountaineering tent is better than a lightweight summer tent. The basin’s moisture and wind can defeat fragile shelters.

If possible, choose non-metal or low-profile pole designs where practical, but do not sacrifice storm strength for theoretical electrical safety. In an electrical event, your behavior matters more: stay low, avoid being isolated, do not touch conductive poles or guy lines, and insulate yourself from wet ground.

### Ground protection

Bring:

- footprint;
- closed-cell foam sit pad;
- high-R-value sleeping pad;
- a second pad or foam backup if conditions are severe.

The ground will draw heat from you. Do not sit directly on wet stone or saturated sediment. Use a sit pad constantly.

### Sleeping bag

Use a sleeping bag rated colder than you think you need. Wet conditions demand margin.

Preferred insulation:

- synthetic bag, or
- high-quality hydrophobic down with careful moisture management.

Bring a dry bag or waterproof stuff sack for the bag. The bag must remain dry. If the bag wets out, your safety margin collapses.

### Sleep pad strategy

Use two layers if necessary:

- inflatable insulated pad;
- closed-cell foam pad beneath or as backup.

Foam is puncture-proof and can serve as an emergency sit pad, rescue sled surface, or insulating platform.

### Sleep in dry clothing

Always change into dry sleep clothes. Do not sleep in wet travel clothes unless survival requires it. Wet clothing inside a sleeping bag can wet the bag and destroy insulation.

### Sleep mask

The basin may have no true darkness. A sleep mask helps maintain circadian rhythm and improves rest. Bring one even if you normally do not use one.

## 8. Camp Selection

Camp placement is not just comfort; it is hazard management.

### Choose stable ground

Look for ground that appears older and more coherent:

- solid rock benches;
- established gravel;
- surfaces not recently cut or deposited;
- areas above active seeps and channels.

Avoid:

- fresh sediment fans;
- newly incised channels;
- saturated hollows;
- thin crusts;
- pool margins that may rise;
- runnels that may pulse;
- warm vents or steaming ground;
- depressions where gas might collect;
- obvious animal gathering points;
- fragile microbial or lichen patches.

### Avoid water paths

Even small channels may carry water suddenly after melt, rain, or pressure release. Do not camp in or beside active runnels. Look upstream for possible flow paths.

### Avoid thermal anomalies

Warm spots are tempting but dangerous. They may indicate:

- unstable ground;
- hidden water;
- scalding temperatures;
- toxic gases;
- thin crust;
- sudden steam pulses.

Observe them from a safe distance. Do not camp on them or use them as heating.

### Avoid electrical exposure

Do not camp on the highest bench or isolated rise. In an open basin, avoid being the most prominent object. But do not camp in a pool or drainage either. Choose a moderate, stable position that is not the highest point and not a water trap.

### Consider cold air drainage

Cold air sinks. If possible, camp slightly above hollow floors where cold pools collect, but not on exposed ridges.

### Leave space for animals

Do not camp between animals and water, warmth, or shelter. If birds or other animals repeatedly alter their path because of your camp, you are poorly placed.

## 9. Stove, Heat, and Cooking

There is no wood and no reason to expect fuel. Do not plan fires. Bring a reliable stove.

### Stove choice

A liquid-fuel stove is often more reliable in cold, wet, high-altitude conditions. Canister stoves can work if temperatures are not too low, but canister pressure drops in cold. If using canisters:

- keep them warm;
- bring a windscreen;
- bring more fuel than expected;
- avoid relying on them exclusively in severe cold.

### Fuel margin

You will need fuel for:

- melting ice or warming water;
- cooking;
- making warm drinks;
- occasional emergency heating;
- drying small items indirectly.

Bring more fuel than you think. Wet environments increase fuel use.

### Cooking safety

Never cook inside a sealed tent. Use the vestibule only with excellent ventilation and a stable setup. Keep flammable gear away. In a dim, wet, cramped environment, stove accidents are a real danger.

### Warm drinks

Warm drinks are not luxury; they are morale, hydration, and core temperature support. Plan for them.

## 10. Water

Water is abundant, but that does not mean it is safe.

### Treat all water

Assume water may contain:

- microbes;
- fine mineral sediment;
- dissolved salts;
- metals;
- geothermal chemicals;
- animal waste;
- alkaline or acidic mineral crust residues.

Use multiple stages:

1. Pre-filter through cloth, coffee filter, or pump prefilter to remove sediment.
2. Use a fine filter, ideally 0.2 micron or better.
3. Use chemical disinfection or UV if viruses or unusual microbes are possible.
4. Use activated carbon if taste, organics, or some chemicals are a concern.

If the mineral content is uncertain, avoid relying solely on chemical treatment. Some dissolved minerals are not removed by standard filters.

### Choose collection points carefully

Prefer:

- moving seeps;
- cold clear flow where possible;
- water not pooled in warm margins;
- water away from animal concentrations;
- water not stained with unusual mineral crust or odor.

Avoid:

- stagnant warm pools;
- water with oily film;
- water with strong sulfurous smell;
- water near gas pulses;
- water with dense biological scum unless absolutely necessary;
- water directly below animal roosts or carcasses.

### Carry water safely

Use:

- insulated bottles to prevent freezing;
- wide-mouth bottles if freezing is likely;
- backup soft bottles;
- hydration bladder only if the tube is insulated;
- spare purification method.

Cold weather can make hydration systems fail. Bottles are more reliable.

### Hydration discipline

Cold suppresses thirst, and altitude increases fluid loss. Drink regularly even if you do not feel thirsty. Watch urine color, frequency, and headache signs.

Bring electrolytes. Plain water alone may not be enough after hours of exertion.

## 11. Food

Do not plan to forage. The basin’s biomass is low, fragile, and potentially contaminated.

### Caloric needs

Cold, wet, high-altitude travel burns large amounts of energy. Plan for high-calorie intake, potentially much higher than normal daily needs. If the traveler is carrying a pack and moving cautiously over rough ground, they may need substantial extra calories.

### Food type

Bring:

- calorie-dense foods;
- warm meals;
- simple carbohydrates for quick energy;
- salty foods;
- comfort foods;
- low-waste packaging;
- foods that can be eaten with gloves or cold hands.

Avoid foods that create many crumbs, strong odors, or waste that must be carefully managed in camp.

### Food storage

All food, trash, and scented items should be sealed in odor-resistant bags or containers. The basin has birds, small predators, insects, and scavengers. Do not feed them accidentally.

There are no trees for hanging food, so use sealed containers and keep food inside the tent vestibule or otherwise secured. Never sleep with food.

## 12. Navigation

The basin’s scale is open but its visibility is soft. Distance is unreliable. Fog can erase features. Dimness and lack of shadows can flatten depth perception.

### Carry multiple navigation systems

Use:

- GPS or equivalent;
- compass;
- altimeter;
- barometer;
- inertial tracker if available;
- paper map if a map can be made;
- pace counting;
- bearing discipline;
- photographs of route if visibility allows.

Do not rely on one method. Electrical anomalies or local physics may degrade electronics.

### Set objective turn-back rules

Before moving, decide:

- maximum time outbound;
- maximum distance;
- minimum visibility;
- barometric threshold;
- health threshold;
- time needed to return before camp or extraction.

Turn back when the rule is reached, not when the landscape becomes frightening.

### Move slowly and deliberately

The terrain punishes haste. Use trekking poles. Test uncertain ground. Keep your center of gravity low. Step on durable rock rather than crusts or saturated sediment.

### Use tether protocol in fog

If visibility drops and the team must move, consider:

- short rope or tether between members;
- frequent verbal contact;
- assigned lead and sweep;
- frequent position checks;
- no wandering off to “look around.”

In very poor visibility, stopping and sheltering may be safer than moving.

### Do not create permanent markers

The basin has no built layer. Do not build cairns, carve marks, tie flags, or leave trail markers unless it is an emergency and you remove every trace afterward. Prefer electronic waypoints and careful route discipline.

## 13. Terrain Hazards

### Saturated ground

Some ground is neither solid rock nor liquid water. It may:

- suck at boots;
- hide voids;
- collapse under weight;
- chill feet quickly;
- form new channels when disturbed.

Test with a pole. If the surface dimples, ripples, or water wells around your steps, avoid it.

### Thin crusts

Pale mineral crusts may be beautiful and dangerous. They may be thin, sharp, or floating. Do not step on them unless their support is certain.

### New channels

Channels are exploratory. They may seem dry, then carry water after a pulse. Do not stand in them while observing upstream processes. They are the landscape’s first drafts.

### Thin ice

Thin ice may cover shallow water or saturated sediment. Test before committing weight. If ice cracks, retreat along your previous tracks.

### Wet rock

Wet rock is slippery, especially with ash-fine sediment or algal film. Move with care. Avoid sudden shifts. Keep hands free when possible.

### Undercut edges

Newly cut gullies may have unstable edges. Do not stand close to a crumbling lip. The ground has not learned its own strength.

### Falling into cold water

If someone enters cold water:

- get them out quickly;
- replace wet layers if possible;
- provide insulation from ground;
- give warm drink;
- monitor for hypothermia;
- avoid further exposure.

Even shallow cold water can be dangerous if it saturates clothing and the person cannot dry.

## 14. Geothermal and Warm Points

Warm points are ecologically important and operationally dangerous.

### Benefits

They may provide:

- liquid water in cold conditions;
- animal observation opportunities;
- local warmth;
- vapor landmarks;
- insight into subsurface heat.

### Dangers

They may also involve:

- scalding water;
- unstable crust;
- hidden voids;
- toxic gases;
- sudden pressure release;
- mineral slicks;
- rapid fog generation.

### Rules for warm points

- Do not camp on or beside them.
- Do not block their flow.
- Do not touch water without testing temperature.
- Do not linger in low vapor-filled hollows.
- Do not breathe concentrated steam or gas.
- Do not assume warmth means safety.

If a warm area smells of sulfur, causes dizziness, makes breathing uncomfortable, or has dead ground nearby, leave immediately.

### Gas monitoring

Carry a portable gas monitor if possible:

- oxygen level;
- carbon dioxide;
- hydrogen sulfide;
- methane;
- sulfur dioxide if volcanic.

If you lack instruments, use conservative behavior: avoid depressions with visible vapor, avoid odd smells, avoid areas where animals refuse to go, and leave if you feel pressure, headache, or nausea.

## 15. Electrical Charge

The basin’s electricity is not theatrical, but it matters.

### Warning signs

- hair lifting;
- skin prickling;
- mist seeming to tremble;
- pools brightening;
- ozone smell;
- unusual stillness;
- animals becoming suddenly quiet;
- broad fog brightening without visible sun.

### What to do

If charge builds:

- move away from open water films and pools if possible;
- do not hold trekking poles, antennas, or long conductive objects upright;
- do not become the highest point;
- avoid isolated protrusions;
- put sensitive electronics in shielded bags;
- crouch low on an insulating pad if caught in the open;
- keep feet together;
- do not lie flat;
- avoid touching metal gear with bare skin during the event.

### Protect electronics

Carry critical devices in:

- waterproof bags;
- anti-static bags;
- conductive pouches or improvised Faraday protection;
- padded cases.

Do not leave communication devices exposed during charge events.

### Fuel caution

Static and fuel vapors are a bad combination. When handling stove fuel, do so with ventilation, care, and no active electrical event.

## 16. Weather and Light

The basin’s weather is continuous rather than dramatic.

### Watch small changes

- fog thickening;
- drizzle beginning without obvious cause;
- frost forming;
- seeps strengthening;
- surfaces darkening;
- wind changing direction;
- pressure dropping;
- pools widening.

These small signs matter more than distant storm theatrics.

### Dimness and depth perception

The light is even and shadowless. This makes it hard to judge:

- slope angle;
- water depth;
- ground texture;
- distance;
- cracks;
- subtle drops.

Use a headlamp at an angle to create artificial shadows when inspecting terrain. Move slowly. Do not trust your first glance.

### Circadian disruption

If there is no true darkness, your body may lose rhythm. Use:

- a watch;
- planned meal times;
- planned sleep window;
- sleep mask;
- red or amber headlamp mode at “night”;
- consistent team routines.

## 17. Wildlife Conduct

Animals are present but concentrated. Your behavior must prevent disturbance.

### General rules

- observe from distance;
- do not feed animals;
- do not approach nests, roosts, or feeding clusters;
- do not block movement routes to water or warmth;
- keep food sealed;
- do not leave scraps;
- do not use strong scents;
- do not play sounds or make loud noises.

### Birds

Birds may appear as small dark facts in the gray basin. They are vulnerable because they concentrate at limited productive points. If your presence causes repeated alarm calls, wing-fluttering, or departure, you are too close.

### Predators

Predators here are likely quick-eyed, patient, and opportunistic. They may investigate unusual objects. Keep gear closed. Do not leave food, waste, or scented items exposed.

### Amphibians and thermal runnels

If there are amphibians in warm runnels, they are tied to rare liquid warmth. Do not touch them, do not alter the water flow, and do not introduce contaminants.

### Insects and larvae

Midges or equivalent insects may gather near water. They are part of the food web. Use physical barriers rather than chemical repellents near water:

- head net;
- wrist cuffs;
- fine mesh over vents if needed;
- avoid scented products.

If repellent is medically necessary, apply carefully away from pools and wash hands afterward.

## 18. Ecology and Planetary Protection

This is a young landscape. Damage may persist because biological recovery is slow.

### Sterilize before and after

If multiverse travel allows cross-contamination, clean gear before entry and after exit:

- brush boots;
- remove soil and organic matter;
- disinfect where appropriate;
- dry thoroughly;
- seal used clothing until cleaned.

Do not carry organisms into or out of the basin inadvertently.

### Stay on durable surfaces

Prefer:

- bare rock;
- established gravel;
- snow if present and stable;
- already disturbed ground if unavoidable.

Avoid:

- moss;
- lichen;
- microbial films;
- new sediment;
- algal blooms;
- fragile crusts.

### Do not alter water flow

Do not:

- dig channels;
- dam seeps;
- move stones to create paths;
- build drainage;
- redirect runnels;
- stack mineral crusts.

The basin is still deciding its drainage. Even small changes may be significant.

### Pack out everything

This includes:

- food waste;
- packaging;
- toilet waste;
- hygiene products;
- used wipes;
- repair scraps;
- broken gear;
- fuel residue if possible;
- anything that falls.

In a place with no built layer, even a forgotten wrapper becomes an artifact.

### Human waste

Because the ground is saturated, cold, and thin, ordinary catholes may be inappropriate. Use portable waste bags if possible. If waste must be buried, do so far from water, in suitable substrate, and only if permitted by local ethics and conditions. In the most fragile version of this basin, pack out all human waste.

### Washing

Avoid washing directly in pools or streams. Use:

- hand sanitizer;
- small amounts of water away from sources;
- biodegradable products only if necessary;
- wide dispersal of greywater away from water and wildlife;
- or better, pack out greywater in a sealed container.

## 19. Health Monitoring

The basin’s dangers are cumulative. Monitor yourself and companions.

### Hypothermia

Early signs:

- shivering;
- clumsiness;
- irritability;
- poor judgment;
- fatigue;
- slurred speech;
- apathy.

Response:

- stop;
- insulate from ground;
- add dry layers;
- provide calories;
- warm drinks;
- shelter;
- prevent further wetting.

### Frostbite

Less likely if the cold is patient rather than extreme, but wet wind can still freeze extremities. Watch ears, nose, cheeks, fingers, toes.

### Altitude illness

Do not push through worsening symptoms. Descend or use oxygen if available. Know emergency medications only if trained and authorized.

### Dehydration

Common in cold altitude because people forget to drink. Watch for dark urine, headache, dizziness, fatigue.

### Wet feet

Inspect frequently. Treat maceration and hot spots early.

### Respiratory irritation

Cold air, mineral dust, volcanic aerosols, or gases may irritate lungs. Carry a mask or respirator. If breathing becomes difficult, leave the area.

### Eye strain

Even dim light can cause strain with wind and glare off wet surfaces. Use eye protection.

### Psychological strain

The openness may produce:

- unease;
- derealization;
- restlessness;
- feeling watched;
- impatience;
- desire to rush;
- fascination that overrides caution.

Counter this with routine:

- regular check-ins;
- scheduled breaks;
- hydration;
- navigation tasks;
- note-taking;
- clear objectives;
- permission to turn back.

## 20. Movement Strategy

### Move like a surveyor, not a tourist

Every step should be chosen. Ask:

- Is this surface coherent?
- Does it weep, dimple, or shift?
- Is it above or below a possible flow path?
- Is there a stable exit if it gives way?
- Am I moving away from or toward a hazard?
- Can I retrace this route in fog?

### Use poles constantly

Poles are not just for balance. They are probes, brakes, rhythm tools, and ground testers.

### Rest on insulation

When stopping, put a foam pad under you. Wet stone draws heat quickly. A short rest without insulation can become a heat-loss event.

### Control pace

The correct pace is slow enough to avoid sweating and fast enough to avoid chilling. This may feel unnaturally deliberate. That is appropriate.

### Keep hands free

Avoid burying essential items in the pack where accessing them requires removing gloves or losing balance. Use accessible pockets and tether small tools.

### Tether critical gear

In a place where you bring the first artifact, losing even a small object is ethically significant. Tether:

- gloves;
- water bottles;
- tools;
- navigation devices;
- camera;
- stove parts;
- dry bags;
- stakes.

## 21. Observation and Documentation

The basin rewards careful observation because its processes are exposed.

### Bring simple instruments

### Sound recording

The basin is quiet but not silent. Recording its small sounds can be valuable. But keep equipment low and protected during electrical charge events.

## 23. Emergency Planning

Assume self-rescue is the only rescue.

### If lost

- Stop.
- Mark your current position electronically.
- Put on insulation.
- Avoid wandering.
- Try to identify known bearings if visible.
- If visibility is bad, shelter and wait if that is safer than moving.
- Use beacon if required.

Do not chase vague shapes in fog.

### If wet out

A wet-out is when clothing and sleep insulation become saturated and lose effectiveness. It is a major emergency in cold wet terrain.

Response:

- get to shelter;
- change into dry layers;
- protect sleep system;
- generate warmth;
- eat and drink;
- dry items if possible;
- consider ending the trip.

### If altitude symptoms worsen

- stop ascent;
- descend if possible;
- use oxygen if available;
- do not leave the affected person alone;
- evacuate if symptoms are severe.

### If someone falls into water or thin crust

- do not rush in blindly;
- extend pole, rope, or pack;
- spread weight;
- pull them to stable ground;
- insulate immediately;
- change wet clothing;
- monitor for hypothermia.

### If electrical storm arrives

- move away from water if possible;
- avoid high isolated positions;
- lower conductive objects;
- insulate yourself from ground;
- assume a low crouch with feet together;
- protect communications devices;
- wait out the event.

### If gas is suspected

- leave immediately;
- move crosswind or upwind if possible;
- avoid low hollows;
- do not rely on smell alone;
- monitor oxygen if possible.

## 25. Arrival Protocol

When you arrive, do not immediately push forward.

### First minutes

1. Confirm extraction anchor and return method.
2. Check atmospheric readings.
3. Check oxygen saturation and breathing comfort.
4. Look for immediate hazards: unstable ground, gas, water paths, electrical signs.
5. Calibrate navigation.
6. Record arrival point.
7. Hydrate.
8. Adjust clothing before sweating or chilling.

### First movement

Move a short distance and assess:

- footing;
- visibility;
- wind;
- temperature;
- team morale;
- route clarity.

Do not let the beauty or strangeness pull you beyond your margin.

### First camp

If staying overnight, choose camp early rather than late. The basin’s light and fog can make it difficult to judge when conditions are deteriorating.

Once camp is established:

- secure shelter;
- change into dry layers before becoming cold;
- hydrate;
- eat;
- store food;
- check weather;
- set watch schedule;
- agree on night duties if needed;
- keep emergency gear accessible.

## 26. Daily Routine

A disciplined routine helps counter the place’s unresolved emptiness.

### Morning

- check weather and pressure;
- inspect shelter for condensation or frost;
- melt/prepare water;
- eat warm food;
- inspect feet and hands;
- pack carefully;
- dry what can be dried;
- sweep camp for micro-trash.

### Travel

- set time and distance limits;
- navigate continuously;
- test ground;
- take scheduled breaks on insulation;
- eat small amounts often;
- drink often;
- watch for electrical or gas signs;
- observe animals without approaching.

### Evening

- establish camp before fatigue;
- change to dry clothes;
- warm drink;
- review route;
- record observations;
- check health;
- secure food and waste;
- protect electronics;
- sleep according to schedule, not according to the strange light.

## 27. What Not to Do

- Do not assume the landscape is stable because it looks quiet.
- Do not drink untreated water because it looks clear.
- Do not camp beside warm vents.
- Do not walk on pale crusts without certainty.
- Do not follow new channels upstream during melt or rain.
- Do not build cairns or leave flags.
- Do not feed animals.
- Do not rely on solar power alone.
- Do not let your sleep system get wet.
- Do not continue navigation into fog without fixed bearings.
- Do not ignore early altitude symptoms.
- Do not treat warm points as comfortable rest spots.
- Do not leave anything behind, even biodegradable items, unless ethically and legally appropriate under strict wilderness rules.

## 28. The Correct Attitude

The basin is not a place to conquer. It is a place to pass through with exact care.

You should think like:

- a mountaineer, because of altitude and exposure;
- a wetland traveler, because water governs everything;
- an engineer, because gradients, pressure, heat, and flow are active;
- a field biologist, because life is fragile and concentrated;
- a conservator, because your presence should leave no record;
- a patient observer, because the place is still becoming.

The emotional core of preparation is humility. The basin does not need you. It will not hold your shape. It will continue its slow, damp, mineral work whether you witness it or not. Your job is to enter without demanding that it become comfortable, legible, or meaningful in human terms.

Bring your shelter, your heat, your water treatment, your navigation, your oxygen margin, your dry socks, your restraint.

Bring no assumptions.

Leave no artifacts.

And if the fog closes, the ground softens, the air thins, the hair on your arms lifts, or the basin begins to feel less like scenery and more like a vast machine slowly laying down its first layers, remember the simplest rule:

> Stop, protect your core, protect your exit, and do not let curiosity outrun ground truth.
