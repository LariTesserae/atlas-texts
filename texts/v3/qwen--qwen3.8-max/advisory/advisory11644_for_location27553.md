Prepare as if you are going to visit a place that has outlived drama.

This is not a young wilderness of predators, passes, and sharp horizons. It is not a haunted ruin in the usual sense, and not a dead city waiting for interpreters. It is an immense high tableland at the end of mountain-making: cold, thin, damp, patient, urban without crowds, alive without animals, and quietly inconsistent in the way very old realities sometimes become. An ordinary human traveler can visit it, but only if they prepare for slowness, solitude, moisture, altitude, and the psychological pressure of a world that does not revolve around animal life.

The greatest danger is not that the place will attack you. It probably will not. The danger is that you will misread it. You will assume distance behaves normally because the light looks ordinary. You will assume a city is abandoned because no one is visible. You will assume silence means emptiness. You will become wet, cold, tired, and perceptually careless. Or you will begin treating the slow, distributed maintenance of the landscape as something meant for you.

So preparation should be practical, psychological, and ethical at the same time.

---

## 1. First, understand what kind of place you are entering

The tableland is:

- **High and cold**, with thin, dry air that nonetheless gathers moisture in hollows, vegetation, stone seams, and fog tides.
- **Immense and almost level**, not with dramatic peaks but with long rises, shallow depressions, worn terraces, and enormous horizons.
- **Wet by accumulation**, not by rivers or rainstorms. Water appears through fog, condensation, temporary channels, ice lenses, and black shallow pools.
- **Lush but not soft**: moss, cushion plants, lichens, stiff ferns, black-green grasses, glass-barked shrubs, fungal networks, and rare precise flowering events.
- **Animal-less**. No insects, birds, fish, tracks, bones, shells, burrows, or wingbeats. The absence is total and pressurized.
- **Urban without population**. Cities exist as patterns of maintenance: channels, cisterns, fog nets, walls, stairways, terraces, tools, and low buildings. They seem inhabited by process rather than by bodies.
- **Reality-worn**. Distances may drift. Streets may fold. Doorways may not lead where expected. Reflections may be too faithful, or slightly wrong. The light remains ordinary, which makes the wrongness more disturbing.

You are entering a place where the human scale is irrelevant. Your body, breath, appetite, urgency, and need for quick feedback will feel intrusive. The correct attitude is not conquest, not exploration as domination, but careful passage.

---

## 2. Decide what kind of visit is appropriate

An ordinary human should not plan this as a casual hike. The location is not necessarily hostile, but it is indifferent to animal needs.

Good reasons to visit:

- Quiet survey.
- Contemplative passage.
- Mapping of stable landmarks.
- Study of botanical, hydrological, or architectural patterns.
- Multiverse travel experience of low-urgency, non-animal ecosystems.
- Respectful observation of distributed maintenance systems.

Bad reasons to visit:

- Seeking treasure.
- Seeking contact with hidden inhabitants.
- Seeking dramatic adventure.
- Seeking solitude as escape without preparation for psychological pressure.
- Attempting to “fix” or improve the cities.
- Attempting to harvest, cultivate, or transplant local life.
- Bringing animals.
- Planning a fast crossing of the tableland.

If you are traveling with others, keep the group small. Two or three people is better than a crowd. Large groups create noise, waste, heat, disturbance, and too many competing perceptions of unstable space. If you travel alone, you must have an external check-in system and a strict return plan.

Do not bring pets, mounts, drones with loud rotors, or any animal companion. The absence of fauna is one of the defining conditions of the place. An animal would be profoundly out of context, likely stressed, possibly endangered by spores, cold, thin air, or the lack of familiar ecological cues. You would also become a source of animal contamination: hair, scent, microbes, waste, fear, and noise.

## 3. Psychological preparation

The silence here is not peaceful in a simple way. It is pressurized. Human beings are accustomed to environments shaped by animal presence: insects, birds, wind in grass, small movements, distant calls, signs of predation and flight. This place has none of that. Life exists, but on a slower timescale. Moss creeps. Roots widen cracks. Fungal networks spread. Frost moves stone. Water appears and disappears. Cities maintain themselves without visible hands.

Prepare your mind for:

### Deep quiet

The landscape absorbs sound. Moss, fog, damp stone, and vegetation soften everything. Sounds are small, clear, widely spaced, and sometimes delayed or unrepeatable. You may hear a drip long after the event that made it. You may hear a creak once and never again.

Do not fill the silence unnecessarily. Avoid loud music, shouting, or constant speech. But also do not rely on total silence for orientation. Bring a quiet timepiece with a soft tick if it helps ground you. Some travelers find that a small mechanical rhythm prevents the mind from inventing presence where there is none.

### Absence of animal reassurance

You may feel watched, not because something is looking at you, but because the environment is organized in ways that suggest intention. Channels are clear. Tools are placed. Moss grows over roofs in maintained patterns. Doorways are framed by trained shrubs. The city is not random.

Resist the urge to personify everything. Say to yourself: *This may be process, not personality. This may be maintenance without a maintainer. This may be intelligence distributed through root, water, stone, and routine.*

### Perceptual unease

The light is ordinary. Shadows fall correctly, mostly. Colors are pale but natural. This makes the wrongness of distance, shape, and connection more disturbing. You may see a street stretch for a day in one direction and fold back on itself in another. You may count three corners of a building from one angle and four from another. You may see a pool reflect a sky whose clouds do not match the sky above.

Do not treat these as personal failures. They are properties of the place. But also do not become fascinated to the point of recklessness. Anomaly is not an invitation.

### Slowness

Your body will want to move faster than the landscape rewards. Distances are deceptive. Weather is local. Fog can create a season in one basin. Frost can arrive without warning. Routes may shorten or lengthen. The place does not respond to urgency.

Practice patience before you go. Spend time in cold, quiet landscapes. Walk slowly with weight. Sit still in fog. Learn to observe without naming. Learn to wait without becoming irritated.

---

## 4. Physical preparation

You should prepare as for a high-altitude cold plateau with damp microclimates and uncertain footing.

### Altitude

The air is high-altitude air: thin, dry, and cold. Even if the elevation does not look dramatic, your body will feel it.

Before visiting:

- Acclimatize in a high, cool environment if possible.
- Learn the early symptoms of altitude sickness
