**Preparing to Visit the Intake Sea**

An ordinary human multiverse traveler should not prepare for the Intake Sea as if it were a natural ocean, a flooded ruin, or a recreational dive site. It is closer to entering a vast submerged factory that has been filled with water and then given an ecology. The place is cold, pressurized, loud, electrically active, current-driven, and governed by machine schedules. Its beauty is real, but its dangers are industrial.

The core rule is this:

> **Do not treat the Intake Sea as water first. Treat it as a working machine that happens to be liquid.**

You are not a fish, not a native organism, and not a component. You are a loose object, a possible contaminant, a potential obstruction, and — if careless — a casualty. Preparation should therefore combine deep-water diving discipline, industrial safety procedure, meteorological awareness, acoustic navigation, and ecological humility.

---

## 1. Decide How You Will Be Present There

For an ordinary human body, direct immersion without protection is not viable. The water is cold, pressurized, chemically treated, and full of machinery. The first preparation is choosing the right envelope for your body.

### Safest: Observation Submersible or Pressure Sphere

If your purpose is observation, the best preparation is to visit inside a certified one-person or two-person submersible rated for the basin’s maximum depth. This should be:

- Pressure-rated well beyond the planned depth.
- Equipped with external lights, cameras, hydrophones, and manipulators if needed.
- Fitted with acoustic communication and positioning gear.
- Protected against entanglement with shrouded thrusters.
- Equipped with emergency buoyancy and a homing beacon.
- Small enough to avoid being mistaken for cargo, debris, or fauna, but large enough to be detected by facility sensors.

This is the most sensible option for a traveler without specialized diving training. You remain at one atmosphere, avoid decompression issues, and reduce the chance of becoming entangled or electrocuted.

### Best for Immersive Work: Atmospheric Diving Suit

If you need to move near structures, inspect surfaces, or experience the basin more directly, an atmospheric diving suit — a powered hard suit that keeps you at surface pressure — is preferable to wet diving. Such a suit should be:

- Rated for the expected depth plus a generous safety margin.
- Made of corrosion-resistant materials compatible with treated water, chlorinated traces, brine, and industrial particulates.
- Fitted with external insulation or thermal control.
- Equipped with redundant life support, scrubbers, oxygen sensors, and power.
- Fitted with non-conductive exterior panels or coatings where possible.
- Equipped with acoustic communication, sonar, depth sensors, compass/gyro, inertial navigation, and a local transponder.
- Fitted with shrouded or guarded thrusters, not exposed propellers.
- Equipped with emergency surface marker, acoustic beacon, and locator strobe.

The atmospheric suit reduces pressure and breathing-gas problems, but it introduces others: limited agility, snag hazards, battery dependence, and the need for mechanical maintenance. It is not casual wear.

### Only for Trained Divers: Closed-Circuit Mixed-Gas Diving

If you are a highly trained technical or commercial diver, you might enter the Intake Sea in a heated drysuit, full-face helmet, and closed-circuit rebreather. This is more intimate but far more dangerous. You would need:

- Mixed-gas planning appropriate to depth.
- Redundant bailout systems.
- Heated undergarments or hot-water suit.
- Full-face mask or helmet with communication.
- Entanglement cutters and redundancy in every critical system.
- Surface support and decompression capability.
- Strict abort criteria.

Open-circuit scuba is less ideal because bubbles may disturb fauna, interfere with sensors, collect under structures, or attract unwanted attention from machines or animals. If open circuit is unavoidable, it should be considered a compromise, not a first choice.

### What You Should Not Do

Do not prepare to enter the Intake Sea as an unprotected swimmer. Do not assume multiverse travel grants environmental resistance. Do not rely on “ordinary diving gear” unless it has been specifically tested against the basin’s water chemistry, currents, electrical fields, and industrial traffic.

Do not enter through active intake stacks, turbine pits, outfall tunnels, drainage galleries, or flood-platform grates unless you are absolutely certain they are isolated, depressurized, locked out, and authorized. In this place, a doorway can become a throat.

## 2. Learn the Intake Sea as a System

The Intake Sea is not stable in the way a natural seabed is stable. It has weather, tides, thermal currents, electrical boundaries, feeding events, maintenance cycles, and acoustic schedules. You should prepare by learning its operational rhythm.

Before visiting, obtain as much of the following as possible:

### Depth and Pressure Charts

You need to know:

- Maximum depth of the intended area.
- Depth layers: upper warm currents, middle neutral water, lower cold volumes, floor trenches.
- Pressure gradients near intakes, outflows, sealed doors, and pump galleries.
- Rated limits of your suit or vehicle.

Do not assume the “floor” is safe just because it is flat. Much of the floor is grating, plating, channels, access hatches, or turbine pits. Some surfaces are reflective and smooth; others are coarse mosaics of rivets, welds, and anode blocks. Footing is artificial and may be slippery, sharp, or unstable.

### Pump Tide Schedules

The basin has tides, but they are mechanical. Intakes pull water in; outflows release it. These cycles can create long, patient draws followed by slow booms of returning flow. You must know:

- When intakes cycle.
- Which sectors are affected.
- How strong the scheduled currents become.
- Whether there are flushing cycles that temporarily transform whole regions into hazardous flow fields.
- When “slack water” occurs, if such a state even exists.

Plan your route so that you are never near an intake during drawdown. If you must cross current lanes, do so during low-flow periods and with enough reserve thrust to escape a surprise reversal.

### Thermal and Brine Weather

Heat from engines creates rising plumes. Cold water sinks along walls. Dense brine discharge can crawl along the floor in slow rivers, while fresher cooling water rides above it. These layers can affect:

- Visibility.
- Buoyancy.
- Propulsion efficiency.
- Thermal load.
- Sensor readings.
- Animal behavior.

Prepare for sudden thermoclines. A warm plume may feel comforting at first, but it may also carry particulates, chemical traces, or turbulence. A cold sheet along a wall may be clean but can sap heat quickly. Crossing a brine layer may change your trim or ballast behavior.

### Surface Rain Events

One of the strangest weather events in the Intake Sea is imported rain. Storm water entering through open intake stacks can descend as a curtain of colder, bubble-rich freshwater through the submerged basin. This can create:

- Sudden visibility loss.
- Freshwater lens formation.
- Temperature shock.
- Bubble fields that confuse sonar.
- Changes in salinity and buoyancy.
- Altered behavior of fish and machines.

If surface storms are expected above, assume the underwater environment may change without warning. Do not linger beneath active intake stacks during rain events unless you are in a designated shelter.

### Acoustic Calendar

Sound is the true weather of the Intake Sea. You should learn:

- The meaning of sonar beacon pulses.
- The pattern of alarm test sequences.
- The sound of gates seating.
- The whine of electric drives.
- The low boom of valves and outfalls.
- The percussion of harvest or feeding protocols.
- The acoustic signatures of cleaning drones, carriers, and pump cycles.

If possible, carry a sound library and train yourself to recognize key signals. In a place where sight fades into blue-green haze, hearing becomes the primary sense for anticipating events.

## 3. Prepare Your Body

Even with advanced equipment, your body remains the weakest system.

### Medical Readiness

An ordinary human should be medically cleared for:

- Cold-water immersion.
- Pressure exposure, if wet diving.
- Confined-space life support.
- High-noise environments.
- Limited mobility and restricted exit.
- Potential delay before rescue.

Pay special attention to:

- Cardiovascular health.
- Lung function.
- Ear, nose, and sinus equalization, if wet diving.
- Dental health, since pressure changes can reveal hidden problems.
- Hydration and nutrition.
- Cold tolerance.
- Anxiety and claustrophobia.

If you are using an atmospheric suit or submersible, pressure equalization is less of an issue, but confinement, noise, vibration, and motion sickness still matter.

### Cold Management

The water is described as cool, not glacial — perhaps around eight to ten degrees, possibly warmer near effluents. That is still cold enough to become dangerous over time. Prepare with:

- Heated undersuit or hot-water circulation if diving.
- Thermal control in a hard suit or submersible.
- Insulated gloves and hood if any wet exposure is possible.
- Core temperature monitoring.
- Warm fluids and high-calorie food before and after.
- Abort criteria based on hand dexterity, shivering, or core temperature drift.

Do not be fooled by warm effluent plumes. They may create local comfort but can also carry particulates, chemicals, or biological growth. Lingering in them may foul your suit or expose you to unknown process water.

### Hydration and Waste

You cannot drink the basin water. It is treated, mineral-sharp, faintly chlorinated, galvanic, and likely unsafe. Carry clean water and a way to drink inside your suit or helmet. For long visits, plan waste management. Do not discharge bodily waste into the basin. The environment is already delicately managed; adding contaminants is rude at best and hazardous at worst.

## 4. Prepare Your Mind

The Intake Sea is psychologically demanding. It is not merely underwater; it is architecturally total. There is no natural ground, no wild stone, no soil, no root, no ordinary shore. Every surface is built. Every current is scheduled. Every sound may mean something mechanical.

### Expect Industrial Sublimity

The place is vast and hollow. Objects are rare and enormous. You may travel through open blue-green vacancy for a long time before encountering a pump tower, a gantry line, or a sealed factory block. This can feel awe-inspiring, but also disorienting.

Prepare mentally for:

- Agoraphobia in three dimensions.
- The feeling of being watched by machines.
- The sense that sounds are closer than they are.
- The possibility that a visible landmark will take much longer to reach than expected.
- The emotional impact of constant low-frequency noise.
- The tension of being inside a system that does not care about your comfort.

Practice calm, task-based thinking. Do not let awe override procedure.

### Accept That You Are Not Free in a Simple Sense

The fauna lives inside a manufactured contract. You will too. Your movement depends on schedules, currents, electrical boundaries, acoustic warnings, and machine traffic. Do not expect to go wherever you wish simply because there is open water. In the Intake Sea, open water is not empty space; it is circulation space, acoustic space, and hazard space.

A good visitor learns to ask:  
*Where is the water going? What is running? What is about to open, close, flush, heat, cool, feed, harvest, or clean?*

## 5. Obtain Permissions and Local Intelligence

Even if the Intake Sea appears automated, assume it is governed. Somewhere, something manufactures, processes, refines, cultures, cools, compresses, and ships. Production continues. You are entering a workplace.

Before arrival, seek:

- Operational authority or custodian contact.
- Visitor clearance, if such a category exists.
- Local transponder codes.
- Acoustic identification protocols.
- Emergency frequencies.
- Restricted-zone maps.
- Electrical exclusion charts.
- Maintenance schedules.
- Harvest or feeding protocols.
- Decontamination requirements.
- Entry and exit lock coordinates.
- Safe refuge points.

If no authority can be identified, proceed as if the environment is both fragile and hostile. Leave nothing, touch nothing, and avoid interfering with machinery or organisms.

Ask these questions if possible:

1. What is the maximum current expected during pump cycles?
2. Which intakes are active during my visit?
3. Are there scheduled flushing events?
4. Which areas have electric exclusion fields?
5. What acoustic alarms indicate immediate evacuation?
6. Where are safe standing zones or refuge bells?
7. Are cleaning drones or scraping arms active?
8. Are there harvest protocols involving guiding sounds or flashes?
9. What is the water chemistry: salinity, pH, chlorine, hydrocarbons, dissolved metals?
10. What is the protocol for foreign objects or organisms?

If you cannot answer these questions, your preparation is incomplete.

## 6. Equipment: What to Bring

The exact gear depends on your mode of entry, but the principles are constant: redundancy, insulation, communication, navigation, hazard detection, and no loose objects.

### Life Support

For any immersed human, life support is sacred.

Bring:

- Primary breathing system rated for the environment.
- Bailout gas supply if wet diving.
- Redundant oxygen sensors.
- Carbon dioxide scrubbing if using closed-circuit or atmospheric systems.
- Humidity control.
- Pressure sensors.
- Manual overrides.
- Emergency power.
- Alarms that are tactile and visual as well as auditory, because the environment is already loud.

Do not trust a single sensor. The water’s particulates, chlorinated traces, and industrial aerosols may foul exposed components.

### Thermal Protection

Bring:

- Heated suit or suit lining.
- Backup heating elements.
- Thermal gloves or heated manipulator liners.
- Hood or helmet thermal layer.
- Temperature monitors.
- Emergency thermal blanket or insulated survival pod if using a submersible.
- Warm drinks in a sealed hydration system.

Remember: metal feels colder than stone in this water. If your suit contacts conductive structures, heat may be drawn away quickly.

### Buoyancy and Trim

If diving:

- Variable buoyancy system.
- Redundant inflation.
- Quick but controlled dump valves.
- Proper weight distribution.
- Emergency lift capability.
- No loose weights that can fall into grating or machinery.

In the Intake Sea, loose objects are dangerous. A dropped weight can enter a drain gallery, strike a turbine housing, foul a cable run, or become conductive debris near electrical systems. Everything must be secured, lanyarded, or stored.

### Propulsion

Movement is not walking. It is drifting, swimming, riding flow lanes, and timing pump tides.

Bring:

- Ducted or shrouded thrusters.
- Backup propulsion.
- Fins if diving, but stiff enough for current work.
- Current meter.
- Drift indicator.
- Ability to move laterally, not just forward.

Avoid exposed propellers. They can entangle in cables, kelp-like polymer frames, netting, biofilm racks, or animal life. Also avoid high-speed thrust near particulate drifts; stirring artificial sediment can reduce visibility and contaminate machinery.

### Navigation

The basin is vast and feature-poor. Visual distance can deceive. Sound can deceive in another way. You need instruments.

Bring:

- Acoustic positioning system.
- Local beacon network, if permitted.
- Inertial navigation.
- Depth gauge.
- Gyrocompass, because magnetic compasses may be distorted by steel architecture.
- Sonar imaging for obstacles and structures.
- Range finder.
- Chart of terraces, platforms, service roads, towers, gantries, and turbine pits.
- Acoustic landmark log.
- Backup paper or slate charts.

Do not rely on “swimming toward the tower.” In a basin this large, a tower may remain apparently unchanged in size for a very long time.

### Communication

Radio behaves poorly underwater. Assume acoustic communication unless you have a hardline or local technology.

Bring:

- Acoustic modem or voice system.
- Backup acoustic pinger.
- Emergency locator beacon.
- Prearranged check-in intervals.
- Light signals.
- Visual marker flares or strobes approved for underwater use.
- A “lost comms” plan.
- A “no response” extraction trigger.

If the basin uses sonar beacons or procedural alarms, your communication system should not interfere with them. Transmit only on authorized channels.

### Illumination

Light is ordinary in the Intake Sea, but it attenuates normally too. Reds thin out. Yellows dull. Distance becomes blue-green, then gray, then suggestion. Maintenance darks can occur.

Bring:

- Primary helmet or vehicle lights.
- Backup lights.
- Wide-angle and spot modes.
- Low-glare setting for close work.
- Red or dim mode if needed for fauna observation, if local rules permit.
- Spare power cells.
- Non-blinding etiquette: do not shine lights directly into machine sensors, animal eyes, or worker habitats.

Be careful with flashes. Sudden strobes may be mistaken for operational signals, harvest cues, or alarms.

### Hazard Sensors

The Intake Sea contains invisible boundaries. Prepare to detect them.

Useful sensors include:

- Depth and pressure sensors.
- Temperature probe.
- Salinity or conductivity probe.
- Turbidity sensor.
- Current meter.
- Acoustic warning receiver.
- Electrical field detector, if local technology permits.
- Vibration sensor.
- Water chemistry sniffer for chlorine, hydrocarbons, or unusual dissolved salts.

If you feel tingling, hair-prickling, muscle twitching, or strange metallic sensations, treat it as an electrical warning and retreat immediately.

### Physical Protection

The flood is young. Edges are sharp. Weld beads are crisp. Paint has been flayed in narrow lines. Fresh scour marks exist. Your exterior should resist:

- Abrasion.
- Cuts.
- Punctures.
- Corrosion.
- Biofilm adhesion.
- Particulate intrusion.

Bring:

- Tough outer suit or vehicle skin.
- Gloves rated for sharp metal.
- Boots with grip on wet steel and polymer, but non-marking.
- Knee and elbow protection if working near surfaces.
- Helmet or face protection.
- Spare seals, patches, underwater tape, and fast-setting repair compound.

Do not bring gear with dangling straps, loose gauges, or unsecured tools. In a place of grating, rails, cables, and suction, every loose end is a future emergency.

### Tools

If you intend to interact with the environment, tools should be industrial, non-sparking, non-conductive where possible, and tethered.

Useful items:

- Multi-tool.
- Line cutters.
- Entanglement shears.
- Soft brush for clearing particulate from sensors.
- Non-conductive probe.
- Sample containers, only if permitted.
- Underwater slate or rugged notebook.
- Camera with acoustic and visual timestamp.
- Marker tags, if authorized.
- Small net or debris bag for your own waste.

Do not bring weapons unless there is a documented predator threat and no nonlethal alternative. Even then, weapons are likely to be dangerous in an industrial acoustic environment and may trigger defensive machine responses.

### Multiverse-Specific Gear

As a multiverse traveler, you may carry return mechanisms, translation devices, or dimensional anchors. Prepare them for a difficult environment.

Ensure:

- Your return method does not depend on local satellite navigation, which likely does not exist.
- Your anchor can function through water, metal, and acoustic noise.
- Your equipment will not be interpreted as a fault by basin systems.
- You can decontaminate before departure to avoid carrying organisms, brine, or industrial particulates to another world.
- You have a manual emergency recall that does not require fine motor control.
- You understand whether dimensional transit after pressure exposure poses decompression or pressure-shock risks.

If your return gate opens directly into the basin, choose the insertion point carefully. Do not materialize near an intake, turbine pit, electrified rail, transformer housing, or active conveyor. Prefer a dry service lock, flooded maintenance bay, or known safe anchorage.

## 7. Entry and Exit Preparation

Entry and exit are among the most dangerous phases. The Intake Sea is not a shore. There is no natural bank where you can simply step in and orient yourself.

### Use a Proper Lock

If possible, enter through:

- A service flood-lock.
- A maintenance bay.
- A personnel pressure chamber.
- A submersible docking trunk.
- A designated visitor aperture.

Avoid:

- Open intake stacks.
- Drainage galleries.
- Turbine pits.
- Pressure grates.
- Cable runs.
- Outfall tunnels.
- Sealed factory blocks unless authorized.

### Check Before You Flood

Before entry, verify:

- Life support.
- Communication.
- Navigation lock.
- Thermal systems.
- Buoyancy control.
- Emergency beacon.
- No loose gear.
- Suit seals.
- Local transponder code.
- Current schedule.
- Alarm status.
- Return route.

If wet diving, conduct pre-breathe and gas checks according to your training. If using a submersible, verify external hatch integrity and acoustic handshake with local control.

### Exit Planning

Plan your exit before you enter. You should know:

- Primary exit point.
- Secondary exit point.
- Time limit.
- Gas or power reserve limit.
- Decompression obligation, if any.
- Current reversal time.
- Maintenance dark period.
- Flushing cycle time.
- Emergency ascent or retrieval procedure.

In the Intake Sea, “just a little farther” is a dangerous phrase. Distance is deceptive. Currents turn. Machines cycle. Light dims. Sound changes. Leave while you still have margin.

## 8. Movement Inside the Intake Sea

Movement here is strange. You do not measure progress by walking. You measure it by drift, thrust, depth layer, and scheduled flow.

### Stay in Known Corridors at First

If you are new to the basin, stay near:

- Lit service roads.
- Marked guide rails, provided they are not energized or moving.
- Acoustic beacon lines.
- Terraced flood-platforms with visible safe paths.
- Maintenance aprons approved for passage.

Avoid:

- Open intake lips.
- Pressure grates.
- Turbine pits.
- Cable trenches.
- Conveyor tubes.
- Electrified rails.
- Transformer housings.
- Valve galleries.
- Sealed doors that may open under pressure.
- Cleaning-drone routes.
- Harvest lanes.

### Use Currents Carefully

Current lanes can carry you kilometers with little effort. But they can also deliver you into hazards. Before riding a flow:

- Confirm its route.
- Confirm its schedule.
- Identify exit points.
- Know where it terminates.
- Monitor current speed.
- Keep enough reserve power to leave the lane.

A current that feels pleasant can become a conveyor into an intake. A slow return flow can become a boom. Do not become comfortable.

### Manage Depth Layers

The basin has vertical climates. Warm upper currents, neutral middle water, cold lower volumes, brine rivers, and freshwater lenses can all affect movement.

When changing depth:

- Adjust buoyancy slowly.
- Watch for thermoclines.
- Monitor suit squeeze or expansion if diving.
- Be ready for visibility changes.
- Listen for pressure changes in nearby structures.
- Avoid lingering at boundaries where machines may discharge or draw water.

### Do Not Trust Visual Distance

A tower may be visible for a long time before it grows closer. A sound may seem near because sound travels well, while its source remains far away. Use instruments, not intuition, to estimate range.

A good practice is to select acoustic landmarks rather than visual ones. A pump tower may look the same for kilometers. A sonar beacon, however, can tell you whether you are approaching, drifting, or being carried sideways.

## 9. Major Hazards and How to Prepare for Them

### Intake Suction

Intakes are among the most lethal features. They can breathe with patient, enormous suction. Water may move toward them slowly at first, then accelerate near grates and lips.

Signs of intake hazard:

- Inward current.
- Particulate streaks converging.
- Bubbles being drawn toward a dark opening.
- Pressure drop or pulling sensation.
- Sound of breathing machinery.
- Warning beacons or acoustic pulses.

Preparation:

- Know intake locations before entry.
- Keep far standoff distance.
- Never enter during drawdown unless certain the system is locked out.
- Carry a current meter.
- Learn to detect subtle flow changes.
- Plan lateral escape routes.

If caught:

- Do not panic.
- Do not swim directly against the flow if it is stronger than you; exhaustion can follow quickly.
- Move sideways out of the current if possible.
- Use structure shadows only if they are safe and not themselves hazardous.
- Avoid being drawn feet-first into a grate; protect your head and life support.
- Use an emergency acoustic shutdown code if one exists.
- Signal immediately.

The best escape from intake suction is to never get near it.

### Pump Tides

Pump cycles can create scheduled pulls and releases. A long draw may make the whole basin lean toward an intake; the release may arrive as a slow boom of returning flow.

Prepare by:

- Carrying tide tables.
- Setting alarms for cycle changes.
- Avoiding narrow channels near cycle changes.
- Staying out of open water when a large gate is due to seat.
- Listening for valve knocks and gate percussion.

### Outflows and Effluent Plumes

Outflows are resources for animals but hazards for visitors. Warm effluent may carry:

- Heat.
- Particulates.
- Chemical traces.
- Nutrient floc.
- Microbial broth.
- Brine.
- Turbulence.

Do not assume a warm plume is safe. It may be pleasant, but it can also foul your sensors, reduce visibility, or attract dense animal crowds and feeding predators.

### Electrical Boundaries

Electric exclusion fields, anti-fouling currents, electrified rails, insulated busbars, transformer housings, and cathodic protection systems are part of the landscape. Some may be invisible until you are already affected.

Prepare by:

- Wearing non-conductive outer layers where possible.
- Avoiding simultaneous contact with different metal structures.
- Keeping hands and tools away from unknown cables and housings.
- Using insulated tools.
- Monitoring for electrical field warnings.
- Learning the local warning signals.
- Retreating immediately if you feel tingling, muscle twitching, or strange metallic taste.

Do not touch sacrificial anodes casually. They are meant to corrode, and the surrounding system may involve controlled electrical potentials.

### Sharp Young Surfaces

The flood is young. Corners are crisp. Weld seams still carry fabrication beads. Paint has been flayed in narrow lines. Scour marks are fresh. Sealant grooves are clean.

Prepare for:

- Abrasion.
- Cuts.
- Suit punctures.
- Glove damage.
- Mask or helmet scratches.

Move slowly near new surfaces. Do not brace against sharp welds. Do not kneel on unknown grating. Artificial sediment may hide gaps, rivet heads, or hatch seams.

### Artificial Sediment

The “sediment” is made: paint flecks, cement dust, polymer wear-particles, corrosion scale, machining fines, lime bloom, gasket rubber fragments, and filtered-process ash. It is not soil. It may be fine, slippery, chemically active, or easily suspended.

Avoid stirring it. It can:

- Reduce visibility.
- Clog filters.
- Irritate life support intakes.
- Obscure hazards.
- Trigger cleaning machines.
- Contaminate biological growth surfaces.

### Entanglement

The environment contains cables, rails, hanging lines, polymer frames, biofilm racks, kelp-like growth, maintenance tethers, and animal attachment structures.

Prepare by:

- Securing all gear.
- Using shrouded thrusters.
- Carrying at least two cutting tools.
- Practicing entanglement drills.
- Avoiding blind reaches into dark cavities.
- Not using tether systems unless they are managed and appropriate.

If entangled:

- Stop.
- Breathe.
- Identify the point of snag.
- Do not thrash.
- Cut or unwind methodically.
- Watch for electrical or mechanical components before cutting anything unknown.

### Machine Traffic

This is not a ruin. Production continues. There may be:

- Cleaning drones.
- Scraping arms.
- Pulse-current devices.
- Chemical dosing systems.
- Gantry cranes.
- Underwater carriers.
- Conveyor tubes.
- Guided platforms.
- Harvest systems.
- Acoustic census devices.

Prepare by:

- Wearing a transponder.
- Using authorized identification codes.
- Staying out of machine work envelopes.
- Not blocking rails or conveyors.
- Yielding to automated systems.
- Observing local right-of-way rules.
- Avoiding flashing lights that might be mistaken for commands.

If a machine approaches, do not assume it sees you as a person. It may see you as fouling, obstruction, fauna, or debris. Make yourself predictable, visible, and non-interfering.

### Fauna

The animals are abundant but arranged. They cluster where conditions favor them. Around warm vents, fish hang in crowds. Along current lanes, filter-feeders encrust grates. In open water, forage fish move in vast curtains. Predators use shadow, cable trenches, and acoustic cover.

Most fauna is not likely to hunt a human, but hazards exist:

- Dense schools can obscure vision.
- Bait balls can attract predators and harvest machines.
- Ray-like animals may stir sediment near your feet.
- Eel-shaped predators may investigate crevices you touch.
- Crustaceans can pinch or scrape.
- Cephalopods may investigate joints, ports, and connectors.

Prepare by:

- Not feeding animals.
- Not touching biofilms or attachment reefs.
- Not imitating feeding sounds or harvest signals.
- Avoiding dense bait clouds.
- Protecting connectors and hoses.
- Keeping hands away from dark cavities.
- Moving calmly if animals approach.

If a cephalopod investigates your suit, do not panic. Protect controls and connectors, discourage gently, and avoid letting it enter gaps. These creatures may be intelligent or at least curious, but they live inside a machine environment and may treat you as an object to be tested.

### Acoustic Overload

The Intake Sea is loud. Water carries noise with solidity. Engines thrum. Turbines hiss. Valves knock. Drives whine. Gates boom. Sonar pulses. Alarms test. Fish drum. Crustaceans click. Schools sound like rain on metal.

Prepare by:

- Using hearing protection with selective filtering.
- Training yourself to recognize important alarms.
- Avoiding active sonar sources unless necessary.
- Keeping communication volume controlled.
- Monitoring for fatigue, anxiety, or startle response.

Sound can deceive. A near alarm may be far. A distant gate may be close enough to affect pressure. Trust instruments and learned signal patterns, not raw instinct.

## 10. Ecological and Ethical Preparation

The Intake Sea is not wild, but it is alive. Its life has been introduced, escaped, cultivated, or adapted. Everything alive negotiates the built. You must do the same.

### Do Not Introduce Contamination

Before entry:

- Decontaminate suit, tools, and vehicle.
- Remove foreign organisms from previous worlds.
- Seal lubricants, adhesives, and chemicals.
- Avoid shedding microplastics, fibers, or metal dust.
- Use non-toxic, basin-compatible materials if possible.

After exit:

- Rinse and inspect.
- Contain any water or sediment brought out.
- Do not release Intake Sea water into another ecosystem.
- Check for hitchhiking organisms, especially larvae, biofilm fragments, crustacean eggs, or microbial mats.

Multiverse travel makes biosecurity critical. A tiny fragment of engineered biofilm or a cyst of adapted microbe could become a disaster elsewhere.

### Respect the Manufactured Contract

Growth here is either crop, tool, or fouling. Animals learn what surfaces are permitted. You should learn too.

Do not:

- Scrape biofilms for fun.
- Break artificial habitat modules.
- Move engineered crustaceans or mollusks.
- Feed fish during controlled pulses.
- Interfere with harvest signals.
- Enter spawning or nursery zones without authorization.
- Damage kelp-like growth racks.
- Disturb bacterial mats near warm outflows.

If you must sample, obtain permission, use sterile tools, and take only what is authorized.

### Observe Without Disrupting

The ecology moves inside rules set by industry. Feeding plumes, exclusion fields, acoustic census pulses, and harvest protocols are not natural events, but they are real events for the animals. Do not insert yourself into those cycles.

If a feeding release begins, observe from a distance. Do not enter the baitfish sphere. Do not follow predators into shadowed machine spaces. Do not assume the machines will pause because you are present.

---

## 11. Planning Your Route

Because the basin is vast and sparse, a route should be planned around systems, not just landmarks.

### Choose a Theme

Decide what kind of visit you want:

- **Industrial observation:** pump towers, turbine housings, sealed factory blocks, gantry lines.
- **Ecological observation:** effluent reefs, filter-feeder grates, forage-fish curtains, warm-vent crowds.
- **Weather observation:** thermal plumes, brine rivers, rain curtains, internal waves.
- **Acoustic immersion:** listening to machine and animal soundscapes from a safe refuge.
- **Structural inspection:** close work on terraces, retaining walls, service roads, and deck plates.

Each requires different clearance and different hazards.

### Choose a Time Window

For a first visit, choose a low-risk operational period:

- Low pump flow.
- No flushing cycle.
- No harvest pulse.
- No surface storm importing rain.
- Normal lighting, not maintenance dark.
- Moderate production, so the place is alive but not turbulent.

High-production periods are more dramatic but more dangerous. Maintenance lows may be calmer but dimmer and less illuminated. Flushing cycles should be avoided entirely unless you are in a protected vessel.

### Define Safe Zones

Identify:

- Entry lock.
- Acoustic beacon checkpoints.
- Lit service platforms.
- Refuge points.
- Emergency ascent or extraction points.
- Areas of known low current.
- Known non-electrified structures.
- Exit lock.

Do not rely on open water as a safe zone. Open water is circulation space. It may be empty, but it is not neutral.

### Set Abort Criteria

Before entering, decide what will make you leave:

- Gas or power reaches reserve.
- Communication loss beyond a set time.
- Suit leak.
- Heating failure.
- Current exceeds expected value.
- Electrical warning detected.
- Alarm pattern indicates shutdown or evacuation.
- Visibility drops below safe threshold.
- Core temperature drops.
- Entanglement occurs.
- You feel disoriented or panicked.

Write these criteria down. In the moment, your mind may bargain. Preparation should not bargain.

## 12. Emergency Preparation

The Intake Sea is not forgiving of improvisation. Emergencies should be anticipated.

### Loss of Communication

If communication fails:

- Stop moving.
- Check equipment.
- Switch to backup acoustic channel.
- Use prearranged beacon pattern.
- Move to last known safe checkpoint only if safe.
- Do not enter unknown structures to search for signal.
- If no contact by the prearranged deadline, execute extraction plan.

### Suit Leak or Breach

If water enters:

- Identify source.
- Seal if possible.
- Switch to internal or backup breathing if breathing gas is compromised.
- Abort.
- Maintain thermal protection.
- Do not remove helmet or mask unless in a dry safe space.
- If flooding becomes uncontrolled, use emergency ascent or retrieval only as last resort and according to training.

### Entanglement

- Stop.
- Breathe.
- Assess.
- Cut or unwrap.
- Avoid energized components.
- Signal for help if needed.
- Do not use thrusters until clear.

### Electrical Hazard

If you suspect electrical exposure:

- Retreat along the path you came.
- Avoid touching metal.
- Do not grab structures for stability.
- Shut down nonessential external sensors.
- Report the location.
- Abort the visit.

### Intake Hazard

If current begins drawing you toward an intake:

- Signal immediately.
- Move laterally.
- Avoid direct exhaustion.
- Use structures only if safe.
- Deploy emergency marker or acoustic shutdown code if available.
- Do not allow yourself to be pulled into a dark opening.

### Hypothermia

- Increase heating.
- Abort.
- Move to warm refuge if authorized.
- Hydrate if possible.
- Monitor cognition.
- Do not continue for the sake of observation.

### Decompression Illness

If wet diving:

- Follow decompression plan.
- Do not ascend rapidly.
- Carry oxygen and emergency medical support.
- Have a chamber available.
- Do not immediately cross dimensional thresholds if pressure changes could worsen symptoms.

## 13. After the Visit

Preparation does not end when you leave the water.

### Decontamination

Rinse and inspect everything. The water may leave:

- Salt.
- Chlorine residues.
- Mineral scale.
- Paint flecks.
- Cement dust.
- Polymer fines.
- Biofilm traces.
- Crustacean larvae.
- Microbial films.

Do not carry these into another world.

### Inspection

Check for:

- Cuts.
- Abrasions.
- Seal damage.
- Corrosion.
- Particulate intrusion.
- Electrical scorching.
- Sensor fouling.
- Thruster entanglement marks.
- Structural stress.

The young flood has sharp edges. Even a successful visit may leave hidden damage.

### Physiological Recovery

Warm the body. Rehydrate. Eat. Rest. If wet diving, observe surface interval rules. Monitor for:

- Delayed decompression symptoms.
- Ear or sinus barotrauma.
- Hypothermia afterdrop.
- Chemical irritation.
- Noise fatigue.
- Anxiety or intrusive sensory memories.

The Intake Sea’s sound may stay with you: the thrum, the knocks, the distant booms, the rain of fish bodies, the procedural alarms. Give yourself time to decompress mentally as well as physically.

### Record What You Learned

Log:

- Routes.
- Currents.
- Schedules.
- Acoustic signals.
- Hazard locations.
- Animal behavior.
- Weather events.
- Equipment failures.
- Near misses.

This is not merely personal data. In a manufactured sea, knowledge is survival. If other travelers follow, your notes may help them avoid becoming part of the basin’s incident log.

## 14. What It Will Feel Like, and How to Be Ready for That

You should prepare for a place that feels immense but not empty. The Intake Sea is mostly liquid vacancy, yet that vacancy is functional. It allows heat to disperse, currents to turn, sound to propagate, and life to move in enormous sparse patterns.

You may travel through nothing for a long time. Then, suddenly, something enormous appears: a black pump tower, a sealed factory block lit like a submerged city, a curtain of hanging cables, a row of turbine housings half lost in blue, a school of fish kilometers long catching the light like buried weather.

The light will seem normal, which is part of the strangeness. It will behave as light should: attenuating, reddening out, turning distance into suggestion. Shadows will be hard near lamps and soft in open water. Shafts will reveal suspended particles like dust in a room.

The sound will be constant. You may feel it before you understand it. The basin is loud in a way air-bound environments rarely are: not just noise, but pressure, vibration, and rhythm. Machines breathe. Metal adjusts. Water moves through gates. Animals drum and click and scatter.

The place will feel tense because it is new. The water has not yet learned the room it has been given. Currents still test corners. Bubbles collect under unfamiliar ledges. Particulate rises from seams that have not settled. Erosion is early and precise. Everything has the feeling of first use.

To be ready for that, cultivate patience. Do not rush. Let your eyes adjust to the blue-green distance. Let your ears learn the machine vocabulary. Move with the current schedule, not against it. Treat every large object as significant. Treat every subtle pressure change as information.

The Intake Sea rewards attention. It does not reward bravado.

## Final Advice

Prepare for the Intake Sea as a technician, a diver, a meteorologist, and a guest. Bring redundancy. Bring humility. Bring quiet instruments. Bring respect for schedules. Bring the willingness to leave when the basin begins to breathe.

The place is not hostile in the way of a predator. It is hostile in the way of a large machine: indifferent, powerful, rhythmic, and exact. But it is also astonishing. A manufactured sea in its first age of flooding, sharp-edged and roaring, learning how to hold the life it has been given.

If you prepare well, you may witness that learning from inside: the cold blue-green light, the distant boom of gates, the silver curtains of forage fish, the warm towers of effluent weather, the rain falling through the sea like a translated storm. You will not conquer the Intake Sea. You will only be permitted to pass through it.

That is the correct attitude.
