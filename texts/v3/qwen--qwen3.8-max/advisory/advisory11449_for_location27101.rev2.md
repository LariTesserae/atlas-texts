Prepare for this place by first accepting what it is: not a lake resort, not a ruin, not a primitive town to be “roughed through,” but an unfinished amphibious world. It is warm, wet, dim, buoyant, chemically alive, and socially delicate. An ordinary human visitor should assume they are too heavy, too loud, too dry, too hurried, and too reliant on stable ground. Preparation means becoming lighter, quieter, more reversible, more patient, and less contaminating.

The core rule is: **do not prepare to conquer the basin; prepare to be tolerated by it.**

---

## 1. The right mindset before anything else

The basin will not feel dangerous in the usual way. There are no obvious predators, no dramatic storms, no great crashing waves. The danger is softer: warmth that exhausts you, water that hides drops and entanglements, thin air that makes you overestimate yourself, silence that makes you careless, and a landscape that changes slowly enough that you fail to notice until your footing is wrong.

You should mentally prepare for:

- Being wet often, perhaps constantly.
- Seeing in dim green-gold light rather than clear daylight.
- Hearing muted, ambiguous sounds.
- Moving slowly through layered routes: walkways, channels, submerged guide-lines, raised platforms.
- Not trusting your eyes entirely; distances may bend, reflections may duplicate structures, and submerged shapes may look nearer or farther than they are.
- Feeling as if the place is alive, not metaphorically only, but structurally: houses grow, floors float, walls crust over, streets become pools, rooms sink and are reused.
- Being surrounded by many human-made presences but very few animals.
- Experiencing a strange intimacy: warm rain, warm water, enclosed mist, small lamps, low voices.

If you arrive expecting crisp adventure, you will probably be clumsy. If you arrive expecting to be taught by a slow, half-formed place, you will do better.

The traveler’s virtues here are:

- **Lightness**: in gear, movement, and impact.
- **Quietness**: in voice, tools, and intention.
- **Buoyancy**: everything should float, or at least not drag you down.
- **Reversibility**: nothing should be permanently fixed, cut, stained, tied, or abandoned without permission.
- **Patience**: the basin operates on long durations. Rushing is a hazard.

## 2. Decide what kind of visit you are making

Before packing, decide whether this is:

1. A brief observational visit.
2. A guided cultural visit.
3. A stay of several days or weeks.
4. A research or recording visit.
5. A solo visit.

The basin is not a place for casual solo wandering unless you are already deeply familiar with it. For an ordinary multiverse traveler, the safest assumption is: **arrive only with a local host or guide, or wait at the edge until contact is made.**

A short visit requires less gear but more restraint. A longer visit requires hydration, skin care, shelter, waste management, and a much deeper commitment to local customs. A research visit requires biosecurity, permissions, and minimal sampling. A solo visit requires a much higher margin of safety and should probably be avoided unless you have prior experience in similar environments.

If possible, arrange the following before arrival:

- A local guide or host.
- Permission to enter inhabited districts.
- Knowledge of current water levels, warm vents, unstable crusts, and restricted areas.
- A mutually understood meeting point and signal system.
- A clear plan for departure and emergency return.

If no contact is possible, treat the basin as inhabited but private. Do not enter submerged structures, do not tie anything to local lines, and do not assume that an apparently empty platform is unowned.

## 3. Physical preparation

### Altitude

The basin is high. The air is thin. Even if the water is warm, your lungs and circulation will be under extra strain.

Before visiting:

- Spend at least one to three days acclimatizing at a similar elevation if possible.
- Avoid heavy exertion immediately after arrival.
- Hydrate carefully.
- Avoid alcohol and sedatives.
- Learn the signs of altitude sickness: headache, nausea, poor sleep, dizziness, unusual fatigue, shortness of breath at rest.
- Know that severe altitude illness is an emergency requiring descent.

Because the basin is warm and humid, altitude symptoms may be mistaken for heat exhaustion or dehydration. Watch yourself carefully.

### Heat tolerance

The water is described as skin-temperature or near it. That is deceptive. Water at skin temperature does not cool you efficiently. If the water is even slightly warmer than your skin, it can become dangerous during exertion. Add humid air, thin mountain air, and constant moisture, and you have a setting where overheating can creep up quietly.

Prepare by:

- Practicing gentle movement in warm, humid conditions.
- Learning to recognize heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, weakness, confusion, rapid pulse, flushed or clammy skin.
- Understanding that sweating may not help much in saturated air.
- Planning frequent rest periods.
- Avoiding heavy loads and fast swimming.
- Keeping the head and neck ventilated when possible.
- Drinking enough water, but not so much plain water that you dilute your electrolytes.

This is not a place for athletic splashing. Move like someone carrying a bowl of warm water that must not spill.

### Swimming and water comfort

You do not need to be an expert diver, but you should be comfortable in low-visibility water. Ideally, you should be able to:

- Float calmly.
- Swim slowly while wearing clothing.
- Keep your face in the water without panic.
- Use a mask or eye protection.
- Follow a rope by touch.
- Work slowly underwater for short periods.
- Hold your breath without hyperventilating.
- Remain calm if your view is obscured by algae, mineral dust, or mist.

Do not rely on dramatic swimming. The correct movement style here is slow, controlled, and quiet. Breaststroke, sidestroke, or gentle underwater glides are better than thrashing crawl sprints.

### Breath-hold safety

If you plan to go below the surface, learn basic breath-hold safety:

- Never hyperventilate before diving.
- Never dive alone.
- Never push through dizziness.
- Never compete for duration.
- Ascend immediately if you feel pressure, confusion, or visual disturbance.
- Understand that shallow-water blackout can happen without warning.

The basin’s underwater spaces are beautiful, but they are also root-filled, dim, and entangling. This is not a place for reckless free diving.

### Balance and footing

You will walk on floating reed mats, pumice platforms, wet mineral crusts, submerged ledges, and living root structures. Practice:

- Walking softly.
- Keeping knees slightly bent.
- Using a pole for balance.
- Shifting weight gradually.
- Testing uncertain surfaces before committing.
- Falling safely into water rather than resisting awkwardly.

A traveler who stomps, lunges, or leaps is a hazard to themselves and to the settlement.

## 4. Medical preparation

An ordinary human should treat this as a medically remote environment. Even if multiverse travel is possible, rescue may not be immediate.

### Consult a medical professional if possible

Before visiting, get advice about:

- Altitude exposure.
- Heat exposure.
- Waterborne infections.
- Wound care in warm mineral water.
- Ear and sinus issues.
- Breath-hold diving risks.
- Any personal cardiac, respiratory, or circulatory vulnerabilities.

### Key medical concerns

#### Dehydration and electrolyte imbalance

Warm humid air, thin altitude air, and constant immersion can dehydrate you without obvious sweating. Bring:

- Clean water.
- Electrolyte salts or tablets.
- A way to monitor hydration, such as urine color awareness.

Do not drink basin water unless a trusted local source is confirmed and, if necessary, treated. The water is mineral-rich, biologically active, and chemically variable. “Warm and green” does not mean safe.

#### Skin breakdown and infection

Prolonged wetness softens skin. Mineral water, algae, and microbial mats may irritate cuts or cause infection. Prepare:

- Waterproof wound dressings.
- Antiseptic suitable for skin.
- Antifungal treatment.
- Barrier balm or resin-based protective ointment.
- Dry footwraps or spare socks.
- A small mirror or method for inspecting your skin.

Any open wound should be covered before entering the water. If a wound becomes red, swollen, painful, or hot, leave the basin and seek care.

#### Ear care

Warm water trapped in the ears can cause infection. Consider:

- Well-fitting ear protection if you will be submerged often.
- Gentle drying methods afterward.
- Avoiding inserting sharp objects into the ear.
- Not diving if you have an active ear infection or recent ear surgery.

#### Eye care

The water is mineralized and biologically active. Avoid direct eye exposure if possible.

- Use a mask or sealed eye protection.
- Do not wear contact lenses unless you have a fully sealed alternative and a clean backup plan. Contacts in warm natural water can invite serious eye infections.
- Bring spare glasses with secure straps if you need vision correction.

#### Respiratory sensitivity

The air is warm, wet, and sometimes mineral-scented or faintly sulfurous. Most of this may be harmless, but some people will react.

If you have asthma, allergies, or lung disease, be cautious. Bring any prescribed inhalers or medications in sealed waterproof packaging. Pay attention to coughing, chest tightness, wheezing, or unusual breathlessness.

#### Heat illness

Bring knowledge more than equipment. Know how to respond to heat exhaustion and heat stroke. Heat stroke is an emergency: confusion, collapse, very hot skin, vomiting, or loss of coordination means immediate cooling and evacuation.

## 5. Biosecurity and contamination control

This basin is young, mineral-rich, and ecologically delicate. Its fauna is sparse. Its microbial mats, algae, reed beds, and root systems are doing the work of building the world. A multiverse traveler is a potential contamination event.

Before arrival:

- Clean all gear thoroughly.
- Remove soil, seeds, spores, insect eggs, fibers, and organic debris from previous worlds.
- Inspect boots, straps, folds, and seams.
- Use clean, sealed water containers.
- Do not bring fresh plant material, soil, animal products, or live organisms.
- Do not bring pets, mounts, or companions animals. They are likely to suffer and may damage the basin.
- Avoid scented soaps, sunscreens, insect repellents, detergents, or lotions unless you know they are safe for this environment.

The safest approach is: **arrive clean, leave nothing, take nothing.**

If you have been in another ecosystem immediately before, consider quarantining or changing gear. The basin’s youth makes it vulnerable. A foreign microbe, alga, fungus, or seed could become a catastrophe over time.

## 6. Gear philosophy: light, quiet, buoyant, non-metallic, reversible

The local culture uses reed, pumice, bone, shell, obsidian, basalt, clay, resin, woven fiber, and living plants. There are no nails, forged beams, or gears. That does not mean a visitor must abandon all outside technology, but you should strongly prefer equipment that:

- Does not rust.
- Does not shed metal particles.
- Does not make sharp noise.
- Does not snag on roots or lines.
- Does not rely on complex machinery.
- Can be repaired with cordage and local materials.
- Can be untied, floated, or packed out.
- Does not permanently alter the environment.

### Avoid if possible

- Heavy boots.
- Metal anchors.
- Screws, nails, clamps.
- Powered pumps.
- Drones.
- Loud speakers.
- Bright white lights.
- Synthetic ropes that shed microfibers.
- Disposable plastics that fragment.
- Chemical detergents.
- Gas canisters with metal fittings.
- Large rigid packs.
- Fishing gear.
- Animal products.
- Souvenirs taken without permission.

If you must bring metal for medical or safety reasons, keep it sealed, accounted for, and never leave it behind.

## 7. Packing list

Pack in sealed, buoyant, tied bundles. Everything should have a lanyard or attachment point. Nothing should dangle freely. If an item falls into the water, it should either float, be easily retrieved, or be considered a contamination emergency.

### Clothing

The climate is warm, wet, and humid, but high altitude and wind at the rim may be cooler. You need layered protection without bulk.

Bring:

- Quick-drying base layers. Avoid heavy cotton.
- A breathable long-sleeved layer for skin protection.
- A light rain cloak or oiled-fiber hooded garment.
- A wide-brimmed rain hat with chin strap, if culturally acceptable.
- Neck protection from warm rain and diffuse altitude light.
- Thin gloves for handling rope, reeds, and mineralized surfaces.
- Secure, quick-drying footwear with good grip and protected toes.
- Spare footwraps or socks.
- A spare dry layer kept sealed for sleep or emergency.
- Muted colors: green, brown, grey, pale stone tones. Avoid flashy fabrics unless for emergency signaling.

Avoid loose ribbons, dangling straps, scarves, or flapping cloth. Everything can snag on roots, lines, or reed screens.

### Footwear

This is important. You need soles thin enough to feel unstable surfaces but protective enough for sharp mineral crusts, obsidian flakes, and hidden edges.

Ideal footwear:

- Grips wet reed and pumice.
- Drains quickly.
- Stays attached without dangling laces.
- Protects toes.
- Can be rinsed clean.
- Does not crush living mats.

Heavy hiking boots are bad here. They are heavy, slow to dry, and may damage surfaces. Bare feet are also risky because of sharp mineral deposits and hidden structures.

### Personal flotation

Even strong swimmers should have some buoyancy aid. The water is dim, entangling, and full of vertical obstacles.

Options:

- A small sealed float pack.
- A pumice-and-fiber flotation collar, if locally appropriate.
- A low-profile buoyancy vest without metal or noisy valves.
- A sealed dry bag with enough trapped air to provide buoyancy.

Avoid bulky life jackets that snag on roots. Avoid anything that makes you top-heavy.

### Mask, snorkel, and face protection

The locals use oiled face coverings and short breathing tubes. As a visitor, you should practice with similar simple equipment.

Bring or prepare:

- A well-fitting mask or sealed eye protection.
- A simple snorkel or local-style breathing tube.
- A spare face covering.
- A method for keeping the mask from fogging, preferably non-toxic.

Full-face dive helmets, scuba gear, and powered respirators are likely excessive, noisy, and culturally intrusive unless there is a specific emergency reason.

### Lighting

The basin is dim. Light matters, but harsh light is wrong for the place.

Preferred lighting:

- Small oil lamps in clay or sealed shells.
- Lamps with membranes of scraped gut or oiled fiber, as described locally.
- Dim amber or orange light.
- Shielded flames that cannot easily tip.
- Spare wicks.
- Clean-burning oil compatible with local use.
- A lamp lanyard.
- A shutter or cover for signaling.

If you bring an electric light, make it:

- Waterproof.
- Non-metallic if possible.
- Low-intensity.
- Warm-colored, not blue-white.
- Silent.
- Sealed against humidity.
- Easy to dim.

Do not use bright floodlights. They will destroy night vision, disturb bioluminescence, and alarm inhabitants.

### Rope and cordage

Cordage is essential, but you must be careful. Do not tie onto local structures unless invited.

Bring:

- Several lengths of strong, low-shed natural or compatible fiber cord.
- Thin line for repairs.
- A throw line.
- A few soft loops that will not cut into reed or root.
- Floating markers, if permitted.
- Carved or smooth float chips for personal route memory, again only if permitted.

Learn knots that can be tied and untied with wet hands. Learn to tie securely without jamming.

### Tools

The local toolkit is simple but refined. A visitor should not bring a noisy industrial kit.

Useful items:

- A small ceramic or obsidian blade, if safely sheathed.
- A bone or wooden needle.
- A stone adze or equivalent shaping tool, only if you have permission to do work.
- A fire-hardened pole for punting and balance.
- A small pad or wrap for muffling tool strikes.
- A woven basket or container for carrying small items.
- A repair kit with resin, cord, pegs, and patches.

Avoid saws, metal hammers, pliers, wrenches, and powered tools unless absolutely necessary and approved.

### Water and food

Bring more safe water than you think you need.

Recommended:

- At least 3–4 liters of safe drinking water per person per day, more if exertion is high.
- Sealed, non-reactive containers.
- Electrolyte tablets or salts.
- Compact rations that do not crumble or leak odor.
- Food in sealed containers.
- A method for packing out all food waste.

Do not assume local food is safe unless offered by trusted inhabitants. Even then, ask about preparation, allergies, and water source. If you are unsure, politely rely on your own supplies.

### Shelter and sleep

If staying overnight, you need to sleep dry or mostly dry.

Bring or arrange:

- A raised sleeping surface or permission to use a platform.
- A breathable canopy or oiled cloth.
- A lightweight sleeping layer suitable for humidity.
- A dry change of clothing reserved only for sleep.
- A small net if insects are present, though they are rare.
- A way to keep your sleeping area from collecting rainwater.

Avoid sleeping directly on young mineral crusts, damp reed, or unstable shelves. Ask where it is safe.

### Hygiene and waste

This is critical. The basin is not a place for casual waste disposal.

Bring:

- A sealed waste container.
- Pack-out bags for all sanitary waste.
- Clean cloths.
- Unscented, non-toxic cleaning materials if approved.
- Barrier balm for feet and hands.
- Ear and skin care supplies.
- Menstrual supplies with pack-out capability, if relevant.

Do not urinate or defecate in channels, pools, gardens, or near root beds. Do not wash with soap in the water. Do not leave food scraps.

### First aid kit

A waterproof first aid kit should include:

- Waterproof dressings.
- Antiseptic.
- Antifungal treatment.
- Blister care.
- Burn gel or dressing.
- Adhesive strips or closure strips.
- Tweezers or forceps, preferably non-metallic.
- Small scissors or cutting blade, safely sheathed.
- Pain and fever medication.
- Anti-inflammatory medication, if appropriate.
- Antihistamines.
- Anti-nausea medication.
- Anti-diarrheal medication.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- Altitude sickness medication, if prescribed.
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics, if prescribed and appropriate.
- Epinephrine auto-injector if you have serious allergies.
- A compact emergency blanket.
- Splint material or knowledge of how to improvise one.
- A written list of personal medical conditions and medications.

Keep all medications sealed and labeled clearly.

### Navigation and communication

Do not rely on maps alone. The basin changes.

Bring:

- Waterproof notebook or tablets.
- A writing method that works when damp.
- A simple signal card with prearranged visual meanings.
- A small mirror or reflective surface for emergency use, not for casual signaling.
- A whistle only for life-threatening emergencies, because noise is culturally disruptive.
- A personal locator or multiverse beacon if you have one, sealed and non-intrusive.

Better than gadgets: local knowledge, guide-lines, lamp signals, and patience.

### Optional scientific or recording gear

If you must record:

- Ask permission first.
- Use silent devices.
- Avoid flashes.
- Seal electronics against humidity.
- Avoid metal and toxic batteries.
- Keep everything tethered.
- Do not record sacred or private submerged areas.
- Do not take samples without explicit permission.
- Sterilize any equipment before and after use.

Sketching may be more appropriate than photography.

## 9. Preparing your body for the water

The basin’s water is not ordinary water. It is warm, mineralized, alive with algae, suspended silt, microbial matter, and chemical variation. You should prepare your body as if entering a long, warm bath that you must work inside.

### Skin

Before immersion:

- Make sure skin is clean but not coated in toxic products.
- Apply a protective barrier to feet, hands, and skin folds if you have local-approved balm.
- Cover cuts completely.
- Remove rings or tight jewelry that can trap moisture or snag.

After immersion:

- Rinse with clean water if available.
- Dry carefully, especially between toes and fingers.
- Inspect for rash, cracks, or irritation.
- Change into dry clothing when possible.

### Ears

If you will submerge:

- Consider ear protection.
- Dry ears afterward.
- Avoid aggressive cleaning.
- Stop diving if pain or fullness develops.

### Eyes

- Use a mask.
- Avoid rubbing eyes with wet hands.
- If irritation occurs, flush with clean water and rest eyes.

### Lungs

- Do not push through chest tightness.
- Avoid breath-hold contests.
- If you feel dizzy, rise slowly and rest.
- If there is a sudden change in smell, warmth, or mist, move to better air.

## 10. Hydration, food, and heat management

Because the environment is warm and humid, thirst may not feel urgent. That is dangerous.

### Drinking

- Drink small amounts regularly.
- Include electrolytes, especially if you are sweating or working.
- Do not overdrink plain water to the point of nausea or confusion.
- Keep water containers sealed and buoyant.
- Mark personal containers clearly without damaging the environment.

### Eating

- Eat light, frequent meals.
- Avoid messy, crumbly, oily foods that can contaminate water.
- Store food above waterline and away from rain drip if possible.
- Pack out all wrappers, peels, bones, shells, and scraps.

### Cooling

In saturated air, cooling is difficult. Useful measures:

- Rest in shade or under canopy.
- Keep head and neck ventilated.
- Slow your movements.
- Remove unnecessary layers.
- Use a small hand fan if culturally acceptable.
- Seek cooler air near non-thermal zones if a guide indicates such places.

Do not rely on jumping into water to cool off. The water itself may be part of the heat problem.

## 11. Movement skills to practice before arrival

### Walking on floating surfaces

Practice:

- Short steps.
- Soft knees.
- Keeping weight centered.
- Using a pole.
- Not pushing off abruptly.
- Stopping immediately if the surface tilts or creaks strangely.

### Walking on mineral crusts

White crusts and pale sinter shelves may be beautiful but unsafe.

- Test with a pole.
- Avoid thin or newly formed crusts.
- Do not jump onto them.
- Do not drag heavy loads across them.
- Ask locals which crusts are mature enough to bear weight.

### Punting and rafting

If you use a flat-bottomed punt or raft:

- Learn to pole from a low stance.
- Avoid pushing off from living root mats.
- Keep the pole from striking submerged structures hard.
- Move slowly through channels.
- Keep gear tied down.
- Do not stand tall in an unstable craft.

### Swimming with gear

Practice swimming slowly while carrying or towing a sealed bundle. The bundle should:

- Float.
- Be tied to you with a quick-release attachment if safe.
- Not wrap around your legs.
- Not drag you downward if it floods.

### Following guide-lines

In dim water, sight is unreliable. Learn to:

- Hold a line lightly.
- Follow knots or markers by touch.
- Count intervals if trained to do so.
- Stop if the line becomes tangled.
- Never wrap a line around your body.
- Keep one hand free when possible.

### Entanglement response

Root curtains, hanging reeds, nets, and lines can entangle.

If caught:

- Stop.
- Do not thrash.
- Exhale slowly if underwater.
- Trace the obstruction with your fingers.
- Back out along the path you came.
- Use a blade only as a last resort and only with great care.
- Signal your buddy if possible.

Practice this calmly in safe conditions before visiting.

## 12. Navigation in a place that refuses to stay fixed

The basin is regional in scale, but visibility is poor and geography is unstable. Do not rely on a single landmark.

### Use local routes

Where possible, travel by:

- Established walkways.
- Known channels.
- Knotted guide-lines.
- Floating markers.
- Lamp positions.
- Recognized district boundaries.
- Guide memory.

### Do not trust sight alone

The water can bend light. Distant platforms may appear nearer, duplicated, or upside down. Mist can make rain and lake surface blend. Bioluminescence can create false trails.

When in doubt:

- Stop.
- Hold a known line or structure if permitted.
- Consult your guide.
- Use tactile memory rather than visual impulse.
- Do not swim toward a light unless you know what it means.

### Personal route discipline

If allowed, use:

- Counted strokes.
- Memorized turns.
- Tactile markers.
- Temporary personal floats that are removed afterward.
- A notebook with simple sketches.

Do not mark local structures permanently. Do not carve into reed, bone, mineral crust, or architectural surfaces.

## 13. Cultural conduct and local etiquette

The settlement is not merely physical; it is a system of knots, rights, responsibilities, and slow maintenance. A visitor must behave with restraint.

### Quietness

The basin has customs of quiet. Sound travels strangely, especially underwater. Sharp noises can confuse distances and disturb inhabitants.

Therefore:

- Speak softly.
- Avoid shouting except in emergencies.
- Do not splash unnecessarily.
- Pad tools if work is required.
- Avoid dropping gear.
- Cough or sneeze into a cloth if possible.
- Do not use loud signals unless life is at risk.

### Visual signals

Learn basic local signals if taught. Common visual signals may include:

- A lifted lamp.
- A moved float.
- A swaying reed bundle.
- A deliberate ripple.
- A raised hand.
- A slow lowering of the body to show non-threat.

Do not invent signals and assume they will be understood. If communicating with locals, keep gestures slow, open, and non-threatening.

### Knots and ownership

Knots are not decoration. They may mark ownership, history, family events, water rises, repairs, warnings, or claims.

Do not:

- Untie local knots.
- Move beaded or carved floats.
- Add your own knots to posts or lines.
- Tie your gear to claimed structures.
- Use a knotted line as a handhold unless it is clearly a public route.
- Treat knot records as curiosities.

If invited to add a knot, understand that you are participating in a record. Do so only with guidance.

### Structures and living architecture

Many buildings are partly alive. Their roots bind stone and pumice. Their walls may still grow. Their floors may float.

Do not:

- Lean heavily on young reed.
- Climb on mineralizing root growths.
- Cut load-bearing roots.
- Stand on delicate sinter shelves.
- Force open submerged doors.
- Enter half-submerged rooms without invitation.
- Anchor to young platforms.
- Drag equipment across living surfaces.

If you are unsure whether something is structural, sacred, private, or alive, ask.

### Fire

Fire is small, domesticated, and carefully contained.

Rules:

- Use lamps only in approved areas.
- Keep flames low and shielded.
- Never leave fire unattended.
- Refuel away from open flame.
- Keep oil away from water channels.
- Have a wet mat, gravel, or smothering method nearby.
- Do not carry open flame while moving quickly.
- Extinguish completely before sleeping or leaving.

If an oil lamp tips or catches:

- Smother if possible.
- Do not panic and scatter burning oil.
- Alert locals with a visual signal if needed.
- Move away from drying fiber and reed stacks.

### Channels and debris

Channels are streets, gardens, circulation paths, and lifelines. Loose debris can strangle root-mats and block movement.

Do not drop:

- Fiber scraps.
- Broken reed.
- Food.
- Wrappers.
- Rope ends.
- Wood chips.
- Ash.
- Personal items.

If you drop something, retrieve it if safe. If not, inform locals.

### Shrines and drowned places

Older submerged rooms may be storage, nurseries, pens, or shrines. Some are practical; some are sacred; some are both.

Do not:

- Touch offerings.
- Enter flooded doorways.
- Move carved lintels.
- Disturb heat-cracked hearth stones.
- Swim through submerged courtyards without permission.
- Treat drowned spaces as ruins for exploration.

If you see something below that looks meaningful, observe quietly and ask.

## 14. Environmental ethics

The basin is young. It is being made. Your presence can alter it simply by introducing weight, heat, chemicals, noise, or organisms.

### Do not harvest casually

Do not take:

- Reed cuttings.
- Mineral crust.
- Pumice.
- Algae.
- Shells.
- Bone.
- Obsidian flakes.
- Seeds.
- Tubers.
- Bioluminescent growths.

Even a small sample may matter.

### Do not feed or disturb fauna

Fauna is rare. Pale eels, translucent shrimp, water-beetles, and exhausted moths are not attractions. Do not chase, feed, capture, or kill them.

### Do not create new channels or platforms

You are a visitor, not a terraformer. Do not:

- Stack stones to make a path.
- Cut reeds to open a view.
- Sink baskets to make footing.
- Tie platforms together.
- Divert water.
- Spread mineral dust.
- “Improve” a campsite.

### Leave no trace, but more than that

Leave no trace is the minimum. In this basin, you should aim to leave no tendency toward change.

That means:

- No scent.
- No fibers.
- No compressed mats.
- No bent reeds unless they recover.
- No altered currents.
- no introduced microbes.
- No memories tied to the landscape without permission.

## 15. Arrival protocol

If you arrive from another world, do not plunge into the basin immediately.

### Step 1: Pause at the edge

Stop on stable ground if available. Let your body adjust to altitude, warmth, humidity, and light. Check yourself for:

- Breathlessness.
- Dizziness.
- Headache.
- Nausea.
- Gear leaks.
- Loose items.

### Step 2: Clean and inspect

If you crossed through another environment, inspect gear for contamination. Rinse boots if appropriate and if you have clean water. Do not introduce foreign mud.

### Step 3: Make contact

If there is a settlement, announce yourself gently. Do not shout. Use a prearranged signal if you have one. If not, wait visibly near a safe edge.

A good arrival posture is:

- Standing or floating calmly.
- Hands visible.
- Gear secured.
- Lamp dimmed or held low.
- No sudden movement.
- No splashing.

### Step 4: Ask before entering

Ask or signal for permission before:

- Entering channels.
- Tying to any structure.
- Climbing platforms.
- Using walkways.
- Submerging.
- Camping.
- Lighting fire.

If communication is difficult, use simple drawings, open hands, and patience. Do not assume permission from silence.

### Step 5: Establish local rules

Once contact is made, try to learn:

- Where it is safe to stand.
- Where not to tie.
- Which water is safe or unsafe.
- Current vent activity.
- Rising or sinking areas.
- Restricted districts.
- Quiet hours.
- Emergency signals.
- Waste procedure.
- Sleeping areas.
- Return route.

## 16. Moving through the basin

### Above water

On walkways:

- Walk singly if the path is narrow.
- Keep your center low.
- Do not lean over rails unless they are load-bearing and intended for that.
- Avoid stepping on drying racks, hanging nets, or loose fiber.
- Do not run.
- Keep your pack tight.

On platforms:

- Distribute weight.
- Avoid jumping.
- Ask before moving stored items.
- Do not sit on edges unless safe.
- Watch for wet, slick mineral film.

### On the surface

In punts or rafts:

- Pole gently.
- Keep to channels.
- Avoid reed beds unless guided.
- Do not stand tall in wind or mist.
- Keep lamps shielded.
- Tie gear to the craft.
- Avoid colliding with root curtains.

If swimming:

- Stay near a line or guide.
- Use slow strokes.
- Keep mask secure.
- Do not dive into unknown shallows.
- Avoid inhaling water.
- Keep mouth closed if possible.

### Underwater

If you go below:

- Go with a buddy.
- Follow a line.
- Keep movements small.
- Do not exhale all your air abruptly.
- Watch for roots, nets, and lines.
- Do not enter submerged rooms unless invited.
- Do not touch offerings or carved surfaces.
- Be aware that sound and direction may confuse you.

If you see bioluminescence:

- Slow down.
- Dim artificial light if safe.
- Do not sweep through it unnecessarily.
- Do not collect it.
- Understand that your movement may leave a glowing trail; remain calm and move out gently.

## 17. Hazards and how to respond

### Drowning or near-drowning

Prevention is primary.

If someone is struggling:

- Do not rush in and become a second victim.
- Extend a pole, line, or float if possible.
- Signal for help.
- Approach from behind if you must physically assist.
- Keep the person’s face above water.
- Once safe, check breathing and warmth.

### Entanglement

If entangled:

- Stop.
- Breathe slowly.
- Do not pull harder.
- Trace the snag.
- Back out if possible.
- Cut only if necessary, and only away from vital roots or lines if you know what you are doing.

### Unstable crust or sinking shelf

If a surface begins to give:

- Spread your weight.
- Lower yourself.
- Move toward known stable ground.
- Do not run.
- Use a pole to test ahead.
- Alert others softly but urgently.

If you fall through:

- Protect your head.
- Do not kick blindly.
- Grasp stable edges.
- Avoid hot or chemically sharp water if you can feel a difference.
- Check for burns, cuts, or irritation after escape.

### Heat exhaustion

Symptoms:

- Weakness.
- Nausea.
- Headache.
- Confusion.
- Rapid pulse.
- Dizziness.

Response:

- Stop activity.
- Move to shade or cooler air.
- Hydrate with electrolytes if conscious.
- Remove excess clothing.
- Fan gently.
- Monitor closely.
- Evacuate if symptoms worsen.

### Heat stroke

Symptoms:

- Confusion.
- Collapse.
- Very hot skin.
- Vomiting.
- Seizure.
- Unresponsiveness.

Response:

- Emergency.
- Cool the person as rapidly as possible.
- Shade, fan, expose skin to air.
- Do not force fluids if not fully conscious.
- Evacuate immediately.

### Altitude sickness

Mild symptoms:

- Headache.
- Poor appetite.
- Nausea.
- Fatigue.
- Poor sleep.

Response:

- Rest.
- Hydrate.
- Avoid exertion.
- Do not ascend further.
- Descend if symptoms worsen.

Severe signs:

- Breathlessness at rest.
- Cough with frothy sputum.
- Confusion.
- Loss of balance.
- Blue lips or severe lethargy.

Response:

- Immediate descent.
- Emergency evacuation.
- Oxygen if available and appropriate.

### Dangerous gases or bad air

The basin has warm springs and mineral activity. Some gases may be harmless at low levels, others not.

Warning signs:

- Sudden stronger sulfur smell.
- Smell disappearing unexpectedly.
- Headache.
- Eye irritation.
- Dizziness.
- Nausea.
- Unexplained dead insects or moths.
- Air that feels heavy or oppressive.

Response:

- Move to fresher air.
- Avoid low pockets and enclosed spaces.
- Do not use open flame if gas is suspected.
- Do not attempt rescue without protection if someone collapses in bad air.
- Use a line or pole from a safe distance if possible.

### Fire

If a lamp or small fire tips:

- Smother with a wet mat, sand, gravel, or clay cover if available.
- Keep it from spreading to reed or fiber.
- Do not splash burning oil wildly.
- Alert locals visually.
- Move flammable items away if safe.

If fire spreads:

- Evacuate.
- Do not become trapped on a floating platform.
- Keep low.
- Protect airway with damp cloth if smoke is present.

### Sudden water rise or platform shift

Most changes are slow, but some can become noticeable during a visit.

Signs:

- A walkway tilting.
- A line hanging differently.
- A channel flowing in a new direction.
- A platform feeling less stable.
- A new warmth in the water.
- A change in algae color.
- Local alarm signals.

Response:

- Stop.
- Move to stable, older ground if known.
- Do not overload platforms.
- Follow local instructions.
- Do not try to “fix” structures unless trained.
- Keep your gear tied and buoyant.

### Lost in mist or dim water

If you become separated:

- Stop immediately.
- Do not swim blindly.
- Hold a known line or stable structure if permitted.
- Make yourself visible with a dim lamp or signal cloth.
- Use prearranged signals.
- Wait for your guide or buddy.
- If you must move, follow established lines, not apparent shortcuts.

Do not chase lights or shapes unless you recognize them.

- Danger.

- Bad air.
- Quiet.

## 19. Gifts and exchange

Be very careful with gifts. In many places, gifts are welcome. Here, they can become contaminants, burdens, or violations.

The safest gift is usually:

- Labor.
- Respect.
- Patience.
- Careful attention.
- Help with approved tasks.

If you wish to bring a material gift, ask first. If allowed, choose:

- Inert.
- Clean.
- Non-biological.
- Non-toxic.
- Easily kept or removed.
- Not shiny or metallic unless culturally appropriate.
- Not heavy.
- Not sharp unless offered as a tool with permission.

Avoid:

- Food from other worlds.
- Plants.
- Animals.
- Soil.
- Jewelry that sheds metal.
- Perfumed items.
- Complex machines.
- Objects that imply ownership or claim.

If in doubt, do not give.

## 20. If you stay overnight or longer

Longer stays require humility. You are not camping in a wilderness; you are lodging inside a living, maintained system.

### Sleep

- Sleep only where approved.
- Keep your sleeping area dry.
- Keep gear tied.
- Keep a lamp or signal within reach.
- Do not sleep near unstable crusts or young root mats.
- Keep your dry clothes sealed.
- Check for rising water before sleeping if locals advise.

### Food

- Eat in designated areas if possible.
- Do not let crumbs fall into water.
- Store food sealed and raised.
- Wash containers without contaminating channels.
- Pack out waste.

### Work

If invited to help, start with simple tasks:

- Carrying light loads.
- Holding lines.
- Passing materials.
- Cleaning safe areas.
- Drying fiber.
- Mending cordage.
- Moving floats under instruction.

Do not:

- Cut.
- Anchor.
- Prune.
- Build.
- Tie structural knots.
- Direct water.
- Move platforms.

until you are taught.

### Daily rhythm

Adopt the local rhythm:

- Move slowly.
- Speak little.
- Observe changes.
- Check your gear.
- Check your skin.
- Hydrate.
- Rest.
- Watch lamps and signals.
- Do not force schedules.

The basin will not speed up for you.

## 21. Psychological preparation

This place can feel uncanny. It is full of human presence but sparse of animals. It is warm like a body, dim like twilight, wet like amniotic space, and quiet like a shrine. Some travelers may find it soothing; others may feel watched, submerged, or unnerved.

Prepare mentally by accepting:

- You may feel constantly damp.
- You may miss crisp air and dry ground.
- You may feel disoriented by green light and mist.
- You may feel emotionally slowed down.
- You may dream strangely.
- You may become unusually aware of ropes, creaks, water pressure, and small changes.

Ground yourself with quiet routines:

- Check your gear at set intervals.
- Keep a small journal.
- Practice slow breathing.
- Maintain hydration and nutrition.
- Do not isolate yourself from your guide or group.
- Speak softly but honestly if you feel unwell.

If the place begins to feel overwhelming, leave. There is no shame in needing solid, dry, cooler ground.

## 22. Special notes for technology

A multiverse traveler may be tempted to bring advanced equipment. In this basin, advanced technology is not automatically superior.

### Electronics

Electronics will suffer from:

- Humidity.
- Mineral corrosion.
- Condensation.
- Water exposure.
- Temperature changes.
- Lack of dry maintenance space.

If you bring electronics:

- Seal them hermetically.
- Use non-reactive casings.
- Keep batteries safe.
- Avoid lithium exposure to water if possible.
- Bring backups.
- Do not rely on them solely.

### Metal

Metal may:

- Rust.
- Stain.
- React with mineral water.
- Offend local custom.
- Damage soft materials.
- Snag and cut living roots.

Prefer:

- Ceramic.
- Stone.
- Bone.
- Wood.
- Fiber.
- Shell.
- Resin.
- Pumice.
- Clay.

### Powered devices

Avoid powered devices unless necessary. Noise, vibration, and turbulence are disruptive. The basin’s communication is subtle; a powered device can erase that subtlety.

Keep one sealed, buoyant emergency bundle containing:

- First aid basics.

- Lamp or light source.
- Spare cord.
- Repair patches.

Before entering, know:

- Where you will exit.

- What signal means “emergency.”
- What signal means “evacuate.”
- Where stable high ground or rim access is.
- What to do if your guide is separated.
- What to do if your return route is delayed.

### If evacuation is needed

- Move toward stable, older ground.
- Keep your emergency bundle tied.
- Avoid overloaded platforms.
- Descend from altitude if altitude illness is suspected.
- Seek fresh air if gas or bad air is suspected.
- Keep warm if moving to the rim or cooler zones.
- Do not leave injured companions alone if avoidable.

## 24. Departure protocol

Leaving well is as important as arriving well.

Before departure:

- Remove all personal items.
- Untie all temporary lines you were authorized to use.
- Pack out all waste.
- Check for dropped objects.
- Inspect sleeping area for fibers, crumbs, or debris.
- Restore any moved items only if instructed.
- Thank your hosts if possible.
- Ask whether anything needs repair or cleaning because of your stay.

After leaving:

- Clean and dry gear.
- Inspect for contamination before entering another world.
- Monitor your health for skin infections, ear infections, respiratory irritation, heat effects, or altitude symptoms.
- Do not share local organisms or materials casually.
- Record what you learned in a way that does not endanger the place.

If you took images or notes, consider whether they should be limited, protected, or contextualized. A fragile world can be harmed by careless attention.

## 25. A traveler’s creed for this basin

If you want a simple code, use this:

**Be light.**  
Carry little. Move gently. Add no weight to young ground.

**Be quiet.**  
Let sound be rare. Let signals be clear. Let silence be respectful.

**Be buoyant.**  
Keep your gear afloat. Keep your body safe. Keep your burdens from dragging others.

**Be reversible.**  
Tie only what can be untied. Build nothing permanent. Leave nothing behind.

**Be patient.**  
The basin is deciding what it will become. Your job is not to decide for it.

**Be clean.**  
Bring no foreign life. Leave no waste. Let the water remain as you found it, or better.

**Be taught.**  
The inhabitants understand growth, weight, water, and time in ways you may not. Listen before acting.

## 26. Final intuitive preparation

Imagine arriving at the rim. The air is thin. Warm rain hangs before you like a curtain. Below, the water is not blue but green-gold, blurred with life and mineral dust. Small orange lamps move along reed walkways. The horizon is not sharp; it is being negotiated by mist, steam, new stone, and floating gardens.

Your first instinct as a traveler from a harder, drier world may be to seek solid ground, firm walls, clear routes, and reliable permanence. That instinct must be softened. Here, solid ground may be young and unsafe. Walls may be alive. Routes may be ropes remembered by touch. Permanence is not the goal; adaptability is.

Prepare your body for warmth that does not cool you. Prepare your lungs for altitude. Prepare your skin for endless moisture. Prepare your eyes for green twilight. Prepare your ears for muffled sound. Prepare your hands for wet knots. Prepare your feet for surfaces that may be floor, plant, crust, or future land.

Above all, prepare your temperament. The basin will not reward force. It will reward care.

If you arrive light, humble, clean, quiet, and willing to move at the pace of growing reeds and mineral crusts, the place may open to you. You will see how a house can begin as a raft and end as a pale honeycombed structure still alive at the edges. You will understand how a street can be a channel, a plaza a pool, a memory a knot, and a drowned room a shrine rather than a loss.

But if you arrive heavy, loud, hurried, extractive, or careless, you will not merely be unsafe. You will be wrong for the place.

So prepare as if you are visiting a world in the middle of becoming. Because you are.
