Prepare as if you are entering a vast, warm, wet, living machine that has already become complete without you.

This is not a ruin to be looted, a wilderness to be conquered, or a city to be “rediscovered.” It is an established condition: high altitude, constant moisture, mineral surfaces, slow industry, dense animal life, no plants, and a pervasive sense that everything has been worn into its proper shape by time, water, and use. An ordinary human visitor can do well here, but only by becoming careful, slow, quiet, and respectful. The main dangers are not dramatic monsters or sudden catastrophes. They are dampness, slipping, exhaustion, dehydration, breathing the wrong aerosol, touching the wrong machine, disturbing the wrong nest, and underestimating how large and slow the place is.

The traveler who will fare best is the one who prepares to remain dry enough, breathe cleanly, move with patience, and leave almost no trace.

---

## 1. The First Intuition: Become a Slow, Dry, Quiet Body

Your primary goal is not exploration in the romantic sense. It is adaptation.

The city will not attack you. It will simply continue doing what it does: condensing water, circulating warmth, moving animals along worn routes, turning machinery at geological patience, and polishing every surface until your ordinary human feet find it uncertain. You must adjust your body to that rhythm.

Think in terms of three governing rules:

1. **Stay dry enough.**  
   Not perfectly dry — that may be impossible — but dry enough to avoid skin breakdown, chilling, fungal infection, blisters, and exhaustion.

2. **Move slowly.**  
   The terrain is slippery, vertical, crowded with life, and threaded with old machinery. Speed will punish you.

3. **Do not become a contaminant.**  
   This place has no plants and a delicate mineral-fungal-animal ecology. Your boots, food, breath, waste, skin flakes, soap, and gear can all matter. Prepare to be biologically disciplined.

If you can internalize those three rules, most other preparations follow naturally.

## 2. Understand What Kind of Place You Are Entering

Before packing, understand the environment’s character.

### It is high

The air is thinner than lowland air. Even if the temperature is warm, altitude can cause headache, fatigue, shortness of breath, poor sleep, and impaired judgment. You may feel fine at first and only notice the altitude when climbing stairs, carrying weight, or trying to sleep.

### It is warm but not simple

The warmth comes from below, from geothermal heat, industrial channels, and dark stone releasing heat slowly. It is not necessarily tropical, but it is steady. Combined with moisture, it creates a bathhouse feeling. You can sweat without realizing it, then become chilled by mist or wind on an exposed upper terrace.

### It is wet without rain

This is important. You are not preparing for a storm. You are preparing for perpetual condensation, seepage, mist, dripping, wet walls, wet stairs, wet railings, wet stone, and shallow water in every depression. The wetness is subtle and total. It gets into boots, cuffs, sleeves, bags, electronics, and skin folds.

### It has no plants

Do not expect familiar comforts: shade trees, grass, edible greens, flowers, soil, pollen, or the psychological relief of greenery. Food cannot be foraged in any ordinary human sense. The base of the ecology is fungal, microbial, and mineral. You should assume that local organisms are not safe to eat, touch, or breathe unless you have expert knowledge — and probably not even then.

### It is densely inhabited

The city is full of animals, but not necessarily animals that behave like mammals in a park. They occupy every surface: walls, ducts, roofs, pipes, gutters, machines, cisterns, and hollow interiors. Most may be small, but their density means you are never truly alone. You must prepare to share space without interfering.

### It is industrial but not human-scaled

The machinery is heavy, slow, electrified, and sometimes half-absorbed into the city. It may look inactive until you notice that a drum has moved an inch, a pump has changed pitch, or a sluice gate is adjusting somewhere below. Do not treat machines as scenery.

### It is vast

This is one of the biggest practical hazards. The city is not a single plaza or temple. It has districts, levels, basins, terraces, rail lines, reservoirs, tunnels, condensing towers, and long industrial yards. Distances may be deceptive in mist. Routes may be three-dimensional. A “short walk” can become a dangerous ordeal if you descend too far, enter the wrong basin, or follow an animal path into a wall.

## 3. Who Should Go, and Who Should Hesitate

An ordinary human can visit, but not every ordinary human is equally suited.

### You should be especially cautious if you have:

- Heart or lung conditions  
- Asthma or reactive airways  
- Severe allergies to mold, fungi, dust, or animal dander  
- Balance problems or weak joints  
- Claustrophobia  
- Fear of dense animals or insects  
- Compromised immune function  
- Pregnancy or conditions worsened by altitude  
- A history of severe altitude sickness  
- Open wounds or chronic skin conditions  
- Dependency on refrigerated medication, unless you can keep it stable

This does not mean such travelers cannot go, but they should prepare more conservatively, perhaps shorten the visit, avoid lower basins, and have a very clear exit plan.

### A good candidate is someone who can:

- Walk slowly for hours on uneven wet stone  
- Climb stairs without panicking  
- Carry a moderate pack  
- Tolerate humidity and damp clothing  
- Sleep despite constant background noise  
- Remain calm when surrounded by animals  
- Follow strict hygiene and contamination discipline  
- Turn back when conditions become marginal

The ideal temperament is patient, observant, and unhurried.

## 4. Pre-Visit Preparation Before You Even Pack

### Acclimatize if possible

If you can spend time at moderate altitude before entering, do so. If not, plan a slow first day. Do not arrive and immediately make a long crossing. Spend the first hours resting, hydrating, observing, and learning the local rhythms.

### Check your body

Before departure:

- Make sure tetanus vaccination is current.  
- Treat any cuts, blisters, or skin infections.  
- Address dental problems; remote pain becomes miserable.  
- If you use contact lenses, switch to glasses if possible. Humidity, spores, and mist make contacts risky.  
- Trim nails. Long toenails will punish you on descents.  
- Start well-hydrated.

### Plan the visit as a reconnaissance, not a conquest

For an ordinary traveler, the first visit should be short. A few hours to one day is enough. If you must stay overnight, do so only after you have tested your gear, your breathing, your footing, and your mental comfort.

### Choose your entry point carefully

If you have any control over where you arrive, avoid:

- Low basins  
- Flooded cisterns  
- Active turbine channels  
- Dense roosting cliffs  
- Narrow tunnels  
- Large condensing arrays exposed to wind  
- Places with obvious dripping from above  
- Areas with strong sulfur or chemical smells

A good entry point would be a middle-level terrace or service walkway: sheltered enough from mist and wind, elevated above standing water, close to visible routes, and not directly under heavy roosts or machinery.

### Establish a return and communication plan

As a multiverse traveler, your return method is your lifeline. Protect it from moisture, heat, and mineral deposits.

- Keep the return device in a waterproof, shockproof case.  
- Store a backup activation method if possible.  
- Set a check-in schedule with someone outside the location.  
- Mark your arrival point mentally and physically, using removable markers only if necessary.  
- Avoid relying solely on electronics; mist, iron, and electrical infrastructure may interfere with signals.

If you lose your way, your first task is to stop, get dry enough, and re-establish orientation before moving.

## 5. Clothing: The Central Problem Is Managing Dampness

You will not stay perfectly dry. Your goal is to manage moisture so your body does not break down.

### Avoid cotton

Cotton absorbs water and stays wet. In a warm wet place it can cause chafing, maceration, and chilling when wind appears. Avoid cotton shirts, jeans, socks, and underwear.

### Use quick-drying layers

A sensible clothing system:

- **Base layer:** Lightweight synthetic or merino wool. Merino is excellent because it resists odor and retains some warmth when damp.  
- **Mid layer:** Light fleece or synthetic insulation. You may not need heavy warmth, but upper terraces and wind can chill you.  
- **Outer layer:** A breathable wind-and-mist shell. A heavy rain jacket may trap sweat; a light shell or smock may be better.  
- **Spare dry layer:** Keep one complete dry set sealed for camp or emergency.

### Protect your neck, hands, and head

A soft hat or wide-brimmed hat helps keep drips off your face. A neck gaiter can protect against mist, spores, and animal contact. Thin gloves protect your hands when gripping wet rails or stone, but they will get wet. Bring a spare pair.

### Choose colors carefully

Wear muted, non-reflective colors: gray, brown, dull blue, stone, rust. Bright colors may attract animals. Shiny surfaces may draw investigation from fliers or curious creatures. Avoid perfumes, scented detergents, and strong-smelling gear.

### Do not bring unnecessary fabric

Every extra piece of cloth becomes a damp problem. Bring only what you need.

## 6. Footwear: Your Most Important Survival System

The city’s surfaces are polished by water, claws, and time. A fall here could be more dangerous than the animals or machines. Footwear is not a minor detail.

### What you want

- Excellent grip on wet stone  
- Drainage or fast drying  
- Ankle support  
- Protection from sharp mineral edges  
- Comfort for long stairs  
- Compatibility with thick socks  
- Ability to be cleaned and disinfected

### What to avoid

- Smooth-soled shoes  
- Heavy waterproof boots that trap water once it gets in  
- New boots never tested on wet stone  
- Open sandals as primary footwear, though they may be useful in camp

A good choice may be sturdy approach shoes or light hiking boots with sticky rubber soles and drainage. If you expect to wade, consider amphibious footwear, but protect your ankles.

### Socks are sacred

Bring more socks than you think you need.

- At least three pairs for a day trip  
- Five or more pairs for overnight  
- Wool or synthetic, never cotton  
- Thin liner socks if you are prone to blisters  
- One completely dry pair reserved only for sleeping

Change socks at the first sign of dampness, hot spots, or discomfort. Wet feet become foolish feet.

### Foot care routine

Every rest break:

1. Check for hot spots.  
2. Dry feet if possible.  
3. Apply anti-chafe balm or antifungal powder if needed.  
4. Rotate socks.  
5. Inspect toes for maceration.

Bring antifungal powder or cream. This environment is exactly the kind of place where ordinary human feet can develop problems quickly.

## 7. Breathing: Protect Your Lungs From Mist, Spores, Dust, and Animal Air

The air may be breathable, but it is not neutral.

It carries:

- Warm mist  
- Mineral dust  
- Fungal spores  
- Microbial aerosols  
- Animal dander or feather-like particulates  
- Dung dust from roosts  
- Possible geothermal gases, especially in lower basins  
- Faint sulfur or metallic industrial smells

You should not assume all of this is harmless.

### Carry respiratory protection

At minimum, bring well-fitting particulate respirators:

- N95/FFP2 for light protection  
- P100/FFP3 or equivalent for better protection  
- A half-mask with particulate filters if you plan to enter basins, roost areas, or dusty industrial spaces

If you suspect gases, use combination cartridges rated for organic vapors and acid gases, but understand their limits. A simple particulate mask will not protect against carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, carbon monoxide, or oxygen-poor air.

### Consider a small gas monitor

If multiverse travel permits, a portable atmospheric monitor is wise. It should check:

- Oxygen level  
- Carbon dioxide  
- Hydrogen sulfide  
- Carbon monoxide  
- Possibly sulfur dioxide or methane, depending on your equipment

If you do not have one, use conservative behavior:

- Avoid low, enclosed basins if you feel headache, dizziness, nausea, or unusual breathlessness.  
- Leave immediately if eyes or throat become irritated.  
- Do not linger where insects avoid or where air feels “thicker.”  
- Do not rely on smell alone.

### Use eye protection

Mist, spores, and small fliers can irritate eyes. Bring:

- Clear or lightly tinted safety glasses  
- Anti-fog solution  
- A spare pair of glasses if you wear prescription lenses  
- No contact lenses if avoidable

If your eyes burn, rinse with clean water and leave the area.

## 8. Hearing: The Loudness Is Cumulative

The place is loud not because of one noise, but because of many layered noises: water, machinery, wings, calls, dripping, pumps, fans, bodies shifting. This can become exhausting, especially when sleeping.

Bring:

- Foam earplugs for sleep  
- High-fidelity earplugs for travel if sound becomes overwhelming  
- Optional noise-reducing ear protection that still allows you to hear hazards

Do not block your hearing completely. You need to hear:

- Changes in machinery  
- Water surges  
- Animal alarm calls  
- Falling debris  
- Your own companions

A good compromise is moderate attenuation rather than total silence.

## 9. Water: Abundant, Warm, and Almost Certainly Unsafe

You are surrounded by water, but you must treat it as a biological and chemical hazard.

### Do not drink untreated water

Even clear condensation may contain:

- Microbial films  
- Birdlike-animal droppings  
- Insect remnants  
- Mineral salts  
- Metal residues  
- Industrial lubricants or byproducts  
- Fungal spores  
- Geothermal sulfur compounds

### Carry enough capacity

For a warm high-altitude environment, plan for at least 2–3 liters per person per day, and possibly more if exertion is high. Carry:

- Two 1-liter bottles as baseline  
- A 2–3 liter reservoir or collapsible container  
- A backup container

### Purify in layers

Use multiple methods:

1. **Prefilter** if water has visible particles.  
2. **Use a fine filter**, ideally 0.2 micron or smaller, to remove bacteria and many protozoa.  
3. **Use chemical treatment** if viral contamination is possible.  
4. **Boil if you have a stove and fuel**, especially for suspicious water.

UV purifiers can help, but they work poorly in cloudy or particle-rich water. Filter first.

### Where to collect, if you must

Best options, in rough order:

1. Moving condensation from upper collection troughs, if accessible and not obviously fouled.  
2. Fast-moving channels above basins, away from animal congregations.  
3. Clear runoff from vertical surfaces, if it does not smell odd.

Avoid:

- Stagnant pools  
- Water with oily sheens  
- Water near microbial mats  
- Water in cisterns with eel-like animals  
- Water that smells strongly of sulfur, metal, or dung  
- Water below dense roosts  
- Water in flooded maintenance tunnels

### Warm water may damage some filters

Some filter membranes do not tolerate very hot water. If the water feels uncomfortably warm, let it cool before filtering. Do not drink water that feels hot enough to burn.

### Replace electrolytes

Purified water lacks minerals, and humidity makes you sweat. Bring electrolyte tablets or powder. Do not substitute local mineral crusts or pressed salt blocks unless you know exactly what they are. Assume they are industrial, not food.

## 10. Food: Bring Everything, Pack Out Everything

There are no plants. That means no familiar foraging. The fungal and animal life is not automatically edible, and unknown organisms may be toxic, allergenic, parasitic, or simply indigestible.

### Bring sealed, ready-to-eat food

Good choices:

- Nut bars  
- Sealed sandwiches  
- Jerky or plant-protein alternatives  
- Nut packets  
- Tortillas or crackers in rigid containers  
- Meal replacement shakes  
- Dried fruit only if securely sealed and not a contamination risk  
- Soft cheeses or shelf-stable pouches if climate-appropriate  
- High-calorie emergency rations

Avoid:

- Fresh fruit with seeds that could escape  
- Loose grains  
- Crumbly foods  
- Strong-smelling foods  
- Anything that could attract animals

### Store food like you are in a city of scavengers

Animals here are not monsters, but they are numerous, curious, and adapted to exploiting surfaces. Food must be:

- Odor-proof if possible  
- In sealed hard containers or robust dry bags  
- Stored away from your sleeping area  
- Never left unattended

Do not cook unless necessary. Cooking creates smells, waste heat, grease, and food particles. If you must cook, use a stove in a safe open area, away from nests, fungal crusts, and machinery. Do not dump food scraps or grease anywhere.

### Pack out all food waste

This includes:

- Wrappers  
- Crumbs  
- Fruit peels  
- Used wipes  
- Grease packets  
- Tea bags  
- Chewed material  
- Anything organic

Do not feed animals. Feeding them could alter behavior, introduce disease, or concentrate predators and scavengers in dangerous ways.

## 11. Shelter and Sleeping: Sleep Dry or Suffer

If you stay overnight, sleep preparation becomes critical. The city never becomes fully dry, and the noise never fully stops.

### Choose a camp site carefully

Look for:

- Elevation above standing water  
- A surface that is worn and stable  
- Protection from overhead drips  
- Shelter from wind  
- Distance from active machinery  
- Distance from dense roosts  
- Distance from drainage channels  
- No signs of animal traffic through the exact spot

Avoid:

- Low corners where water collects  
- Beneath nests or roosting ledges  
- Near pressure valves or sluice gates  
- Inside ducts or hollow machines  
- On top of fungal crusts  
- In flooded or semi-flooded areas  
- Under loose nesting material  
- In narrow channels that could fill if pumps change

A middle terrace, slightly recessed but not a drainage corner, is often better than the open condensing district or the low basins.

### Sleep off the wet surface

Do not lie directly on stone. Bring:

- Insulated sleeping pad  
- Lightweight cot if feasible  
- Closed-cell foam pad as backup  
- Groundsheet or durable tarp

Stone will draw heat and conduct dampness even when the air feels warm.

### Use a breathable shelter

A fully sealed tent may fill with condensation from your own breath. A good system might be:

- Inner mesh shelter to keep animals out  
- Tarp or rainfly angled to shed drips  
- Drip lines or gutters to guide water away  
- Small vestibule for wet boots and pack

You may need to anchor to stone. Since stakes are useless, bring:

- Cordage  
- Webbing straps  
- Soft anchors  
- Small sandbags or stuff sacks you can fill with clean stone, then empty and pack out  
- Non-marking hooks only if absolutely necessary and permitted

Do not damage surfaces or wedge gear into living crusts.

### Keep a dry sleep kit

Your sleeping clothes should never be worn while traveling. Keep them sealed:

- Dry base layer  
- Dry socks  
- Light insulation  
- Hat if upper levels are windy

Sleeping in damp clothes is a common way to become chilled and miserable.

### Protect your head and ears

Even if the air is warm, constant dripping and noise can make rest difficult. Use:

- Earplugs  
- Hood or hat  
- Eye mask if light is disturbing  
- A small dry sack for your headlamp and return device

### Do not sleep with food

Store food sealed and separated from your sleeping area. Animals may investigate smells.

## 12. Navigation: The City Can Swallow Your Sense of Direction

The biggest orientation problem is not darkness. It is repetition, mist, verticality, and scale.

### Carry navigation tools

Bring:

- Paper map if available  
- Waterproof notebook  
- Pencil or grease pencil  
- Compass, but expect local iron to disturb it  
- Inertial or gyroscopic compass if possible  
- Altimeter if altitude zones matter  
- Pedometer or step counter  
- Watch  
- Whistle for emergencies  
- Small marker flags or removable tape, only if you can remove them completely

Do not rely entirely on electronic maps. Moisture and metal will humble your devices.

### Learn the logic of water

Water is the city’s main organizer.

- Water generally moves downward toward basins.  
- Channels, gutters, and pipes indicate flow.  
- Upper condensing districts collect fog and send water down.  
- Lower basins receive water and may recycle it upward through pumps.

If you are lost, ask: Am I moving toward deeper water, or toward air and light? In many cases, moving upward toward condensing structures may improve visibility, but it may expose you to wind. Moving downward may lead to thick air, insects, and larger animals. Neither direction is automatically safe.

### Use landmarks that persist

Good landmarks:

- Large condensing frames  
- Distinctive turbine channels  
- Broad stair switchbacks  
- Unusual retaining walls  
- Junctions of multiple pipes  
- Rail lines  
- Reservoir edges  
- Roosting towers

Poor landmarks:

- Small nests  
- Fungal patches  
- Temporary pools  
- Animal trails  
- Mist openings  
- Sounds, because moisture distorts them

### Mark carefully

If you must mark a route, use temporary, removable methods. Do not carve stone, paint walls, or damage surfaces. The city’s wear is part of its living structure.

### Travel conservatively

Before moving deep into the city:

- Note the time.  
- Estimate how long return will take, then double it.  
- Turn back before fatigue.  
- Avoid entering tunnels unless necessary.  
- Do not follow animal paths blindly.  
- Do not descend into basins without a clear reason and exit route.

## 13. Movement: Walk Like the City Wore You Smooth Too

Your movement should be quiet, deliberate, and balanced.

### The basic gait

- Keep your center of gravity low.  
- Shorten your steps.  
- Place your whole foot down when possible.  
- Test uncertain surfaces with a pole before committing weight.  
- Use three points of contact on stairs and steep sections.  
- Avoid stepping on dark slick films, pale fungal crusts, or wet mineral deposits.

### What not to step on

- Microbial films: extremely slippery.  
- Fungal crusts: fragile and ecologically important.  
- Nesting crusts: may hide eggs or young.  
- Rounded stone edges: polished by water and claws.  
- Metal grates glazed with condensation.  
- Salt-streaked surfaces that may be brittle or sharp.  
- Loose debris that may conceal a drain or animal burrow.

### Use a walking pole

A lightweight trekking pole or staff can help test pools, check stability, and reduce knee strain on stairs. Use a broad, non-sparking tip if possible. Do not jab it into fungal mats or nests.

### Watch overhead

The city is used vertically. Be aware of:

- Drips from upper levels  
- Wings passing through grates  
- Animals moving along beams  
- Old nesting material that may shift  
- Cables and pipes crossing walkways  
- Low lintels and tunnel ceilings

A lightweight helmet or bump cap is wise in industrial tiers.

### Keep loose things secured

Machinery and animals both exploit loose objects.

- Tie back hair.  
- Avoid scarves or dangling straps.  
- Clip tools to your pack.  
- Close all zippers.  
- Do not let a water bottle swing from a carabiner near gears.

## 14. Animals: You Are a Guest in a Crowded Reef

The animals are not described as monstrous or malicious. They are dense, adapted, and busy. Your job is to avoid disrupting them and avoid being bitten, scratched, swarmed, or startled.

### General rules

- Observe from a distance.  
- Do not touch animals.  
- Do not feed animals.  
- Do not corner animals.  
- Do not put hands into holes, pipes, or dark crevices.  
- Do not disturb nests, egg masses, molting clusters, or roosts.  
- Do not block established animal routes.  
- Do not make sudden loud noises unless necessary for safety.  
- Keep food sealed.  
- Keep your pack closed.

### Insects

Insects may rise in columns near warm vents. If you encounter dense clouds:

- Cover mouth and nose if you have a mask.  
- Lower or close eyes.  
- Move around, not through.  
- Do not flail wildly.  
- Check clothing afterward for trapped individuals.

If insects are attracted to lights, avoid bright lights at night. Use red or dim modes if possible.

### Crustacean-like scavengers

These creatures may be armored and patient. They are likely harmless if left alone, but do not put hands into channels where they feed. They may pinch if threatened.

### Amphibious flat-bodied creatures

They may look inert in warm runoff. Do not step on them or disturb them. They may move suddenly. Avoid touching because their skin could secrete irritating compounds.

### Snails and limpet-like grazers

They leave clean trails, but their surfaces may be slick. Do not scrape them with bare hands. Some may carry parasites.

### Eel-like cistern animals

Avoid entering cisterns or flooded tunnels. You do not know their size, behavior, or whether they can entangle limbs. If you see movement in deep water, back away.

### Birdlike roosting populations

These may be one of the most significant hazards, not because they are aggressive, but because they are numerous.

Risks include:

- Droppings making surfaces slippery  
- Respiratory irritation from dried particulates  
- Startled mass movement  
- Noise  
- Nesting material falling  
- Aggressive defense during breeding or molting periods

If you must pass near roosts:

- Wear respiratory protection.  
- Avoid looking directly into dense roosting masses if that provokes them.  
- Move slowly.  
- Do not climb beneath active roosts.  
- Do not touch nests.  
- Leave immediately if the flock begins to shift as one body.

### Larger animals in basins

The description says larger animals wade or swim with confidence. Give them space. Do not approach. Do not attempt to herd or photograph them closely. If one moves toward you, back away slowly and avoid blocking its route.

### If an animal approaches you

Most likely it is curious, opportunistic, or using a route you happen to occupy.

1. Stop.  
2. Do not run.  
3. Make yourself look calm but not trapped.  
4. Back away if possible.  
5. Let it pass.  
6. Do not throw things unless safety requires it.  
7. If it becomes aggressive, use noise, light, or a barrier before physical contact.

### If bitten or scratched

- Wash immediately with clean water.  
- Disinfect.  
- Stop bleeding.  
- Cover wound completely.  
- Take note of the animal’s appearance.  
- Watch for infection.  
- Leave the area if possible.  
- Seek medical care as soon as you can exit.

Assume unknown bites may require antibiotics.

## 15. Fungi and Microbial Surfaces: Do Not Treat Them Like Decorations

The fungal and microbial layers are the base of the ecology. They are also potentially hazardous to ordinary human lungs and skin.

### Hazards

- Spores  
- Allergic reactions  
- Respiratory irritation  
- Slippery surfaces  
- Fragile crusts  
- Unknown toxins  
- Powdery growths near vents

### Rules

- Do not disturb fungal crusts.  
- Do not sit on them.  
- Do not scrape them.  
- Do not breathe dust from powdery growths.  
- Wear gloves if you must touch surfaces near them.  
- Wear a mask in dense fungal areas.  
- Clean gear afterward.

If you develop cough, fever, chest tightness, eye irritation, or skin rash after exposure, leave and seek medical attention.

## 16. Machinery: Slow Does Not Mean Safe

The machines are part of the city’s metabolism. They move water, heat, and power. They may be slow enough to seem harmless, but slow machinery can have enormous torque.

### Keep distance from:

- Turbine channels  
- Rotating drums  
- Gears  
- Fans  
- Hoists  
- Sluice gates  
- Pressure valves  
- Pump housings  
- Boilers and heat exchangers  
- Rail switches  
- Cables and insulators  
- Junction boxes  
- Steam or vapor vents

### Do not touch

- Cables, even if they look insulated  
- Glazed insulators, because moisture may make them conductive  
- Leaking valves  
- Warm metal unless you know it is safe  
- Control mechanisms  
- Pressure gauges  
- Moving parts  
- Abandoned-looking machines that are still partially active

### Watch for changes

If you hear:

- A new clang  
- A change in pump rhythm  
- A hiss of pressure  
- A gate adjusting  
- Water suddenly speeding up  
- A fan changing pitch  

Move away from machinery and channels.

### Heat hazards

Warm surfaces are common, but some may be hotter than they look. Test metal with the back of a gloved hand before leaning or sitting. Do not place gear directly on warm machinery. Do not use industrial heat to dry clothing unless you are certain it is safe and will not damage equipment or start contamination.

### Electrical hazards

Water and electricity are a bad combination. Assume all exposed electrical infrastructure is dangerous.

- Do not touch junction boxes.  
- Do not climb poles.  
- Do not lean on metal structures near cables.  
- Do not use wet gloves to touch anything electrical.  
- Do not attempt to tap power for charging.  
- Keep devices away from condensation drip lines.

## 17. Hygiene and Waste: Leave No Biological Trace

This is crucial because the ecology is plantless, mineral, fungal, and animal. Human waste, soap, food particles, and even skin flakes can matter.

### Human waste

The safest approach is to pack it out.

Bring:

- Waste bags  
- Absorbent powder  
- Sealable odor-proof bags  
- Toilet paper in a seal bag  
- Hand sanitizer

Do not defecate in water channels, basins, cisterns, or near nests. Do not bury waste in stone where nothing can decompose it normally. If you must urinate, avoid all standing water and food-web areas. In a sensitive ecology, packing out urine with absorbent material is the most responsible option for extended visits.

### Washing

Do not wash with soap in the city’s water system.

- Use hand sanitizer.  
- Use unscented wipes.  
- If you must rinse, use a small amount of clean water in a container and pack out the wastewater.  
- Avoid biodegradable soap unless you are certain it is safe; even biodegradable products can disrupt unfamiliar ecologies.

### Menstrual products

Pack out all products. Use sealable bags. Do not leave anything in drains or pools.

### Wound care waste

Pack out used bandages, wipes, and packaging. Blood may attract scavengers; cover wounds carefully and dispose of materials securely.

## 18. Biosecurity: Do Not Bring Green Into the Plantless City

The absence of plants is not just aesthetic. It means the ecology may be unusually sensitive to seeds, plant matter, soil, pollen, and foreign microbes.

### Before arrival

- Scrub boots thoroughly.  
- Remove soil from gear.  
- Check cuffs, pockets, and bags for seeds or plant fragments.  
- Do not carry fresh fruit, flowers, herbs, or unprocessed plant material.

## 26. A Sensible First Visit Plan

If you are an ordinary human traveler, your first visit should look like this:

### Before entry

1. Check gear.  
2. Seal dry clothes and electronics.  
3. Confirm return device function.  
4. Hydrate.  
5. Put on respirator or have it ready.  
6. Tie back hair and secure straps.  
7. Tell your contact your planned duration.

### On arrival

1. Stop and do not move immediately.  
2. Assess footing.  
3. Check air: breathe through mask if uncertain.  
4. Listen to machinery and animal rhythms.  
5. Identify up, down, water flow, and nearest shelter.  
6. Mark your arrival point mentally.  
7. Drink a small amount of water.  
8. Adjust clothing before walking.

### First hour

- Stay near arrival point.  
- Walk short loops.  
- Test surfaces.  
- Observe animal routes without blocking them.  
- Note landmarks.  
- Avoid descending into basins.  
- Avoid touching machines.

### First three hours

- Move through middle tiers only if confident.  
- Keep one clear route back.  
- Take breaks every 30–45 minutes.  
- Change socks if damp.  
- Eat small amounts.  
- Do not push into deep industrial zones.

### Before leaving

- Check boots for organisms.  
- Pack out all waste.  
- Remove any temporary markers.  
- Confirm no food scraps remain.  
- Return to your arrival point early.  
- Do not wait until fatigue or mist thickens.

## 27. Emergency Scenarios and Responses

### You slip and fall

1. Stop moving.  
2. Check for head injury, bleeding, or fracture.  
3. If able, move off the wet path.  
4. Clean and cover wounds.  
5. Do not continue if dizzy or unstable.  
6. Exit if pain worsens.

### You get lost

1. Stop immediately.  
2. Do not wander.  
3. Find shelter from drips and wind.  
4. Drink water.  
5. Check your last known landmark.  
6. Use map, compass, or memory of water flow.  
7. If uncertain, stay put and signal rather than descending blindly.

### Your gear gets soaked

1. Separate wet items from dry items.  
2. Protect return device first.  
3. Move to shelter.  
4. Change socks and base layer if possible.  
5. Use dry bag as emergency barrier.  
6. Do not continue long distances if you lose your dry sleep kit.

### You feel breathless or dizzy

1. Put on respiratory protection if not already wearing it.  
2. Move upward or outward from enclosed basins.  
3. Rest.  
4. Hydrate.  
5. If symptoms persist, exit.  
6. Do not assume it is only altitude if there is chemical smell or eye irritation.

### You encounter dense animal movement

1. Stop.  
2. Lower your profile slightly.  
3. Do not run.  
4. Let the movement pass.  
5. Back away if the flow is directed at you.  
6. Cover face if insects are involved.

### You touch a machine and it moves

1. Withdraw slowly.  
2. Do not pull against suction or rotation.  
3. Shut nothing unless you understand it.  
4. Move away from linked mechanisms.  
5. Check for injuries.  
6. Leave the area.

### You suspect electric shock risk

1. Do not touch the source.  
2. Move away without touching metal.  
3. If a device is sparking, do not retrieve it.  
4. Leave the area.  
5. Check for burns if shocked.

### You are bitten

1. Get to safety.  
2. Wash wound.  
3. Disinfect.  
4. Cover.  
5. Monitor.  
6. Exit for medical care.

## 28. The Ethical Stance of the Visitor

This city is not waiting to be saved. It is not broken. It is not a machine that needs you to restart it. It is not a garden that needs replanting. It is not a zoo that needs management. It has found its form.

Your presence should be almost invisible.

Do not try to:

- Clear fungal growth  
- Repair machines you do not understand  
- Alter water channels  
- Rescue animals unless clearly and immediately endangered by you  
- Introduce greenery  
- Make the place “cleaner”  
- Improve nests  
- Feed populations  
- Change routes  
- Leave gifts or offerings

The best offering is restraint.

## 29. What the Experience Will Feel Like

If you prepare well, the visit will feel strange and gradually intelligible.

At first you will notice the wetness. Then the absence of plants. Then the scale. Then the machinery. Then the animals. Then the fact that all of these are not separate things but one slow system.

Your boots will find polished stone. Your clothes will dampen at cuffs and collar. The air will taste warm, mineral, faintly metallic, perhaps faintly sulfurous. Sound will surround you: water trickling, pumps throbbing, wings passing, calls echoing through ducts. You may feel watched, but not hunted. You may feel crowded, but not attacked. You may feel small, but not insignificant.

If you stand still, something will move near you. A claw will tap inside a pipe. Wings will brush a grate. A body will slide through a dark gap. A call will rise from a vent. The city will not stop for you. It will simply include you briefly, as one more warm body passing through its volume.

If you are impatient, you will hate it. If you are careful, you may come to love it.

## 30. Final Preparation Checklist

Before entering, ask yourself:

- Can I stay dry enough?  
- Can I breathe safely?  
- Can I walk slowly on wet stone for hours?  
- Do I have enough clean water?  
- Do I have all food sealed?  
- Can I pack out all waste?  
- Is my return device protected?  
- Do I know when to turn back?  
- Am I ready to leave nothing behind?  
- Am I ready to treat the city as a living system, not a backdrop?

If the answer is yes, then you are as prepared as an ordinary human can reasonably be.

Enter slowly. Keep your socks dry. Respect the water. Keep your hands away from machines and nests. Do not drink the pools. Do not feed the inhabitants. Do not introduce green things. Let the city be wet, loud, warm, mineral, and complete without you.

Your visit should feel like being briefly admitted into a vast, patient metabolism — and then withdrawing before it remembers you were there.
