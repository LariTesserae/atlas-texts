## Preparation for Visiting the Warm Hollow

This is not a place to “explore” in the ordinary sense. It is more like entering the inside of a slow, breathing organism: hot, damp, dim, crowded with quiet life, and balanced by habits older than tools. An ordinary human visitor should prepare less like a spelunker and more like a surgeon entering a living terrarium — with humility, containment, patience, and an obsession about not disturbing what is already there.

The first rule of preparation is this: **make yourself small, slow, sealed, quiet, and temporary.**

---

# 1. Decide Whether Entry Is Necessary

Before preparing gear, prepare judgment.

This hollow is small, enclosed, biologically dense, and extremely sensitive. A human body inside it is not a neutral presence. You will bring heat, carbon dioxide, moisture, scent, skin flakes, microbes, clothing fibers, and movement into a space where every drop of water and every breath of air matters. In such a place, a visitor can be an event.

If possible, do not enter at all. Observe from the threshold. Use a remote probe if your multiverse travel allows one. Leave a passive sensor outside and study the chamber’s rhythms before committing a living human body to it.

If entry is necessary, make it minimal:

- One traveler is better than two.
- Two are better than a team.
- A short visit is better than a long one.
- A visit during a calm phase is better than one during a heat pulse.
- A visitor who can remain still is better than one who must constantly move.

If the traveler has any of the following, they should probably not enter:

- Claustrophobia severe enough to cause panic.
- Respiratory illness, asthma easily triggered by humidity or spores, or poor mask tolerance.
- Heat intolerance, heart strain, blood pressure issues, or susceptibility to heat exhaustion.
- Immune weakness, open wounds, fungal infections, or recent illness.
- A psychological need to control, collect, clean, or “fix” natural spaces.

This hollow will not reward force. It will only tolerate gentleness.

---

# 2. Understand What the Place Will Do to an Ordinary Human Body

The environment is beautiful, but physiologically demanding.

### Heat and humidity

The hollow is hot in a wet, mineral way. That is dangerous for an ordinary human because sweat does not cool efficiently in saturated air. The body may overheat even without exertion.

Assume the chamber has:

- High temperature.
- Near-saturated humidity.
- Poor evaporative cooling.
- Localized hot spots near seams and vents.
- Sudden warm pulses from below.

Even if the temperature is not extreme by desert standards, the wet-bulb effect can make it oppressive. A visitor should expect:

- Heavy sweating that does not evaporate.
- Rapid pulse.
- Fogging of eyewear.
- Skin feeling constantly wet.
- Dizziness if hydration or cooling fails.
- Mental slowing if heat stress begins.

### Air quality

The air may be breathable, but it may also contain:

- Spores.
- Bacterial aerosols.
- Mineral vapors.
- Carbon dioxide pooling in low basins.
- Sulfurous or metallic gases from heated seams.
- Fine mist and organic particulates.

Do not assume safety because the place supports life. It supports life adapted to that place. An ordinary human lung is a foreign, vulnerable thing there.

### Darkness and dim light

The chamber is lit by faint natural light, bioluminescence, insect glow, and heat-loving mosses. Human eyes will need time to adapt. Bright light would be invasive and possibly harmful to the inhabitants.

Prepare for:

- Low contrast.
- Glowing surfaces.
- Shadows that move.
- Difficulty judging depth.
- The temptation to use too much artificial light.

### Claustrophobic intimacy

The hollow is small enough to cross in a few dozen paces, but it is not empty. It is full of life, textures, moisture, warmth, and listening stillness. A visitor may feel:

- Watched.
- Wrapped.
- Pressed upon.
- Intrusive.
- Strangely emotional.

The place may feel womblike, tomblike, or shrine-like depending on the visitor’s mind.
