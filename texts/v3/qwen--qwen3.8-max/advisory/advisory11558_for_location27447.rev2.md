## Preparation Brief for Visiting the Bright Green Underland

To visit this place, do not prepare as if you are entering a jungle, a cave, a wetland, or a ruin. Prepare as if you are being allowed, briefly, inside a vast warm organ that is already complete in its incompleteness. The basin is not described as hostile, but it is not built for human convenience. It is hot, humid, still, dense, quiet, pathless, and almost entirely alive. Its strangeness is not that it will attack you, but that it may absorb your presence without making any visible response. Nothing there seems to erode, collapse, or advance into a new state. That means your mistakes may not “weather away” in the ordinary sense. You must assume that every footprint, breath, hair, crumb, fiber, sweat-drop, and chemical trace is an intrusion into a closed equilibrium.

An ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare in three ways: practically, biologically, and ethically. You need to keep your body safe in saturated heat, keep your mind oriented in a pathless green interior, and keep the basin unchanged by your passage.

---

# 1. Understand What the Place Will Ask of You

The basin’s main challenges are subtle.

There are no obvious monsters, no storms, no cliffs, no rivers to drown in, no night predators. But there are conditions that can wear down an unprepared person:

- **Hot, still, saturated air.**  
  This is the kind of heat where sweat does not evaporate well. Your body may feel sticky, heavy, and overly aware of skin. Heat exhaustion can arrive quietly.

- **No reliable water source.**  
  Water appears in pulses: bead-lines, narrow rains, temporary pools. You cannot count on it. You must carry all drinking water you may need.

- **No paths.**  
  The landscape is dense with living architecture. Movement requires parting, sliding, bending, and reading pressure rather than following trails.

- **Low ceiling and enclosed spaces.**  
  The luminous canopy is relatively low. Root curtains, frond walls, and stem clusters can create claustrophobic compression.

- **Poor sound carry.**  
  Voices do not travel well. Calls may be smothered by tissue. Group communication must be deliberate.

- **Soft shadows and diffuse light.**  
  The light is bright but directionless. Depth perception may flatten. Glossy surfaces, mineral crusts, and cupped hollows may be harder to read.

- **No ordinary day-night cycle.**  
  The basin dims only slightly into jade twilight. Time can become psychologically thin. You must impose your own schedule.

- **Almost no animal life.**  
  This can feel beautiful at first, then lonely or eerie. The absence of moving creatures makes the plant density feel total.

- **A powerful sense of stillness.**  
  The place does not seem to accumulate history. This may soothe some travelers and disturb others. Prepare mentally not to become passive, drowsy, or strangely indifferent to your own safety.

The basin does not need you to conquer it. It needs you to be careful, clean, calm, and brief.

---

# 2. Decide Whether You Should Enter

Not every traveler should enter every place.

Be especially cautious if you have:

- Poor heat tolerance.
- Heart or lung conditions.
- Severe claustrophobia.
- Panic disorders or acute dissociation.
- Open wounds, rashes, fungal infections, or active illness.
- Reduced mobility that would make slow, careful movement difficult.
- Allergies to pollen, mold, sap, or unknown plant volatiles.
- A tendency to overexert in humid conditions.

This does not mean the basin is poisonous. It means your body and the basin’s atmosphere are not automatically compatible. Heat and humidity can magnify small weaknesses.

If you are sick, do not go. If you are shedding virus, bacteria, skin infection, or respiratory illness, you are a contamination risk to a place with very little fauna and a closed biological logic. If you have a fresh cut, cover it completely with waterproof dressing. If you cannot cover it reliably, postpone.

If this is your first visit, make it short. A first entry should be measured in hours, not days. Enter, observe, learn the feel of the air, move carefully, and leave while you still have margin.

---

# 3. Prepare Your Body Before Entry

The basin is hot and humid. Your greatest physical risks are dehydration, heat exhaustion, chafing, skin maceration, and poor judgment.

Before entering:

- **Hydrate well in advance.**  
  Do not arrive already thirsty. Drink water steadily in the hours before entry.

- **Take electrolytes seriously.**  
  In saturated heat, you may sweat heavily but not cool efficiently. Plain water alone can dilute your salts. Bring electrolyte tablets or oral rehydration salts.

- **Avoid alcohol.**  
  Alcohol worsens dehydration and impairs heat regulation.

- **Avoid heavy meals immediately before entry.**  
  Eat enough, but choose light, digestible food. Digestion adds heat load.

- **Rest first.**  
  Fatigue makes heat harder to tolerate.

- **Use the toilet before entry.**  
  Waste management inside the basin is critical. You do not want to be caught needing a latrine where no latrine should exist.

- **Cool your body beforehand if possible.**  
  A cool shower, cooling towel, or air-conditioned rest period can give you a buffer.

- **Do not enter wearing perfumes, scented soaps, deodorant sprays, essential oils, incense smoke, or strong lotions.**  
  The air is still and biological. Strong scents are intrusive and may affect rare fauna or plant tissues.

If you are medically vulnerable, carry personal medication and a clear written emergency plan. Heat can accelerate certain medical problems, and the basin’s silence can make symptoms harder to notice until they are advanced.

---

# 4. Treat the Basin as a Biosecurity Environment

The most important rule is this:

**Do not bring the outside in. Do not take the basin out.**

This place is made almost entirely of living tissue and seems to maintain a perfect internal balance. Ordinary soil microbes, human pathogens, seeds, spores, food particles, skin flakes, hair, fibers, dust, and chemical residues could be meaningful disturbances even if no immediate change is visible.

Before entry, assume you are preparing for a clean-room visit to a living body.

### Clean yourself

- Shower beforehand using unscented products if possible.
- Do not apply scented lotion, sunscreen aerosols, insect repellent, perfume, cologne, hair products, or strong medicinal ointments unless medically necessary.
- Tie back long hair.
- Keep nails short to avoid snagging and tearing plant tissue.
- Avoid shedding clothing.

### Clean your gear

Every item should be inspected and cleaned before entry.

Do not bring gear that has:

- Soil on it.
- Mud in tread.
- Plant fragments.
- Seeds.
- Animal hair.
- Food residue.
- Rust.
- Loose fibers.
- Flaking paint.
- Crumbling foam.
- Old adhesive residue.

Your boots should be scrubbed and dry. Your pack should be free of crumbs and lint. Your pockets should be emptied of paper scraps, tissues, wrappers, and forgotten organic matter.

### Avoid natural materials

Do not bring items made of materials that may carry biological contaminants or decay:

- Wooden handles.
- Leather.
- Wool that sheds.
- Cotton that sheds and holds moisture.
- Paper maps.
- Bamboo tools.
- Cork that may crumble.
- Natural cordage.
- Seed-based snacks.
- Fresh fruit.
- Nuts in shells.
- Flowers.
- Herbal charms.
- Soil-based souvenirs.

Use inert, sealed, synthetic, or sterilized materials instead.

### Quarantine if required

If your multiverse travel system has decontamination protocols, use them. If local authorities or guides require quarantine before or after entry, obey them. The basin’s equilibrium is more important than your convenience.

# 5. Clothing: Wear Smooth, Light, Breathable, and Fully Covering

The basin is warm enough that you may be tempted to wear very little. That would be a mistake.

You need to protect your skin from:

- Heat.
- Humidity.
- Leaf edges.
- Root curtains.
- Mineral crusts.
- Silica-like glazes.
- Condensation drips.
- Your own sweat and chafing.
- Contact with unknown plant surfaces.
- Rare slow-moving fauna you do not wish to crush or disturb.

But you also need to avoid overheating.

The ideal clothing is light in weight but complete in coverage.

### Base layer

Wear a very light, moisture-managing, long-sleeved layer. In near-saturation, no fabric will keep you truly dry, but you want something that moves sweat away from the skin and dries reasonably fast.

Avoid cotton. Cotton becomes heavy, wet, and slow to dry. It also sheds fibers.

Choose smooth synthetic or technical fabric if available. If you have access to advanced travel gear, use a breathable, low-shed, quick-dry material.

### Outer layer

A thin, smooth, snag-resistant outer layer is useful. It should not be a heavy waterproof raincoat; that will trap heat. Instead, choose a breathable splash layer with a hood. It can shed condensation drips and protect you when passing through root curtains.

It should be close-fitting enough not to catch on stems, but loose enough to allow air movement.

Avoid:

- Dangling drawstrings.
- Loose straps.
- Fringe.
- Scarves.
- Untucked flaps.
- Large hoods that block vision unless needed.
- Abrasive outer surfaces.

### Trousers

Wear long trousers. They should be light, smooth, and quick-drying. Avoid heavy cargo pants with many external pockets that can snag. Zippered or sealed pockets are better than open ones.

Do not wear shorts. The floor and low vegetation are too close, and the surfaces are too varied.

### Footwear

This is important.

The floor is alive, glossy, sometimes damp, sometimes sharp with mineral crusts, sometimes soft like cork. You need footwear that protects without biting into the surface.

Ideal footwear:

- Closed-toe.
- Soft but protective sole.
- Non-marking.
- Good grip on wet waxy surfaces.
- No spikes, cleats, or sharp lugs.
- Quick-drying.
- Breathable enough not to become a sweat trap.
- Secure at the heel.
- No metal parts that could corrode or shed.

Do not wear:

- Sandals.
- Flip-flops.
- Bare feet.
- Heavy mountaineering boots with aggressive lugs.
- Shoes with metal crampons.
- Shoes with muddy treads.
- Shoes that squeak loudly.
- Shoes that shed rubber or foam.

Bring spare socks in a sealed dry bag. Wet feet in humid heat can blister and macerate quickly. If your feet become soaked from a temporary pool or local rain, changing socks can prevent injury.

### Gloves

Thin gloves are wise. They protect your hands if you need to part vegetation or brace yourself, and they reduce direct transfer of oils and salts to living surfaces.

Choose thin, breathable, abrasion-resistant gloves. They do not need to be heavy. If you are prone to overheating, bring gloves that can be tucked away easily.

Avoid powdered gloves. Avoid gloves that shed. Avoid thick leather unless absolutely necessary, and even then consider contamination risk.

### Headwear

A soft, broad-brimmed hat or hood with a small brim can help with condensation drips and low vegetation. It should not be rigid or wide enough to snag on root curtains.

A hard helmet is probably unnecessary unless the ceiling is unusually low in your entry area, but a lightly padded cap could be useful if you are tall or clumsy.

### Eye protection

The light is bright but diffuse. You probably do not need dark sunglasses. In fact, dark lenses may make the green twilight harder to read.

Better choices:

- Clear wraparound glasses.
- Anti-fog protection glasses.
- Light neutral lenses if the brightness feels intense.
- Goggles if you are wearing a mask and need eye protection from drips or spores.

Leaf tips, bead-lines, and falling condensation can be irritating. Eye protection is especially useful when moving through dense frond walls.

### Mask or breathing protection

The atmosphere is described as unusual but not hostile. Still, there are reasons to protect your breathing:

- The air is saturated with plant emissions.
- Pollen, spores, mineral dust, or volatile sap may be present.
- You may need to protect the basin from your breath.
- Small enclosed grown rooms may have poor airflow.
- If you have allergies or asthma, you may react unexpectedly.

A light respirator or filtered mask can be useful. However, masks in hot humid air can become uncomfortable quickly. If you use one, test it beforehand.

A better option, if available, is a filtered hood or powered air-purifying system with low heat burden. If not, a clean, breathable mask with spare filters is acceptable.

Do not wear a mask so restrictive that it causes panic or heat stress. If you feel suffocated, move to a more open area, slow your breathing, and assess whether you need to leave.

# 6. Heat Management: Respect the Saturated Air

The basin’s heat is not dramatic. It is close, even, and persistent. It does not burn; it envelops.

Because the air is still and humid, evaporative cooling is poor. Your sweat may remain on your skin. Your body may feel warm even when you are not exerting yourself much.

### Carry more water than you think you need

There are no rivers. No permanent lakes. No reliable springs.

You must carry your drinking water.

For a short visit of a few hours, bring more water than you expect to drink. For hot, humid environments, many people need at least one liter for a moderate outing, and often more depending on exertion, body size, and heat tolerance. If you are carrying gear or moving through dense vegetation, your needs increase.

Do not rely on the basin’s water events. They are local, temporary, and biologically intimate.

### Bring electrolytes

Sweat removes salts. If you drink only water for a long period, you may develop hyponatremia-like symptoms: headache, nausea, confusion, weakness.

Bring:

- Electrolyte tablets.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- Salty snacks if they do not crumble.

Use them according to your body’s needs, not just thirst.

### Avoid overexertion

Move slowly. This is not a place for rushing.

Do not:

- Run.
- Jump.
- Climb aggressively.
- Push through dense growth by force.
- Carry unnecessarily heavy loads.
- Take long detours without rest.

Every burst of effort increases heat load.

### Use cooling aids if available

If you have access to ordinary travel technology, consider:

- A small battery-powered fan with guarded blades.
- A cooling neck ring.
- Phase-change cooling vest.
- Pre-cooled gel packs sealed against leaks.
- A damp cloth stored in a sealed bag for brief cooling at rest stops.

Be cautious with evaporative cooling methods. In saturated air, they may not work well.

Do not bring chemical cooling packs that can rupture and spill unknown fluids into the basin.

### Know the signs of heat illness

Stop and reassess if you notice:

- Dizziness.
- Nausea.
- Headache.
- Confusion.
- Irritability.
- Heavy sweating that suddenly stops.
- Cold, clammy skin.
- Rapid pulse.
- Muscle cramps.
- Unusual clumsiness.
- A sense that the greenness is closing in.

Do not push through heat symptoms. The basin has no emergency services inside it. You must be able to leave under your own power.

---

# 7. Breathing, Air, and Enclosed Spaces

The basin feels like being inside an organ. That means some spaces may behave like chambers, pockets, or lungs. The air is not described as toxic, but it may be uneven.

In open areas, breathing should probably feel warm, vegetal, faintly sweet, and mineral. In small grown rooms, seed-cases, or half-closed pods, air may be stale, humid, or enriched with plant gases.

### Do not linger in small closed structures

The grown rooms are beautiful and strange, but many are incomplete, roofless, or oddly sealed. Some have no proper entrance. Some are little more than curved walls. Some may be air traps.

Do not crawl into:

- Closed seed-capsules.
- Hollow pods with small openings.
- Partial chambers with no ventilation.
- Dense stem rings.
- Low ceiling pockets where your breath may accumulate.

If you enter a grown room, choose one with a clear entrance and exit. Do not stay long. Keep your mask or breathing protection available.

### Monitor air if possible

If you have a small environmental monitor, bring it. Useful readings might include:

- Oxygen level.
- Carbon dioxide level.
- Temperature.
- Humidity.
- Volatile organic compounds, if available.

Do not assume that because the basin is alive, every pocket is breathable. Living systems can produce oxygen, but they can also produce CO₂, methane, alcohols, terpene vapors, or other compounds depending on local biology.

If you feel headache, pressure, drowsiness, shortness of breath, or confusion, leave the area immediately.

### Avoid exhaling directly into small spaces

If you are unmasked, do not lean into hollow pods and breathe heavily. Your breath adds heat, moisture, CO₂, and microbes. If you are masked, you reduce both contamination and personal exposure.

---

# 8. Food: Bring Clean, Sealed, Crumb-Free Rations

Do not plan to forage. Do not eat the plants. Do not drink from pools. Do not taste condensation because the air smells sweet. Do not chew leaves. Do not test fungi, glazes, mineral crusts, or seed structures.

Food should be:

- Sealed.
- Non-crumbly.
- Odor-controlled.
- Free of seeds.
- Free of fresh fruit.
- Free of shells.
- Easy to eat quickly.
- Packed in waste-free or fully recoverable wrappers.

Good options:

- Compressed ration bars.
- Gel packets.
- Electrolyte chews that do not melt.
- Sealed nut butters if permitted and non-shedding.
- Pre-packaged soft meals in pouches if you need real food.

Avoid:

- Fresh fruit.
- Nuts in shells.
- Bread that crumbs.
- Cookies.
- Trail mix with loose particles.
- Candy that melts.
- Meat products that spoil in heat.
- Anything sticky that can smear on your hands or gear.

If you drop food, retrieve every fragment. If you cannot retrieve it, treat it as contamination and note the location.

Do not bury food. Do not leave food “for animals.” There are almost no animals here, and the basin’s balance is not based on scavenging.

---

# 9. Waste: Carry Everything Out

This is one of the most important preparations.

The basin has no soil, no ordinary decomposition, no rivers to carry waste away, and almost no fauna to process organic matter. Human waste must not enter the environment.

### Urination

Do not urinate on the plant floor. Do not urinate into pools, cups, hollows, stems, or seed-cases.

For short visits, control intake and use the toilet before entry. For longer visits, carry a portable urination system:

- A sealable bottle or bag.
- A funnel device if helpful.
- Absorbent waste bags.
- Odor-sealing bags.
- A separate dry bag for storage afterward.

### Solid waste

For anything beyond a very short visit, you need a portable waste system.

Bring:

- Sealable waste bags.
- Absorbent gel bags or charcoal-lined bags.
- Toilet paper or wipes in sealed packaging.
- Hand sanitizer that does not drip or aerosolize.
- A rigid or semi-rigid container if bags may puncture.

Do not bury waste. There is no soil. Do not hide waste under leaves. Do not place it in grown rooms. Do not leave it in hollows.

### Sweat, saliva, mucus, and skin

You cannot fully prevent sweating, but you can minimize contamination.

- Wear a headband or hood to keep sweat from dripping.
- Wipe sweat with a cloth only if you will carry the cloth out.
- Do not spit.
- Do not blow your nose onto surfaces.
- If you must clear your nose, use a tissue and seal it.
- If you sneeze or cough, do it into your mask, elbow, or sealed tissue.
- Do not shed skin flakes deliberately.
- Do not scratch open skin.

This may sound extreme, but the basin’s stillness makes small intrusions significant.

---

# 10. Water: Carry It, Protect It, Treat Local Water Only as Emergency

The basin’s water is not landscape water. It is event-water. It appears as condensation, bead-lines, local rains, and temporary pools. It may be clean in some sense, but it is also part of the basin’s internal circulation. It is not safe by human standards unless tested and treated.

### Carry drinking water in safe containers

Use:

- BPA-free bottles.
- Hydration bladders with sealed valves.
- Metal or inert containers if they do not shed coatings.
- Collapsible water bags that do not leak.

Protect containers from heat and puncture. If a hydration bladder leaks, you lose both water and dry gear.

### Do not drink from the basin unless emergency

Temporary pools are body-warm and may contain translucent worms, plant sugars, mineral sap, microbial films, or other biological material. They are not drinking fountains.

If you are forced by emergency to use local water:

- Prefer freshly falling local rain over standing pools.
- Catch it with a clean surface if possible.
- Avoid water that contains visible organisms.
- Avoid water with film, foam, strong sweetness, or mineral crust residue.
- Filter if possible.
- Use UV purification if available.
- Use chemical purification only if necessary, and keep all chemical waste sealed.

Do not wash yourself in the pools. Do not rinse containers in them. Do not add purification chemicals back into the basin.

### Protect water from heat

Warm water is less refreshing and may accelerate microbial growth in containers. Keep water insulated if possible. Carry it inside your pack, shielded from direct contact with hot surfaces.

# 11. Navigation: Prepare for a Place Without Paths

The basin has regional scale, but it is dense, layered, and pathless. It has pressures rather than roads. You cannot simply follow a trail. You must move with attention.

### Do not rely on one method

Use several navigation aids:

- Inertial tracker.
- Compass, but assume it may be unreliable near mineral veins.
- Dead reckoning.
- Sketch map.
- Visual landmarks.
- Personal beacon.
- Group line-of-sight discipline.
- Timed checkpoints.

The ceiling’s luminous veins, pillar groves, terraced shelves, and central rises may help orientation, but they can also look similar. Do not trust your memory alone.

### Use landmarks carefully

Possible landmarks include:

- A central rise.
- A distinctive pillar grove.
- A terrace pattern.
- A canopy area where the upper membrane glows brighter.
- A cluster of unusually thick stems.
- A half-grown room with a unique shape.

Do not alter landmarks. Do not break stems to create sightlines. Do not tie markers to plants.

### Avoid marking the environment

Do not leave:

- Ribbons.
- Paint.
- Chalk.
- Flags.
- Cairns.
- Stacked leaf forms.
- Cut branches.
- Scratched symbols.
- Adhesive labels.
- String lines.

If absolutely necessary for emergency navigation, use only temporary, inert, freestanding markers that you remove immediately afterward. Better yet, do not use them at all.

### Move slowly and read pressure

The vegetation will tell you where movement is possible.

When entering dense areas:

- Stop before forcing.
- Part leaves gently.
- Use forearms rather than shoulders when possible.
- Do not tear.
- Do not bend stems beyond slight flex.
- If root curtains block you, part them slowly.
- If frond walls feel too dense, look for a lower or higher gap.
- If a space presses against you, retreat.

The basin does not have paths, but it does have tolerances. Respect them.

### Watch the ceiling and floor

The ceiling is relatively low. Watch for:

- Hanging root curtains.
- Leaf ribs.
- Condensation beads.
- Descending stems.
- Narrow arches.
- Low mineral veins.

The floor is not uniform. Watch for:

- Shallow moisture cups.
- Glossy cuticle patches.
- Raised leaf ribs.
- Sharp mineral crusts.
- Swollen growth points.
- Loose-looking pith.
- Small leaf mats that may be delicate.
- Hollow seed-capsules.

Step with care. Distribute weight. Do not jump. Do not drag feet.

### Group travel

Do not enter alone if you can avoid it. A small group is safer than a large group.

Recommended group size: two to four people. Larger groups create more heat, noise, trampling, and contamination.

In group travel:

- Keep visual contact.
- Use short verbal check-ins.
- Do not shout.
- Establish hand signals.
- Assign a point person and a rear person.
- Check frequently for heat stress.
- Avoid spreading out so widely that you trample multiple corridors.
- Do not let one person push ahead.
- Do not let anyone wander off to investigate a structure without telling the group.

Sound is absorbed. Do not rely on voices over distance.

# 12. Communication: Expect Quiet and Poor Carry

The basin is lined with silence. Sounds are small and short-lived. This is beautiful, but it creates practical problems.

### Do not rely on echo or long-distance calls

There are no echoes. Voices may die quickly. A whistle may not carry far. If you become separated, shouting may not help.

Use:

- Visual signals.
- Low-power LED signals.
- Vibration alerts on wrist devices.
- Short-range communication devices.
- Prearranged meeting points.
- Timed check-ins.

### Radios may be unreliable

Dense wet plant tissue can attenuate radio signals. Mineral veins may interfere. Assume communication may be patchy.

If you use devices:

- Keep them sealed against humidity.
- Use waterproof cases.
- Keep batteries protected from heat.
- Avoid external antennas that snag.
- Do not attach devices to plants.
- Do not leave repeaters or nodes behind unless authorized and removable.

### Keep noise low

The basin is quiet. Loud mechanical noise feels intrusive and may disturb rare fauna.

Choose quiet gear:

- Silent zippers or covered closures.
- Non-rustling fabric if possible.
- Quiet fans.
- No musical instruments.
- No speakers.
- No loud alarms unless emergency.
- No drones with buzzing rotors.

If you must use audio, use one ear only and keep volume very low. Keep the other ear open to the basin’s small sounds: condensation ticks, stem creaks, water pockets opening and closing.

# 13. Tools and Equipment: Bring Less, Not More

This is a place where built things exist without tools. That is a clue. You should minimize tools.

Do not bring:

- Machetes.
- Axes.
- Saws.
- Knives for cutting vegetation.
- Hatchets.
- Pruners.
- Shovels.
- Picks.
- Hammers.
- Stakes.
- Nails.
- Screws.
- Adhesive tape.
- Glue.
- Wire.
- Rope for tying to plants.
- Clamps.
- Suction cups.
- Anchors.
- Pitons.
- Chains.
- Heavy tripods with spikes.

If you need a tool for safety or medical reasons, it should be:

- Small.
- Blunt if possible.
- Sheathed.
- Clean.
- Used only on your own gear.
- Never used on the basin.

A small pair of medical scissors or tweezers may be reasonable in a first aid kit. Do not use them to trim plants.

### Avoid anything that sheds

Every item should be evaluated for shedding:

- Does it shed fibers?
- Does it crumble?
- Does it rust?
- Does it flake?
- Does it leak powder?
- Does it have loose threads?
- Does it have paper labels?
- Does it have adhesive backing?
- Does it have brittle plastic?

Choose stable, sealed, inert materials.

# 14. Shelter and Rest: Prefer Day Visits

The simplest preparation is to avoid overnight exposure unless necessary. A day visit reduces heat stress, waste problems, contamination risk, and psychological strain.

If you must rest:

- Choose an open area if possible.
- Sit on a clean pad, not directly on living tissue.
- Avoid crushing rosettes or small leaf mats.
- Avoid sitting in moisture cups.
- Avoid leaning against swollen growth points.
- Avoid resting under areas where condensation beads are gathering.
- Keep your pack closed.
- Check your body for overheating.
- Drink before you feel desperate.

### If you must sleep in the basin

Overnight stays should be considered advanced, not ordinary. If required, prepare carefully.

Bring:

- A self-supporting shelter.
- No stakes.
- No guy lines tied to plants.
- No adhesive anchors.
- A broad footprint that distributes weight.
- A sleeping pad with a smooth, clean underside.
- A sleep mask, because the basin only dims slightly.
- Ear protection if needed, but keep one ear aware.
- A quiet fan if heat is dangerous.
- Extra water.
- Waste system.
- Lighting with low glare.

Do not use a tent that requires cutting, flattening, or clearing vegetation. Do not clear a campsite. Do not move leaves or stems. Do not create a “floor.”

A better shelter is a freestanding mesh or canopy pod that sits lightly on broad pads. It should not trap heat excessively. If it becomes a humid bubble, it may worsen heat stress.

Do not sleep inside closed seed-cases or incomplete grown rooms unless emergency forces you to. Many may have poor airflow. If you must use one:

- Choose a room with a clear exit.
- Do not block the entrance.
- Monitor air.
- Keep your sleep session short.
- Do not cook inside.
- Do not remove any tissue.
- Leave immediately if the air feels heavy.

### Sleeping without darkness

The basin never becomes truly dark. It shifts into deep jade twilight. That can make sleep difficult.

Use:

- A blackout eye mask.
- A consistent sleep timer.
- A watch alarm.
- A pre-planned wake interval.

Do not let the lack of night persuade you to sleep longer than planned.

# 15. Hygiene: Stay Clean Without Washing the Basin

You will sweat. You will feel sticky. You may want to rinse off. Do not use the basin’s pools or moisture events as a shower.

### Body hygiene

Use:

- Unscented wipes in sealed packaging.
- A small damp cloth carried in a sealed bag.
- A dry cloth for chafing areas.
- Anti-chafe balm in a sealed container if needed.
- Foot powder only if it can be applied without shedding into the environment; otherwise use cream or balm.

Do not use soap in the basin. Do not use aerosol products. Do not use shampoo. Do not wash clothes in pools. Do not wring sweaty clothing over the floor.

If your skin becomes wet from local rain, dry it gently with a cloth you carry out. Prolonged wetness can cause maceration, especially between toes and under straps.

### Hand hygiene

Use sanitizer carefully. Do not let it drip. Alcohol-based products are flammable and biologically intrusive. Use small amounts and rub fully into skin.

If you use wipes, seal them afterward.

### Toilet hygiene

Plan it as if you are in a fragile clean room. Everything leaves with you.

Bring:

- Sealable waste bags.
- Hand sanitizer.
- Wipes.
- A small rigid container if bags may leak.
- A separate bag for used hygiene items.

Do not leave used wipes, tissues, or sanitary products anywhere in the basin.

# 16. Interacting with the Plant Architecture

The basin is made of plants, but it is not a garden. It is structural, living, and delicately balanced.

### Do not cut, tear, peel, or break

Do not:

- Cut leaves.
- Peel bark-like tissue.
- Snap stems.
- Pull root curtains aside forcefully.
- Dig into floor pith.
- Break mineral crusts.
- Chip glazes.
- Open swollen growth points.
- Detach seed-capsules.
- Remove leaf ribs.
- Collect “souvenirs.”

The place may compensate for damage, but that does not mean damage is harmless. Compensation is not forgiveness.

### Do not climb unless necessary

The thick stems are structural, but they are also living and faintly elastic. Climbing may compress, abrade, or distort them. It may also put you near low ceilings and fragile tissues.

If you must brace yourself:

- Use broad palms.
- Avoid growth points.
- Avoid new swollen tips.
- Avoid mineral crust edges.
- Do not grip small leaf forms.
- Do not put full weight on thin leaf ribs.

### Do not complete incomplete structures

This is a subtle but important ethical point.

The basin contains half-arches, incomplete walls, roofless huts, and pods without entrances. These are not puzzles. They are not invitations. They are not construction sites waiting for you.

Do not:

- Add material.
- Bend stems to close gaps.
- Place leaves into missing wall segments.
- Prop up fallen tissue.
- “Fix” a broken arch.
- Arrange plant fiber into a platform.
- Make a seat.
- Make a sign.
- Make an offering.

The basin is locked in permanent beginning. Your role is not to finish it.

### Do not enter every grown room

Some grown rooms are beautiful. Some feel deliberately shelter-like. Some may seem made for rest. But they are grown by the basin’s logic, not for human habitation.

Enter only if necessary, and then briefly. Avoid:

- Rooms with no clear exit.
- Rooms with low ceilings and no airflow.
- Rooms that require squeezing.
- Rooms with water collecting inside.
- Rooms with many new growth points.
- Rooms where your shoulders or pack would touch both sides.

If a room feels like it is holding your breath, leave.

# 17. Fauna: Rare, Slow, and Not Yours to Touch

The basin is almost entirely plant. Fauna is rare. You may see very little that moves with animal intention.

If you see:

- A pale seed-beetle: step around it. Do not poke it. Do not pick it up.
- A translucent worm in a pool: do not touch it. Do not disturb the water.
- A soft white pollinator: remain still. Do not chase it. Do not photograph with flash. Do not swat at it if it comes near.

Do not feed anything. Do not imitate animal sounds. Do not use bait. Do not set traps. Do not collect specimens.

The absence of animals should make you more careful, not less. There are no predators to worry about, but there are also no buffers. You are one of the few mobile heterotrophs in a plant-made world.

# 18. Pools, Moisture Cups, and Water Events

Water here is not scenery. It is circulation.

### Temporary pools

Do not step into temporary pools unless absolutely unavoidable. They are body-warm, shallow, and biologically active.

Risks:

- Slipping.
- Contaminating the water.
- Exposure to unknown organisms.
- Disturbing capillary tissue.
- Damaging pool linings.
- Getting feet wet and macerated.

If you must cross a shallow pool:

- Test footing.
- Move slowly.
- Do not splash.
- Do not use chemicals.
- Dry your feet afterward if possible.

### Condensation and bead-lines

Condensation beads may fall without warning. Protect your face, eyes, and electronics.

Do not:

- Lick beads.
- Collect beads by scraping surfaces.
- Insert objects into bead-lines.
- Divert bead-lines into containers for convenience.
- Shake leaves to make water fall.

If a bead-line runs along a waxy groove, treat it as part of the basin’s plumbing.

### Local rains

These are quiet, narrow, and intermittent. If one begins near you:

- Put on your hood.
- Protect electronics.
- Decide whether to wait or move around it.
- Do not try to channel the rain.
- Do not collect large amounts.
- Do not assume it is sterile.

Local rain may feel pleasant, but it can soak clothing and make surfaces slick.

# 19. Lighting and Vision

The basin is bright for a place below the surface, but the light is diffuse. There is no true sun-shape. Shadows are shallow and green. This can be soothing, but it can also flatten depth perception.

### Do not rely on flash photography

The basin is already luminous. Flash is unnecessary and may disturb rare fauna. It can also create harsh reflections on wet surfaces.

If you photograph:

- Use silent shutter if possible.
- Use anti-fog protection for lenses.
- Use a lens hood to protect from drips.
- Use low continuous light only if needed.
- Avoid pointing bright light at growth points.
- Avoid repeated flash in dense animal habitats, though fauna are rare.

### Personal lighting

You probably do not need strong illumination unless the basin dims beyond expectation or you enter an enclosed space.

Bring:

- A small headlamp or chest light.
- Red or low-white mode.
- Diffused beam.
- Waterproof housing.
- Extra batteries.

Do not bring:

- Open flames.
- Lanterns with fuel.
- Candles.
- Incense.
- Spark-producing devices.
- High-heat lamps.

Even if the basin is damp, fire is unacceptable. The atmosphere may be oxygen-rich or chemically unusual. Plant tissues may contain flammable resins. Do not risk it.

### Depth perception

Because shadows are soft, it may be hard to judge:

- Small drops.
- Hollows.
- Sharp crust edges.
- Moisture cups.
- Stem gaps.
- Ceiling height.

Move slower than you think necessary. Look down and up frequently. If you are tall, be especially careful under root lattices and leaf ribs.

# 20. Sound and Silence

The basin absorbs noise. This is one of its most intimate qualities.

You will hear small sounds:

- Condensation ticking on waxy leaves.
- Stem creaks.
- Leaf edges whispering.
- Water pockets opening and closing.
- Your own breathing.
- Your own heartbeat in your ears.
- Fabric movement.

Do not fill the silence unnecessarily.

### Prepare psychologically

Some people find deep quiet calming. Others find it oppressive. The basin may feel as if it is listening, even if there is no mind there.

If silence unsettles you:

- Keep a regular check-in schedule.
- Use a watch alarm.
- Focus on small environmental sounds.
- Breathe slowly.
- Do not use loud personal audio to comfort yourself.
- If you use audio, keep it barely audible and one-ear only.

Do not shout to test the acoustics. Do not clap for echoes. There may be none, and the attempt will feel wrong.

# 21. Psychological Preparation: The Stillness of Outcome

The basin’s most unsettling quality is not danger. It is the stillness of outcome. Nothing seems to advance into a future. Nothing erodes. Nothing succeeds. Nothing replaces anything else. The place is always beginning and always unchanged.

This can affect travelers in strange ways.

You may feel:

- Deep peace.
- Drowsiness.
- Emotional softening.
- Loss of urgency.
- A strange desire to remain.
- A sense that your plans are unnecessary.
- A feeling of being held.
- A feeling of being absorbed.
- Grief for histories that do not happen.
- Irritation at the place’s lack of progression.
- Fear that you are not leaving a trace even while you are present.

Prepare by maintaining small structures of selfhood.

### Carry time with you

Use:

- A watch.
- Alarms.
- Checkpoint times.
- A planned exit time.
- A journal or voice notes.
- A small inert tactile object that reminds you of outside life.

Do not leave the object behind. Do not make a shrine. Keep it in your pocket or sealed pouch.

### Keep a narrative

Take notes. Record times. Name your observations privately. Maintain a sense of sequence:

- Entered at time X.
- Reached central rise at time Y.
- Rested at time Z.
- Water remaining.
- Body condition.
- Planned exit.

This helps prevent the basin’s timelessness from becoming psychological drift.

### Do not over-meditate

Meditation can be beautiful here, but heat and stillness can make you passive. If you sit, set a timer. Do not close your eyes so long that you forget hydration, posture, or exit.

### If you feel too calm, check your body

Calm can be genuine, but it can also be heat fatigue, dehydration, mild CO₂ buildup, or dissociation.

Ask yourself:

- Have I drunk water recently?
- Have I taken electrolytes?
- Am I breathing easily?
- Is my head clear?
- Do I know where I am?
- Do I know how to leave?
- Am I sweating appropriately?
- Do I need to cool down?

If any answer is wrong, act.

# 23. First Aid and Medical Preparation

The basin is not full of predators, but it can injure through heat, slips, cuts, irritation, and disorientation.

Your first aid kit should be compact, sealed, and clean.

Include:

- Oral rehydration salts.
- Electrolyte tablets.
- Sterile wound dressings.
- Waterproof adhesive bandages.
- Blister pads.
- Antiseptic wipes that can be packed out.
- Small blunt tweezers.
- Medical tape that does not shed.
- Saline rinse for eyes or wounds.
- Antihistamine, if appropriate for you.
- Pain/fever reducer, if appropriate for you.
- Anti-nausea medication, if appropriate.
- Personal prescriptions.
- Cooling bandana or gel pack.
- Emergency blanket, lightweight.
- Small sealable bags for waste.

Avoid aerosol sprays, powdered medications that can drift, and strong-smelling ointments unless sealed.

### Heat exhaustion protocol

If someone shows signs of heat exhaustion:

1. Stop moving.
2. Move to the most open area nearby.
3. Sit on a pad or clean barrier.
4. Loosen gear.
5. Drink water with electrolytes.
6. Use fan or cooling aid.
7. Dampen skin only if you have water to spare and can manage it without contaminating the basin.
8. Do not continue if symptoms do not improve.
9. Exit as soon as safe.

### Heat stroke warning

If someone becomes confused, stops sweating, collapses, has very hot skin, slurred speech, or loses consciousness, treat it as emergency. Cool the person, protect their airway, and evacuate immediately. Do not worry about minor trace concerns in that moment; life comes first. But afterward, account for contamination as best you can.

### Injuries from surfaces

If cut by mineral crust or leaf edge:

1. Move away from the source.
2. Clean the wound with safe water or saline.
3. Apply waterproof dressing.
4. Monitor for irritation or infection after exit.
5. Do not leave bloody materials behind.

If sap or mineral glaze contacts eyes:

1. Do not rub.
2. Rinse with clean saline or water if available.
3. Blink gently.
4. Remove contact lenses if irritation continues.
5. Exit if pain, redness, or blurred vision persists.

### Allergic reaction

If you develop itching, hives, swelling, wheezing, or throat tightness, treat according to your medical plan and exit. Do not assume the basin’s sweetness means safety.

# 24. Movement Etiquette Inside the Basin

Once inside, your behavior should be soft, slow, and precise.

### Walk as if the floor is skin

The floor gives slightly underfoot. It is warm and alive. Treat it with care.

- Step lightly.
- Avoid dragging feet.
- Avoid sharp pivots.
- Avoid jumping.
- Avoid repeated pacing in one spot.
- Avoid stepping on small glossy leaf mats if a broader surface is available.
- Avoid swollen growth points.
- Avoid moisture cups.
- Avoid mineral crust edges.

### Move through density like water

When vegetation is dense:

- Slow down.
- Look for gaps.
- Turn your body sideways if needed.
- Protect your face.
- Keep pack tight.
- Do not yank.
- Do not tear.
- If something resists, do not force it.

The place has pressures. Pressure is information.

### Keep your hands gentle

Use hands to part, not pull. Use the backs of your hands when possible to avoid gripping delicate surfaces. Do not grab thin stems. Do not squeeze new growth.

### Keep your pack closed

Open packs shed objects. They also snag. Keep zippers closed and straps tucked.

### Do not lean unnecessarily

Do not lean against:

- Half-arches.
- Thin stems.
- Leaf ribs.
- Grown walls.
- Seed-cases.
- New growth points.

If you need to rest, sit on your pad in an open area.

# 25. If You Become Lost

The basin’s density and diffuse light can make disorientation easy.

If you realize you are lost:

1. Stop immediately.
2. Do not push forward.
3. Check water, heat, and mental state.
4. Check your tracker or compass, but do not trust one source alone.
5. Try to remember your last confirmed landmark.
6. If safe, backtrack slowly along your recent path.
7. If backtracking is uncertain, move toward the nearest open rise if you can see one.
8. Do not create a new path by force.
9. Do not leave gear behind as markers unless it is an emergency and you will retrieve it.
10. If you cannot orient, stay visible, hydrate, cool down, and use your beacon.

Do not panic because the basin is quiet. Quiet does not mean hostile. But it does mean you must be self-reliant.

# 26. If You Damage Something

Ideally, you will damage nothing. If you do:

- Stop.
- Do not try to repair it with foreign materials.
- Do not apply tape, glue, ointment, or chemicals.
- Do not cut away damaged tissue.
- If a clean natural fragment has fallen, leave it where it fell unless it is contaminated by your gear or chemicals.
- If the fragment is contaminated, seal it and carry it out only if removal is safer than leaving it; otherwise report the incident.
- Record what happened.
- Report to relevant authorities or guides after exit.

Do not attempt to “fix” the basin. It operates by its own logic.

# 27. If You See Built or Grown Rooms

The grown rooms are among the most fascinating features: seed-cases like roofless huts, rings of fused stems, arched leaf ribs, low platforms, incomplete walls. They have architecture without manufacture.

Your attitude should be respectful restraint.

### Observe

You may look, sketch, photograph, and quietly study.

### Do not occupy

Unless emergency requires shelter, do not sit, sleep, or store gear inside them. They are not campsites.

### Do not alter

Do not:

- Clear debris.
- Flatten floors.
- Add walls.
- Close gaps.
- Open entrances.
- Break obstructions.
- Move platforms.
- Rearrange stem rings.

### Do not interpret them as invitations

An incomplete arch is not asking you to finish it. A smooth-floored pod is not asking you to lie inside it. A circular wall lacking one segment is not asking you to fill the gap.

The basin is stable in its incompleteness. Let it remain so.

# 29. Multiverse-Specific Preparation

As an ordinary multiverse traveler, you may have access to return keys, anchors, suits, or transit platforms. Use them responsibly.

### Set your anchor outside the most sensitive zone if possible

If your transit method allows, do not open directly into dense tissue. Choose an entrance hollow, threshold, or antechamber outside the most delicate interior. Avoid energy discharge, heat, wind, or light bursts inside the basin.

### Shield your transit gear

Make sure your return device does not:

- Leak coolant.
- Emit ozone.
- Scatter dust.
- Produce sparks.
- Shed metal flakes.
- Hum loudly.
- Radiate heat into nearby tissue.

### Keep return keys sealed and redundant

Humidity and dense tissue may interfere with electronics. Keep your return mechanism in a waterproof, impact-resistant pouch. Have a backup if possible.

### Leave a trip plan

Tell someone outside:

- Where you are going.
- Expected duration.
- Entry point.
- Return signal.
- Emergency protocol.
- What to do if you miss check-in.

Do not assume rescue will be easy inside a pathless living hollow.

### Decontaminate on exit

Use your multiverse infrastructure to clean yourself and your gear after leaving. Do not carry basin moisture, spores, or tissue into another world.

# 30. Exit Procedure

Leaving is part of the preparation.

Before you depart the basin:

1. Check your body for heat stress, dizziness, cuts, rash, or breathing difficulty.
2. Check your water and gear.
3. Make sure nothing is left behind.
4. Make sure no wrappers, tissues, wipes, hair ties, bandages, or equipment fragments are missing.
5. Seal all waste.
6. Seal wet or sweaty clothing in a separate bag.
7. Remove any temporary markers if you were forced to use them.
8. Look around for accidental disturbance.
9. Leave quietly.
10. Do not take anything.

After exit:

- Hydrate.
- Cool down gradually.
- Shower if possible.
- Inspect skin for rash, cuts, or irritation.
- Check eyes and lungs for delayed reaction.
- Clean and dry gear thoroughly.
- Inspect boots, seams, pockets, and pack folds for biological material.
- Dispose of waste according to biosecurity rules.
- Report any damage, symptoms, or anomalies.
- Do not bring water, plants, mineral crusts, seeds, beetles, worms, or tissue into another environment.

If you develop fever, rash, breathing trouble, eye pain, nausea, or unusual fatigue afterward, seek medical attention and mention the basin’s hot, humid, plant-dense environment.

# 33. The Right Inner Attitude

The basin is not a challenge to overcome. It is not a resource to use. It is not a backdrop for heroic travel photography. It is not a ruin waiting for interpretation. It is not a garden waiting for a gardener. It is not a machine waiting for a mechanism. It is a living equilibrium held in permanent beginning.

The right attitude is something like this:

- Move slowly.
- Breathe gently.
- Carry everything.
- Touch little.
- Break nothing.
- Drink your own water.
- Keep your waste.
- Speak softly.
- Watch your heat.
- Respect silence.

You are not there to make history. You are there to pass through without adding any.

# 34. Final Intuitive Guidance

Imagine standing there for the first time.

The air is warm and thick against your skin. The light is white-gold and green, spread everywhere without a sun. The floor gives faintly beneath your feet, not soft like mud, but alive like dense fruit-flesh or warm cork. Around you, layers of vegetation press close: glossy low mats, waist-high rosettes, shoulder-high fronds, stems like columns, root curtains hanging in the middle distance. The ceiling glows overhead like a living membrane. Condensation ticks somewhere nearby. A stem creaks almost inaudibly. The silence is not empty; it is full of absorbed sound.

You feel held.

That is the moment to be careful.

The beauty may make you want to linger, touch, lean, taste, collect, rest inside a grown room, or push deeper into the fronds. Instead, you should check your water, adjust your mask, slow your breathing, and move as if you are passing through a sleeping body.

Prepare to be warm. Prepare to be quiet. Prepare to be self-contained. Prepare to carry out everything you bring. Prepare to leave no story behind except your own private memory.

The basin does not need your mark. It does not need your tools. It does not need your fire, your food scraps, your footprints, your offerings, your repairs, or your names.

Prepare simply: clean body, sealed gear, enough water, cool head, patient hands, and the humility to be brief.
