It lies below the surrounding surface, but it is not enclosed. A traveler descending through one of the narrow break-lines would find that the floor of the world has dropped away into a sunken region several kilometers wide: a chain of collapse hollows, raw stone shelves, and long shallow basins that connect into a dim regional plain. This is not a tunnel and not a single chamber. It has the breadth of a valley system, only roofless in places, vaulted in others, with the level of the land above it lost in gray openings far overhead.

The terrain is young. Everything looks newly broken and still deciding where to settle. Huge slabs of pale limestone and darker chert lie angled against one another, their edges crisp, their fracture faces unsoftened. Talus is not rounded into comfortable scree; it stands in sharp fins and awkward, blade-like piles. Collapse pits open abruptly between benches of bedrock. There is almost no soil, only mineral dust, grit, and thin crusts of biological growth in the dampest cracks. Where water has begun to find routes, it has etched shallow channels, but these too are fresh: narrow, jagged, and undecided, more like first scratches than mature streambeds. The place feels recently emerged into its present form, as if the stone has only lately stopped falling.

Light is weak and diluted. Daylight reaches only through vertical throats, split fissures, and collapsed skylights, and even then it arrives grayed by mist and distance. It does not beam so much as seep. In the wider hollows there is a permanent twilight: enough to distinguish pale rock from dark wetness, enough to see the faint green film of algae near water, but not enough to give the landscape color in any rich sense. The air itself seems to lower the light. Moisture hangs in it as a fine aerosol, produced by condensation on cold stone and by the respiration of countless small bodies.

It is a damp place without a single obvious source of wetness. There is no river in full flood, no lake, no marsh. Instead there are seeps, bead-lines of water on rock faces, shallow pockets in the stone that hold clear water, and the constant slow drip from overhead ledges. Sometimes a higher shaft carries the rumor of rain—a hiss, a sudden metallic smell, a brief quickening of drops—but the event never becomes weather. The total wetness of the place remains the same.
