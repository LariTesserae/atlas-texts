Below the dry mineral crust, under a surface that has forgotten the idea of rivers, there is an open region that can only be called a landscape because it has horizons, weather, inhabitants, and roads — even though almost every part of it is made of plants.

It is below the surface, but not in the manner of a cave. It is below the surface in the manner of a vast inhabited stratum, a regional underplain sealed away from any external hydrology. Above it lies a pale expanse of salt, powdered mineral, and wind-abraded emptiness. Below that crust, separated by a thick roof of compacted vegetal matter and engineered support, the world opens outward.

There is no water here.

Not as puddle, not as vapor-heavy air, not as drip or spring or condensation. The place is anhydrous in its deepest habits. The chemistry of life has been arranged around other solvents, other transports, other necessities. What would be moisture in another world exists here as resin, volatile sap, cool ester films, mineral salts, and electric gradients through living fiber. The air is dry enough to make every motion faintly static. It does not feel parched so much as perfected in its dryness, as if water were a redundant invention that the landscape had discarded early.

The temperature is cool. It has the stable chill of an enclosed underlayer: not cold enough to bite, not warm enough to invite languor. The air rests against the skin like clean metal or old paper. It carries very little scent, except when currents move through harvested zones or engine halls; then there is a faint smell of crushed leaves, resin dust, warm cellulose, and electrified mineral.

And yet this is not a barren place.

It is almost entirely vegetal.

Every surface is plant, or derived from plant, or in the process of becoming plant. The ground is a compressed mosaic of root mat, fallen fiber, pollenite crusts, and hardened stem fragments. The walls of the wider basin are built from densely packed xylem strands, living culms, interwoven runners, and old architectural growth that has been stiffened by mineral infusion. The ceiling far overhead is a lattice of bound stalks and translucent leaf-plates, reinforced by industrial cables and mirrored light shafts. In some places the plant matter is still alive and slowly moving; in others it has been processed into something like timber, lacquer, insulation, or machinery. But even the processed material remembers its botanical origin.

Vegetation here is not a population of individual plants scattered across soil. It is the substrate itself. The landscape is made of it. It is the geology, the architecture, the fuel, the insulation, the roadbed, the acoustic dampening, and the living medium through which the whole region breathes. Because of this, the place has a strange visual paradox: it is totally vegetal, but not crowded in the usual sense. There are few thickets, few obvious bushes, few standing forests. Instead, there are broad open fields of pale fiber, long hollows, low ridges, terraces, and empty intervals. The world is filled with plantness, but sparsely furnished with discrete objects. Space remains.

That sparseness gives the landscape its quiet dignity.

One can stand on a broad plain of matted root filaments and see far. The horizon bends gently upward at the edges because the underplain is not truly flat; it is a regional depression shaped by growth, harvest, collapse, and slow accumulation. There are shallow basins of powdered pollen, ramps of cut stem-bundles, long embankments where shredded vegetation has been piled into artificial dunes, and wide empty reaches where nothing has yet been finished. The land is emerging rather than settled. It is under construction by biology, industry, and dry erosion all at once.

This is a place in its first draft.

New terrain is constantly being laid down or exposed. In one district, harvesters have cut into a wall of compacted plant-strata, leaving raw vertical faces striped with pale fiber and darker resin lines. The cut surfaces are not weathered yet; they still have the roughness of recent industry. Nearby, wind — such as wind exists here — has begun to move loose chaff into ripples. Those ripples are not ancient dunes. They are embryonic dunes, just beginning to understand their own shape. In another area, a collapsed growth shelf has spilled a cascade of hollow tubes across a lower plain. Workers and machines have not cleared it. Instead, they have begun integrating it, laying walkways across the tubes, running cables through them, letting beetles and spore-feeders colonize the shaded gaps. Erosion here is not merely destruction. It is pre-formation. It is raw material seeking a future arrangement.

Because the scale is open, the effect is not claustrophobic despite being underground. The underplain extends beyond the immediate view into a regional spread of districts, hollows, production zones, and half-formed terrain. It is town-sized or valley-sized rather than chamber-sized, a multi-feature landscape connected by paths, rails, ventilation shafts, and the slow circulation of air through living passages. From higher terraces one can see several zones at once: a residential stack built into a curved root wall, a factory field with low chimneys, a broad nursery plain where new growth is trained over frames, a sparse transit corridor where electric tugs move silently on braided tracks.

The built environment is urban, but not dense.

It has the logic of a city — infrastructure, zoning, circulation, maintenance, layered construction — yet it is spread thin. There are large gaps between structures. There are empty plazas that are not plazas but living membrane fields reserved for future growth. There are industrial blocks separated by buffers of fibrous no-man’s-land. There are habitation tiers cut into the sides of great root bulges, connected by bridges of woven stem and metal, but the tiers are interrupted by blank stretches, unfinished facades, or access ramps leading to nothing yet. The city feels less like a crowded settlement than like an organization occupying a vast living body with respect and caution.

The buildings are partly constructed and partly cultivated. Walls are made from shaped plant tissue still capable of slow repair. Roofs are supported by living ribs guided over frames. Some structures are obviously old and have been reinforced with industrial materials: steel struts, ceramic plates, insulated panels, conductive cabling. Others are new and raw, their surfaces still pale, their joints still visibly experimental. In places, machinery has been bolted directly into plant architecture, with brackets and dampers inserted to prevent damage. In other places, machines appear to have been grown around, as if the landscape had accepted them as strange organs.

Industry is present everywhere, but it is not loud.

There are engines, but they are muffled by the absorbent plant matter around them. There are factories, but their processes are sealed or slow. There are conveyors, crushers, distillation columns, resin tanks, electric winches, heat exchangers, and sorting frames. The technology is industrial in scale and function: it moves material, refines products, generates power, maintains atmosphere, and supports a population. Yet the surrounding vegetation swallows much of the mechanical noise. Sound behaves oddly here. It does not ring. It does not echo far. The fibrous world takes each impact, footfall, voice, and engine throb and shortens it.

Mostly, the landscape is quiet.

This quiet is one of its defining qualities. It is not the silence of absence. It is the silence of absorption. The underplain is full of small processes, but they occur at low volume. There is a soft ticking of cooling metal near a generator housing. There is a faint whisper as loose fiber shifts under a dry draft. There is a distant, almost sub-audible pulse from ventilation machinery. There are clicks and rustles from fauna, but even these are subdued, as if the creatures have evolved to avoid wasting sound. Occasionally a train or tug passes along a track, producing a muted hum that rises briefly and then disappears into the walls. Occasionally a spore release occurs, a soft exhalation from a high growth ridge, followed by a long hush of drifting particles.

The light is normal.

That is one of the stranger things about the place. After expecting darkness, or bioluminescent weirdness, or industrial amber, one finds instead a balanced, ordinary illumination. It comes down through a network of surface shafts, mirrored conduits, and engineered apertures that bring daylight into the upper levels. The light is diffused by ceiling membranes, polished leaf-surfaces, and suspended reflector vanes. It is supplemented by electric lamps with white, steady output. There is nothing theatrical about it. It is not golden, not green, not spectral. It is simply light, practical and even, allowing distances to be judged and work to be done. Shadows are soft because the illumination arrives from many directions. In the farthest districts, the light becomes paler and more intermittent, but it remains recognizably normal.

Under this light, the landscape appears both raw and carefully managed.

The atmosphere is active. There are cycles here, though they are not weather in the familiar sense. There are thermal drafts caused by engines, by surface temperature differentials, and by metabolic heat from large growth masses. These drafts move in slow shifts, carrying fine particles, pollen dust, and the occasional scent of resin. There are spore tides: periods when certain ridges or ceiling fields release clouds of dry reproductive material into the air. The clouds do not fall like rain. They drift, layer, settle, and are later gathered by machines or consumed by fauna. There are electrostatic fluctuations, especially in harvested zones where dry fiber has been exposed. These fluctuations are rarely dangerous, but they cause faint crackles along cables, raise tiny hairs on exposed skin, and make loose dust tremble in patterns.

The ecosystem is in motion.

Plants grow, but not always visibly. Some zones appear unchanged for days, then a membrane floor will have thickened overnight. Some architectural culms elongate so slowly that their movement is measured by markers rather than sight. Other growth is rapid and opportunistic, filling gaps, sealing cuts, or colonizing abandoned machinery. There are cultivated plains where new substrate is coaxed into sheets, terraces, or load-bearing forms. There are wilder patches where growth follows its own logic, producing folded mats, hollow blisters, and brittle towers that may later collapse into new ground.

Fauna is abundant, though often quiet and small.

The landscape supports many creatures because the plant mass provides food, shelter, pathways, and chemical gradients. Most animals here are dry-bodied, efficient, and low-noise. There are swarms of tiny husk-beetles moving through powdered fiber, consuming dead material and leaving behind finer grains that can be carried by air. There are long, flat-bodied grazers that rasp living surfaces without damaging structural tissue, their movements coordinated in slow waves. There are pale, many-jointed things that live inside hollow stems and emerge only when spore density rises. There are aerial forms, though they do not fly like birds; they drift on thermal currents, using broad membranes or charged filaments to remain suspended. They are quiet in motion, more like bits of the landscape detached than separate animals.

Predators exist, but they are not loud.

A hunter here might be a flattened, root-colored crawler that waits beside a ventilation seam until smaller feeders pass. It might be a suspended glider that drops from a ceiling rib and interceptes drifting spore-sailors. It might be something almost plant-like itself, indistinguishable from a growth node until its surface opens and draws prey inward. Predation is one of the active processes in the landscape, but it happens with restrained urgency. The abundance of fauna does not manifest as roaring or flocking chaos. It manifests as presence: the sense that every surface, fold, and gap contains life going about its business.

The animals have adapted to a world without water. Their bodies conserve and recycle their internal solvents. Many have shells or coatings that reduce loss. Their waste is dry, granular, or resinous. Some feed on spores; others on resin secretions, structural fiber, dead plant mats, or the microbial-like films that exist in microscopic niches. Some are drawn to electrical infrastructure, taking energy from warm cables or from static gradients. Others avoid machinery entirely, living in older plant territories where industrial disturbance has not reached.

The relationship between the built and the living is complicated.

This is not a city placed upon land. It is a city negotiated with a living substrate. Industrial activity must account for growth, repair, chemical exchange, and the needs of fauna. In turn, the vegetation benefits from structure, pruning, nutrient redistribution, and protection from collapse. The built environment channels air, light, and material through the plant body of the landscape. The plant body, in turn, filters dust, stabilizes temperature, absorbs sound, and provides new raw mass. Neither side is fully in control.

The workers, inhabitants, or caretakers of this place — whoever they are — move with a kind of sparse competence. They do not crowd the landscape. They appear in small numbers: a pair checking a cable anchor, a lone operator monitoring a resin line, a maintenance crew cutting a new passage through fiber that bleeds pale dust instead of sap. Their machines are practical and slightly overbuilt, designed to avoid sparks and excessive heat. Fire is not impossible here, but it is treated as a catastrophic idea. The place is dry, fibrous, and full of fine airborne particles. Industry must therefore be careful, sealed, and slow. Power systems favor enclosed combustion, electric storage, heat exchange, and chemical conversion rather than open flame.

The infrastructure is layered because the landscape is layered.

Near the ceiling are light shafts, reflector galleries, ventilation ducts, and monitoring platforms. Below those are cultivation terraces, cable runs, and residential or storage niches carved into thick growth walls. At ground level are the main circulation routes: packed fiber paths, rail lines, service roads, conveyor channels, and broad empty intervals reserved for future expansion. Beneath the immediate ground surface are root conduits, chemical veins, buried sensor lines, and older strata of compressed plant matter. Some of those strata are dead material, preserved by dryness. Some are dormant. Some are still faintly active, responding to pressure or chemical signals over long timescales.

The urban quality of the place comes from its organization rather than its density.

There are districts specialized by function. A refinery zone may contain rows of sealed tanks, heated coils, and stacked columns where plant exudates are separated into fuels, sealants, and industrial feedstocks. A fabrication district may hold looms, presses, extrusion frames, and curing racks, all fed by prepared fiber. A growth-control district may be almost empty, with only low platforms, sensor posts, and access hatches into living tissue. A residential zone may have stacked cells, communal halls, storage alcoves, and quiet observation points looking out over the plain. Between these districts lie wide transitional spaces, partly because safety requires buffers and partly because the landscape itself needs room to change.

The rawness of the terrain gives the city an unfinished feeling.

Some areas have been planned but not completed. Foundations exist without structures. Channels have been cut but not lined. Bridges end in open air, waiting for the other side to grow into place. In other areas, older construction has been overtaken by new growth, creating hybrid forms: staircases partly absorbed into root walls, lamp posts embraced by pale fiber, rail tracks lifted slightly by slow uplift beneath them. Nothing looks ruined exactly. Ruin implies abandonment. Here, even the obsolete parts are being metabolized or reused.

The emerging erosion is visible in the way surfaces are still deciding their final texture. Fresh cuts remain sharp. Deposits of loose material have not yet compacted. Ripples are fragile. New slopes shed dust too easily. Some hills are not hills yet, but accumulations being guided into shape by wind, gravity, and deliberate seeding. The landscape is becoming. It has not arrived at a stable mature form. It is raw, but not primitive. Its rawness is the product of ongoing construction by multiple forces.

One can imagine standing in a hollow near the center of this underplain. The ground underfoot is firm but faintly yielding, made of interlocked fibers and pale particulate. To one side rises a long slope of bundled stem material, terraced and reinforced with metal pins. Above, a ceiling of woven growth glows softly where daylight shafts distribute their light. In the distance, a factory cluster sits low against a darker wall, its chimneys releasing not smoke but nearly invisible vapor that dissipates quickly. A rail line curves past, empty for the moment. There are no crowds. There are no loud alarms. There is only the sense of a large, organized, living system proceeding through its daily work.

Then a spore release begins.

A ridge in the middle distance shimmers faintly as dry particles lift from its surface. The movement is not explosive; it is more like a slow exhalation. The cloud expands sideways, caught by a thermal draft, and begins to cross the open space. Almost immediately, small fauna respond. Drifting membrane-feeders turn toward it. Ground-level grazers shift position. Somewhere unseen, machines adjust filtration. The light remains normal, but the air becomes subtly textured, filled with pale motes moving in orderly bands. The event is active, ecological, and industrial at once. It is weather, agriculture, and pollution-control all combined, though none of those words fits perfectly.

The quiet continues.

That is the deep character of the place: active but not noisy, built but not crowded, vegetal but not green in any simple sense, underground but open, industrial but restrained. It is a dry, cool, emerging landscape where plant matter is the world itself and where urban life has learned to occupy that world without overwhelming it.

The rules of the place are different from those of wetter, louder, more mineral landscapes.

Nothing here is truly inert. Even what seems like ground may be a slow organ. Nothing should be cut without understanding what flows through it. Nothing should be heated carelessly. Sound is precious and easily intrusive. Distance is normal; one does not expect continuous settlement. Light is trusted but not abundant enough to waste. The fauna are not ornaments; they are necessary processes. The city is not separate from the plant body; it is a set of agreements made inside it. And because the landscape is still forming, every act of construction also alters what the terrain will become next.

It is a strange place, but not incomprehensible. Its strangeness lies in its combination: total vegetation, absent water, underground openness, industrial order, sparse habitation, quiet abundance. It feels like a world in the middle of inventing its own geography, using plants, machines, animals, and dry light as tools.

Above, the surface remains salt-white and empty.

Below, the underplain continues its slow, active becoming: a regional city inside a living, anhydrous body, cool and mostly silent, full of small lives, lit by ordinary light, and always just beginning.
