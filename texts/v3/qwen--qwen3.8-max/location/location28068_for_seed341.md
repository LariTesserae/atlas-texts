**The Low Ward of the Pump-Yards**

It lies below the level of the surrounding marsh, a small built basin sunk into hot, wet ground. The horizon is close and low: dark reed banks, sloping levees, black watercourses, and the blunt silhouettes of pump-houses. The whole place is no larger than a hamlet or a cathedral court, yet it has the organized complexity of a town. Channels cross one another under raised walkways. Sluice-gates stand in rows. Engine halls rise from water on plinths of glazed brick. Pipe galleries climb above the roofs in tiers, wrapped in condensation and trailing roots. Everything is arranged, but not crowded. There is much open water between the structures, much empty air, much dark space where the town seems to have deliberately left room for moisture to gather.

The water table is at the surface. In places it is only a finger’s depth below the paving; in others it rises openly into shallow black streets. The ground is made of old, water-smoothed materials: rounded basalt setts, tiles glazed green by long wetness, brick worn to a soft edge, iron darkened with patina, timber blackened but still firm. Nothing looks newly broken. The erosion is old and settled. The place has not been overcome by water so much as entered into a long agreement with it. Stone sweats. Walls weep in thin, continuous threads. Gutters run even when no rain falls, because the air itself is heavy enough to condense on cool metal and drip from every edge.

Heat stands in the basin like a weight. It comes up from the water, from the engines, from the sunless marsh beyond the levees. The air is thick, saturated, and warm enough to make surfaces slick almost instantly. Mist hangs in the lower galleries. Steam rises from the warmer channels and drifts under the pipe-works in slow, dim banks. Where vapor touches cooler stone, it becomes rain again: a fine, local drizzle that begins under roofs and beneath leaves, as though the place makes its own weather from its own breath. The atmosphere is not merely wet; it is continuously becoming water.

Light is dim. The sky above is a low ceiling of vapor and shadow, crossed by gantries, cables, and the crowns of tall condenser stacks. Even at midday the place remains in a greenish half-light, filtered through mist, wet glass, and broad leaves. At irregular intervals, electric lamps come on inside wire cages: amber, copper, sometimes a sickly mineral green. Their glow reflects in standing water and makes halos in the damp air. The darkness is not empty. It is full of suspended droplets, faintly shining films of moisture, and the dull gleam of wet machinery.

The built fabric is urban but sparse. A few large structures occupy the basin like islands: a pump house with square chimneys, a cistern tower banded with rusted iron, a long roofed gallery of valves, a row of transformer sheds crowned with ceramic insulators. Between them are open courts, flooded lanes
