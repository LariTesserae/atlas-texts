## The High Green Scaffolds

It looks, at first, like a flooded city lifted into the sky by some impossible act of growth. But there are no builders here in any human sense. No tools, no machines, no cut stone, no metal, no road laid over dead ground. Everything that appears made has been made by plants: grown, woven, inflated, fused, split, repaired, and rebuilt. The landscape is a high, hot, wet, plant-made region suspended above cloud and steam, open and sparse in its arrangement, yet total in its substance. There is no animal life anywhere in it. No birds, no insects, no fish in the warm pools, no footprints, no eyes watching from cover. The only movement comes from water, wind, heat, and living tissue.

The elevation is strange and absolute. This is not a valley sitting on a mountain; it is a valley that has become its own height. Broad platforms of root and stem stand above a lower world of mist, their undersides dangling with pale aerial roots, their upper surfaces arranged into terraces, basins, ramps, and open courts. From a distance, the region resembles a series of enormous green tablelands, separated by wide gaps of hot air, shining water, and drifting cloud-vapor. The scale is not intimate. It extends outward into a hazy horizon of separated features: one vast platform here, another far off to the left, a distant crown of hollow spires beyond that, and between them, open distances filled with light, steam, and sound.

The light is ordinary. That is one of the unsettling things about the place. The sun is normal, the shadows are normal, the pale blue of high air is normal. There is no magical tint, no unnatural glow. Plain daylight reveals the impossible fact of the landscape: that the ground, the walls, the towers, the bridges, the pools, and the cliffs are all constructed from living vegetation. The weirdness does not come from darkness or hallucination. It comes from seeing familiar architectural forms — stairs, buttresses, arches, parapets, chambers, causeways — made entirely of green tissue, leaf-skin, pith, root-fiber, and water.

The terrain is newly formed and still young in its details. Nothing has been softened by long erosion. The edges are sharp. Fresh root-platforms have crisp lips and pale, unweathered undersides. Newly opened stem-towers still carry dried bud-sheaths clinging along their sides like discarded wrappings. Leaf-tiles on the upper surfaces have clean creases. Broken growths show pale fibrous splinters rather than rot or polish. The whole place feels recently emerged, as if it was only just lifted into being by a surge of growth, pressure, and heat. There has not been enough time for the water to round everything down. Instead, the landscape has the raw clarity of something still becoming.

There is water everywhere, but not as a passive feature. Water is the pulse of the place.

The upper platforms are shallowly wet. Broad depressions in the living surface hold warm pools edged by upright reedlike growths. Thin sheets of water slide across leaf-surfaces with a slow, glossy movement, then gather in channels that run along the spines of raised stems. From the sides of the platforms, water weeps in constant threads. Some of it falls as clear strands into the cloud below; some evaporates before it reaches the lower air, becoming local mist. After each storm, the entire region glistens. The plant-surfaces drink quickly, then exhale vapor back into the hot air. Condensation forms on the cooler undersides of broad leaf-plates and drips upward? No — it drips downward in fine beading lines, making the lower faces of the platforms look as if they are sweating.

The atmosphere is hot and saturated. At this height, the air should be thin and cool, but the platforms trap and recycle warmth. Sunlight strikes the dark green upper surfaces and heats them. Beneath the upper skin of the platforms, metabolic heat from dense living tissue adds to the warmth. Hot air rises from the open gaps between platforms, carrying moisture with it. Clouds form locally, burst, and are torn apart again. Rain is frequent but chaotic: one platform may be under a brief downpour while another nearby stands in bright light, its surfaces steaming. The humidity is heavy enough that the horizon shimmers. Breath would feel thick, green, and close. The smell is of hot leaves, wet fiber, crushed grass, steamed resin, and clean rain with no dust in it.

Because the landscape is built from living tissue, the materials are unlike ordinary terrain. The “stone” is compressed root-mass, dense and springy, cut through with capillary channels. The “timber” is not wood as a dead material but living stem-column tissue, rigid with pressure and still capable of repair. Some surfaces are covered in a short, tight moss-like growth that acts like a water-retaining skin. Others are made of tall, hollow reed-forms arranged in ranks, their joints sealed with translucent plant-resin. There are membranes like stretched leaf-leather, pale and veined, used by the structure as flexible roofs or rain-catchers. There are fibrous ropes of living root that cross gaps between platforms, not laid by hands but grown toward moisture and tension. There are pith-chambers filled with warm gas, swollen into buoyant bladders that help keep the higher platforms aloft.

Everything is constructed, but construction here means growth, not manufacture. Staircases exist because successive leaf-bases overlap in ascending tiers. Bridges exist because roots have been guided by humidity gradients to interlock across empty air. Towers exist because hollow stems compete for light and venting, rising one above another until they form clustered spires. Pools exist because broad leaves have curved into basins and sealed their seams with waxy secretions. Walls exist because dense side-branching has woven itself into a living hedge. Arches exist because stems have bent toward one another and fused where they touch. The place has the logic of architecture without architects. It is built by turgor, tropism, pressure, fusion, secretion, and death-and-regrowth. No hammer has touched it. No saw. No fired brick. No machine.

The density is low. This is not a jungle choked with continuous growth. The presences are few, large, and widely spaced. The platforms themselves are open, with long empty reaches of shallow water, bare leaf-pavement, or smooth root-skin between the major structures. A single tower-cluster may stand alone on a platform the size of a town square, surrounded by nothing but wet green surface and sky. The gaps between platforms are even wider: hundreds of meters, sometimes kilometers, of open air, cloud, and falling water. The landscape has room in it. Emptiness is part of its structure. The eye can travel long distances without meeting an obstacle, and yet the whole field, when considered as a system, is saturated with plant-life. Even the open pools rest on living floors. Even the thin membranes over the void are leaves. Even the apparent emptiness is held inside a vegetal construction.

The sound is loud.

It is loud in a way that has no mechanical source, which makes it all the more overwhelming. Water falls constantly from platform edges, not in one great roar but in many separate voices: thin hissing threads, broad sheets, sudden gulps from overflow basins, and deep splashes into upper pools. Wind moves through the hollow stems and produces a vast array of tones — low booms, reedy drones, sharp whistles, and long hollow moans that rise and fall with the gusts. The platforms creak. The living bridges strain. Buoyant pith-chambers groan as pressure changes within them. When the heat intensifies, water inside certain sealed stems expands, and the stems release it in sudden percussive bursts. Seed-vessels dry and crack like knuckles. Leaf-surfaces drum under rain. Storm-wind striking the broad membranes makes a sound like enormous canvas being snapped by giants.

There is no animal noise to soften it. No calls, no wings, no chittering. The loudness is entirely elemental and botanical. It is the sound of a landscape working on itself.

The ecology of the place is complete without animals. Plants reproduce here by water, wind, steam, and explosive release. Some species produce buoyant seed-rafts that drift across the shallow upper pools until they find a wet seam in which to lodge. Others vent spores or seeds from heated capsules, using pressure to scatter them across the open gaps. Some roots detach and become independent floating starters, drifting on the lower air until they catch moisture and begin to weave new mats. There is no grazing. There is no predation. Death comes from storm damage, desiccation, overpressure, collapse, or self-pruning. Fallen tissues do not lie long. They are reabsorbed, overgrown, compressed into new platforms, or broken down by heat and moisture into a warm vegetable pulp that living roots draw back into the system. The landscape feeds itself with its own bodies.

Because there are no animals, the forms of the place seem to imitate animal purpose without serving it. There are chambers that look like rooms but contain only water. There are stairways that lead to pools. There are balconies open to nothing but wind. There are narrow corridors formed by upright leaves, wide enough for a body, yet no body uses them. There are amphitheaters of curved root filled with steam. These are not ruins, and they are not symbols. They are functional organs of a plant-made environment. The “rooms” are humidity chambers. The “stairs” are layered growth fronts. The “balconies” are condensation arrays. The “corridors” are venting passages. The resemblance to inhabited architecture is strong, but the inhabitants are not creatures. The inhabitants are the processes themselves.

The place is turbulent. Change does not arrive slowly here; it surges.

Heat builds through the day. The upper surfaces warm, the air below warms, and the pressure within the platforms rises. Water that was resting in shallow basins begins to move as internal channels open and close. Certain towers start to vent steam. The hollow reeds deepen their note. Across a wide platform, leaf-membranes lift slightly at their edges, letting hot air escape. Then a local storm forms in the gap between platforms, fed by the rising vapor. Rain strikes one side of the landscape while the other remains lit by hard sun. Lightning, if it comes, is not the only violence. The landscape itself participates in the storm.

A platform may rise slightly as its buoyant tissues fill with gas. A stem-tower may split open from internal pressure, sending hot water down its sides. A bridge of roots may tighten as one side lifts, then tear free and fall in slow, fibrous strands into the cloud below. A pool may overflow catastrophically, cutting a temporary waterfall across a plaza that existed only moments before. A section of edge may calve away, not as rock but as a living raft, drifting off into the open air while its trailing roots still drink from the mist.
