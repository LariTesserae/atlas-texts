It is a high, bright, water-drenched mineral world without a single green thing.

The landscape opens wide under a thin, glaring sky: a broad basin or caldera-like shelf, miles across, where pale terraces, shallow pools, and long causeways stretch away into heat-shimmer. There is no grass, no scrub, no tree, no creeping vine. The ground is made of stone, salt, fired clay, rust-streaked rock, white calcium crusts, seams of black volcanic glass, and smooth wet mineral skins that gleam as if freshly washed. Rain has just passed, or passes often here; every hollow holds water, every slope glistens, and the rocks themselves seem to sweat. In places the cliffs weep in thin silver threads, drawing moisture from the humid air through hygroscopic salts. Condensation beads on iron pipes, ceramic walls, and the undersides of broad stone ledges, then falls in soft, spaced drops.

The heat is strong despite the elevation. The sun strikes white surfaces and doubles itself in glare. Steam rises from warm runnels and shallow thermal pools, not in thick columns but in faint wavering veils that make the distance tremble. The air is wet, bright, and heavy with a smell of hot flint, salt, faint sulfur, and clean electrified metal. High above, clouds form and dissolve quickly, driven by the basin’s own heat. Weather here is a cycle of evaporation, condensation, brief intense rain, and return to bright stillness. Nothing about the place feels newly broken; its forms have settled over long use. The terraces are worn smooth, the channels rounded, the stone edges softened by water and mineral deposition. Erosion has not made chaos so much as patience: everything is weathered, balanced, and accustomed to being wet.

Across this open basin lies a sparse urban field rather than a compact city. It is settlement-scale but widely scattered: districts separated by empty pools, salt flats, steam vents, and broad stretches of shining mineral ground. There are pump-houses, kilns, cooling halls, warehouses, rail spurs, pipe-bridges, workshops, dwellings, and low arcaded yards, but much space remains between them. The built environment feels industrial and practical: engines, turbines, boilers, condensers, electric lines, ceramic insulators, iron walkways, sluice gates, and brick smokeless chimneys. Yet the machinery is often sunk into vaults, half-submerged in water, or housed behind thick walls of glazed stone, so that its presence is felt more as pressure and warmth than as noise. Power comes from geothermal heat, falling water, and steam-driven generators. Electricity hums along pale porcelain knobs and overhead cables, and at night the city’s lamps would burn white and steady, though by day the light is already severe.

The materials of the city match the mineral world around it. There is no wood. Structures are built from fired clay, basalt, salt-glazed brick, iron, copper alloy, glass, and polished stone. Surfaces are coated in pale mineral residue from constant wetting and drying. Pipes run everywhere: some carry hot water, some carry cooler condensed water, some release steam, some drain brine into evaporation pans. Streets are causeways of fitted stone and grated metal, raised above the shallows. In open places, long troughs and channels guide water from weeping rock faces into cisterns or industrial baths. The city has existed long enough to look grown into the basin, not imposed upon it. Its edges are rounded, its lower walls stained, its mechanisms repaired and resealed generation after generation.

Life is abundant here, but not plant life. The base of the ecosystem is microbial and fungal: dark felts of heat-loving microorganisms clinging to warm stones, pale filamentous growths in shaded cracks, slick mineral films that coat the insides of slow-moving channels. These are grazed by swarms of small creatures: brine flies with mica-bright wings, translucent shrimp-like swimmers, flat white crabs with porcelain shells, segmented crawlers that move along the wet crusts, and long eel-bodied things that slip through warm runnels with ceramic-smooth scales. Larger animals stalk them: heron-like waders with bare throats and heat-shaded eyes, low-bodied amphibious hunters that breathe through moist skin, pale serpents that swim silently through shallow pools, and broad-winged scavengers that ride thermal columns above the basin. Some creatures seem almost mineral themselves, their shells crusted with salt, their movements slow and careful, as if they too have learned the basin’s patience.

The ecology moves constantly, but quietly. Flocks lift from the water in loose formations, wings making soft papery sounds. Predators step delicately across crusts that would shatter under heavier weight. Eggs are laid in warm hollows where mineral water keeps them heated. Swarms dim the air for moments, then settle. There is no rustle of leaves, no wind through grass, no creak of branches. Instead, life announces itself in small wet sounds: a foot lifting from slick stone, a shell clicking against another shell, the faint parting of water, a low trill from a hidden throat.

The silence is one of the place’s strangest qualities. For a landscape so wet, so hot, so industrial, it is remarkably quiet. The porous mineral crusts absorb sound. Steam and humid layers scatter it. Machines are engineered to run low and slow, muffled by water jackets and stone chambers. Wheels are wrapped, valves balanced, pipes curved to avoid shrieking pressure. Even the animals seem adapted to quiet, as if loudness would crack the bright stillness. When sound does appear, it is intimate and near: a drip from a ledge, the whisper of condensation sliding down a wall, the distant throb of a pump felt more than heard, the faint tick of cooling metal.

The world operates by rules shaped by water, heat, and mineral deposition. Nothing green can root here; the surface is too alkaline, too salty, too hot, too constantly sealed by crust. Water is everywhere but rarely simple to use: some pools are nearly fresh from condensation, others are bitter with dissolved minerals, others hot enough to scald. The city survives by sorting waters, moving them, cooling them, and harvesting the cleanest condensation from towers, stone veils, and high terraces. Anything left still too long becomes coated in pale crust. Channels must be cleared. Pipes must be descaled. Surfaces must be scraped. Maintenance is a continuous, quiet labor.

There is something both post-life and intensely alive about the place. It has no green softness, no ordinary pastoral comfort, yet it teems. It is a mineral marsh-city, an industrial basin of weeping rocks and silent engines, bright enough to hurt the eyes, wet enough to mirror the sky, hot enough to make the horizon breathe. The scale is open and regional, the presence of inhabitants scattered, the emptiness as important as the structures. Everywhere there is motion without uproar: water moving, heat rising, animals feeding, machines turning, crust forming, rain gathering, light striking wet stone. It is strange, settled, active, and almost entirely hushed.
