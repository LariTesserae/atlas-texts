It is a high, warm, wet city of stone and iron, built on a plateau so broad that its own weather gathers over its rooftops and hangs there in slow sheets.

The first impression is of water without rain. The air is full of it, but not as storm or downpour. It arrives as condensation, as warm mist, as weeping from rock faces, as thin ribbons of runoff trickling down walls that have been wet for centuries. Gutters run constantly. Corners shine. Every low place holds a shallow pool, not deep enough to be a lake, not temporary enough to be a puddle. The landscape feels recently touched by water everywhere, though the water is never dramatic. It seeps. It beads. It gathers on horizontal surfaces and leaves slow trails down vertical ones.

There are no plants.

No grass in the cracks, no vines on the walls, no trees above the streets. The place is mineral and animal. The surfaces are basalt, brick, iron, salt-streaked concrete, copper gone dull, ceramic pipes, glass fogged by vapor, and stone so long exposed to moisture that it has developed a dark, polished skin. Where living cover exists, it is not green. It is fungal: pale filaments in shaded joints, leathery crusts in warm crevices, blackish microbial films slicking the sides of canals, powdery growths around vents where mineral-rich water evaporates. These are the primary living surfaces, the base of the place’s food web, but they do not give the landscape the feel of a forest or a meadow. They make it feel like a warm machine slowly being inhabited by patient organisms.

The elevation is high enough to matter. The air is thinner than lowland air, but it is not cold. Heat rises from below through fractures in the plateau, from old geothermal systems, from industrial channels, from dark rock that absorbs the ordinary daylight and releases it slowly. The warmth is not tropical, not oppressive, but steady. It gives the place a bathhouse dampness. Mist does not freeze; it lingers. Pools stay tepid. Stone sweats. Metal is warm to the touch in sheltered places. The sky is usually bright but softened by high cloud, and the light is normal, clear, unexceptional. It does not glow with unnatural color. It simply diffuses through moisture and makes the whole city shine quietly.

The city itself is vast. Not merely a town, not a settlement that can be crossed in an afternoon, but an extensive built landscape with districts, levels, thoroughfares, reservoirs, terraces, rail lines, ducts, and industrial yards. It spreads across the plateau in long bands and stacked layers, following the old drainage of the land. From a distance it looks like a natural formation that has been partially ordered: ridges of rooflines, cliffs of retaining wall, canyons of service lanes, broad basins where water slows and spreads. Up close, the construction is heavy, old, and practical. Nothing here is decorative for its own sake. The architecture is shaped by water, heat, weight, and use.

The buildings are terraced into the elevation. Lower levels sit near the saturated ground, where the floor is often wet and channels carry slow water between structures. Middle levels are dense with workshops, storage vaults, roosting galleries, condensing towers, and stacked habitation blocks. Upper levels catch the air. They are fitted with broad mesh screens, angled louvers, and iron combs that pull moisture from moving fog. Water runs down from those screens into troughs, then into pipes, then into cisterns, then into the lower city, then into treatment pools, then back up again through pumps and heated lines. The whole place is organized around the movement of warm water.

Industry is everywhere, but it is not frantic. The technology is mechanical, electrified, heavy, and slow. There are turbines set into channels where water descends through stone throats. There are pumps with green-black iron casings, their surfaces beaded with moisture. There are boilers and heat exchangers warmed by geothermal circulation rather than fire. There are cables strung between poles, insulators glazed with condensation, junction boxes mounted high to escape the wet. There are rails, hoists, sluice gates, fans, pressure valves, chimneys releasing pale vapor, and long sheds whose interiors are filled with moving parts. Some machines still operate with obvious purpose: grinding mineral crusts, lifting water, turning slow drums, pressing salts into blocks, driving electric generators. Others seem half-absorbed into the landscape, their functions blurred by time, their housings colonized by fungal felts and nesting animals, their gears moving only when water pushes them.

The city is not ruined. That is important. It is weathered, yes. Settled. Worn smooth in the places where bodies and water have passed for a long time. But it is in balance. The edges of stairs have been rounded by countless feet and claws. The iron railings have lost their sharpness to rust and mineral deposit. The stone walls have acquired a soft, eroded look, as if the city has been standing in this warm moisture for ages and has stopped resisting it. There is no sense of recent collapse. There is no fresh rubble, no emergency, no abandonment panic. The place has simply become what it is.

And what it is, above all, is inhabited.

Fauna overwhelms it.

Not as monsters, not as a plague, but as a fact of density. Life is packed into every usable space. The city’s ledges, ducts, beams, arches, tanks, gutters, and hollow walls are occupied. There are roosts along every sheltered horizontal surface. There are nests cemented into corners with mud, saliva, mineral crust, and chewed fiber harvested from fungal growths. There are burrows in soft brick, tunnels in old insulation, colonies in abandoned machinery, egg masses clinging to damp pipes. Animals move along established routes with the seriousness of commuters. The air is not empty. It has layers of motion: low crawlers on wet stone, mid-level skitterers along walls, high fliers crossing between towers in loose, streaming bands.

Most of the animals are small, but their numbers make them immense. There are dark-bodied insects with broad wings that shed water easily, rising in slow columns where warm air leaks from vents. There are crustacean-like scavengers in the channels, armored and patient, picking microbial films from submerged stone. There are smooth amphibious creatures with flattened bodies that lie in warm runoff and flick their throats. There are snails and limpet-shaped grazers scraping slick surfaces, leaving clean trails behind them. There are long, eel-like animals in the deeper cisterns, moving through flooded maintenance tunnels. There are birds, or birdlike creatures, in enormous roosting populations: narrow-bodied, damp-feathered, loud, constantly shifting on rails and roof ridges. They are not graceful in any delicate way. They are practical, crowded, and adapted to wet stone.

The fauna does not merely decorate the city. It shapes it. Roosts add bulk to ledges. Generations of nesting material harden into architectural crusts. Burrowing changes the internal structure of walls. Dung and discarded shells accumulate in pits, become nutrient beds for fungi and microbial mats, which in turn feed more animals. The city has become an artificial reef of warm wet surfaces, and the animals are its living tissue. Their presence is near-touching everywhere. If you stand still, something will soon move near you: a claw taps inside a pipe, wings brush a grate, a body slides in a dark gap, a call sounds from a vent. The air feels populated even when no individual animal is visible.

The soundscape is loud, but not in the way of a single sharp noise. It is loud as a sum. Water is always present: dripping, trickling, rushing through gutters, falling into pools, hissing over warm metal, pooling and overflowing with slow blubbers. Beneath that is the industrial layer: the low throb of turbines, the hum of electrified lines, the groan of old pumps, the periodic clang of a gate adjusting, the creak of fans turning in damp air. Above and through all that is the animal noise: calls, chirps, croaks, whistles, rasps, wingbeats, the dry clatter of beaks or mandibles, the soft collective rustle of thousands of bodies shifting at once. No one sound dominates. The loudness is environmental. It surrounds you.

The pace is slow.

This is not a place of sudden events. Water moves through it in long cycles. Machines turn at rates that make their motion hard to see from one moment to the next. Animals have long roosting periods, long molting intervals, slow seasonal swells in population, slow migrations between upper and lower levels as condensation patterns shift. The city does not bustle. It accumulates. Changes happen by deposition: mineral crust thickens a pipe, a nest becomes a ledge, a ledge becomes a shelf, a channel narrows by fractions, a walkway rises under compacted debris. Even erosion is gentle here. It does not tear; it rounds. It does not destroy; it adjusts.

Because the place is so vast, it has zones.

Near the lowest basins, the ground is marshy but mineral. There are no reeds, no sedges, no green fringe. Instead there are wide flats of wet stone, shallow terraces of warm water, and dark microbial mats that look like spilled oil under normal daylight. The air there is thicker, warmer, and more crowded. Insects rise from the water in dense clouds. Grazers move across the slick surfaces in slow herds. Larger animals wade or swim through the channels, not with urgency, but with the confidence of creatures that have no predators capable of surprising them in such crowded space. The smell is not foul, but strong: warm stone, dissolved salts, wet animal bodies, fungal growth, faint sulfur, and the clean metallic scent of electrified air.

Above those basins rise the residential and industrial tiers. Here the city is dense in a more built sense. Structures press close together. Walkways overlap. Stairs climb in long switchbacks. Pipes run horizontally along walls in bundles. Doors and openings are placed not for views but for shelter, drainage, and access. Many openings are enlarged by use, their edges worn into smooth ovals. Some streets are half-covered, becoming tunnels where condensation drips from ceilings and animals move in shadowed streams. The density is layered: creatures above on beams, creatures below in drains, creatures in the walls themselves. The volume is used. Nothing is left empty if it can be occupied.

Higher still are the condensing districts. These are the most open parts of the city, but also among the most active. Tall frames hold mesh and fins. Fog passes through them and becomes water. The collected moisture runs into troughs with a continuous tinkling sound. The structures here are lighter, more skeletal, but they are not clean. They are streaked with deposits, wired with old cables, dotted with nests, and inhabited by animals that prefer the windward side: quick fliers, sunning insects, predators that wait where prey must pass. The light is brightest here. Normal daylight reflects off wet metal and pale stone. When the wind shifts, the mist briefly thins, and the city becomes visible for immense distances: roof after roof, terrace after terrace, basin after basin, all the way to the horizon.

The rules of the world are simple and firm.

Water must be guided, not stopped. Every surface is angled or grooved. Every threshold has a channel. Every machine is mounted above the wettest line unless it is meant to be submerged. Heat must be distributed. Warmth rises from below and is moved through pipes and ducts to keep the city above the chill of altitude. Space must be shared. Animals do not avoid the built environment; they negotiate it. They use heat vents, nest in machinery, follow water flows, and occupy the same routes so persistently that those routes become worn smooth. Food is not grown in fields. It is scraped, filtered, cultivated in damp corners, harvested from fungal crusts, caught in the air, or produced indirectly by the industrial chemistry of the city. The place runs on mineral energy, thermal gradients, electric power, and biological recycling.

There is no wilderness outside the city in the usual sense. The plateau continues in the same condition: high, wet, warm, plantless, eroded into long forms, crossed by slow water. The city is not an intrusion upon it. It is an intensification of it. The built structures create more surfaces, more heat, more shelter, more food, more niches. That is why the fauna is overwhelming here. The city multiplies life by multiplying edges.

The strangeness of the place is moderate. It is unusual, but not impossible. A visitor would understand it gradually, not all at once. First: wet stone. Then: no plants. Then: the size. Then: the machines. Then: the animals. The realization comes slowly that the city is not dead, not empty, not waiting for its original inhabitants to return. It has become complete in another way. Its purpose has shifted from serving people, if it ever did, to supporting a dense, warm, wet, mineral ecology. The industry remains because the industry maintains water and heat. The animals remain because the structures provide space. The structures remain because the water, heat, and animals preserve them in their slow cycle of wear and repair.

At times, the whole landscape seems almost still. The light changes very little. The mist thickens and thins. Pumps sound in the distance. Birds shift on the rooftops like a single body. Water collects on a lip, swells, falls. A machine turns once. A fungal crust expands by a fraction. A generation of insects rises, feeds, and is eaten. Nothing hurries. The place has the patience of established erosion. It has already passed through its violent phases. What remains is a settled condition: a vast, loud, wet, warm, high urban ecology with no green life, no silence, and no urgency.

If you walk through it, you feel observed without being threatened. You are one more body in a dense volume. Your boots find traction on stone polished by water and claws. Your clothes dampen. The air tastes mineral and warm. Sounds come from every direction, softened by moisture but not muffled. Everywhere you look there is use: a gutter used as a feeding path, a duct used as a roost, a broken fan used as a perch, a wall used as a nursery, a factory used as a cliff, a city used as a habitat.

It is not beautiful in a gentle pastoral way. It is beautiful as a system that has found its form. A wet highland machine. A plantless metropolis. A warm mineral reef at altitude. A slow, loud, living city of water, stone, industry, and animals, established beyond the need for sudden change.
