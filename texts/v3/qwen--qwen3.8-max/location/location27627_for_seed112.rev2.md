You stand on a high, dry, living plateau where there is no water in any ordinary sense: no river, no lake, no dew, no rain, no moisture clinging to the air. The absence is not emptiness. It has been filled by plant life so complete that the world itself seems made of vegetation, though the vegetation has learned to exist without wetness.

The terrain is a continent-wide tableland of compacted root-felt, mineralized bark, fallen leaf-sheaths, resinous sediment, and pale saline crusts. It is high enough that the light feels sharpened. The sky is a bright, thin, almost bleached blue-white, and the sun strikes the landscape with a clean, hard clarity. The air is cool, dry, and restless. It carries no smell of water. Instead it carries dust, pollen, powdered leaf-glass, resin vapors, ozone, and the faint metallic sharpness of electrostatic discharge.

This is an established place. Its forms are not new. Eons of dry wind and drifting particulate have worn everything into settled shapes: broad swellings of root-mat, rounded ridges of hardened cellulose, shallow basins filled with powdered leaf-mineral, long dunes of yellow pollen, escarpments of compressed plant-fiber polished smooth by grain-winds. The erosion is not violent anymore; it is the patient erosion of a world that has remained in its own state for a long time. The land looks weathered, balanced, and permanent, even while it roars.

The vegetation is total, but sparse in its presences. The ground itself is alive in a broad, slow way: a continuous crust of creeping photosynthetic tissue, buried rhizome networks, mineral-secreting mats, and dry fungal-like plant symbionts. Yet the large standing forms are widely spaced. Kilometers may pass between the greatest organisms. Between them there is open ground, pale and bright, crossed by wind-lines and drifting veils of spore-dust. The sparseness gives the landscape a sense of immense scale: each major plant-form becomes a landmark, a dark vertical fact against the luminous distance.

Those great plant-forms are unlike ordinary trees. They are tall, hollow, tower-like organisms, some resembling organ pipes, some resembling chimneys, some resembling clustered industrial stacks grown from lignin and glassy leaf-scale. They do not need water. Their chemistry is anhydrous, built on resins, oils, waxes, dry salts, electrolytic gels, and photochemical reactions that would seem more at home in a battery than in a leaf. Their surfaces are coated with pale reflective scales that keep them cool in the bright high sun and shed abrasive dust. Their trunks are fluted, ribbed, and vented. Wind passes through them. Light enters their translucent panels. Their interiors hum.

This is where the landscape becomes industrial without being built. There are no constructed structures here: no walls, no roads, no machines assembled by hands, no abandoned factories, no artifacts of any kind. Yet the living systems operate with industrial intensity. The great hollow plants are processing organisms. They draw dry minerals from the root-crust, separate salts, concentrate metals, generate electrical charge, refine resins, and manufacture staggering quantities of seeds, fibers, spores, oils, and protective shells. Their bodies are full of internal conduits, pressure chambers, vibrating membranes, chemical bladders, heat-exchange vanes, and rotating or oscillating seed mechanisms. They look grown, but they behave like engines, refineries, and production lines.

Their crowns are especially loud. Some are crowned with stiff, curved leaves that act like turbine blades, turning slowly in the wind with a deep periodic whomp. Others have clusters of dry seed capsules that strike one another like metal percussion. Some release spores in sudden pressurized blasts, sending white plumes hundreds of meters into the cool air. Others have taut, wire-like tendrils that sing in the wind, producing high, keening tones that cut through the lower roar. The plants themselves generate electricity. Charge gathers in their dry outer tissues and discharges in bright, snapping filaments between spines, especially when pollen storms build static in the air. At times the upper canopy of a distant tower flickers with small blue-white arcs, like a natural electrical plant working without rest.

The sound is deafening. It is not one sound but a layered industrial atmosphere: the continuous rush of dry wind over vast rough surfaces; the hollow booming of tower-stems; the grinding and clicking of seed mechanisms; the percussive rattling of hard leaves; the electric crackle of discharge; the drone of airborne fauna; the soft but constant hiss of pollen and dust moving across the crust. In sheltered hollows the noise can become a physical pressure. In open stretches it is a wall of vibration. There are brief lulls when the wind shifts, but they only make the next wave of noise feel larger.

Fauna is abundant, though not gentle. The animals have adapted to the dryness by taking fluids from living tissues: resinous plant oils, waxy body fluids, oily nectars, electrolyte-rich secretions. There are vast grazing herds of low, armored creatures that crop the living crust with broad grinding mouths, moving slowly across the root-mat like dark knots. Their feet are wide and padded, distributing their weight over the brittle surface. They make deep chest-resonances that add to the noise, low calls that travel through the ground. Above them, the air is full of winged forms: swarms of translucent insects with stiff, clicking wings; larger gliders that ride the thermal pulses from the plant towers; filter-feeders that swim through pollen clouds; scavengers that pick apart shed bark, broken seed-casings, and the dry remains of smaller animals.

Some of the fauna behaves almost like part of the landscape’s machinery. There are pollinators with metallic-looking bristles that collect charge as they move, discharging it when they touch receptive floral structures. There are seed-carriers that collect hard oily seeds in body pouches and scatter them while fleeing from electrical bursts. There are small creatures that live inside the vents of the tower-plants, feeding on waste heat and chemical byproducts, emerging in coordinated clouds when internal pressure changes. Nothing here is domesticated or engineered in the sense of made things, but the relationships are so dense and functional that the ecosystem resembles a self-running plant-factory.

The scale is vast enough that the landscape creates its own weather. Not rain, never rain. Instead: pollen fronts, dust squalls, spore storms, electrostatic waves, heat shimmers from sun-struck towers, and dry fog made of fine resin particles that glows brightly when the sun passes through it. Clouds of airborne particulate form over dense clusters of plant towers and drift across the plateau, occasionally collapsing into sudden downfalls of seed or fiber. These events are turbulent and chaotic. A calm bright hour can become a white roaring squall of pollen in minutes. The air thickens. The light turns diffuse. The sound rises from a roar to a shaking pressure. Then the storm passes, leaving the crust dusted with fresh yellow or gray powder, and the towers continue their work.

The turbulence is not destruction for its own sake. It is the visible expression of production. The place is constantly shedding, releasing, charging, vibrating, grinding, and regenerating. Old leaves shatter into mineral flakes. Seed-pods burst. Bark panels split and fall away. Vents clog, then clear with explosive pops. Electrical discharges ignite brief flares of resin vapor that burn blue and vanish. Swarms rise, crash, reform. Herds shift in response to sound and vibration. The landscape is hyperactive, but it is also ancient. Its chaos has settled into a pattern. Its violence is routine. It has found a stable state of perpetual industrial turmoil.

Because the density is sparse, the distances feel enormous. From one great tower to the next, the ground may be open, pale, and almost barren in appearance, though the crust beneath is alive. Travel through the landscape would feel like moving through a bright, cool, deafening emptiness punctuated by colossal living machines. The towers would be visible long before they were reached. Their noise would arrive first: a low mechanical hum, then a layered roar, then the sharp percussion of seed mechanisms and the crackle of static. As you approached, the air would grow warmer from their metabolic processes, drier, more charged, more filled with drifting particles.

There are no signs of construction. No road leads to the towers. No platform is laid beneath them. No pipes are attached from outside. Every conduit is internal, every mechanism grown, every industrial process contained within living tissue. This is one of the place’s strangest qualities: it looks untouched, but it does not feel primitive. It feels optimized. It feels productive. It feels like a factory civilization that never built anything because it became industrial through biology.

Light defines the place. The brightness is severe because of the high elevation and lack of atmospheric moisture. Shadows are crisp. The reflective leaf-scales glitter across the distance. Pollen clouds turn into luminous veils. Electrical sparks are visible even in daylight as small blue scratches in the air. The pale ground bounces light upward into the undersides of towers, revealing their internal vanes and hanging fiber-bundles. At times the whole landscape seems to shine with a cold, dry, operational brilliance.

The coolness keeps it from becoming a furnace. Despite the bright light and the internal heat of the plant engines, the thin air carries warmth away. The wind is constant and often cold. Surfaces that face away from the sun can feel almost frigid. The organisms use this thermal contrast: warm interiors, cool exteriors, day-side charge, night-side discharge. The temperature gradient helps drive circulation within the towers, moving dense oils and electrolytic fluids through their bodies without water.

At night, if night comes here, the noise would not stop. It might change. The towers would cool, their structures contracting with deep knocks and groans. Electrical discharge might become more frequent as charge redistributes. The bright sky would darken, but the landscape would still be lit by biological sparks, slow red heat from seed-chambers, and the faint luminescence of chemical vents. The fauna would not fall silent. Nocturnal grazers would move through the dark crust, their calls vibrating through the root-mat. The world would remain loud, dry, and awake.

The rules of this place are severe and clear. There is no free water; thirst here means a lack of compatible solvents, not a lack of rain. There are no built things; anything that resembles technology is grown, shed, consumed, or recycled by living systems. There is no quiet; sound is structural, produced by wind, growth, seed release, animal movement, and electrostatic tension. There is no stillness; the landscape is always producing, discharging, and dispersing. And there is no smallness; even the sparsest patch of open ground belongs to a continental living mass with weather of its own.

The result is a landscape that feels both untouched and industrial, both empty and overproduced, both ancient and violently active. It is a high, bright, cool, waterless world of total vegetation: a sparse continent of living machinery, roaring under a hard sky.
