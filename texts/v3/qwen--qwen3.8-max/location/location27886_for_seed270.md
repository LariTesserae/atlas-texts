There is a hidden hollow below the low salt plain, a pocket set down in the ground as if something heavy had once pressed its thumb into the world and then been forgotten. It is not a valley and not quite a cave. It is a small, contained basin, a sunken room of mineral dust and old industrial stone, cool and dry and loud. The entrance is narrow: a crack between warped slabs of fused brick and slag, barely wide enough to slip through sideways. Beyond it, the place opens into a shallow interior no larger than a hall, low enough that the air feels settled and close, though never damp. There is no water here. Not in pools, not in seepage, not in mist, not in the memory of rain. The world has been stripped of that comfort entirely.

Normal light enters through fissures in the buried roof and through gaps in corroded vent shafts. It is ordinary light, pale and grey-white, unremarkable in color or behavior. It falls in blunt shafts across the basin and reveals the place without mercy: a dry mineral landscape with no plants, no leaves, no green stems, no moss, no soft living carpet. Everything is hard, worn, and crowded.

The ground is a nearly flat pan of powdered salt, crushed ceramic, oxidized metal fragments, glassy slag, and fine bone-white grit. It is ancient in the way of places that have already passed through ruin and come out the other side. Erosion has not been dramatic here; there are no floods, no storms, no glaciers. Instead, time has worked by dust, by vibration, by the endless rasp of small bodies and drifting mineral powder. All sharp edges have been softened. Old industrial shapes have been worn down to smooth shoulders and rounded stumps. The place feels settled into its final form: not freshly abandoned, not slowly collapsing, but already arrived at a stable, patient end-state. It is old beyond use, yet not still.

It is dense with presences.

The floor itself seems to shift. It is not bare mineral but layered with life that has no need of water or vegetation. Pale salt-fleas move in quick, jerking colonies across the crust. Black beetles with flattened bodies push through drifts of powdered brick, their elytra scuffed to dull shine. Long, legless grubs lie half-buried in the dust, waiting for vibrations. Mites cluster in rust-colored patches wherever iron remains exposed. In places the ground appears to breathe because so many small bodies are moving at once, each one adjusting to heat, charge, or the approach of predators.

The walls are no emptier. They are lined with roosting animals. Dry-winged moths or moth-like creatures hang in overlapping rows, their bodies slim and angular, their wings folded tight like sheets of cut mica. They are not beautiful in any gentle way. They are numerous. Their thoraxes carry faint metallic sheens, and when they shift, the wall seems to ripple. Between them crawl long-bodied things with too many legs, pale and deliberate, threading through gaps in the old masonry. Every crack, every pipe, every recessed bolt-hole has been occupied. The pocket is not inhabited; it is filled.

The air above the basin is layered too. Swarms hang in loose columns, moving in slow spirals around heat sources and electrical points. Some are tiny gnats, no bigger than grains of sand, making a high thin sound as they beat their wings. Others are heavier, beetle-winged, clicking when they collide. They do not fly like creatures from a green world. Their movement is intermittent, pulsed, tuned to currents of warm air and static charge. When a machine below shudders into a new rhythm, the swarm changes shape all at once, folding inward or scattering against the ceiling.

The noise is loud and nearly continuous.

It begins as a low industrial drone: old motors, transformers, buried fans, and distant engines still doing something they were never fully shut down from doing. Over that base tone comes the dry rain of claws and shells on metal, the papery rasp of wings, the ticking of cooling parts, the sudden crack of stressed stone, and the chittering calls of animals warning one another. Some creatures stridulate by scraping ridged legs across hardened wing-cases. Others drum on hollow pipes. The loudest sound is not any one animal but the combined motion of thousands, a sound like a factory full of loose paper and small tools being shaken in slow circles.

There is no plant life. Nothing photosynthesizes in the ordinary way. Nothing flowers. Nothing roots itself in soil and reaches upward. The base of the place’s ecology is stranger and simpler.

On metal surfaces, especially near old terminals and leaking cables, there grows a black varnish-like film. It is not a plant. It is not even quite like fungus. It is a dry, chemolithotrophic crust, thin as paint, feeding on electron gradients between dissimilar metals, on sulfates, on ancient hydrocarbons locked in grease seams. Where electrical current leaks, the crust thickens and becomes faintly warm. It flakes at the edges into waxy black particles, and those particles are grazed by the smallest fauna. In turn, those grazers are eaten by larger hunters, and the dead are broken down by still smaller mouths. The ecosystem is a closed, dry loop of mineral chemistry, old industrial residue, and animal bodies.

Water would be a disruption here. The creatures are sealed and economical. Their cuticles are tight, their breathing pores controlled, their metabolisms built around conserving internal solvents.
