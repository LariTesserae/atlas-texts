This is a high, warm wetland that has become almost entirely its own living substance. It lies elevated above the lower world, but altitude here does not mean cold or bare stone. The air is thick, humid, and slow, holding heat the way wet wool holds warmth. The land is lifted into a dim sky, yet it feels enclosed, as if the whole place has grown a ceiling of vapor and leaf over itself. There is no sharp horizon. Distance softens into green-gray mist, into layers of hanging root, low cloud, and saturated air.

The terrain is ancient beyond easy comprehension. It has been weathered down, settled, and stilled until it has reached something like its final shape. Once there may have been mountains here, or ridges, or cliffs, but those have been worn away and composted into gentleness. What remains is a vast near-flattened expanse: low swells, shallow basins, long soft rises, and wide depressions where water gathers. Nothing is angular. Nothing feels newly made. Every contour has been rounded by age, by water, and by the patient pressure of living matter growing over and through it. The landscape does not look ruined or eroded so much as completed. It has entered its oldest stable state.

Plants are not merely present here. They are the ground, the architecture, the weather-skin, the machinery of the place. The surface is a continuous living mass: moss, peat, root, stem, leaf, and creeping tissue bound into one another until there is no clear distinction between soil and organism. To step on it is not to step on earth but on a deep, springy, waterlogged felt of vegetation. It yields slightly, then recovers. Press anywhere and moisture rises. Cut into it, if anything could cut it, and you would find layer upon layer of root-lace, compressed leaf-matter, living threads, and dark saturated plant-flesh. The world here is made of green and brown wetness, not stone.

Stone is rare, and when it appears it is old beyond expression. Smooth, rounded, and low, stones emerge like the worn knuckles of the world beneath the plant-skin. They weep. Water beads on their moss-covered faces and trickles downward in thin, interrupted lines. Some are half-swallowed by creeping growth; others are barely visible as hard presences under cushions of liverwort and root. They do not feel like geology so much as relics from an earlier phase of the world, already being digested by the living landscape.

Water is everywhere, but not as open flood. It is near the surface, moving by capillary patience rather than rushing flow. The air itself seems to condense. Fine mist gathers on hairs, on leaf-fringes, on the fuzzy skins of stems. Droplets form, enlarge, and fall into cups of leaf, into hollow roots, into shallow basins of woven plant-fiber. There are frequent small sounds of water arriving: a bead slipping from a frond, a pool filling by sips, a soft tick against stretched membrane. The whole place feels as though it has just rained, or is about to rain, or is remembering rain.

The water table lies close beneath the surface. In low places it breaks through as warm, dark pools, shallow meres, and slow seeps. These pools are often not true lakes but enlarged leaf-pits, root-bowls, or collapsed hollows where plant-matter has thickened into living basins. The water is tannic, soft, and still, reflecting not sky but the green dimness above. In some places it barely moves. In others it travels in threads through the plant-body, guided by slope, root-channel, and the slow suction of thirsty tissues. There are no rivers here in the ordinary sense, only countless delicate circulations.

The warmth is not fiery but constant. It comes from the thick air, from the heat held in the living mass, from shallow biological decay, and perhaps from deep, old warmth still rising faintly through the ancient ground. The temperature seldom changes quickly. Day and night blur into one another under the dim canopy. The place feels feverish in a gentle way: moist, close, and alive, but not violent. Everything is softened by humidity. Nothing cracks. Nothing becomes brittle. Even the air feels plush.

Light is dim. The sky, where it can be seen at all, is a pale smear behind veils of cloud, mist, and vegetation. The upper layers of the plant-world may receive more illumination, but down where the landscape is experienced, the light is greenish, submarine, and diffuse. There are no hard shadows. Instead there are gradients of murk: brighter where a thin place in the canopy allows a weak glow through, darker where root-shelves and hanging growths overlap. Sometimes the dimness is so complete that the eye invents shapes; sometimes a faint luminous sheen on wet surfaces makes it seem as if the world is lit from within its own moisture.

Because the place is vast enough to generate its own weather, it does not depend entirely on the sky above. The living mass exhales vapor. Transpiration from countless leaves feeds a low internal cloud layer, which drifts through the upper hollows and condenses again on cooler surfaces. Weather here is local and slow: fog tides, warm drizzles, brief mist-falls, and long periods of still, saturated air.
