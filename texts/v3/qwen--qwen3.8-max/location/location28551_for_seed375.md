**The Anhydrous Ward**

There is a small city in a dry hollow, and no hand made it.

It lies below the level of the surrounding plain, a low, basin-like depression no larger than a hamlet and shaped inward like the nave of a cathedral whose roof has been mostly removed. The rim around it is sharp and new. This is not an old, worn land. The edges of the pit are still crisp: fractured slabs, angular ridges, thin fins of mineral standing upright where the ground recently split and settled. Nothing here has been rounded by rain or softened by long weather. The place feels newly emerged, as if it has only just been permitted to exist.

There is no water.

That is the first fact of the landscape, and the fact that governs everything else. There are no puddles, no damp stains, no seeps, no riverbeds, no channels carved by flow. The ground is mineral to the point of abstraction: pale salt crusts, white anhydrite plates, brittle translucent crystals, dark glassy veins, iron-dusted powder, and fine ash-like grit that moves in extremely slow drifts when the air changes temperature. The air itself is cool and dry, so dry that it seems to draw moisture out of the idea of moisture. It carries no scent of rain, no memory of wetness. If anything, it smells of cold stone, crushed salt, faint ozone, and the metallic sharpness of dust under static charge.

The light is dim. Not dark, but subdued. A weak sun appears as a pale disk behind veils of suspended particulate, and the sky above the ward has a chalky, washed-out quality, as though the atmosphere has been diluted with powdered mineral. The light that reaches the basin is soft, gray-amber, directionless enough to mute shadows but not erase them. In the deeper parts of the settlement, where overhangs and stacked structures block the sky, a faint secondary glow sometimes appears: a cold luminescence from stressed crystal lattices, or from biological films that emit a dull blue-green light when disturbed. It is not illumination made by devices. There are no devices here. No tools, no machines, no lamps, no worked metal, no artificial mechanisms. Everything that seems built is grown, secreted, accumulated, or arranged by living processes alone.

And yet the place is built.

That is the second strange fact.

The hollow contains an urban form. It has streets, though they are not paved in any familiar sense. It has walls, terraces, ramps, vaulted cells, buttresses, bridges, stair-like terraces, narrow passages, broad empty plazas, and tall thin towers that rise in clustered groups. The construction is layered: low chambers stacked above one another, open galleries riding atop thicker bases, slender arches connecting separated masses, balconies of fused salt and glassy mineral projecting over vacant drops. There is infrastructure in the pattern of the place — avenues aligned with air currents, causeways crossing lower depressions, retaining structures holding back loose talus, funnel-shaped openings that guide dust and gas instead of water, dry canals that serve as channels for drifting particles, plazas positioned where thermal drafts naturally gather and spin.

It looks like a settlement. It feels like a city district that has been compressed into a small, bounded room.

But it is empty.

Not utterly empty. Not dead. Empty in the way a vast cathedral is empty when only a few presences occupy its volume. The ward has a great deal of constructed space and very little occupying mass. Most chambers are vacant. Most streets are unused. Most towers stand with their openings facing the dim air and nothing moving inside them. The density of presences is low: one slow creature may occupy an entire terrace, a single growth front may claim a whole wall, a rare predator may patrol a long stretch of upper gallery and meet nothing for hours. The built environment is dense in structure, but the space between presences is large. There is room enough here for silence to remain intact.

The materials are beautiful and inhospitable.

The lower structures are made of pale evaporite minerals: layered white and gray masses, sometimes fibrous, sometimes satin-smooth, sometimes broken into translucent plates that look like frozen sheets of milk-colored glass. Higher up, the construction becomes darker and more brittle: black vitreous crusts, thin obsidian-like panels, reddish-brown iron-stained lattices, and chalky yellow bands rich with sulfur compounds. Many surfaces are coated with extremely fine dust, so fine that it behaves almost like a fluid when disturbed. Where the dust has gathered deeply, it swallows sound and makes the ground feel soft, though beneath it the mineral surfaces remain hard and knife-edged.

Because the features are young, they are dangerous in a quiet way. Edges have not dulled. Fractures have not been weathered into safe slopes. A fallen slab still has the sharpness of a fresh break. Crystal fins protrude like blades. Stair-like growths are irregular. Handholds are brittle. The terrain does not invite touch. It invites caution.

The vegetation is sparse and unlike ordinary vegetation.

What grows here does not drink water. It is not green in the familiar sense. There are thin black filaments, almost like wire, rooted in cracks where mineral stress provides chemical gradients. There are stiff, pale fronds that look as though they have been carved from salt, their surfaces covered in microscopic facets that orient toward the dim light. There are low crusts of rust-colored growth, dry and papery, clinging to shaded undersides. In a few protected alcoves stand taller forms — slender columns no thicker than a finger, translucent and faintly glowing at their tips, rising in small clusters like ceremonial candles. They do not sway unless moved by air currents. They do not rustle. They are not soft. They are closer to mineral than to leaf, yet they are alive in some slow, austere way.

These organisms do not need open water. Their chemistry is sealed, internal, and alien enough to feel mineral rather than fleshy. They draw energy from light, thermal gradients, electrostatic potentials, and the slow exchange of ions through dry surfaces. Their growth is measured in tiny increments. When one of the pale fronds breaks, it does not bleed. It snaps with a faint, almost inaudible tick and leaves behind a dry, sparkling fracture.

Fauna is rare.

There are very few moving creatures in the ward, and they seem to belong to the silence rather than disturb it. The most common are low, flat-bodied grazers, not quite insect-like, not quite reptilian, more like animated leaves of dull parchment or thin sheets of smoked glass. They move slowly across walls and floors, feeding on microbial crusts and charged dust. Some are so thin that they appear two-dimensional when viewed edge-on. Others have folded membranes that allow them to detach from high ledges and glide in the weak thermal drafts, dropping silently from one terrace to another.

These gliders are prey, though predation here is not dramatic. There are hunters, but they are few and patient. One kind resembles a dark hook-jawed knot of sinew and mineralized cuticle, barely larger than a hand, waiting in the angle between wall and ceiling. It does not chase. It aligns itself with the faint electric fields created by moving dust and drifting organisms, then drops when something passes below. The impact is nearly soundless. The struggle is brief. The predator does not tear flesh so much as crack and absorb. Afterwards, the empty husk may remain lodged in a crevice for a long time, slowly being incorporated into the wall by creeping mineral growth.

There are also things that move too slowly to be noticed as animals at first glance. Some are colonial. Some are more like behaviors than bodies: a shifting patch of surface, a slow opening in a wall, a lattice that extends a few millimeters in one direction over several days and then retracts when the air chemistry changes. The ecology of the ward is not abundant, but it is active. It is an ecosystem operating at a different tempo, with rare encounters and long intervals between events.

The silence is one of the place’s most powerful qualities.

It is not that the ward makes no sound. It is that sound itself seems limited here. The fine mineral powder absorbs vibration. The dry air carries it poorly. The large empty chambers do not echo as expected; instead, they seem to swallow noises and return only faint pressure. The loudest ordinary sound is the whisper of dust moving across a hard surface. Sometimes there is a soft rasping as a grazer drags its body over crystal. Sometimes a brittle flake detaches from a wall and falls, making a tiny click that feels disproportionately large because nothing else is happening. Occasionally, during changes in temperature, the larger structures contract or expand and produce a low, almost subaudible creak, more felt in the bones than heard.

This is not a silent place because it is dead. It is silent because its activities are too dry, too slow, too sparse, and too finely particulate to make much noise.

The weather of the ward is strange and active without water.

There are cycles. The cool air settles in the basin when the outside plain loses heat, creating a shallow pool of denser atmosphere. Then, as weak light warms the rim and the upper surfaces of the towers, thin thermal currents begin to rise. These currents lift the finest dust and carry it through the streets in slow, curling veils. The dust is not smoke and not fog, but it behaves like both. It moves in dry rivers through the avenues, gathers in plazas, spills over low walls, and drains through funnel-shaped openings into lower chambers. This is the ward’s version of flow, its replacement for water.

At certain times, electrostatic charge builds in the drifting particles. The towers become faintly charged. The black filamentous plants orient themselves toward the strongest gradients. The gliders adjust their membranes and ride the fields rather than the air alone. Small silent discharges flicker between spires — not lightning exactly, but pale branching flashes too weak and too brief to illuminate more than a few centimeters of surface. These discharges trigger biological responses. Spore-like particles are released from dry crusts. Certain sealed cells in the walls open by tiny amounts. Some growths extend feeding surfaces. The whole city responds as if the static were a kind of weather-signal.

There are also episodes of collapse.

Because the place is young and brittle, pieces of it fail. A slender fin cracks from a tower. A shelf of salt sheds a plate. An arch that has been slowly growing thinner for years finally breaks and falls into an empty chamber below. These events are small compared to the whole ward, but they are important. They create new surfaces. They expose fresh mineral layers. They open passages that were closed, or seal passages that were open. The urban plan is not fixed. It changes without intention. A route that existed yesterday may be blocked today by a fallen blade of glassy stone. A wall may split to reveal a nested chamber full of still air and faint luminescence. The city rearranges itself through growth and fracture, not through design.

This is what makes the ward feel both built and wild.

It has the order of settlement — repeated forms, circulation paths, layered occupancy, shared orientation, infrastructure-like regularity — but no planner. The order emerges from the behavior of organisms and the physics of the place. Growth follows air currents. Structures strengthen where stress is repeated.
