**The Cistern of Dry Sound**

High above a pale, waterless expanse — a plain of salt, mineral dust, and old ash — there is a small built place that does not belong to the mountain beneath it. It is not carved from the high rock. It is not set upon natural stone. Every surface has been made, placed, joined, sealed, and finished. If you were to pry up one of its floor plates, you would find more making underneath: older brick, older metal, older clay, older seams of bitumen and salt-glass. There is no bedrock here. There is no soil. There is no dampness. There is no root, no burrow, no seed, no memory of green things.

It is small, as such places go — a single bounded volume, cathedral-scale at most. From one end to the other it might be forty paces, perhaps less, and its ceiling is not a vault of open air but a low, crowded canopy of manufactured forms. Yet within those limits it is packed so completely that it feels larger than it is, and also more compressed, as if every cubic handbreadth has been claimed, tuned, filled, and then forbidden to change.

The place is warm. Not hot enough to burn, but warm in the way a kiln remains warm long after the fire has been taken away. The heat is dry and even. It does not rise in currents. It does not pool in corners. It does not shimmer. There are no gradients here, no moving fronts of temperature, no drafts, no invisible rivers of air. The high altitude outside might imply thin wind and cold, but inside the built chamber the air is still, mineral, and uniformly warm. It tastes of copper, fired clay, old salt, and dust that has long since ceased to be dust and has become instead a fine, fixed powder sintered into surfaces.

There is no water.

Not liquid water. Not ice. Not vapor thick enough to call humidity. Not condensation on metal, not damp in mortar, not dark stains where moisture has crept. The chemistry of the place is dry. Its mortars are salt-bound and bitumen-sealed. Its metals are kept from ordinary decay by the absence of wetness. Its clays were fired until they became something closer to stone. Even the air seems to draw no breath from any sea or leaf or living throat. It is a dry mineral atmosphere, stable and ancient.

Light is dim.

There are narrow slits high in the walls, covered with thin sheets of mica or smoky mineral glass, but they admit little brightness. The sky beyond them appears not blue but a dull copper-brown, thickened by suspended mineral haze.
