Below the salt-field there is a buried open country, cool and dry and strangely alive.

The surface above is a pale mineral plain: cracked salt, dust-fine grit, sealed pans, old evaporation terraces long since emptied of any liquid. There is no water there, and no water below. No seep, no drip, no condensation beading on walls. The world has been made without it. What replaces it is not thirst but a different kind of chemistry: wax, resin, oil, salt, static charge, and the slow metabolism of plants that have learned to build geology.

The landscape below is not a cave in any ordinary sense. It is a vast, planted hollow beneath the crust, a subterranean plain or inverted valley held open by enormous living structures. The ceiling is a low sky of packed root-fiber, hardened bark-plates, and translucent salt lenses. Through these lenses, and through carefully maintained shafts lined with mirrors and pale glass, ordinary daylight comes down. It is normal light — noon-white, clear, unsentimental — but because it falls in columns through dry air and drifting spore-dust, it makes the emptiness visible. The light does not glow with fantasy. It simply arrives, measured and real, and shows how large the place is.

It is regional in scale. One can stand on a walkway lashed to a trunk-terrace and see far across the hollow: distant vertical growths, suspended platforms, dark engine-houses, bridges like threads, light shafts standing like pale pillars. The space between is not crowded. Much of it is open air, cool void, and quiet. The presences are scattered — a town here, a grove-engine there, a line of moving fauna crossing a far root-bridge — balanced against long stretches of emptiness.

Everything that would elsewhere be stone or soil is, in some form, vegetation.

The floor is a dry felt of compressed leaves, shed bark, broken stems, seed-cases, and mineralized fiber. In places it is only newly forming: soft banks of debris, raw embryonic platforms, scaffolds of root descending from above to bind the accumulating mass. This is a landscape still coming into being. Some districts are already stable enough for streets and buildings; others are pre-formation zones, all loose fiber, half-grown lattices, and raw organic geology. You can see where the next floor will be, but it is not yet finished. The world is being assembled by growth, accident, and industry at the same time.

The dominant plants are enormous, but they are not trees as a surface-dweller would recognize them. Some grow downward from the ceiling, anchored in the root-mat above, their trunks hanging into the void, their crowns spreading below like inverted canopies. Others rise from the accumulating floor as stiff, columnar thickets. Their surfaces are cool to the touch, waxed, salt-rimmed, faintly metallic. They are not lush in the wet sense. They are dry, fibrous, architectural. Their leaves are plates and blades, some broad and pale, some narrow and dark, all coated in substances that prevent loss and conduct charge. They do not sweat. They do not drip. They creak.

Because there is no free water, the ecology runs on dry and oily substances. Sap is resinous or waxy. Nutrients move in thick lipid compounds, volatile terpenes, and mineral brines locked in crystalline form. The plants draw energy from imported light, from heat differentials, from salt chemistry, and from the mechanical motion of their own moving parts. Some leaves function almost like solar panels; others like static combs; others like valves. The vegetation is not merely alive; it is infrastructural. It behaves like a living civic system.

The built environment is urban, but not dense. Settlements are organized around the great plants and the spaces between them. A single trunk may carry a whole ward: spiral galleries, hanging platforms, drying racks, seed silos, elevator cages, engine rooms. Nearby trunks may be joined by tensioned root-bridges or cable tramways. Streets are made from compressed leaf-plate and bound fiber, their surfaces dry and slightly springy. Railings are living stems trained into shape. Walls are woven root panels, sometimes still growing, sometimes dead and lacquered until they shine like dark horn.

The towns have industry. This is not a pastoral undergarden. There are factories built into hollow boles, long sheds of spinning machinery, kilns that bake resin bricks, presses that crush seed-husks into fuel cakes, mills that grind mineral leaf-fall into usable powder. Engines work without steam: dry-air pistons, seed-oil burners, heated expansion chambers, flywheels, belt drives, triboelectric generators. Copper wires and ceramic insulators run along living bark. Static charge is harvested from the rubbing of fibrous leaves in the thermal winds. In some places the air smells faintly of warm wax, salt dust, crushed plant matter, and electrified metal.

Yet for all its industry, the place remains mostly quiet.

The vegetation absorbs sound. The dry fiber floor muffles footfalls. The machinery is often slow, muffled, and deeply housed. What noise there is tends to be soft: the whisper of leaf-plates adjusting, the low creak of a trunk under load, the distant thump of an engine, the tick of cooling metal, the faint rush of dry spore-haze moving through a duct. Even the urban wards seem to speak in lowered voices. Bells, clicks, and signal chimes are used sparingly, as if the place has learned that sound travels too far and costs too much.

The atmosphere is cool. Not cold, but cellar-cool, steady, with a clean mineral sharpness. There is no dampness. The air feels polished. It carries very fine particles: spores, salt dust, shredded fiber, pollen-like industrial powders. These do not fall like rain. They drift, rise, gather in bands, and settle according to thermal tides. The weather here is not aqueous but mechanical and biological. When the surface above heats the salt lenses, columns of air stir beneath. Warm light creates slow updrafts; shadowed zones create descents. Spore releases happen in pulses. Static builds along leaf edges until the air itself seems to prick. Sometimes a dry storm passes: a rustling wind, a cascade of mineral scales from high crowns, a faint crackle across the combs, then stillness again.

Fauna is abundant, but it is quiet fauna.

The animals are adapted to dryness and silence. Many have waxed shells, felted feet, folded wings, or bodies that store oil rather than water. Grazers move along the trunk surfaces in slow herds, scraping bark-plates or cropping spore growths. They are not loud; their feeding is a soft rasping. Pale insects with broad membranous wings drift between platforms, not swarming but traveling in sparse lines, like commuting commuters. Long-legged predators wait on cable paths and root-junctions, patient and still, their bodies camouflaged as bark or salt crust. There are scavengers in the floor felt, beetles in the engine-houses, mites in the ducts, gliding things that use thermal drafts to cross the open void between settlements.

Predator and prey are part of the motion of the place. The ecosystem is active. Herds shift when certain trunks shed their leaf-plates. Pollinators, or creatures that serve a similar function, move when the industrial groves release their chemical signals. Predators follow. Some animals have learned to feed on waste heat from engines; others nest in the insulation of old machinery; others are drawn to static charge and can be seen clustering near the combs during dry storms. The fauna does not feel separate from the built environment. It has entered the city and become part of its metabolism.

The strangeness of the landscape is not flamboyant. It is the strangeness of a world whose categories have shifted. Plant is substrate. Growth is construction. Industry is horticulture. A building may heal. A bridge may need pruning. A factory may shed its outer skin once a season. A street may be alive enough to require feeding. A machine may become overgrown and must be negotiated with rather than simply repaired.

The rules of the place follow from its dryness and its living material.

Water is absent, almost mythic. Any liquid present is oil, resin, solvent, or bound cellular fluid. To speak of rain is to speak of something alien and dangerous. Salt is structure. Wax is protection. Resin is repair. Static is power. Light is imported and therefore precious, though it is ordinary light, not a supernatural glow. Growth is not wildness; it is the method of building. To make a wall, one trains a root. To make a floor, one lays fiber and lets it bind. To expand a district, one grafts and encourages. To demolish, one starves, prunes, or burns carefully.

The landscape is also in motion because it is incomplete.

New root-scaffolds descend into the void, feeling for support. Debris accumulates where airflow slows. Platforms thicken. Embryonic hills form from compacted leaf-fall and seed-cases. Some areas are closed off while they consolidate; others are opened when the fiber has bonded enough to carry weight
