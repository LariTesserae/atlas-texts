**The Drip Organ**

Below the surface, in a warm hollow no larger than a small cathedral, there is a place where the world is made almost entirely of one plant and almost entirely of noise.

It is not a cave with plants in it. It is a plant that has become a below-surface city.

The floor is a broad raft of living roots, interwoven so tightly that there is no soil beneath them, no bare stone, no dead ground. The roots rise in low terraces, dip into black cisterns, and lift again into dry walkways worn smooth by centuries of careful traffic. In places they are dry enough to walk on without slipping; in others they glisten with condensation and run with thin, deliberate streams. Water does not dominate here, but it is never absent. It appears intermittently: a drip from high leaves, a sudden brief rain that begins nowhere, a pool trembling in a root-bowl, a channel that fills and emptens according to some slow internal weather.

The air is warm, not hot, and damp in a soft, clinging way. It feels breathed. It carries the smell of green sap, wet bark, warm metal, frog-skin, old oil, and the faint sweetness of fermenting pollen. There is no sunlight. The illumination is dim and greenish, coming from sap-lamps in brass cages, from low amber filaments in sealed glass, from the faint phosphorescence of plant veins, and from drifting pollen that glows just enough to make the darkness feel inhabited. Shadows are long but not empty. Things move inside them.

The plant itself is sparse in its large forms. There are no thickets, no crowded undergrowth, no tangled green walls. Instead, a small number of enormous structures occupy the chamber like architectural members. Seven or eight vast canes rise from the root floor, spaced widely apart, each one as broad as a tower and hollow inside. They are ribbed, pale-green and brown, their surfaces polished by touch and moisture, their bases wrapped in old iron bands padded with layers of rubbery leaf. Higher up, the canes open into flared mouths and narrow throats, forming pipes. Above them, the ceiling is a vault of overlapping leaves, translucent in places, thick and leathery in others, their edges curled into gutters, their undersides beaded with moisture.

Between the canes there is space. Much of the chamber is open dark, air, and echo. The place feels sparse because its permanent presences are few and widely separated: one cane, one platform, one pool, one walkway, one suspended factory pod, one stair. But the emptiness is not vacant. It is filled with motion, sound, wingbeats, drips, and animal weather.
