A drowned highland lies here, warm and almost entirely given over to water. It is not an ocean of salt, nor a lowland swamp, but a high inland sea held in a basin newly opened by earth-movement and sealed by fresh volcanic rock. From above, it might seem only a dark green mirror set among raw, sharp mountains. From within, it is a submerged region: a patient, liquid country where the land continues downward and outward under water, thick with life.

The terrain is young. Fault walls drop in clean black steps. Basalt shelves jut into the water with edges still unrounded. Ridges of cooled lava lie in folded ropes and broken spines, their surfaces harsh to the touch. There is little soft soil, because erosion has barely begun its long work. Instead the bottom is made of angular stone, glassy fragments, white pumice banks, mineral crusts, and bright deposits of silica and iron. Some places glitter faintly where hot springs seep from cracks; others are matte with green and rust-colored microbial films. The rock has the look of something recently broken and not yet worn gentle.

Yet the water softens everything. It is warm, mineral-rich, and buoyant, heated by shallow geothermal veins and by the strong high-altitude sun. The lake lies far above ordinary lowlands, near cloud level, where the air would be thin and cool, but below the surface the heat is held. The water does not feel like mountain snowmelt. It feels like a living bath, not hot enough to scald, but deeply warm, carrying dissolved salts, silica, plant sugars, and fine organic mucilage. This gives it a slight thickness, a softness, a sense that the liquid itself is full of substance.

Light behaves normally here, but beautifully. Daylight enters from above as broad sheets and shifting columns. In the morning the upper water turns pale gold; at midday it becomes clear turquoise where the mineral content allows, then deep jade where vegetation thickens. Cloud shadows pass over the surface like slow animals grazing on light. When storms come, the surface becomes a hammered ceiling, but below there is no violence, only a dimming and a faint increase in pressure. There is no need for artificial light, no glow of devices, no luminous machinery. The world is lit by sun, cloud, and water.

The scale is open. The submerged basin extends beyond immediate sight: terraces, drowned gullies, shoals of pumice, submerged ridges, hot spring gardens, and broad green plains of rooted water-plants. One could move through it for days without seeing the same feature twice. It is not a small pool, not a narrow cave, but a regional landscape, a valley-sized water-world with many districts and moods. In some places the bottom is shallow enough that light touches every stone. In others it falls away into cooler green darkness where the youngest faults disappear into depth.

Vegetation dominates the place. It is lush to the point of saturation. There are no constructed structures, no walls, no docks, no tools, no artifacts of any kind; the only architecture is biological. Plants and microbial life have filled every available volume.

Near the bottom, dark rock is coated with living skin. Cyanobacterial mats spread in emerald, olive, and copper shades, holding mineral grains in place. Around warm vents, frond-like growths rise in soft plumes, their tissues reinforced with silica. Rooted aquatic plants grip the fresh lava with tough, cord-like roots, slowly working into cracks and widening them over years. Their stems are flexible, black-green, adapted to pressure and current; their leaves are broad ribbons or finely divided filaments, depending on the flow of water around them. Some carry small gas bladders, pale and translucent, that pull their upper parts toward the light.

The strangest abundance is near the surface. A living ceiling of floating vegetation hangs just beneath the water’s skin. Broad leaves interlock with buoyant stems and bladder-bearing plants, forming a discontinuous roof of green. From this roof, roots descend in long curtains, sometimes for meters, swaying almost imperceptibly. These hanging roots trap drifting particles, host algae, and create a dense inverted forest. Looking upward from below, one sees a layered canopy, broken by gaps where light falls in slats, with roots and leaves silhouetted against the bright surface. It is a forest growing downward into water, not upward into air.

Between bottom and canopy, the midwater is crowded. Detached leaves drift in slow spirals. Spores, pollen, plant fragments, and microbial flocs hang like pale snow. Pumice grains, buoyant and porous, float at odd levels, caught in films of organic slime. Bubbles rise from vents and decaying matter, but they do not always ascend cleanly; they catch in root threads, gather in silver beads, and form temporary columns that vanish when disturbed. The water is dense with presences. There is no empty blue void. Every volume seems occupied by something growing, drifting, clinging, filtering, or slowly dissolving.

The density is not chaotic, but layered. One can almost read the water by strata: mineral haze near the vents, plant-mote clouds in the middle distances, root-tangles near the floating meadows, and dark benthic carpets below. Movement through the place becomes intimate. There is always something near enough to touch: a leaf edge, a root fiber, a veil of algae, a rough stone face furred with new growth. The landscape does not stand apart for observation; it presses close.
