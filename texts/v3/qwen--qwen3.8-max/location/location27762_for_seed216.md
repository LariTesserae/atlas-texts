**The High Lattice Marsh**

At first, the place seems almost empty because it is so large and because most of it is air.

You stand on a wide, springy terrace of woven root and living moss, high enough that the world below has become a slow sea of warm cloud. The light is dim and green-gray, filtered through layers of mist, upper fronds, and vast translucent membranes of stretched leaf-skin. There is no direct sun. There is only a diffuse brightness that seems to come from the humidity itself, as if the atmosphere has been steeped too long in pale tea. The air is warm, thin, and wet. It feels breathed. It clings to skin, to bark, to fiber, to everything.

This is a landscape made entirely of constructed life.

There is no raw stone here, no untouched soil, no natural bedrock. Every surface has been seeded, trained, woven, pruned, hollowed, grafted, or coaxed into shape over ages so long that the distinction between building and growing has worn away. The terrain is plant-as-architecture: terrace floors of interlaced roots; buttresses of living wood; columns swollen like cisterns; bridges of braided cane that curve into fog; hanging gardens that serve as weather-screens; vaulted halls formed from the inverted interiors of ancient trunks. Even the cliffs, where cliffs appear to be, are layered walls of compressed bark, aerial roots, and resin-hardened fiber. They only look geological because they have been weathered into patience.

Water is everywhere, but not as flood. It appears as film, bead, thread, veil, and pool. The atmosphere condenses constantly. Moisture forms on waxed leaves, gathers along grooved stems, slides down fibrous channels, and trembles at the edges of marshy shelves before falling into the hollows below. After every mist-tide, the whole structure weeps. Water runs through capillary channels in the walls. It collects in broad leaf-basins. It sits in shallow black pools at the edges of terraces where reed-like plants lean over the surface and drink. In places, the ground itself seems to sweat when stepped upon, leaving footprints that briefly fill with clear, tepid water.

These are the marsh-rims of the height: not lowland bogs, but high, constructed wetlands balanced on open air. The wetter places have soft, sponge-like floors of moss, liverwort, and shredded bark. The drier walkways are corduroyed with old stems, polished smooth by long use. Water stains run down every vertical surface in long mineralless streaks. Nothing is sharp anymore. The place has existed so long in its own state that all edges have softened. Erosion here is not collapse; it is settlement. The landscape has been rounded into balance by drip, mist, warmth, root-pressure, and time.

The scale is difficult to accept from a single terrace.

The lattice extends beyond sight, beyond weather, into its own horizon. It is vast enough to have local climates: rain shadows beneath upper canopies, warm updrafts inside vertical shafts, cold pockets where mist pools and refuses to move, windless chambers where spores hang motionless for days. Some of the hollows are so wide that clouds form inside them. Distant structures appear as faint vertical smudges through the haze, like towers seen through water. The world does not feel planetary in the ordinary sense; it feels continental, constructed, and suspended. It has its own pressure systems, its own condensation cycles, its own quiet thunder of shifting tension in living wood.

Yet for all its size, the volume is sparse.

Density here is low. The landscape is mostly interval. A person, an animal, a lit spore-lantern, a hanging bridge, a fruiting wall—these are events separated by enormous stretches of humid emptiness. You can walk for a long time and see nothing but root, mist, and the faint green glow of old growth. The built environment is not packed solid; it is a framework around voids. Hollows open beneath floors. Shafts descend through layers of darkness. Arches span distances so wide that the far side seems imaginary. The place gives the impression of a cathedral whose nave has been expanded until it became weather.

Abundance exists, but it is spread so thinly that it feels like secrecy.

Fauna is present in great numbers, yet rarely obvious. Life fills the niches of the living structure: small amphibians with damp, bark-patterned skin rest inside leaf-funnels; flat-bodied insects skim over pools without breaking them; gliding grazers move between terraces on membranes that look like lichen; blind, slow creatures browse the inner root-walls where fungal threads are thick; aerial things with soft wings ride the warm thermals in silence. In the marsh pools there are eel-like cleaners, translucent larvae, and flattened things that resemble wet leaves until they unfold. At certain hours, the air fills with pale insects that rise like steam. At others, a predator passes through a hollow and the surrounding life rearranges itself without making a sound.

The quiet is one of the deepest qualities of the place.

Sound does not travel normally here. Wet vegetation absorbs it. Vast distance dilutes it. The architecture seems to listen. A single drop falling into a pool can feel like an announcement. The creak of a root-bridge under its own weight arrives slowly, like a thought remembered too late. Footsteps make a soft, fibrous hush. Animal calls, when they occur, are low, brief, and often below the threshold of hearing—vibrations in the floor more than noises in the air. Even predator and prey move with restraint. Violence exists, but it is muffled by moss, mist, and scale.

The light remains dim through most cycles.

Above, the sky is a ceiling of cloud and upper vegetation, so shadows are weak and directionless. In darker recesses, the landscape supplies its own illumination: veins of phosphorescent fungus along handholds, seedpods with faint blue interiors, moss that brightens when touched, clusters of glowing spores suspended in still air like slow lightning. The light is never bright enough to reveal everything. It is enough to suggest edges, to make pools look deep, to turn distant walkways into thin green lines. It makes the built world feel inhabited even when no one is visible.

Everything is made, but the making is primitive in the sense of being intimate, manual, and ancient.

No metal rings here. No engines hum. The technologies of maintenance are grafting knives of bone and hardened wood, thorn pins, resin ties, bark cords, woven slings, clay valves, heated sap, stone scrapers, and patient hands. The great works are not machined but cultivated. A wall is not quarried; it is persuaded over generations. A bridge is not merely tied; it is taught where to grow.
