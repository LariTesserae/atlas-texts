**The High Phloem Terrace**

It is loud before it becomes a place.

First there is pressure: a deep, dry chord rising through the feet and into the chest, as if the ground itself were a struck vessel. Then the dimness resolves into distance, and you understand that you are standing on a high, cool, open tableland made entirely of plant matter, with no water anywhere.

Not in pools. Not in clouds. Not in the air as mist. Not in the tissues of the ground as sap in any familiar sense. The Terrace has passed beyond water. Its life is bound in crystalline electrolytes, waxy dielectric fluids, gas-filled lattices, and slow electric turgor. If you carried water here in a sealed vessel, the place would sense it as a violation or a miracle. Root-fields would lean toward it. The towns would sing warning harmonics. Something ancient and administrative would take notice.

The terrain is elevated and open, lifted into thin cool air where the horizon bends softly under a low, muted light. The sky is not truly blue; it is a dim, particulate grey-violet, veiled by high aerosol dust and drifting spore-hazes that never condense into rain. The sun, if there is a sun, is a pale diffusion, more a direction than a source. Shadows are soft and uncertain. In the deepest hollows and beneath the eaves of inhabited stems, the ground itself supplies light: faint amber veins, cold blue capillary lines, slow phosphorescent pulses moving through living tissue like thoughts through a vast mind.

Everything here is vegetation, but not in the sense of plants growing upon a landscape. The landscape is the plant. The ground beneath your feet is a compressed, ancient dermis of interlocked fibers, bark-plates, callus tissue, and mineralized leaf-strata. It is worn smooth by unimaginable time. There are no sharp young ravines, no raw cliffs, no fresh scars. Erosion here has reached a final, settled condition. The forms remaining are old beyond ordinary geology: rounded swells, shallow basins, broad bosses of polished cuticle, low ridges shaped like the backs of sleeping organs. The surface has been abraded by wind, by dry particulate weather, by vibration, by the long pressure of its own history, until it has become almost calm.

This is not a forest in any common sense. The tall forms are sparse, spaced kilometers apart across the Terrace like the remaining notes of a song that once filled the world. Between them lie immense open expanses of living skin: tessellated plates of bark, fields of dormant spore-crust, smooth pale tracts that flex faintly underfoot. The density of prominent presences is low. There is much emptiness. But the emptiness is not dead. It is maintained. It is part of the organism.

The sparse vertical giants are called by many names in the settlements — organ-stems, pillar-husks, resonance trees, city-roots. They rise from the Terrace as hollow, fluted towers of hardened vegetable tissue, some hundreds of meters high, their surfaces carved by ancient wind into acoustic grooves. They are not trees as other worlds know trees. They are architectural organs. Their interiors are chambered, their walls layered with living insulation, their crowns reduced to arrays of tines, vanes, seed-ports, and weather-sensitive filaments. They do not drink. They listen, absorb dim light, harvest electrostatic charge, and stabilize the local fields that make settlement possible.

Around and within these stems are the towns.

They are urban in organization but not in crowding. The built environment here is settlement-scale, deliberate, layered, and infrastructural, yet it remains balanced against the surrounding emptiness. A town is not a collection of separate buildings so much as a cultivated node: a negotiated thickening of the living substrate. Terraces of overlapping leaf-tile grow outward from the base of a pillar-husk. Root-stairs spiral into the bark. Bridges of woven fiber stretch between nearby stems, taut as harp strings. Seed-vaults hang in sheltered recesses. Air-locks made of contracting sphincter-valves protect interior chambers from dust storms. Markets are grown in shallow amphitheaters where sound can be directed without becoming dangerous. Roads are not paved; they are selected pathways of hardened callus, maintained by local growth, marked by low bioluminescent ribs.

The construction is organized, but the technology is beyond anything modern engineering would comfortably recognize.

There are no visible machines in the ordinary sense. No engines of stamped metal, no crude combustion, no exposed gears. Instead, the towns are maintained by biological systems operating at scales and principles that ordinary physics would struggle to name. Leaves function as quantum photonic processors, folding weak light into computational states. Seed-kernels act as gravitic anchors, subtly altering local weight to keep tall structures stable in high wind. Molecular looms in the walls repair damage fiber by fiber. Root lattices perform distributed prediction, calculating weather pressure, acoustic load, and migration patterns days before they arrive. Some of the infrastructure appears inert until addressed in the correct vibration; then a wall becomes a doorway, a floor becomes a lift, a ceiling opens like a pupil.

The built world does not consume energy so much as tune it. It borrows charge from electrostatic fronts, stores light in topological knots within living tissue, and redistributes heat through phase-shifting waxes. The towns are quiet in their mechanisms but loud in their total effect, because every piece of architecture participates in the acoustic life of the Terrace.

Sound is the dominant fact.

The place is loud. Not merely noisy, but physically resonant. Wind passes through the hollow stems and produces long bass notes that travel through the ground faster than through the air. The open bark-plains flex under thermal pressure and emit creaking, drum-like pulses. Seed-pods the size of rooms split and close with percussive cracks. Fauna call across kilometers in infrasonic booms, rapid clicking trills, or metallic harmonic shrieks. Towns answer one another with structured chords, using sound not only for communication but for growth-control, weather-bracing, boundary maintenance, and law.

In many places, the sound pressure is strong enough to be felt in the teeth and ribs. Conversation by ordinary speech is difficult. The inhabitants, whether human, post-human, or something else entirely adapted to the Terrace, rely on hand-sign, bone-conduction masks, touch-signals through railings, and low-frequency codes carried through the substrate. Silence, when it occurs, is not natural. Silence is engineered, expensive, and slightly frightening.

The atmosphere is cool and dry, crisp enough that every sound seems to have a hard edge. The air is thin from elevation but breathable, exhaled and regulated by the living plain. It tastes of resin, dust, ozone, and something like cold metal. There is no humidity, no smell of rain, no dampness in the throat. Instead there is a clean, austere dryness, a sensation of being in a vast sealed instrument.

The dim light gives the Terrace a perpetual late-afternoon quality, though day and night still exist as subtle shifts in pressure, temperature, and sound. During the brighter phases, the spore-haze glows faintly with scattered illumination, and the upper vanes of the pillar-husks open to receive it. During the dark phases, the towns become more visible as networks of soft light: amber veins along paths, blue-green pulses in maintenance channels, red warning rings around unstable resonance zones.

Fauna are abundant, though their abundance is distributed strangely across the open space.

The emptiness is full of movement. Herds of vane-striders cross the bark-plains on stilted, tuning-fork legs, their bodies light and angular, their flanks spread with thin photosensitive membranes. They do not graze on grass, because there is no grass in the ordinary sense. They crop charged spore-crust, lick static from the surface fibers, and absorb weak currents from the living skin. Their movements are nervous and precise. When a predator’s sound approaches, they form circles, membranes angled to deflect or cancel the offending frequency.

Above them move drum-moths, broad-winged creatures with thoracic membranes that beat not only for flight but for signaling. Their wingbeats produce rolling rhythms that can be heard for kilometers. They serve as pollinators, dispersal agents, and living repeaters for the towns’ acoustic messages. Swarms of cymbal-locusts pass in dry clouds, their bodies rasping together so loudly that they generate localized heat. The locusts are followed by piped foxes — slender, reed-boned hunters that emit narrow tonal calls to disorient prey. Their mouths are not wet; they feed through fibrous mandibles and absorb nutrients from compressed biological pellets.

In the deeper basins graze husk-cattle: low, armored animals with broad scraping mouths that remove only the uppermost dead layer of the Terrace skin. Their grazing is part of maintenance.
