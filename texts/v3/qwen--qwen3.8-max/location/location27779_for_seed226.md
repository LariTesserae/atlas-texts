It is a small, high place, more room than region: a wet basin held in the curve of new rock, no larger than a hamlet or the nave of a cathedral if the cathedral had been laid open to the sky. The air is cool and close to water. Rain has just passed, or is always just passing here; every surface beads, seeps, or quietly sheds moisture. Pale stone shines with a skin of wetness, and in the hollows the ground is soft enough to accept a footprint and fill it slowly with clear water.

The terrain is newly come into being, or nearly so. There is little erosion here, no deep gullies, no long-worn valleys, no patient cutting into old shapes. Instead the rock seems embryonic: rounded, sealed, freshly surfaced, as if the land has only just decided to become land. It rises in low shelves and blunt terraces, some natural, some guided by intention. Water is near the surface everywhere. It weeps from joints in the rock, beads on cool faces, gathers in thin marshy margins where sedge and moss make a trembling green hem. In places the atmosphere itself seems to condense, drawing damp out of the moving air and laying it on stone like a second skin.

Yet the place is not wild in the sense of being unworked. It is urban, but an urbanism of beginning: a settlement under construction before it has become crowded. Streets exist as alignments of stone and compacted gravel. Terraces step upward in broad, careful increments. Channels have been cut or coaxed through the ground, carrying water with a slow, deliberate obedience. There are foundation walls, low and thick, marking rooms that have not yet received roofs. There are empty plazas, or what will be plazas: flat, bounded spaces where nothing stands yet except the memory of measurement. The city is present first as infrastructure, as intention, as a pattern imposed on the wet ground.

Because the place is so empty, its built forms seem to occupy more idea than volume. The density is low. A few figures move among the works, small against the open sky and the wide scaffolds. Between the terraces, cisterns, and raised walkways there is vast, hollow space. Sound fills that emptiness and makes it feel even larger. This is a loud landscape, not with voices or traffic, but with water, wood, stone, and resonance.

Water speaks constantly. It drips from weeping rock faces into shallow basins. It runs through wooden troughs and clay-lined channels with a continuous, cold murmur that rises in places to a rushing roar where the gradient tightens. It falls from one terrace to another in thin, silver curtains, striking stone with a hollow, percussive sound. In some parts of the basin the water has been directed into resonant chambers, whether by design or accident it is hard to say; the result is a deep, slow booming that seems to come from the ground itself. The sound is not frantic. It is patient, repeated, immense.

Over this water-noise come the sounds of making. Hand tools strike stone. Wooden mauls thump against wedges. Ropes creak under tension. Simple cranes, built of lashed timber and counterweighted with rock, groan as they lift blocks no larger than a torso. The technology is primitive but precise: split stone, shaped timber, cordage, leather, clay, a little soft metal used sparingly for pins, chisels, and fittings. Nothing here is powered by engines. Everything depends on hands, levers, gravity, water, and repetition. The workers move with the unhurried certainty of people whose construction schedule is measured in seasons and generations, not days.

The vegetation is surprisingly lush for a high, cool, stony place. Water has made greenness possible in every crack and seam. Moss carpets the shaded ledges in thick, cold cushions. Liverworts cling to weeping faces. Ferns unfurl from pockets of accumulated grit, their fronds dark and glossy with moisture. Sedges and dwarf grasses fringe the marshy margins, bending under the weight of droplets. In the wetter depressions, low creeping plants form dense mats, and pale rootlets spread through mineral soil that is barely soil at all. Where wooden scaffolds have stood long enough, algae darkens the grain and moss begins to seal the joints.

This is not a forest. There are few large plants, and the cool elevation keeps growth low and tenacious rather than tall. But it is lush in texture and coverage: a close, wet, living skin over the new stone. The air smells of cold water, crushed sedge, mineral dust, and the green bitterness of damp leaves. It is a clean smell, sharp but not severe.

Fauna is rare. The place does not yet belong to animals. A few insects hover over the still pools, their movements quick and isolated. Small amphibians shelter under wet stones, blinking when disturbed. Occasionally a high bird passes overhead, not lingering. The ecology is mostly vegetal, microbial, aquatic in the smallest sense: life spreading across surfaces, threading through water films, waiting for the landscape to mature into something more inhabitable. The scarcity of animals makes the human figures feel even fewer, and the empty city even more like a work being prepared for future bodies.

The light is ordinary. There is no strange glow, no theatrical color, no supernatural dimness. It is the normal light of a high, cool region after rain: bright but diffused, clouds moving quickly, the sun appearing and disappearing without drama. Wet surfaces reflect the sky in broken sheets. Stone glows pale where it is dry enough to catch the light, dark where water still holds it. The normality of the light makes the place’s oddities more believable, as if the unusual elements have simply been allowed to occur under honest daylight.

One of those oddities is the sense that the landscape is not merely being built but also being formed. The boundary between geology and construction is uncertain. Some walls look stacked; others look grown. Water carrying dissolved mineral has been guided over forms and frames, leaving crusts and rinds that harden into stone. In places, wooden templates have been left in position so long that mineral deposition has begun to incorporate them, turning carpentry into armature. The workers do not seem to oppose this. They work with it. They place stone where stone wishes to accumulate. They cut channels where water already leans. They strike sounding stones and listen to the answer before deciding where to set the next course.

This gives the place a faintly unnatural logic. The city appears to be emerging from its own future. Wet patches on flat rock sometimes trace the outlines of walls that have not yet been raised. Lines of darker damp run along planned streets, as if rain remembers the survey marks better than the masons do. Echoes in the basin arrive with too much clarity, repeating hammer-blows and waterfalls in ways that make the space feel pre-occupied. Nothing here is grotesque or alarming. The weirdness is quiet, procedural, almost administrative: the world behaves as though intention has already soaked into the geology.

The rules of the place seem to follow from that condition. Water comes first. No wall is laid where water refuses to pass; no basin is filled where water refuses to stay. Sound is used to test and settle materials. A stone is not trusted until it has been struck and heard. Silence is treated with caution, because silence may mean a blocked channel, a dry bed, a failing form. The workers speak little while lifting, but they call at set intervals, short loud phrases that bounce across the terraces and return altered. These calls seem less for communication than for coordination with the place itself, keeping human rhythm aligned with the slower rhythm of water and stone.

There are also rules of emptiness. The city is not filled as quickly as it could be. Spaces are left open. Rooms are bounded but unroofed. Streets are wide beyond present need. The builders seem to understand vacancy not as absence but as capacity. The hollow scale of the place is part of its function. It allows sound to move. It allows water to be heard. It allows the settlement to remain legible as a plan before it becomes a habitation.

Movement through the landscape is slow. The ground is wet, the air cool, the distances deceptively short because the emptiness makes every object seem far away. To cross one terrace is to hear boots suck faintly at damp stone, to feel cold seep through soles, to see breath briefly whiten. To climb a stair is to pass from one level of water-sound to another: a drip here, a rush there, a deep resonant throat below. The whole basin changes gradually. Nothing hurries. Mineral crust thickens by fractions. Moss advances by fingertips. Timber darkens with weather. Stone hardens where water has been persuaded to leave its burden.

From a distance, the place might look abandoned. Up close, it is merely early. It is a small urban basin at high elevation, cool and wet, lush with low plants, almost empty of people and animals, loud with water and handwork. It is built with simple tools and patient knowledge, organized by streets and channels before it has houses and crowds. Its geology is still emerging, its streets still being taught to the ground, its architecture still halfway between making and growing.

If one stands in the center and listens, the landscape resolves into a slow, resonant construction: water falling, hammers answering, stone drinking, green life stitching itself into every moist seam. The city is not yet full. It may not be full for a very long time. But it is already loud with the patient noise of becoming.
