## The Drowned Grove-City of High Mere

At the upper end of a cloud-scoured hollow, where the mountain should have narrowed into rock, it instead opens into water.

There is no stone here, no gravel bed, no mineral shore. The high basin is made entirely of living and once-living plant matter: old root masses, compressed leaf beds, peat terraces, woven stems, bark shelves, and the slow, patient architecture of vegetation that has been growing, dying, sinking, and becoming floor for longer than anyone can count. The place is small by the standards of landscapes — a cathedral-sized hollow, a hamlet-sized pool, a bounded room of water and green. Yet it is urban in its order. Channels run where streets should be. Terraces step downward in deliberate curves. Openings in root walls are too regular to be accidents. This is a settled place, an inhabited place, a place that has been shaped by intention over generations, even though almost everything in it is submerged.

The water is the first fact.

It fills the basin from the lower root-floors to the bright, trembling surface above. It is cool, clear, and greenly tinted, thickened with plant essence and fine suspended fibers that drift like slow snow. The temperature is not cruel, but it is unmistakably cool: the kind of cold that makes movement thoughtful, that slows the body, that reminds any swimmer that this place belongs to the water before it belongs to them. At this elevation, the air above the pool is thin and clean, and the light is ordinary daylight — neither magical nor dim — but once it passes through the surface it becomes submarine. Shafts of pale gold fall through the water in slanted columns. Cloud shadows move across them. When rain comes, and it often does, the surface becomes a drumskin, and the whole pool darkens with new water.

From above, the place might look like a high mountain mere caught in a bowl of vegetation. From within, it is a town.

The basin is arranged around a central open stretch of water, wide and empty, with several deeper shafts where roots descend in tangled vertical gardens. Around this central water are terraces: broad shelves of living root and compressed peat, once perhaps used for procession or planting, now softened by long immersion. Some terraces are barely submerged, their upper edges kissing the surface. Others lie deep enough that their outlines are blurred by green distance. The erosion of ages has rounded everything. No edge is sharp. No corner looks new. The water has touched every surface until each form seems to have accepted its shape. This is not a ruin freshly drowned. It is a place that has been underwater long enough for submersion to become its balance.

The built environment is sparse, but not random.

There are dwellings, if one can call them that: hollowed seed-cases the size of rooms, anchored into root walls and opened with elliptical doors. There are platforms of lashed reeds and living stems, some still growing, some long stabilized into woody floors. There are arched passages where water moves from one terrace to another through living tubes of hollow trunk. There are weirs made of woven branches, fish pens of flexible withy, mooring loops of braided root, and narrow stairways cut or grown into the sides of root banks. The settlement is layered. Lower levels are older and more completely drowned, their openings dark, their surfaces furred with moss and small clinging plants. Middle levels are inhabited or maintained. Upper levels break the surface in places, forming low decks, breathing platforms, and perches where waterbirds gather.

The density is low. Much of the place is open water. A swimmer can cross long distances without touching anything, passing between isolated structures as though between islands in a flooded grove. The emptiness is part of the urban character. The city is not a crowd of buildings but a careful arrangement of presences separated by liquid space. Silence, distance, and interval are treated as if they are civic materials. A chamber may be left empty simply because the water there is needed for movement, sound, or current. A terrace may be bare because it is a listening place, or because fish spawn there, or because the old roots beneath it must not be burdened.

Everything is made from plant.

That is the second fact, and perhaps the deeper one.

The landscape does not merely contain vegetation; it is vegetation. The walls are living root systems, braided and thickened by growth. The floors are mats of compressed leaves, peat, and creeping stems. The columns are trunks or great aerial roots, some no wider than a wrist, some broad enough to hold rooms inside them. The ceilings, where they exist, are undersides of interlaced branches or shelves of shelf-fungus-like growths, though even those are more plant than fungus, more alive than dead. Bark has become panel. Reeds have become screens. Broad leaves have become translucent windows in the upper chambers where air remains. Thin vines have become ropes. Fibers have been twisted into cordage. Seed pods have become vessels. Bladders have become floats.

Where other worlds might use stone, metal, brick, or fired clay, this one uses growth.

The technology is primitive in the sense that it is near to the hand and close to the material. Tools are made of bone, shell, antler, hardwood, and sharpened reed. There are simple blades of obsidian or dark volcanic glass where such things have arrived from far below, traded upward through many hands. A little metal exists — a dull copper hook, a small bronze needle, a ceremonially wrapped fish-gig — but metal is rare, inherited, and not produced here. Nothing is cast in fire beneath the water. Nothing is mined. The primary techniques are weaving, lashing, pruning, binding, bending, soaking, and waiting. Many structures are not built so much as guided: a stem is encouraged to arch, a root is trained to close around a frame, a living wall is coaxed to thicken around an opening until the desired shape becomes part of the plant’s own body.

This gives the place its weathered, established feeling. There are no fresh wounds, no raw cuts, no look of sudden construction. Every joint is smoothed. Every lashing is softened by water and growth. Every threshold is worn by slow passage. The city seems to have grown into its present state over long durations. It is not decaying. It is settled. It has become its own condition.

The ecology is abundant.

Because the entire basin is alive, fauna is everywhere, though not in dense, pressing swarms. Life is distributed through niches, layers, and intervals. Small silver fish move in loose companies through the upper light. Larger, darker fish rest in the shaded chambers below, their fins stirring only when current or prey demands it. Eels or eel-like creatures slide between root gaps, using the architecture as both shelter and hunting ground. Crayfish and similar crustaceans patrol the lower terraces, their claws working over old leaf beds. Water beetles glide through open water. Caddis-like larvae build cases from plant fragments and carry them like moving seeds. Newts and salamanders rest in cool hollows, their spotted bodies nearly invisible against mottled bark.

Birds belong to the surface and the air pockets. Dippers and waterfowl appear where the pool opens to sky. Heron-like birds stand on emergent platforms, patient as carved posts. At certain times, swift diving birds pass through the surface with startling force, vanish, and rise again with fish. Insects skim the upper water or hatch in submerged leaf folds. Otters or otter-like mammals may pass through at dusk, moving along the channels with fluid certainty, leaving barely a ripple.

Nothing here is ornamental in a simple way. Every living thing has a role. Fish keep certain growths cropped. Crustaceans clean settled matter from channels. Birds bring nutrients from beyond the basin and carry seeds or fragments away. Insects pollinate emergent flowers. The roots filter the water, oxygenate it, and stabilize the architecture. The inhabitants, if they intervene, do so with restraint. They prune where growth would choke a passage. They open a channel when silt and leaf-fall begin to block it. They harvest reeds in rotation. They encourage the plants that make air, the plants that strengthen walls, and the plants that draw fish. The ecosystem is not untouched, but it is not dominated. It is maintained.

The people of the place, if one encounters them, are few.

They move slowly, adapted to the cool water and the vertical spaces. They may wear simple wraps of woven fiber or cured plant leather. They use reed breathing tubes when working near the surface, and in deeper places they rely on held breath, trained lungs, and knowledge of where air pockets remain. Some chambers still hold air beneath their domed roofs, trapped by the shape of the living structure and replenished by oxygen-giving plants. These air bells are precious, not because the place is hostile, but because they allow rest, speech, tool-work, and fireless gathering. In them, voices sound strange: wet, resonant, amplified by curved plant walls and the water below.

Their settlements are not crowded because their way of life does not require crowds. The basin can support many animals, but it supports only a small number of makers. Too many bodies would disturb the water, foul the channels, overharvest the reeds, or frighten the fish. The urban order is therefore one of spacing. A family or work-group may occupy one chamber and several nearby platforms. Another may live across the central water, visible only as a ripple, a hanging net, a line of drying fibers. The distance between presences is not emptiness but etiquette, ecology, and design.

The sound is loud.

This surprises those who expect submerged places to be mute. Here, water carries sound with astonishing clarity, and the living city is full of it. Rain striking the surface makes a constant, shimmering drumming, sometimes soft, sometimes a wall of noise when storms close the high basin in gray sheets. Water moving through hollow stems produces low organ tones. Current passing over weirs creates hushes and rushes. Roots creak as they shift under slow pressure. Fish make knocking, croaking, or drumming sounds, especially in spawning seasons when they gather in the deeper chambers. Frogs and amphibians call from air pockets near the surface. Birds answer from above. Crustaceans click and scrape. Woven gates tap against their posts when current changes.

The inhabitants also use sound deliberately. Because visibility is green and broken, and because the city is spread out through water, sound becomes a practical medium. A knocked rhythm on a root column can carry a message across the basin. A struck seed-pod can mark time. A low call through a hollow reed can signal between divers. There are places where the architecture focuses sound, so that a whisper in one chamber can be heard in another, or where the water itself seems to hum with remembered noise. Loudness here is not chaos. It is the city breathing.

The light is ordinary, but the water makes it extraordinary.

During the day, the pool receives the same light as any high place: clear, cool, sometimes fierce when the sun breaks through, sometimes diffused to pearl by cloud. Under the surface, that light becomes layered. Near the top it is bright and wavering, full of moving caustics. Lower down it turns jade, then olive, then a dusky green-brown where old roots hold their shapes in near-darkness. Floating particles catch the beams. Schools of fish flash through them. Air bubbles rise in silver trains. The translucent leaf panels of upper rooms glow with soft green-gold when the sun is behind them. There is no need for artificial illumination in the day, and the inhabitants seem to prefer it that way. Light belongs to the sky, the water, and the living surfaces. It is not something they try to own.

The atmosphere is cool, wet, and saturated.

Even in air pockets, there is no true dryness. Everything holds moisture. Bark is slick. Rope is supple. Floors are springy underfoot. The smell is not foul; it is the smell of cold green life: crushed leaves, wet wood, tannin, algae, fish-scale brightness, clean rain. In deeper water, the sensation is of being held in a slow, living body. The current is gentle but present, moving from inflows of rain and high seepage toward hidden drains below. It is not a river. It is more like the circulation of blood through a vast plant. A swimmer can feel it against the skin: a faint pull, a suggestion, a direction.
