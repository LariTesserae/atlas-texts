**The Nulltide Cistern**

High in a newly broken mountain, above the dry thin air where ordinary weather gives up, there is a drowned place with no surface.

It sits in a black cirque of fresh volcanic glass, a high-altitude wound of sharp basalt and obsidian panels split so recently that the edges still look manufactured. There is no rounding here, no softening by wind or long rain. The stone has the cruelty of first exposure: ridges like blades, stair-step fractures, crystalline faces that catch what little light exists and return it as cold splinters. The elevation is severe, the world below reduced to cloud-scars and distant ridgelines, but the landscape itself is not airy. It is submerged.

The entire place is liquid.

A volume of cool, black water hangs inside the mountain’s hollow, not in a basin, not behind a dam, and not in any way that current physics would approve. It is held in a rough cathedral-shaped envelope by buried engines older than the summit’s name. Outside the water-boundary, the mountain is dry and wind-scoured. Inside, there is only the deep, still pressure of a sea compressed into a room. There is no shore. There is no ceiling except the closing of the field into darkness. There is no surface to mark escape or sky. Every direction is water until the boundary, and the boundary is not a wall but a condition: beyond it, liquid ceases to be permitted.

The water is cool, a few degrees above freezing, but not actively cold in the way of moving glacial melt. It does not circulate. It has no thermal layers, no current, no convection. A particle of heat introduced into it does not spread normally; it remains local, contained, as if the medium itself has been instructed not to change. The temperature is less a climate than a constant.

Within this suspended dark, a small urban settlement exists.

It is not a city in extent, but it is urban in logic: planned, layered, engineered, full of civic intention. The scale is compact — a flooded hamlet arranged inside a vertical nave of water, perhaps large enough to contain a cathedral if the cathedral had been dissolved into streets, chambers, locks, and pressure-ways. Buildings are sparse and widely separated, leaving broad intervals of empty black water between them. The place feels designed for many more presences than it contains. There are plazas with no crowds, processional ramps leading into shadow, terraces without foot traffic, arches that frame nothing but depth.

The constructions are made of dark ceramic, glazed basalt, pale metal that does not corrode, and sheets of a glassy composite that bends light just enough to make the eye doubt distance. Streets are not roads but three-dimensional channels, marked by guide-rails, mooring rings, sunken pylons, and low balustrades of polished stone. Stairs ascend through water as if for amphibious pilgrims. Doorways stand open into flooded rooms. Windows are thick lenses, some dark, some faintly glowing from within with maintenance light that has no visible source.

The urban fabric is layered: lower levels of machinery vaults and pressure galleries, middle levels of habitation and ritual spaces, upper levels of thin walkways and signal masts that no longer signal anything. Infrastructure is everywhere but mostly idle. Conduits run along walls in neat bundles. Lock-gates stand open, their mechanisms frozen in perfect readiness. Drainage channels are filled with the same water they were meant to drain. Aqueducts carry nothing, because there is no flow. The settlement has the density of civic planning without the density of bodies. Much of it is emptiness, carefully made.

At the center of the cistern, where a town square would be, stands a structure that is part shrine, part machine: a vertical ring of dark metal set around a column of water that is slightly clearer than the rest. This is one of the anchors of the null-gradient engines. It emits no light, only a sensation of absolute steadiness. Near it, the water feels less like a substance and more like a law. Pressure is equalized in ways that should not be possible. Depth becomes informational rather than physical. You can move downward without your ears changing; you can rise without buoyancy tugging at you. The body receives the water as cool, heavy, and calm, but not as crushing.

This is one of the rules of the Nulltide Cistern: gradients have been removed.

There is no tide. No current. No circulation. No sediment drift. No chemical depletion. No meaningful decay. The water does not age. The stone does not weather further. The metal does not oxidize. The faint mineral taste of the liquid — iron, salt, old snow, deep rock — remains identical at every point and every moment. If a bubble is introduced, it does not rise; it hangs where placed until some microscopic correction removes it without violence. If a light is turned on, it illuminates a volume with hard edges, as if photons themselves have been asked not to wander.

The place is dark, but not absolutely black.

Light here is rare and deliberate. There are thin lines of cold luminescence set into doorframes and stair edges, blue-white and faint, like afterimages of electricity. Some buildings carry internal glows so dim they seem remembered rather than seen. The obsidian walls reflect these lights in broken fragments, making the cistern appear larger and more fractured than it is. Above, the water closes into a darkness that has no ceiling visible to the eye. Below, the floor is a mosaic of fresh-cut stone and metal panels, their seams glowing faintly where the engines breathe their impossible steadiness into the world.

One of the strangest features is the Rain Wall.

At the eastern edge of the settlement, inside the all-liquid volume, there hangs a sheet of rain.

It is not falling.

Thousands of droplets are suspended in a vertical plane, each one separated from the surrounding water by a skin of altered physics. They look like black beads, then like tiny mirrors, then like lenses containing inverted rooms. They form a curtain or barrier between two districts of the drowned hamlet. The rain does not merge, drip, tremble, or descend. It is weather arrested mid-meaning. It suggests a storm that was interrupted not by shelter, but by ontology. The droplets are perfectly spaced, some clustered in diagonal strings, others scattered as if caught in a gust that was then made permanent. They reflect the faint settlement lights as points of pinprick silver. In darkness, the Rain Wall is almost invisible. When a rare maintenance glow passes nearby, it becomes suddenly present, glittering with severe delicacy.

No one knows whether it is decoration, boundary, memorial, or component of the stabilization system. It may be all four. It may be a fossil of the flood that filled the cistern, preserved as proof that water once had weather.

The atmosphere — if water can be called atmosphere — is hushed. Sound behaves strangely. It carries far, but without urgency. Voices, if there were voices, would become rounded and directionless. Metal taps would not ring; they would bloom briefly and stop, as if the medium refused to let echoes accumulate. For the most part there is silence: not the silence of absence, but the silence of equilibrium. Beneath that silence lies a sub-audible steadiness from the engines, felt in the teeth and sternum more than heard. It is not a hum. It is closer to the memory of a note that has not changed since before the mountain broke.

Very rarely, there is a sound like a single glass bead falling onto stone. It happens without visible cause. It may be the cistern correcting something no observer can detect. It may be nothing at all.

Life is rare and mostly not green.

There are no plants. No algae. No kelp. No moss. No lichen. No plankton with chlorophyll. The cistern rejects photosynthesis as irrelevant. In the joints between ceramic panels, there are pale filaments that may be fungal or may be something simpler: mineral-eating threads, white as salt, growing only where the engineered surfaces permit them. They do not sway. They do not spread visibly. They exist as a faint frost on dark material, metabolizing microscopic imperfections in the construction.

On some basalt faces there are smooth patches of chemolithotrophic film, gray-blue and matte, arranged in geometric outlines too regular for accident. These films do not bloom or retreat. They are maintained, not wild. They may be part of the machinery’s ecology, or the machinery may be part of theirs.

Fauna is rarer still.

In the whole cistern there may be only a handful of creatures that qualify. They are pale, translucent, eel-thin things no longer than a hand, with no eyes and no obvious mouths. They hang in doorways or beneath arches, motionless for long intervals, their bodies faintly luminous from within. They do not swim so much as occupy selected coordinates. Sometimes one flexes once, slowly, as if recalling that motion is possible. They feed, if feeding is the word, on trace differentials emitted by the engines: leakage, permission, waste information. They are not predators. They are not scavengers. They seem more like living instruments left in place to monitor a condition that never varies.

Occasionally one of them drifts near the Rain Wall and pauses there, its body reflecting in a hundred tiny droplets. Then it stops moving, and the wall repeats it as a constellation of pale threads. After a long time, it continues — one slow bend, one small change of position, then stillness again.

The ecology is therefore almost theoretical. It does not cycle. It does not compete. It does not flourish. It persists as a minor footnote inside a larger stability.

The built world reinforces this sense of permanence. Doors are weighted to remain open or closed without hinges deciding otherwise. Furniture, where it exists, is anchored or grown from the floor: benches of black glass, tables like shallow basins, sleeping niches filled with a softer gel that still contains no air. There are rooms that appear to have been baths before the entire place became bath. There are corridors lined with empty racks, as if for instruments that were removed before the water arrived. Some chambers contain only a single lamp and a ring set into the floor, suggesting ritual rather than residence.

The settlement feels waiting, but not abandoned.

It is not ruined. There is no collapse, no silt, no biological fouling, no corrosion. Its youth is preserved by the same principle that preserves the water. The sharpness of the newly fractured mountain outside is matched by the crispness of the construction within. Every edge is exact. Every gap is intentional. Nothing has been worn down by use, because use has been suspended. Nothing has been improved by time, because time has been denied authority here.

To move through the Nulltide Cistern is to move through a town dreamt into ocean and then denied sequence. You pass from one dark volume to another, between spaced buildings whose windows glow faintly as if someone left a lamp on centuries ago and the lamp has never understood the concept of ending. You cross plazas where the only footprints are geological: fresh fractures in the stone, angular depressions, crystal faces that catch your light and scatter it into brief blue sparks. You ascend stairways that lead to upper galleries, then descend again into deeper chambers where the water feels thicker, though pressure never changes.

The sparse arrangement makes each structure feel solitary, important, and slightly wrong. A habitation block floats in the dark like a reliquary. A machine hall opens its long colonnade to nothing. A narrow bridge of pale metal spans a black drop, connecting two platforms that both appear unused. The emptiness is not wasteful; it is structural. The city was made with room around it, as if the water itself needed space to remain lawful.

There is a sense that the cistern is not merely submerged but sealed against becoming.

No new sediment enters. No temperature front arrives. No storm reaches the interior. The high mountain outside may freeze and burn under sun, but inside there is only this: cool water, dark stone, faint lights, rare creatures, and the Rain Wall holding its arrested weather in perfect silence.

It is a landscape of high altitude and deep-sea psychology. It is mineral, engineered, and post-botanical. It is young in stone but ancient in stillness. It is small enough to be crossed in a short swim, yet organized with the seriousness of a city. It is mostly quiet, mostly dark, mostly empty — but not dead. It is stable to the point of sanctity.

And beneath everything, in the water, in the walls, in the black glass of the mountain, there is the feeling that the place is not merely maintained but proven: a demonstration that a world can be made to stop changing without being made to end.
