**The High Sward**

The first thing is the emptiness.

Not a dead emptiness, not a wasteland, but room: a huge, warm, open quiet stretched out under ordinary sunlight. The horizon runs away in every direction with very little to interrupt it. There are no cliffs, no boulders, no forests, no ruins of any substantial kind. The land seems almost too simple to believe at first—a vast, level expanse, faintly springy underfoot, rising in shallow blisters and falling into long, soft hollows, extending far beyond the reach of easy sight. It has the scale of a region, a country of open ground, a place where weather moves in broad sheets and distances deceive.

Then the sound arrives.

It begins as a thin, high drone, so constant it might be the air itself. Then layers enter: a rasping stridulation from within the ground, a wet clicking from shallow pools, a reedy whistle from far off, the sudden burst of wings. At certain hours, especially when the sun is high or when rain has just passed, the noise becomes overwhelming—not mechanical, not industrial, but biological. It presses on the ears. It fills the open space until the plain seems to be vibrating with life.

This is a landscape that has finished becoming.

It sits at an extreme height, lifted far above the lower world. Clouds do not always pass overhead here; often they pass below, spreading in slow white seas along the unseen slopes beyond the rim of the plain. The air is thin but not cold. By some accident of latitude, sunlight, and the living surface’s own heat retention, the place remains warm. The light is normal—sun, cloud-shadow, ordinary day—but at this elevation it feels especially clean and direct. Shadows are sharp at noon. The sky is pale and immense. In the afternoon, cumulus towers build themselves over the plain, cast moving dark patches across the surface, and sometimes break into brief, warm rain.

There is no stone here, or almost none that a traveler would recognize as stone.

The ground itself is plant.

Not merely covered by plants, but made of them. What seems like soil is a dense, ancient felt of roots, rhizomes, fallen fibers, living filaments, and compressed vegetative matter. What seem like pebbles are often hardened seed cases, resin nodules, lignified growths, or plant-made mineral grains rounded by age and motion. The surface has the give of cork, the warmth of sun-baked skin, and a faint elasticity. It recovers from footprints slowly, not like mud but like tired flesh. In places it is dry and papery; in others it is damp, dark, and spongy. It smells green, sweet, faintly fermented, like warm leaves after rain.
