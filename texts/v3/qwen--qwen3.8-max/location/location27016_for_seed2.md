Below the surface, where one might expect bedrock, there is an ocean.

It is not a natural sea that found its way into caverns or fissures. It is installed. The water was let in deliberately, metered through colossal sluices, treated, oxygenated, salted, and settled into a space built to receive it. The world above remains dry, or at least passable; beneath that outer skin lies a submerged industrial expanse so broad that it has begun to behave like a planet of its own.

The first thing you notice is that there is no ground in the ordinary sense. Every surface is made.

The floor is a manufactured plain of riveted steel plates, cast concrete basins, ceramic linings, polymer seams, grated walkways, and bolted access panels. It is not one material but many, chosen for strength, corrosion resistance, thermal conductivity, ease of repair, or simple economy. Some areas are smooth as tank walls. Others are ribbed, studded, or crossed with rail channels. In places the surface rises into broad industrial terraces; in others it drops away into intake trenches, pump shafts, and turbine pits. There are no stones, no pebbles, no sandbars. The nearest thing to gravel is loose scale, rust flakes, molted shells, and fragments of engineered cable sheathing gathered in low spots by current.

This is a young underwater landscape. The flood is recent enough that the water has not softened anything. Edges are still sharp. Weld beads stand proud. Cut corners remain knife-bright where flow has not yet worn them. There is no rounded cobble, no polished natural curve, no ancient softness. Erosion has begun, but only in mechanical ways: scour marks around intakes, cavitation pitting beneath pump housings, streaks of orange-brown oxidation weeping from stressed bolts, pale abrasion trails where current carries suspended particles against a wall. The sea has not yet had time to become geology. It is still a structure under water.

The scale is difficult to hold in the mind. The ocean is not merely large; it is vast enough to have its own weather. Currents move through it like atmospheric fronts. Thermal plumes rise from factory districts and drift for kilometers before collapsing into cooler layers. Internal waves roll invisibly through the water column, generated by pump schedules and heat exchangers, breaking against the upper works of submerged industrial ranges. There are gyres broad enough to be bent by the world’s rotation, and pressure differentials that shift the water level by centimeters across hundreds of kilometers. In open zones, a person or vehicle could travel for hours through clear, cool, empty water and see nothing but faint blue-green distance, the occasional distant flash of a lamp array, and the slow movement of fish.

It is an empty vastness, but not a dead one.

The light is normal. That is one of the strange-seeming but entirely practical facts of the place. The sea lies below the surface, yet it is not lit by unnatural glows or bioluminescent mystery. Sunlight reaches the upper water through engineered shafts: mirror wells, prism columns, diffuser plates, and heavy lens arrays set into the ceiling of the world above. In some regions the surface shutters open during the day and spill pale, moving daylight down in broad shafts. In others, full-spectrum industrial lamps take over where natural light cannot reach. The result is ordinary illumination: white-blue, functional, sometimes harsh, sometimes softened by suspended particles. Caustics move across steel floors. Shadows are crisp near light wells and hazy in deeper water. There are no magical colors, no impossible radiance. It looks like daylight filtered through water, because that is what it is.

The ceiling itself is a landscape overhead. From below, the underside of the surface appears as a dark firmament of girders, service tracks, sealed hatches, pipe bridges, lamp fields, and ventilation ribs. Where the light shafts pierce it, the water becomes columnar, bright pillars descending into the blue. The ceiling is not sky, but it performs the work of sky: it gives day, regulates light, sheds condensate, and carries the machinery that allows the sea below to exist.

The temperature is cool. Most of the water sits in a range that feels austere rather than hostile: cold enough to make bare skin ache within minutes, cool enough that a diver’s breath fogs inside a helmet if the suit is not heated, but not so cold that the sea becomes inert. Near the industrial heat sources, the water warms by a few degrees. Those warm seams are important. Life gathers there. Currents form around them. Predators patrol their edges. The difference between a cold open plain and a warm pump-gallery can be as significant as the difference between winter coast and summer shelf.

The water itself is managed. It is not pristine wilderness. It is filtered, circulated, chemically monitored, and moved by machines. There are salinity gradients where brine is discharged from processing works, and fresher lenses where condensate or meltwater enters from ceiling systems. Dense cold water sometimes sinks in curtains that look, from a distance, like underwater rain. These plumes are not weather in the aerial sense, but they behave like weather: they create fronts, alter visibility, trigger biological responses, and push nutrients through the system.
