This is a high, drowned, industrial sea: a cold ocean lying over newly lifted mountain terrain, held in place by machines and pressure, loud beyond ordinary hearing. It is not a coast, not a shoreline, not a flooded valley in any gentle sense. It is a submerged altitude. The water has risen into the high country, or the high country has been raised into the water, and now the two exist locked together in a state that has lasted long enough to seem permanent.

The first thing you understand, if you can perceive it without being destroyed by the pressure and sound, is that the world here is mineral and liquid. There is no green. There are no forests, no kelp, no grasses, no reeds, no algae blooms, no floating meadows, no pollen, no root systems, no soft organic margins. Plant life has never taken hold, or was erased long ago. The surfaces are stone, metal, ice, salt, concrete, and water. Where life exists, it is animal or simpler than animal: colorless films, threadlike fungal mats, microbial crusts, colonies of things that feed on current, mineral, electricity, or waste heat. Nothing photosynthesizes in the way of green leaves. The light is ordinary, but the biology is not.

The terrain is young. That youth is visible everywhere in the sharpness of the drowned land. These mountains have not had time to be softened. Their ridges are knife-edged. Their cliffs are broken into angular slabs. Their slopes are fields of fresh scree, great tumbled boulders, fractured basalt, pale granite, black fault glass, and iron-stained rock faces. There is little sediment in the sense of soil or organic ooze. There is mineral dust, crushed stone, metallic precipitate, and cold chemical snow, but not the soft decay of a living land. Every edge looks recently cut. Every ravine looks like a wound still opening. The water does not blur this; it clarifies it.

The water itself is clear and heavy. It is not tropical blue, not murky green, not the black of a deep ocean trench. It has a cold silver clarity, lit from above by normal daylight or by ordinary industrial illumination so steady and white that it feels like permanent noon. Light enters the water in broad sheets and hard beams. Because the liquid contains so little organic matter, visibility can be immense. You can look across distances that should be impossible in water and see a cliff face, a tower, a shoal of animals, or a machine hanging in the gray brightness. Shadows are sharp. Reflections move like metal. There is no dreamy softness here. The world is too cold, too new, too mineral for that.

It is high. Not merely deep, but high. The drowned range stands elevated above older, lower worlds. If there is a surface, it is far up, a bright plane of pressure and light, sometimes calm, sometimes vibrating with internal weather. The mountains beneath the water are not gentle foothills; they are a high-altitude wilderness submerged. Peaks rise toward the light until they nearly touch it. Passes become waterways. Valleys become channels. Cliff faces become vertical plains of cold current. The pressure is not only the pressure of depth; it is the pressure of a world held unnaturally in position. The sea does not belong here by ordinary geography. It belongs here by force, by engineering, by some vast act of retention.

The scale is enormous. This is not a lake, not a reservoir in any municipal sense. It is wide enough to generate its own weather, though that weather is strange and mostly internal. There are pressure fronts that move through the water like silent thunder. There are curtains of colder water falling in dense sheets, like rain inside the sea. There are internal waves, slow and huge, that travel along invisible density boundaries. There are bubble fogs and cavitation storms near the machinery, where the liquid churns white and roars. There are regions of absolute clarity next to regions of mineral haze. The place has horizons, but they are blue-gray and featureless. It has distances, but those distances are measured in echoes as much as sight.

And yet it is empty.

That emptiness is essential. For long stretches there is almost nothing: no landmark, no structure, no animal, no movement except the faint drift of mineral particles and the pressure of sound. The volume is vast and hollow. One can travel through enormous open water and encounter no solid thing. The world feels less inhabited than spaced. Presences are rare. When something appears — a rock spire, a machine, a shoal, a cliff — it arrives almost violently after the void. The emptiness makes every object feel isolated, significant, and exposed.

The built marks are scattered and industrial. They are not cities. They are not dense settlements. They are occasional artifacts, single structures, partial remains, small stations, and enormous machines placed far apart.
