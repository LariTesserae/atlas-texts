It is a hidden low hollow, sunk beneath a broad mineral plain and entered only by a narrow fissure in the rock. From outside there is no sign of it: no green edge, no vapor, no sound except a faint pressure in the air when the ground exhales. Inside, the place opens into a single enclosed basin, a pocket basin large enough to contain a small town but small enough to feel like a room carved into the planet’s heat.

There is no water. Not in pools, not in canals, not in clouds, not in dew, not locked as frost or weeping from stone. The word “river” would be meaningless here except as a name for channels of moving air and drifting dust. The hollow is dry in an absolute sense: dry mineral, dry chemistry, dry life.

The terrain is made of low terraces, saltstone, dark basalt, yellow sulfur veins, and baked ceramic. The ground has been worn into established forms. Nothing looks newly broken. Edges have softened into shoulders. Stairs are rounded by long abrasion. Walls lean in gentle, settled ways, as if the place has existed in exactly this state for ages and has reached a balance with its own slow decay. Erosion here is not violent; it is habitual, a constant polishing by hot air, mineral dust, and the restless expansion and contraction of heated stone.

The basin is built.

That is the first strange thing. It is not merely a natural pocket. Terraces rise around the hollow in organized tiers. Dry canals cut through the floor, their beds lined with fitted stone. Narrow streets curve between low structures. There are plazas, ramps, retaining walls, vent-shafts, stairways leading to blocked doorways, and tall chimneys or towers that appear to have been raised to catch something from the air. The construction is settlement-scale: enough organization to suggest streets, districts, circulation, infrastructure. Yet it is empty. The density of presences is very low. There are vast volumes of air between objects, long open courtyards with only a single standing stone, empty rooms whose floors have become gardens, silent avenues where nothing moves except heat-shimmer and drifting spores.

The technology is simple. The stone was shaped by hand-tools. Metal appears only in small amounts: dark bronze pins, hammered fittings, corroded copper strips reinforcing a cracked lintel, simple lever-catches on vent covers, rough bolts set into ceramic sockets. There are no complex machines, no precision mechanisms. The place depends on mass, angle, aperture, and patience. Its engineering is the engineering of placed stones and directed drafts.

Light is dim. The hollow does not receive direct sky. A high lattice of mineral crust or old architectural shielding admits only a muted glow, brownish and weak, like sunlight remembered through smoked glass. In the deeper tiers, illumination comes from other sources: dull red seams where rock stays hot, pale luminescent crusts on salt, faint sulfur-glow in channels where gases move. Shadows are soft but numerous. Nothing is fully lit. Even at the brightest hour, the basin feels like it is holding its breath inside a permanent amber twilight.

The heat is constant and serious. It does not burn in a sudden way unless one touches metal or stone near a vent, but it presses. The air is dry and dense with mineral taste: sulfur, hot dust, salt, oxidized copper, baked clay. Heat rises from vents in the floor, moves through the canals, climbs the terraces, and escapes through the towers in slow pulses. The whole hollow breathes thermally. It inhales and exhales on a cycle too slow for casual notice, but the vegetation responds to it.

And there is vegetation. Lush, in its own impossible way.

No green wetness, no succulent leaves, no thirsting roots. Instead, the hollow is thick with dry, heat-adapted growth that has filled the urban frame like a garden reclaiming a temple. The plants are not like ordinary plants. Their chemistry is not water-based. Their tissues are waxy, fibrous, mineralized, sometimes metallic in sheen. Some are black and copper-threaded. Some are pale yellow, brittle as sulfur crystals. Some are glassy and smoky, hollow-stemmed, clicking when they move. They flourish without water, feeding on heat gradients, sulfur compounds, salt vapor, and the chemical tension of the rocks.

On the lower terraces grow sulfur-ferns with fronds of laminated yellow and black. They do not unfurl toward light but toward warmth. Their veins carry thin internal fluids that are not water: oily, solvent-like substances that move through capillary channels and give the fronds a faint smell of struck matches when they are disturbed. In the shaded corners spreads wax-thread moss, a dense copper-green carpet that exudes tiny beads of heat-stable wax. It softens the stone beneath it and makes the terraces glow faintly where the luminescent crusts reflect from its surface.

Along the dry canals stand glass canes: tall, hollow, jointed stems like smoked quartz. They have almost no leaves. Their bodies are the organs of exchange. They draw hot mineral vapor from the canal beds and release fine crystalline waste along their joints. When air moves through them, they produce a thin resonant tone, as if the city has been planted with organ pipes. In open plazas grow bell-pods, squat stalked plants with dry membranous fruits hanging from curved ribs. The pods do not ripen by rotting or softening. They ripen by tension. When the temperature reaches a certain point, the membranes tighten and split with a sharp ringing crack, releasing clouds of spore-dust into the rising heat.

There are also ember-bulbs buried beneath the terrace gravel: rounded mineralized growths that store heat and glow very faintly at their cores. They are not fire, but they hold warmth long after the upper air has cooled, and around them other growths gather in dense clusters. In places the vegetation becomes almost abundant enough to call jungle, though it is a jungle of dry rustle, mineral shine, and heat-motion rather than damp leaf and shadow.

No animal life exists here.

That absence is one of the defining facts of the place. There are no insects among the sulfur-ferns. No birds in the towers. No tracks in the dust. No bones in the empty rooms. No eyes watch from doorways. The ecology has no herbivores, no predators, no scavengers. The lushness is not grazed. Growth accumulates, hardens, sheds, mineralizes, and is reused by chemical weathering and the slow circulation of the basin. The absence of animal life makes the place feel both peaceful and alien. There is no hunger in the hollow. There is also no animal comfort. No breath, no heartbeat, no small movement in the dark.

Instead, the place is loud.

The sound is not animal. It is architectural, thermal, botanical. Hot air moving through the dry canals creates a low continuous rush, sometimes rising into a moan when the vent-shafts open. The glass canes ring and tick. Bell-pods crack and chime when they split. The wax-thread moss makes a soft abrasive whisper when thermal expansion travels across the terraces. Stone joints pop and settle. Dust avalanches in tiny hisses down the stairways. The towers amplify certain frequencies, turning the basin into a resonant chamber. At times the whole hidden city seems to be humming with a low chord made of heat.

The sound is not random. It follows cycles. When the deep thermal vents pulse, the canals breathe harder, the canes pitch upward, and the bell-pods open in scattered percussion. When the air cools slightly, the stone contracts and gives off sharp ticks. When spore release begins, there is a dry rustling like paper being torn far away. The urban form of the hollow shapes these sounds: streets become flues, plazas become drums, empty rooms become echo chambers. The built environment is not merely a settlement; it is an acoustic instrument tuned to the basin’s heat.

The landscape is active. Nothing here is still for long, though the activity is not the movement of creatures. There are weather cycles without rain. Thermal tides move through the hollow in slow waves. Hot gas rises from lower vents, carries dust and spores upward, deposits fine salt on the terraces, then cools and sinks along the walls. Miniature dry squalls spin in the plazas, lifting veils of mineral powder and making the glass canes sway. Spore clouds drift through the streets in dim golden bands, catching the weak light before settling into cracks where new growth may begin.

The vegetation has its own pace. Sulfur-ferns open their fronds when the floor temperature rises. Wax-thread moss thickens where chemical vapor pools. Bell-pods wait for precise heat thresholds, then burst in sudden reproductive events. Glass canes grow by accretion, adding silica segments during high-vapor flows. The plants do not move quickly, but over time they alter the city: blocking one vent, opening another, stabilizing a wall, redirecting air through a new channel. The built environment and the vegetation maintain one another. The city shapes the heat; the heat shapes the plants; the plants reshape the city.

Because there is no water, erosion is different. It is thermal, chemical, aeolian, crystalline. Salt crusts expand and fracture. Sulfur veins soften slightly in the hottest zones and then stiffen again. Dust polishes stone. Roots that are not true roots penetrate cracks and widen them by slow mineral deposition. The hollow weathers into itself. Its forms are stable because they have been refined by repetition. Every curve has been tested by heat and airflow. Every terrace has settled into a working angle.

The rules of this world are simple but unfamiliar.

Heat is the primary currency. It moves air, opens structures, triggers growth, and carries spores. Sound is not merely noise; it is a signal of state. A change in pitch means a change in pressure, temperature, or chemical flow. The city listens in its own way, because its apertures respond to vibration. Density is low; emptiness is functional. The vast open spaces are not wasted. They are resonance volumes, drift corridors, heat-exchange chambers. Absence is part of the design.

Water is not merely missing; it is irrelevant. The chemistry of the place is built around sulfur, salts, silica, metals, heat-stable waxes, and solvent-like internal fluids. Life here does not thirst. It does not sweat. It does not wilt in the ordinary sense. It hardens, accumulates, crystallizes, rings, and sheds.

The built environment suggests intention, but not current habitation. It is urban without citizens. Its streets are for air. Its plazas are for sporefall. Its towers are for sound and heat. Its rooms are for slow chemical gardening. Perhaps it was made by vanished hands. Perhaps the vegetation learned to build in this shape over long ages. The place does not answer. It only continues.

It is intimate because it is hidden and contained, yet it feels vast because of the emptiness inside. It is hot but dim. It is lush but dry. It is built but uninhabited. It is alive but animal-less. It is loud without voices. It is active without hurry. The hollow holds its paradoxes in a settled equilibrium, like a sealed instrument playing to itself in the dark.
