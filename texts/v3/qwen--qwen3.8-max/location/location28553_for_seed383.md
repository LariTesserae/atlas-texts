Below the dry surface, in a country of old stone and falling light, there is an open underground basin where a city exists without green things.

It is not dark there. Daylight enters through high shafts and sink-throats in the ceiling, falling in pale columns until it strikes white mineral crusts, polished limestone, and broad sheets of still water. The light is passed onward by carefully angled stone faces, shell-bright panels, and shallow mirror pools, so that the whole place glows with a cool, almost milky brightness. The air is damp and cool, carrying a faint mineral sharpness, like wet chalk and cold salt. There is no weather in the ordinary sense, yet there is weather enough: mist gathers under the ceiling, beads on the stone, and sometimes falls as a thin, silent rain.

The terrain is broad and ancient. The basin has been worn down through immense ages into a low, rounded landscape of shallow pans, gentle rises, worn terraces, and long hollows where moisture collects. Nothing is sharp. Every ridge, step, and hollow has the softened look of something that has already reached its final shape. The stone is old beyond reckoning: pale limestone, gypsum, calcite crusts, seams of chert, and floors of compacted marl that accept footprints only briefly before they are erased by drifting dust and slow seepage. Erosion here is not violent. It has already done its worst, or its best, and what remains is a settled, patient world.

There is no plant life.

No leaf, root, stem, pollen, bark, or grass belongs here. The very idea of wood is absent. In place of vegetation are mineral skins, wet microbial films, crusts of pale slime, and in damper cracks, felts of fungal thread that spread like pale wool over stone. These living surfaces are not green. They are gray-white, bone-pale, ash-blue, and sometimes faintly luminous where moisture collects and light lies longest. They form the base of the basin’s life. Where they thicken, fauna gathers.

And fauna is abundant, though it appears in scattered bursts rather than evenly filling the space.

In the shallow water pockets, blind crustaceans rake across the bottom with slow, deliberate legs. Translucent worms rise when the air changes pressure. Flat, pale creatures like eyeless beetles move over wet stone, grazing on microbial crust. In deeper cisterns, fish without pigment turn in silent schools, their bodies flashing as reflected light passes through the water. Wingless insects cluster near damp walls, and larger scavengers — soft-footed, cautious, mostly mute — come down from higher galleries when moisture returns. At times the basin seems almost empty; then a seep opens, a mist thickens, a pool fills, and life appears everywhere at once, moving through the bright stone hollows as if summoned.

The city is built into this ecology rather than above it.

It is urban in extent and organization, but sparse in density: a regional settlement of low platforms, terraced courts, sunken cisterns, causeways, ramps, drying racks, and broad open floors where little is built at all. Structures are spread widely across the basin, connected by long paths and worn processional ways. There are neighborhoods, storage courts, water-trapping terraces, animal pens made from stone ribs and stretched hide, light-yards where mirrors are tended, and old layered districts where newer floors rest atop the compacted remains of earlier ones. Yet there is much empty space. The city breathes. One can stand in a bright plaza and see only a few figures moving far away, their forms made small by distance and pale stone.

Everything is made by hand, and nothing here belongs to metal.

The people use chipped chert, ground bone, shell, antler, stone hammers, abrasive sands, cords of sinew and fungal fiber, stiff hides, and polished calcite where a reflective surface is needed. Their tools are simple but patient. They do not forge; they shape, bind, grind, fit, and maintain. Because there is no timber, architecture relies on compressive stone, stacked slabs, bone-and-shell frames, tensioned hides, and the reuse of ancient fragments found in the basin. Much of the built world is not constructed anew but rearranged, adjusted, lifted, tied, untied, and settled again into older forms.

Water is the restless heart of the place.

It does not rule openly, as a great river might, but arrives intermittently: condensation along ceiling ribs, brief drizzle from cold upper stone, seepage through hidden cracks, temporary channels that appear for a day and vanish for many. Pools form in the pans. Thin films of water slide across smooth floors. Cisterns fill by degrees. The inhabitants have built grooved catchment stones, dew-channels, shallow basins, and hide-lined troughs to guide this uncertain moisture. They know where the damp will return, but not always when. Their city is made to tolerate absence and sudden presence alike.

The atmosphere is bright, cool, and strangely silent.

Sound behaves differently here. The porous stone, damp dust, fungal felts, and thick mineral crusts absorb noise. Voices are kept low. Much communication is by gesture, hand-sign, tapped rhythm on bone, or the careful positioning of objects. The city can be full of movement and still seem hushed. Footfalls are soft. Water beads and falls with tiny sounds. Chitin rasps faintly over rock. A school of pale fish turns without a splash. Even when many people work at once — adjusting mirrors, hauling nets, shifting platforms, guiding animals away from newly flooded hollows — the effect is not roar but rustle, a vast quiet industry.

Yet the place is turbulent.

Its turbulence is not the turbulence of storms that tear, but of constant reorganization. Channels migrate. Pools appear where floors were dry. Mist thickens, then thins. Light shifts as water films move over reflective surfaces, causing whole districts to brighten or dim. Fauna surges in response: grazers follow moisture, predators follow grazers, scavengers follow both. People respond in turn, moving racks, closing sluices, opening temporary pens, redirecting foot traffic, covering stored goods with hide sheets. The city is always being retuned. It does not resist change; it has been made to slide with it.

This is why the built environment feels both urban and temporary. There are permanent routes, ancient foundations, and inherited stone works, but many structures are designed to be moved. Platforms rest on adjustable stone feet. Screens can be lowered. Rope-bound shelters can be loosened and re-erected elsewhere. Causeways have gaps that can be bridged or abandoned. The infrastructure is organized, even dense in its logic, but not crowded in its physical presence. The city occupies the basin like a pattern laid over a breathing surface.

The world has rules shaped by its strangeness.

Nothing green is expected, so nothing green is missed. Light is treated as a common inheritance: mirror-keepers maintain the angles of bright stone, and no one may block a light-path without cause. Water has right of way. If a seep opens beneath a dwelling, the dwelling moves. Sound is conserved, not merely because the stone muffles it, but because loudness is considered clumsy in a place where small signals carry far and sudden noise can disturb animal movement. The ancient stone is not cut wantonly; it is already finished. New work must sit lightly upon it.

Because the basin is below the surface yet bright, it feels inverted but not hostile. The brightness is not warm. It is clean, cold, and slightly severe, reflected from pale mineral and still water. Shadows are soft-edged. Dust hangs in the light shafts like suspended flour. Where the light touches wet stone, the rock seems almost alive; where it withdraws, the stone returns to its old gray patience.

The ecology is unusual but coherent. Without plants, the basin feeds itself through microbial crusts, fungal mats, falling organic dust from surface shafts, animal carcasses, mineral-rich water, and the ceaseless recycling of its own inhabitants. People cultivate the living films in shallow trays, encourage fungal felts in damp alcoves, manage breeding pools for crustaceans and fish, and harvest shell, chitin, bone, hide, and fiber from the fauna around them. They do not clear fields; they tend wet stone. They do not harvest forests; they maintain surfaces. Their agriculture, if it can be called that, is a careful negotiation with moisture, light, and mineral life.

The built layers reveal age. Lower courts are worn almost featureless. Older ramps have been softened by generations of bare feet and dragged loads. Former water channels have been incorporated into streets; former streets have become channels again. In some districts, one can see the outlines of older settlements beneath the present arrangement: sunken circles, buried steps, half-filled troughs, and low walls reused as foundations. The city has not grown upward so much as it has settled inward, becoming more fitted to the basin with each passing era.

The scale is regional. The basin is not a single room but a linked landscape of cavern-vales, low divides, shaft-lit plains, and half-hidden depressions extending beyond immediate sight. Some districts are separated by wide empty pans; others cling to the edges of ancient terraces. Travel between them is by foot, by low hand-drawn carts over smooth stone, or by shallow rafts where temporary water allows. The city is therefore not compact. It is a scattering of inhabited places within a larger mineral field, each cluster connected to the others by shared water routes, light-lines, and customary paths.

The most striking quality is the contrast between stillness and motion. The stone itself seems settled into final calm, yet the basin is full of change. Mist appears and disappears. Animals migrate in short, intense pulses. Water arrives, transforms a hollow into a bright pool, then leaves it dry and marked with white mineral residue. People move through the open courts with quiet urgency, adjusting the city as though tuning an instrument. Light flares on wet walls, dims, then returns. All of this happens without much noise. The landscape is turbulent, but its turbulence is hushed.

It is a mineral world made habitable not by abundance of green life, but by attention. The inhabitants have learned the moods of damp, the paths of light, the timing of animal gatherings, and the patience required by ancient stone. Their city is sparse, bright, cool, and oddly peaceful despite its constant change. It feels less like a settlement imposed on the land than like a careful pattern agreed upon between people, fauna, water, and rock.

In the end, the place remains below the surface, beneath the dry world above, and yet it is not hidden in shadow. It lies open in its own underground light: a pale, water-stained, silent urban basin, old beyond measure, sparsely built, abundantly alive in its own non-green way, and always moving without raising its voice.
