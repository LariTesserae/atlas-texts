The place is a high, hot, plant-made tableland so vast that weather is born inside it.

There is no ordinary ground here. What seems to be terrain is accumulated vegetation compressed by time into something like geography: root-felted plains, fallen leaf-layers hardened into leather and dark fibrous stone, bark ridges worn smooth by long ages of wind and passage, buttress roots rising like weathered cliffs. Nothing is mineral in the usual sense. The “rocks” are ancient seed-cases, knotted burls, fallen branch joints, and dense heartwood masses that have been dried, polished, and settled into the landscape for so long that they hold the stillness of geology. This is an established world, not a raw one. Its forms have been lived in, worn down, smoothed out, and balanced. The surfaces have the patience of old land.

Yet it is not thick or crowded in the way a jungle might be. The great presences are few and widely spaced. Colossal stalks, broad crowns, ribbed trunk-forms, and slow-spreading root platforms stand far apart, separated by open air, heat shimmer, and long distances. The landscape feels sparse in its density of major bodies, balanced with emptiness. Between the huge living forms there is room for wind to move, for sound to gather, for light to fall in wide sheets. The vegetation is total in the sense that everything belongs to plant life — the floor, the slopes, the hollows, the architecture of the horizon — but the composition is spare, monumental, and airy.

The elevation gives the place a lifted clarity. It sits high enough that clouds often pass below it or snag on the uppermost crown-spines, but the air is not cold; it is hot, dry in the open, and damp in hidden places. The light is normal daylight, ordinary sun and shadow, though heat haze bends the distances and makes far trunks seem to breathe. At midday the upper surfaces radiate warmth. Bark, leaf wax, and resin hold the sun’s energy, then release it slowly as the light changes.

Water exists here in pockets and intervals rather than abundance. There are no broad rivers or standing seas. Instead, moisture appears as condensation on leaf hairs at dawn, as brief local rains that fall in scattered cells across the plant plain, as mist drawn up from lower cloud layers, and as hidden reservoirs within the living body of the landscape. Water collects in knot-bowls, in hollow stems, in the throats of pitcher-like growths, in waxy leaf cups, and in shaded crevices where evaporation is slower. Some surfaces sweat faintly during the hottest hours, releasing droplets that bead on fibrous skin and vanish before they fall far. The world is damp, but only just enough to remind one that plant life is drinking, storing, and losing water all the time.

The heat makes the plant matter smell: green sap, dry chaff, warm resin, fermented fruit, dusty pollen, and the sharp sweetness of wounded leaves. The air is not still for long. Because the terrain is vast and unevenly heated, it creates its own circulations. Warm columns rise from sunlit bark plains. Cooler drafts sink through shaded hollows. Wind corridors form between the separated giants. Some crowns generate local rain shadows; others catch mist and drip it down into lower root terraces. A single broad leaf canopy can be the roof of one weather cell while, a few kilometers away, sunlight falls through open space onto sun-baked leaf plains. The landscape is large enough to have its own climate arguments.

The strangeness of the place is not that it is incomprehensible, but that the familiar categories have shifted. A hill may be a root swelling. A canyon may be a split in an ancient stem. A cave may be a hollow internode where animals have widened the opening over generations. A plain may be a fallen leaf that has been colonized, stitched together by fungal threads and rootlets, and adopted as ground. The world is alive in all directions, but it has settled into the long patience of terrain. It does not thrash or visibly move at every moment. Still, there is an underlying biological awareness to the place. Some surfaces close slightly when touched. Some hollows change their resonance when wind passes. Some flowers open only when certain vibrations reach them. The landscape is not conscious in a simple way, but it is responsive.

And it is loud.

The sound is one of the first things that would overwhelm an outsider. The fauna of this place is not merely present; it is a pressure. Much of it remains hidden at first, but its voices, movements, and effects fill the air. There is a continuous, deafening weave of insect sound: stridulation, wing-beat, chewing, percussion, rasping, clicking, and high thin cries that merge into a wall of noise. Some of it comes from tiny creatures hidden in bark seams. Some comes from vast aerial swarms that move like weather across the sparse expanse. Pollinator clouds can darken the light temporarily, not because they are constant, but because they arrive in overwhelming pulses. When they descend on flowering structures the size of platforms, the noise becomes physical: a buzzing so dense it feels as though the air itself has thickened.

Bird-like, bat-like, and insect-like fauna move between the plant forms. There are grazers that crop tough leaf mats, climbers that travel along root bridges, borers that tunnel into dead heartwood, predators that hunt in the acoustic confusion, and scavengers that disappear into fallen plant masses. The fauna is overwhelming in its ecological weight: every surface is used, every cavity is occupied or contested, every water pocket has visitors. Yet because the major plant forms are widely spaced, the animals often seem to emerge suddenly from emptiness. One moment there is only heat and distance; the next, a procession of creatures crosses a bark ridge, or a swarm rises from a hidden hollow, or a chorus begins so loudly that the chest vibrates.

The sound is not only animal in origin. The fauna uses the plant architecture as an instrument. Hollow stems become drums. Dry seed-cases rattle when touched by wings. Taut membranes of bark vibrate in response to calls. Certain creatures strike wood to mark territory. Others rub body parts against ribbed surfaces to produce amplified raspings. Some flowers or fruits may even rely on sound: triggered by particular frequencies, they release pollen or drop seeds. At peak times, the whole landscape becomes a resonant chamber. The noise can be beautiful, maddening, and dangerous all at once. Communication here must be loud, patterned, or tactile. Silence would be suspicious.

The ecosystem is active, not static. Daily cycles govern much of its motion. Dawn brings condensation, cautious movement, and the first testing calls. Midday brings heat, shimmering air, and a lull in large visible activity, though hidden chewing, nesting, and predation continue. Afternoon brings thermal turbulence, sometimes brief rain, sometimes sudden wind that shakes the high crowns and sends loose chaff spiraling. Dusk brings another explosion of sound as the fauna reasserts itself in the cooler light. There are also longer pulses: flowering events, seed falls, insect emergences, migrations of canopy grazers, predator swarms following prey clouds. The place is in motion even when its forms seem ancient and still.

Because the world is plant-as-substrate, erosion has a biological character. Surfaces wear under footfall, mandible, root pressure, sap flow, heat cracking, and the slow abrasion of wind-carried chaff. Trails are polished into root slopes. Hollows widen where animals drink or nest. Bark flakes fall like shingles from old cliffs. Dead tissue is not simply weathered; it is entered, cleaned, inhabited, and converted. The landscape has been shaped by use over long ages. It is not new growth. It has the settled feel of a place whose forms have found their equilibrium.

Marks of making are scattered and modest. Built presence is low here. There are small structures, faint traces, partial remains: a platform lashed into the crotch of a branch, a windbreak made from shed bracts, a ladder of braided aerial roots, a rope line anchored with thorns, a hollowed seed-shell used as a water vessel, a hearth of carbonized pith contained within a ring of dense bark. Some carvings have been made on living surfaces, but the living surface has healed around them, turning marks into swollen ridges. Nothing is allowed to remain entirely separate from the plant world for long. If a structure is abandoned, it is overgrown, chewed, nested in, or folded back into the living mass.

The technology suggested by these traces is simple and hand-made: cordage, knots, pegs, resin adhesives, sharpened thorns, flaked dense wood, seed-shell blades, beetle-mandible cutting tools, woven shelters, fire-drills, and perhaps small amounts of crude metal used as precious edges or ornaments. Any metal here would be rare, likely obtained from deep root nodules, traded fragments, or fallen material, worked with hammerstones of hardened heartwood rather than mined from open stone. Tools are practical, replaceable, and intimate with the body. They are made to be carried, hidden, repaired, or discarded without leaving permanent scars on the terrain.

Where people or person-like inhabitants appear in this landscape, they seem few. Their traces are scattered because the place is immense and because the ecosystem does not favor permanent, dense construction. To build too much is to invite growth, decay, insect attention, or structural failure. To clear too widely is to provoke the living substrate to close the gap. The wisest marks are light: a lashing here, a cut there, a small platform maintained for a season, a path kept open by repeated passage rather than by heavy engineering. The world tolerates brief habitation better than it tolerates domination.

The rules of the place follow from its materials. Water must be known by its hidden locations: which knot fills after rain, which pitcher plant can be tapped, which leaf cup holds clean condensation, which root swells store moisture through heat. Shelter must be grown, lashed, or found in hollows rather than quarried. Fire is both valuable and risky, because nearly everything is plant matter, even if much of it is damp, living, or too massive to ignite easily. Sound is not merely atmosphere but information: a change in the chorus may signal predators, weather, flowering, or the approach of a swarm. Movement must respect scale. Distances that look open are not necessarily safe or quick to cross, because the ground is uneven, alive, and full of hidden inhabitants.

There is a strange grandeur to it. The horizon is not made of stone mountains or open desert but of plant forms at the scale of landforms, spaced by emptiness and filled with noise. One can stand on a broad, warm bark plain and see nothing but scattered colossi, heat haze, and pale sky, while the sound of millions of unseen lives presses in from every direction. The landscape feels both empty and overfull: empty of crowded matter, full of presence. Sparse in structure, overwhelming in activity. Dry enough to feel exposed, damp enough to sustain hidden reservoirs. Hot, high, ancient, and alive in every direction.

It is a place where the botanical has become terrain, where fauna has become weather, and where silence is only ever a temporary gap between waves of sound.
