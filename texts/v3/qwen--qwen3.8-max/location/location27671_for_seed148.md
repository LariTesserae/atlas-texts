**The Hollow Nursery**

It is cool first, and dark; then damp.

Not wet in any generous or open way. The place has only intermittent moisture, a nervous, beading wetness that appears where air meets living surface. Condensation forms along the ribs of stems, gathers in the hollows of folded leaves, trembles, and falls in single drops. Sometimes a thin mist thickens under the upper lattice, and for a few minutes there is a private rain so localized that one side of an alley glistens while the other remains merely cold. Pockets of water collect in basins made of overlapping leaf-sheaths, in sealed root-knots, in industrial troughs lined with black, waterproof bark. These pools are small, still, and important. They are not rivers. They are reserves, cups, memories of water held inside a larger dryness.

The whole place is low. It lies sunk beneath the level of whatever surrounds it, a shallow basin or interior sump where cool air settles and refuses to leave. It is not vast in extent. You could cross it in a short time if the distances behaved. It has the scale of a grove-room, a cathedral made compact, a hamlet folded into a living enclosure. Its boundaries are definite: a ring of braided root-columns, a rampart of compressed culms, a curtain of hanging aerial fibers beaded with moisture. Beyond those edges there may be other conditions, but here the air is enclosed, held, slightly pressurized, as if the landscape is being kept in a state of careful incubation.

Everything that seems solid is plant.

That is the first true law of the place. Vegetation is not scattered across the terrain; vegetation is the terrain. The ground underfoot is a dense felt of roots, mossy filaments, flattened stems, and shed leaf-fiber compacted into something that yields slightly, then holds. It is not soil so much as living architecture pressed into service as floor. In some places it is spongy and dark; in others it has been polished by growth into a smooth, semi-vitrified surface like lacquered wood. Where stone would be expected, there is hardened pith. Where metal might appear, there are veins of bright sap, coppery strands drawn up by hyperaccumulator roots, or black cellulose slicked with moisture. The walls are woven culms, stacked leaf-stalks, laminated bark, living trunks fused into panels. Ceilings, where they exist, are made of overlapping dark fronds or interlaced vines from which condensation drips. The place is built out of itself.

Yet for all its vegetal substance, it is unmistakably urban.

There are streets. They run in partial grids and broken alignments, too regular to be natural, too quiet to be ordinary. They are paved with flattened root-planks and worn leafstone, their edges raised into low curbs that seem only recently formed. Alleys open between tall, hollow structures. Staircases ascend to platforms that may not yet exist. Terraces step down toward reservoirs no larger than courtyard pools. There are quays without water enough for boats, archways without buildings on the other side, plazas where nothing stands except a few thin poles of living wood. The settlement has the grammar of a town: zones, passages, thresholds, infrastructure, repetition. It has the feel of a place intended for many, but currently occupied by almost no one.

The built environment is layered. Below the walking surfaces, translucent ducts carry sap, condensed water, and pale threads of mycelium. At mid-level, walkways and railings of bent cane connect hollow blocks. Above, the space is crossed by suspended conduits, tension roots, and hanging organs of the city that might be lamps, vents, or immature towers. The layering is not crowded. It is spare, skeletal, full of intervals. The urban fabric has been stretched thin across a large volume, leaving great empty interiors, rooms mostly air, corridors with nothing in them but darkness and the faint smell of wet green wood.

Industry is present, but it has been made quiet and strange.

Engines stand in open sheds of woven stem, their bodies composed of polished heartwood, living gaskets, and chambers of translucent sap. Pistons rise and fall with a slow, biological patience. They do not clang; they breathe. Turbines made of layered leaf-vanes turn in currents of cool air or in the faint circulation of water through root-pipes. Boilers, if that is what they are, swell gently with heat generated not by fire but by metabolic intensity: dense knots of plant tissue fermenting, oxidizing, pressing energy into vascular systems. Electrical phenomena exist too, but they are subdued. Sparks travel along coppery root-threads in brief, dull flashes. Mosses glow faintly when charged. In one place, a row of seed-sized insulators clicks softly as current passes through them, each click no louder than a beetle tapping wood.

Factories line certain blocks, though they produce little that is obvious. Some extrude long ribbons of leaf-glass, dark and flexible, which harden into panels when touched by mist. Others press pollen, fiber, and mineral sap into bricks that remain soft for hours before becoming dense. Others draw out cables from inner bark, or secrete thin sheets of waterproof cuticle for roofing. One low structure appears to manufacture absence: it rhythmically opens its valves, exhales a cold breath, and leaves behind a newly hollowed room, empty as a held breath. Machinery here does not dominate the landscape. It is integrated into the plant body, half organ and half device, as if industrialization evolved from botany instead of replacing it.

The atmosphere is cool and dim. Darkness is the dominant light condition. This is not the darkness of night under open sky, but the darkness of enclosure, of deep foliage, of interiors without sun. What little illumination exists is weak and partial. Fungal veins emit a cold greenish glow along the sides of wet trunks. Bioluminescent slime traces the joints where sap pipes meet. Occasionally a furnace-heart deep inside a factory brightens to a dull amber, visible only as a stain through translucent bark. Some animals carry their own faint lights: a drifting moth with pale wing-edges, an eel in a black pool whose flank flashes blue once and is gone. These glows do not illuminate the place so much as reveal how much of it remains unseen.

Despite its emptiness, the landscape is abundant in fauna.

The animals are not large crowds in the open; they are presence distributed through cracks, pools, ducts, hollows, and the upper dark. Their biomass is low, their number high. They fill the place without filling it up. In the damp folds of root-felt, pale rootlice move in slow processions, cleaning surfaces with tiny mandibles. Beetles the color of wet bark patrol pipe joints, tapping them, listening for blockages. Slugs leave silver trails that evaporate quickly in the cool air. Worms threaded through living walls contract as vibrations pass, tightening the structures they inhabit.

In the water pockets, life is concentrated. Black leaf-basins contain fish almost without pigment, their bodies needle-thin, turning in unison when a drop strikes the surface. Blind tadpoles graze on bacterial films. Eels, or eel-like things, occupy the larger conduits, moving through pipe systems as if through veins, their bodies flexible enough to follow the city’s changing plumbing. In one reservoir, a pale frog sits motionless on a floating leaf-platform; when a gnat lands near it, the frog does not strike so much as arrive already having struck, a small predator folded into the rhythm of the place.

In the air, there are moths, gnats, lacewings, mantid-like hunters with narrow bodies, and flies whose wings make no sound. Some of these creatures pollinate structural organs, entering flower-valves and emerging dusted with adhesive spores. Some feed on excess growth, pruning new shoots before they can block walkways. Some are predators of other animals; others seem to prey on mistakes. A swarm of tiny transparent insects may gather around a newly formed doorway, tasting its geometry, then disperse, leaving the doorway slightly different. Bats or bat-like creatures roost in the upper conduits, but they are sparse and quiet, their wings opening and closing like dark leaves.

Ecology here is inseparable from construction.

The plants grow, and in growing they build. The animals maintain, and in maintaining they alter. Machinery accelerates both. A drip falls, and where it lands a rootlet thickens. A piston lifts, and a channel is pressed into soft sediment. A swarm feeds on a wall, and the wall responds by secreting a harder layer. A predator passes through an alley, and the alley becomes narrower or longer depending on what it hunted. Nothing is merely decorative. Even stillness seems functional.
