The place is called, by those who live inside it, the Dry Court.

From the high plateau outside, it is almost impossible to find. The land has been worn down by unimaginable age: mountains reduced to pale, level shoulders, old cities ground into mineral seams, ridges softened into long, horizontal silence. The world here has reached its late, settled shape. Nothing is sharp anymore except the wind. In one expanse of sun-baked stone and salt-dusted pavement there is only a narrow seam, a hairline shadow between ancient slabs, barely wide enough for a body to slip through sideways. Warm air breathes out of it, carrying dust and a dense, living smell.

Inside, the hidden pocket opens downward into a buried chamber no larger than a city block, though it contains a city’s worth of use.

It may once have been a cistern, an amphitheater, a market hall, or a temple court; its original purpose has been exhausted. Now it is a sealed dry hollow, roofed by fallen slabs and living growth, lit only by cracks, slots, and a few slanted shafts that never fully reach the floor. There is no water here. No pool, no drip, no damp stone, no mist, no rain-channel, no dark shine of moisture. The old word for “reservoir” has become a word for “room.” The floor is a compacted mixture of salt, powdered stone, leaf litter, insect wings, guano, resin, and generations of dried growth. It gives slightly underfoot like hardened felt.

The first thing a visitor notices is not the dim green light or the heat, but the sound.

The Dry Court is loud.

It is a loudness without water: no splash, no trickle, no wet chorus. Instead there is a dry, crowded roar made of wings, claws, rasping mouthparts, cracking seedpods, shifting dust, human voices, rope creaks, stone pebbles falling through old chutes, and the constant wind moving through hidden architectural throats. The chamber amplifies everything. A beetle taking flight becomes a small drumbeat. A flock shifting in the upper shadows becomes a sudden gust of applause. Arguments, calls, warnings, and laughter bounce off worn walls until the air itself seems occupied by noise.

The second thing noticed is the warmth.

Outside, the elevation gives the sky a thin, hard brightness, and the exposed plateau can be bitterly cold when wind passes over it. But the pocket holds heat. Stone stores the sun’s memory. Bodies crowd the ledges. Composting plant matter generates a slow, steady warmth. The air inside is close, dry, and comfortably heated, like the breath of an animal that has been lying in the sun. It is not humid. There is no wetness on the skin, no chill of evaporation. The warmth feels mineral and intimate: warmed dust, warmed resin, warmed feathers, warmed stone.

The third thing is the green.

By every expectation, the place should be barren. It is not. It is lush.

Vegetation fills every joint, ledge, crack, and ancient cornice. It grows in thick cushions, rosettes, hanging ropes, inflated bulbs, and waxy-leaved vines. The plants are dry to the touch, often armored or oily, but they are abundant and vigorous. Their colors are not the bright, careless green of wet places; they are sage, olive, bronze, gray-green, dark lacquer, and waxen blue. Many leaves are thick and sealed. Some are folded like shut hands. Some are covered in fine pale hairs that trap dust and reflect the strongest light. Others swell with sap or store moisture in translucent inner tissue that glows faintly when cut.

No plant drinks freely here. There is no soil in the ordinary sense, only a layered mulch of decay and mineral dust. Roots spread in nets over old masonry, dissolve ancient salts, penetrate cracked mortar, and mine bound moisture from crystal structures and buried organic veins. The vegetation lives by thrift and enclosure. When a stem is broken, sap beads slowly, then seals into resin. When leaves fall, they do not rot wetly; they dry, crack, and are eaten by beetles, moths, and fungi in sheltered pockets where metabolic heat loosens their fibers.

The lushness is vertical.

From the floor upward, the chamber is stacked with life. Low cushions of lichen-like growth cover the stones where the dimmest light gathers. Above them rise clusters of fat-stemmed shrubs with small hard leaves. Their trunks store sap, their bark peels in papery scrolls, and their flowers open only in the warmest part of the day, releasing a heavy, oily pollen that clings to insect legs. Vines climb the old walls in dense cables, some bearing seedpods that rattle when touched, others producing fleshy fruits that are dry outside and rich within. Hanging gardens of aerial rosettes grip cornices and broken arches. They collect dust, dead insects, and shed feathers, digesting whatever falls into their cupped centers.

In the brightest slots, narrow columns of light drop through the canopy and become crowded with airborne life. Dust motes hang thick. Winged insects move through the beams in shifting clouds. The light itself looks solid, a green-gold substance filtered through leaves and haze. Below the light shafts, shadows are warm and brown. In the deeper recesses, where ancient ceilings remain intact, the darkness is softened only by small fat-lamps, glowing embers in stone cages, and the faint cold gleam of larvae in certain undisturbed crevices.

The fauna is overwhelming.

It is not that the animals are all large. Most are small, quick, and numerous beyond counting. Beetles move everywhere: black, copper, ivory, striped, horned, flat, round, slow, darting. They are the chamber’s workers, breaking down litter, consuming dead plant matter, cleaning carcasses before desiccation can take them. Ants run in braided trails along ledges and rope bridges, carrying seeds, insect eggs, and fragments of dried skin. Moths rest in dense clusters on the undersides of leaves, rising in soft explosions when disturbed. There are flies that never touch water, bees or bee-like insects with dry bodies and pollen-dusted legs, crickets in the wall seams, and pale nymphs that live in the deep litter.

Reptiles rule many of the warm surfaces. Geckos and flat-bodied lizards press against sun-heated stone, their eyes tracking movement with quick mechanical turns. Some are tiny, no longer than a finger; others are broad and patient, waiting near nesting holes for young birds or careless mice. Whip-tailed hunters move through the vine ropes in the middle levels. In the driest corners, pale snakes slip between nests and seed stores, their scales whispering against old stone.

Birds occupy the upper chamber. They do not sing with the liquid sweetness of wet-country birds; their calls are sharp, rattling, metallic, or percussive. Swift-winged fliers hunt insects in the light shafts. Small, hard-billed birds feed on seeds and dry fruits, moving in quick family groups along the planted ledges.
