## The Dry Choir Below

Below the last shelf of black rock, the world stops being a surface and becomes a volume.

It is a sealed hollow under the planet’s skin, not quite a cave and not quite an ocean, because there is no water here and no sky. The ceiling is a continent of underslung stone, too high to see except as a change in pressure and warmth. The floor is a broken mineral field, recently opened, still carrying the violence of its making in every edge. Nothing has had time to become smooth. Nothing has been rounded by rain, softened by wind, or worn by flowing streams. The place is young in the way a fresh fracture is young: sharp, exact, and severe.

It is warm. Not a wet warmth, not a swampy or living warmth, but the stored heat of deep mineral bodies, a dry closeness that presses against skin and stone alike. The rock holds heat evenly. The dust holds it too. There is no chill anywhere, no draft, no falling current of cold air. Temperature does not vary enough to make weather in the ordinary sense. Yet the place is vast enough to have its own climates, or rather its own permanent arrangements of climate: zones of denser dust, shelves of stiller air, fields of static charge, pressure layers that feel like invisible floors. A traveler could move for days through one local condition and call it a season, though nothing changes.

There is no water.

That absence defines everything.

No pools sit in the hollows. No moisture beads on the stone. No damp dark smell rises from the depths. The minerals are dry to the point of seeming thirsty, though the landscape itself does not suffer thirst. It simply has no relationship to water. Salt crusts lie in pale sheets across the lower basins, but they are not the residue of vanished seas; they are mineral plains in their own right, crystalline and sealed. Veins of anhydrous crystal cut through basalt like frozen lightning. Glassy flows of dark volcanic substance stand in ribbons and slabs, their surfaces still brilliant, still freshly broken. Every shard can cut. Every crystal point is intact. The world looks newly made, yet it has already reached a strange permanence.

Darkness is the native condition.

It is not absolute darkness, but nearly. The eye, at first, finds nothing. Then it learns to interpret absence and faintness. There are dim suggestions of distance: a deeper black where a fissure opens, a pale ghosting where salt crust reflects even nonexistent light, a faint amber pulse far away in the dust. Some of the fauna give off weak luminescence, not bright enough to illuminate the landscape, only enough to mark themselves as presences. Certain minerals glow when disturbed, a brief blue triboluminescent shimmer when grains rub, crack, or shift under pressure. These flashes are rare and small. They do not reveal the place; they emphasize how much of it remains unseen.

The hollow is dense.

Not dense in the way of solid rock, but dense with occupancy. The medium filling the space is not air as an open sky knows it, but a warm, dry, mineral suspension: fine rock flour, salt dust, metallic particulates, shed fragments of carapace, powdered crystal, and living motes too small to name. It is thick enough to feel against the body. It is near-touching. Move through it and you are aware of layers: coarser grit near the lower floor, finer veils higher up, charged bands that cling to skin and hair, suspended strata where tiny organisms hang in enormous numbers. There is no empty room here. Even where nothing large is visible, the dark is full.

This density gives the place its quiet.

Sound does not ring. It is swallowed by dust, dampened by suspended particles, broken into short dull distances. There is no water to drip. No wind to moan. No plant life to rustle. The dominant sound is almost nothing: a faint granular hush, the softest possible abrasion of mineral motes touching one another. Beneath that, if you listen, there are the small sounds of abundant life. Dry clicks. Tiny percussive taps. The scrape of a crystalline limb against stone. A papery shifting of membranes. A low, almost subsonic pulse from something too large to see. Occasionally a clear glassy note sounds when a spine or claw strikes a fresh crystal edge, and then the silence closes over it again.

Nothing here is green.

There is no vegetation. No photosynthetic film, no root-thread, no leaf-analogue, no stalk or spore-frond seeking light. The world is mineral and animal, or things that resemble animals more than plants. What grows here grows as crust, lattice, shell, spine, membrane, colony, or swarm. Some surfaces are furred with tiny crystalline projections, but these are not plants; they are either mineral accretions or the feeding structures of sessile fauna. Other surfaces seem polished, until touched, and then they move. The boundary between terrain and creature is unreliable.

The fauna are abundant.

They do not live as surface animals live. They are not dependent on drinking, on green food, on sunlight, or on wet chemistry. Their biology is stranger: dry-lattice bodies, bound internal gels, ion paths through glassy membranes, metabolisms tuned to heat differentials, pressure, mineral charge, and the faint electrical potentials generated when crystals flex. They feed on charged dust, on thin mineral films, on one another, on the energy stored in fresh fractures, on the residual warmth of the planet’s interior. They are slow in some ways and sudden in others. Their abundance is not an abundance of frenzy, but of saturation. There are many bodies per volume. Many presences. Many niches packed close together in the dark.

Some of the commonest forms are almost invisible. They drift in the suspended mineral haze as tiny angular bodies, like living shards, turning slowly in place. Others form sheets over rock faces, no thicker than a fingernail, grazing microscopic charge from the stone. Larger creatures move through the denser dust as if through water, though they do not swim in any liquid sense. They filter, sieves, or lure. There are broad flat things like rays made of overlapping dark plates, gliding along boundaries between dust layers. There are worm-like forms segmented like carved beads, each segment bearing fine comb-structures that gather mineral grains. There are hollow-bodied floaters, translucent and faintly glowing, trailing charged filaments through the dark. There are urchin-like clusters of mineral spines that roll slowly across salt crusts, cracking tiny crystals beneath themselves to release charge.

Some fauna are colonial. A seeming drift of dust may be thousands of minute organisms moving in agreement. A ledge may be a sleeping congregation. A spire may be built not from stone alone but from the interlocked bodies of creatures that have gone still for long enough to be mistaken for architecture. This is part of the place’s strangeness: the terrain is not entirely inert, and the life is not entirely separate from the mineral world. The sharp young rocks are surrounded by organisms that treat stone as food, shelter, signal, and extension of the body.

The largest creatures are felt more than seen. They occupy pressure layers far overhead or rest in basins where the dust is thickest. Their movements are rare and slow, but when they shift, the suspension around them changes density. A traveler might experience this as a sudden thickening of the dark, a new resistance against the face, a change in the way sound carries, or a faint rise in static charge. There is no panic in these events. They are not attacks. They are simply the movements of vast bodies inside a vast system.

The place has weather, but weather without time.

Because the hollow is so large, its internal medium arranges itself into regions. One zone may be finer and quieter, with dust so thin it behaves like dry smoke. Another may be crowded with floating motes and suspended organisms, so dense that movement becomes labor. Elsewhere, static tension collects along crystal ridges until the air itself seems to hold its breath. These regions do not pass like storms. They do not alternate by day or night. They remain. They are climatic facts, not events. To cross from one layer to another is to enter a different local physics: different buoyancy, different visibility, different sound, different proximity of living things. The weather here is spatial, not temporal. It is a map, not a forecast.

This stability is profound.

The landscape is in equilibrium. No gradient sharpens. No season turns. No erosion begins. No moisture condenses. No wind builds. No tide arrives. The young sharpness of the terrain persists because there is nothing to wear it down. A flake struck from a core remains exactly where it falls until another body moves it. A footprint in the dust may stay for ages, not because the dust is hard, but because nothing disturbs it. The world has the newness of a recent fracture and the stillness of a sealed tomb. It is both freshly made and finished.

Within this unchanged dark, there are marks of making.

Not cities. Not roads. Not machines. The built things are scattered, small, and primitive. They appear as low cairns of selected stone flakes, arranged with deliberate balance. Shallow scrapes in the mineral floor are lined with polished fragments, not for comfort but for some signal or use. There are shelters made from shed plates of fauna, propped together like dry shells. There are frames of bone or chitin lashed with twisted fiber, though the fiber is not plant-made; it comes from sinew-analogues, spun filaments, or peeled membranes of animal origin. Occasional artifacts lie half-buried in the dust: awls of native copper hammered cold, hooks carved from dense bone, shallow cups made from vertebrae, needles with eyes drilled by patient abrasion, simple picks of obsidian hafted to grips of layered hide-like material.

These structures are not numerous. They are scattered across ledges, tucked beneath overhangs, placed near charge-rich crystal seams, or positioned at the edges of denser dust banks. Some are temporary. Some seem old, though age is hard to judge where nothing weathers. The tools suggest hand-work: scraping, chipping, binding, polishing, patient repetition. Metal is rare and simple, worked by hammering rather than casting. Fire, if it exists here at all, is not a common tool; there is little fuel and no need for flame. Instead, the makers use geothermal warmth, stored body heat, glow-fauna in hollow shells, and the faint luminescence of disturbed minerals. Their technology is intimate and tactile, made for darkness and close work.

It is not always clear whether the makers are a separate kind of fauna or merely the most dexterous branch of the local life. They leave signs rather than settlements. Sometimes their structures seem inhabited. Sometimes they seem ceremonial. Sometimes they seem to be traps for dust-creatures, or lures, or listening devices. A row of bone pins may mark a boundary. A stack of glass flakes may be a cache. A shallow bowl of salt may collect charged motes. The meaning is not obvious. The place does not explain itself.

The rules of this landscape are not surface rules.

Distance is measured less by horizon than by density. A region of open, thin dust may allow swift movement, while a nearby band of crowded suspension slows the body and muddles direction. Light is not the primary sense. Navigation is by pressure, texture, static pull, sound return, and the behavior of small fauna. A person standing still becomes part of the local geometry. Creatures may settle near a motionless body, mistaking it for stone, or may test it with fine filaments until it moves.

Metal is noticeable here. A simple copper awl or iron-rich tool can gather faint charge, attract dust, or create a small tick of static when moved. The primitive makers may use this property deliberately: a hung sliver of metal becomes a signal, a lure, or a warning. In the dark, a tool can announce you before your body does.

Because there is no free water, any moisture is alien. Sweat, breath-moisture, blood, or tears would be anomalies in this dry equilibrium. They would not belong. The local fauna might respond to such things with curiosity rather than aggression, drawn by the strangeness of a substance their world does not contain. A drop of water here would be more conspicuous than a flame. It would not refresh the landscape. It would violate it.

The world feels crowded even when nothing can be seen.

That is its deepest character. It is not empty darkness. It is full darkness. Full of mineral, full of warmth, full of suspended matter, full of waiting bodies, full of small lives arranged in layers. The silence is not the silence of absence but of saturation. The sharp ground, the dense medium, the faint glows, the scattered tools, the unchanged temperature, the sealed scale—all of it produces a place that feels complete to the point of pressure.

To stand there is to be inside a vast, warm, dry organ of the planet. The walls are too far to matter. The ceiling is a fact, not a sight. The dust touches you. The fauna are near. The artifacts, when found, are small and quiet underhand. Nothing erodes. Nothing softens. Nothing flows. The landscape remains new and final, dark and abundant, mostly silent, endlessly still.

It is a mineral world with a pulse.

Not a pulse of water, or wind, or green growth, but of packed presences in the dark: a dry, crowded, warm, unchanging deep, where life has found its strange footing and the ground itself seems half-alive.
