It is a high, dry, city-sized emptiness.

The landscape exists at an extreme altitude, above the level where weather becomes familiar. There is no water here in any meaningful sense: no liquid, no dampness, no ice, no dew, no memory of rain. The air is cool and thin, and so desiccated that it feels less like atmosphere and more like a fine mineral vapor held against the face. Breathing it, if one can breathe it at all, gives the sensation of inhaling powdered stone, cold glass, and old static.

The terrain is open, regional, and terraced — a vast high basin or stepped plateau extending far beyond immediate sight. It is not a wilderness of random rock, but neither is it a city in any ordinary sense. It is both. The ground has been organized into broad levels, long platforms, sunken avenues, dry plazas, and stairways that climb from one mineral shelf to the next. The scale is urban: districts, causeways, retaining walls, processional routes, hollow towers, amphitheaters, and roofless blocks arranged with the logic of settlement. Yet there are no devices, no machines, no tools, no wires, no engineered systems. Nothing here was built by technology. The construction is older and stranger than that: grown, deposited, secreted, settled, or perhaps precipitated by some ancient non-aqueous process that left behind the pattern of habitation without leaving instruments.

The materials are mineral and severe. Salt-white stone, pale ashite, black volcanic glass, pocked pumice, dull basalt, and crystalline crusts make up the terraces and walls. Many surfaces are weathered smooth, not by water but by long ages of wind, dust, thermal stress, and electrostatic abrasion. The erosion is established and balanced. Nothing looks freshly broken. Edges have been softened into flutes and grooves. Towers lean slightly as if they have been standing so long they have become part of the wind’s grammar. Stair treads are worn not by feet but by the slow passage of charged grains. The place has the patience of a form that has remained in its own state for epochs.

There is almost no density. The city is empty in a way that feels voluminous. Streets are wide and unoccupied. Plazas hold nothing but fine powder. Colonnades stand around silence. Between one presence and the next there may be hundreds of meters, then kilometers, of open, cool, dim space. It is not ruined so much as depopulated, or perhaps it was never populated by anything that could be counted easily. The emptiness is not inert. It is sparse. There is room here for processes too slow and too large to notice immediately.

The light is dim. The sun, if there is one, appears as a muted disc through high mineral haze, casting weak copper, gray-violet, and pewter tones across the terraces. The sky is not fully bright even at its brightest. It shades from dusty lavender near the horizon to a nearly black blue overhead, where faint stars remain visible despite the day. Shadows are soft but deep. Surfaces do not glitter with moisture; they glow faintly with crystalline dust, and in darker recesses the stone gives off a cold, phosphorescent suggestion, as if the city remembers light more than it receives it.

No plant life exists. There is no green, no root, no leaf, no pollen, no wood. Yet the place is not entirely without simpler biology, or whatever passes for biology in a world without water. In shaded cracks, beneath overhangs, and along the undersides of terraces grow pale felts and brittle crusts: mineral mycelia, static lichens, crystalline mats that are neither fungus nor stone but something closer to organized chemistry. They feed on temperature gradients, charge differentials, and the slow deposition of airborne minerals. They do not grow toward light so much as toward stability. In some places they form intricate skin-like maps across walls; in others they appear as white seams in black glass, pulsing so faintly that one might mistake the movement for eye strain.

Fauna is rare. One may travel a long time and see nothing but dust and architecture. But occasionally there are presences: translucent mites the size of hands moving along charged surfaces; thin, flat creatures like mica moths that do not fly so much as glide on thermal sheets; long pale worms of compressed mineral that inhabit cracks and emerge only when the temperature shifts. Some of them graze on the crystalline felts. Others hunt, if hunting can mean waiting for centuries beside a likely path until a smaller organism passes within reach. A predator here might be almost indistinguishable from a wall: a broad, camouflaged, lithic patience that contracts once per season. The ecosystem moves at a mineral pace, but it is not still.

Sound is scarce. The landscape is dominated by silence, but not absolute silence. There are quiet sounds: the whisper of fine powder sliding across stone; the soft tick of cooling crystal; a faint hiss when electrostatic charge lifts dust from a plaza; the dry rasp of something moving where nothing should move. Loud noises seem wrong here, not forbidden but incompatible, as if the air lacks the generosity to carry them. A footfall would be muffled. A voice would fall short. The city absorbs sound the way it absorbs distance.

The place is active, though its activity is not violent. Multiple processes are in motion across long intervals. Cool thermal tides move through the avenues as the dim light shifts. Dust rises from sunward terraces and settles in shadowed depressions. Static blooms make grains climb walls in thin silver threads. Mineral crusts grow by fractions. Wind polishes exposed edges. Hollow towers act as resonators, drawing in fine particles and exhaling them elsewhere. The rare fauna migrate between warm and cool surfaces, following invisible gradients through the urban maze. Even the architecture seems to participate: certain stairways become more pronounced after dust has settled; certain doorways vanish until a change in charge reveals them. The city breathes without lungs, without water, without machinery.

The strangeness is severe. Reality at this elevation and in this dryness is not entirely settled. Distances behave poorly. A tower that appears nearby may require a long descent, a crossing, and a return before it can be reached. A street may slope downward in both directions. Some terraces seem to exist only when approached indirectly; look straight at them and their outlines thin, becoming uncertain or duplicating into three or four possible versions. Gravity has local opinions. In certain plazas, dust falls upward in slow spirals. In some stairwells, the next step is not the one expected but one adjacent in memory. Echoes occasionally arrive before the sound that caused them, though there is rarely enough sound to echo.

This is not magic in the decorative sense. It is a breakdown of reliable relations between matter, distance, and observation. The world here has become too high, too dry, too empty, and too old to maintain ordinary continuity. The city persists because it is arranged in patterns that accommodate instability. Its streets are not fixed lines but probabilities that settle briefly under attention. Its buildings are less objects than agreements among dust, charge, and stone. To move through it, one would have to abandon insistence. Direct purpose might fail. Tools might become meaningless. A device would not merely be useless; it would likely be rejected by the local logic, because the place has no framework for engineered causality.

The built environment reinforces this unease. The urban forms are recognizable enough: there are thresholds, walls, elevated walkways, enclosed courtyards, broad ceremonial routes, and layered construction rising in tiers. But their function is unclear. No market squares, no dwellings, no temples, no roads meant for wheels. There are basins that hold powder instead of water. There are channels that guide dust instead of rivers. There are high balconies overlooking nothing. There are doorways that open onto other doorways. The city feels designed for gathering, but for gatherings that do not use bodies, or perhaps for bodies that occur only rarely, in vast intervals, like comets.

At times the horizon itself seems to fold. From certain points on the upper terraces, the basin appears to curve upward at the edges, as if the landscape is a shallow bowl seen from inside its own reflection. The far districts may seem to recede into haze, then suddenly sharpen, revealing every stone with impossible clarity. The dim light does not cast consistent shadows. Some shadows point away from the sun. Others remain fixed regardless of the light’s position. In the deepest empty spaces, the absence of presence becomes almost architectural: a hollow district, a vacant plaza, a silent avenue made of nothing but scale.

And yet it remains cool, quiet, and established. There is no frenzy here, no collapse, no storm of destruction. The breakdown of reality is not chaotic; it is weathered. It has settled into its own balance. The city exists in a long, low, active equilibrium: dust moving, charge shifting, crusts spreading, rare creatures passing through, light dimming and returning, distances folding and unfolding. It is an urban landscape without inhabitants, a mineral ecology without plants, a high-altitude world without water, a living pattern without technology, a place whose activity is almost silence and whose silence is almost structure.

If one stood there, one would feel exposed, cooled, and subtly displaced. The air would feel thin against the skin. The ground would be hard, dry, and ancient. The dim city would extend in every direction, terraced and vacant, waiting without urgency. Something might move far away — a flicker of pale mineral life crossing a plaza, or a wall deciding to become a passage. Then stillness again. Then the soft slide of dust. Then the sense that the landscape is not empty because nothing is there, but because what is there refuses to be counted.
