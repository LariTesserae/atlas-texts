The first thing is the sound.

It arrives before the shape of the place resolves: a deep, wet, continuous roar, like a city standing inside a waterfall that has learned to speak. Then the eye adjusts, and the landscape opens—low, warm, shining, endless.

It is a vast urban basin, a settled country of stone and water without a single plant. There is no green here. No grass, no tree, no vine, no leaf, no pollen. The world is mineral, fungal, animal, and built. Everything else has been shaped around water, heat, density, and motion.

The land lies low. It feels sunk into the world, not in a catastrophic way, but as if the whole region has always been a receiving place: a broad, warm catchment where moisture gathers and remains. The horizon does not rise into hills so much as it thickens into distance—tier after tier of roofs, terraces, cisterns, aqueducts, bridges, sluices, and stacked dwellings, all softened by humidity and age. The elevation is slight enough that water never fully leaves. It sits near the surface. It beads on walls. It gathers under gratings. It breathes through the city in slow, warm pulses.

The light is ordinary. That is one of the strangest things about it. The sun is normal, the sky is normal, the pale gold-white glare on wet stone is normal. There is no exotic color, no theatrical gloom. Just warm daylight filtered through a dense, humid atmosphere, turning the air into a faint luminous veil. Reflections multiply everywhere: on glazed brick, on dark canal water, on polished metal fittings, on the slick shoulders of old ceramic walls. The city glitters not with jewels but with moisture.

Water is not scenery here. It is infrastructure, weather, schedule, and habit.

Rain falls often, but not always from the sky. Much of it seems to come from the place itself. The built surfaces sweat. The stone weeps. Warm air condenses against shaded walls, and droplets gather along pipe seams, cornices, railings, and the undersides of bridges. There are frequent brief squalls born from the city’s own heat and evaporation: local storms that boil up over crowded districts, spill hard for a few minutes, then collapse into steaming mist. The streets are built to receive this. They are grooved, sloped, channeled, and pierced with drains. Water moves through them in sheets, ribbons, and sudden surges.

At the edges of the denser districts, the city frays into something like a marsh, except it is a marsh without plants. There are shallow flooded plazas, warm mineral pools, gravel shoals, salt crusts, rust-colored bacterial films, and black fungal felts stretched across submerged steps. Instead of reeds, there are ceramic pilings, corroded bronze rails, collapsed arcades, and clusters of water-smoothed stone. Instead of grass, there are microbial mats, slick threads, and living crusts that metabolize warmth, minerals, and dissolved waste. The water is not clear and not dirty in any simple sense. It is rich, worked, circulated. It carries heat, sound, nutrients, and microscopic life.

The temperature is warm, close to the skin. The air feels thick but not unbearable, more like a bathhouse with the door left open than a desert or a swamp. It moves constantly. Warm drafts rise from roof engines and sunlit terraces; cooler air is pulled through shaded canal corridors; moisture condenses, falls, evaporates, and rises again. The place has its own weather because it is large enough to make one. Heat islands form over dense market levels. Vapor columns climb from industrial cisterns. Sudden breezes carry spray, salt, fungal spores, and the metallic smell of wet machinery.

There is no wilderness outside the city so much as there are different intensities of city. The built environment is continuous, layered, and ancient in its settled form. It has existed long enough to have been worn into balance. The erosion is not violent. It is the erosion of use: steps hollowed by countless feet, stone edges rounded by water, metal polished by touch, surfaces darkened by mineral stains, old repairs fused into the whole. Nothing looks newly made, but nothing looks abandoned either. The landscape has the calm of a system that has found its own operating rhythm.

The materials are heavy, tactile, and water-tuned. Basalt. Salt-glazed ceramic. Porous pumice-stone. Oxidized bronze. Glassy slag blocks. Compressed mineral brick. Pale calcium panels. Copper mesh. Black iron. Surfaces are often glazed where water needs to run, rough where traction is needed, porous where evaporation is desired, sealed where storage matters. Many walls have a faint sheen, not from polish but from perpetual dampness. Some surfaces seem almost alive: self-healing mortars that close hairline cracks, mineral coatings that slowly re-crystallize, smart ceramics that change their capillary behavior with temperature, thin metallic membranes that pull moisture from the air.

The technology is advanced enough to feel municipal rather than miraculous. It is everywhere, but it does not announce itself. There are acoustic sluice gates that open and close with shaped sound instead of moving parts. There are pressure-sensitive floorstones that redirect flow when too much weight gathers. There are maintenance swarms no larger than beetles, moving along pipe joints and wall seams, cleaning mineral buildup, tightening fixtures, carrying away debris. There are heat exchangers buried beneath streets, condensation towers integrated into rooflines, and deep channels where warm water circulates through the city’s foundations. Some of the engineering feels beyond the reach of ordinary physics—subtle gravity assists in the largest drains, materials with internal structures too fine to see, surfaces that seem to decide what to absorb and what to shed—but the inhabitants treat it as routine. It is plumbing, not wonder.

The city is packed. Not merely crowded, but saturated. There is no unused interval. Every gap has a purpose, every surface a tenant, every volume a second use. A wall is not a wall alone: it is wall, pipe, conduit, roosting bracket, fungal bed, sensor lace, advertisement plate, shrine niche, and moisture trap. A bridge is not merely a crossing: it is a market spine, a water carrier, a roost, a lookout, a cable route, and a shelter for hanging dwellings beneath its arch. Staircases double as spillways. Rooftops double as condensers. Undercroft spaces double as nurseries for aquatic animals or storage tanks. Even the air feels occupied: crossed by flight paths, transit cables, signal hums, drifting maintenance devices, and the endless movement of winged fauna.

This density does not read as chaos. It reads as compression. The place has too much life, too much water, too much movement, and too much construction for emptiness to survive. Everything has been folded into everything else.

The architecture rises in layers. Older levels lie below, smooth and water-darkened, their openings widened by age and flow. Newer levels above are tighter, busier, more intricately serviced, full of hanging tanks, awnings, ducts, and temporary stalls. Between them are intermediate floors, mezzanines, bridges, and external galleries. Some districts are mostly residential: stacked rooms with wet cloths drying over rails, window boxes filled not with plants but with fungal cultures or mineral filters, doorways darkened by humidity and human use. Others are industrial: cistern yards, heat vents, drying racks for fungal sheets, vats, filtration halls, and humming turbine courts. Others still are commercial: bazaars that spill across platforms and down stairwells, where goods are shouted, signed, or pulsed through bone-conduction devices because ordinary speech cannot survive the noise.

The sound is deafening.

It is not one sound but many sounds fused into a physical pressure. Water rushing through open channels. Water striking stone. Water falling from roof chains into basins. Sluice gates groaning or ringing as they shift. The low blast of pressure valves releasing steam. The clatter of markets. The creak of packed crowds moving over wet wood, metal, and ceramic. The cries of animals. The hum of engines. The resonant tolling of large civic bells, not for ceremony but for coordination: flood warnings, gate changes, roost clearances, transit openings. The architecture amplifies all of it. Curved walls focus sound. Canal surfaces carry it. Dense stone reflects it back. In some places, the noise is so continuous that silence would feel more alarming than any alarm.

People communicate in adapted ways. Raised voices work only at close range. Hand signs, gestures, patterned lights, and small personal transmitters carry meaning through the roar. In the oldest districts, there are even floor-rhythms: coded taps sent through structural beams, used by workers who maintain the deep drains. Sound is not merely heard here. It is a material condition, like heat or damp.

And yet the place is abundantly alive.

The absence of plant life does not mean sterility. It means the base of the living world is elsewhere. Fungal mats spread across warm, shaded walls in black, brown, and rust-colored layers. Microbial films coat the interiors of slow-moving drains. Chemotrophic crusts grow around mineral seeps. Engineered biofilms line nutrient channels, converting warmth, dissolved compounds, and atmospheric carbon into edible sludge. These are the greenless foundations of the ecology. From them rises a dense, noisy, restless fauna.

There are insects everywhere, though not in the simple plague sense. Some are small and glittering, with lacquer-dark shells adapted to wet stone. Some are soft-bodied larvae that live in canal filters and become winged adults in brief, roaring emergences. Moth-like creatures cluster around heat vents. Midges hang in clouds above fungal beds. Beetles with flattened bodies move through narrow wall seams, eating spores, waste, and dead fungal matter. There are aquatic crustaceans in the gutters—small, quick, pale things that scavenge organic particles and are themselves hunted by fish, birds, and amphibians.

The water teems. Eel-like animals move through the deeper channels, their bodies sleek and muscular, adapted to warm mineral-rich flow. Flat-bodied bottom feeders scrape bacterial films from submerged stone. Small shoals of filter feeders rise where nutrient-rich water is released from processing towers. In the wider flooded basins at the city’s margins, there are larger aquatic animals: slow armored scavengers, amphibious predators with blunt heads, and occasional massive filterers kept in check by civic systems. None of these are decorative. They are workers in the city’s metabolism. They clean, consume, convert, and are consumed in turn.

Birds, or bird-like fauna, occupy the upper architecture in huge numbers. They nest in every available cavity: under eaves, inside abandoned ducts, behind shuttered vents, in the hollow cores of old towers. Some are small and swift, catching insects in the humid air. Others are heavy-bodied and social, roosting in dense communal shelves, their bodies adding heat and noise to entire façades. There are pale water-birds that walk the edges of cisterns, hunting small aquatic animals. There are dark, oil-feathered divers that drop from canal bridges. Their droppings are collected, washed, and fed back into nutrient systems. Nothing is allowed to remain waste for long.

Amphibians thrive in the warmer, quieter pools: broad-bodied, loud, perpetually moist creatures whose calls add another layer to the din. They are especially common in the lower levels, where condensation gathers and the light is dimmer. There are also mammals, or mammal equivalents: rats, or rat-like animals, clever and numerous; small climbers that move through pipe spaces; larger domesticated creatures used for hauling, heat, or protein in certain districts. The fauna are not separate from the city. They are part of its furniture, its sanitation, its economy, and its sound.

Because there are no plants, animals and fungi occupy roles that would elsewhere be shared with greenery. Fungal beds provide texture, shelter, food, and surface chemistry. They soften hard edges.
