At the bottom of a low, warm basin lies a city of wet stone, shallow water, and constant noise. It is not a green place. There are no grasses on the margins, no reeds in the channels, no trees on the terraces. The world here is mineral and animal: pale crusts of deposited lime, dark basalt grit, wet clay, salt bloom, slick microbial films, and the accumulated bodies and works of countless creatures. The air is thick, close, and dim, as if a rainstorm has just passed but never actually arrived. Water is everywhere — standing in thin sheets, seeping from walls, condensing on ceilings, running through gutters, welling up through joints in the stone.

The basin is low, sunk beneath the surrounding land, and the water table lies almost at the surface. In places the ground is not quite ground yet: it is a crust over mud, a newly hardened skin of sinter, a soft white stone still chalky under the thumb. The landscape looks freshly made, not eroded into mature hills or valleys. Its forms are embryonic — low steps, half-formed terraces, shallow pans, narrow rills that have not yet cut deep, broad flats where water spreads and hesitates. Yet this newness is not a prelude to some later shape. The place has no future form waiting beneath it. It is held in a steady condition, always coming into being, never becoming different.

The city fills the basin in dense layers. It is not a city of streets alone, but of waterways, footbridges, ramps, quays, stepped platforms, fish pens, settling tanks, cisterns, sluices, roost towers, drying racks, and low buildings packed close together. Structures rise from the wet ground and overhang the channels. They are built of the materials available: porous limestone, rough sinter, black volcanic stone, fired clay, shell, bone, hardened hides, woven gut, and soft native copper worked with hammers and anvils. There is no timber. No plant provides fiber, plank, or beam. Where a flexible material is needed, sinew, hide, hair, or long strands of fungal cordage serve. Where strength is needed, stone is shaped, stacked, or grown.

Because the stone itself is still forming, construction is a kind of husbandry. Workers guide mineral-rich water along clay channels and into shallow molds. The water deposits fine layers of pale crust around frames of stitched hide, ribs of bone, or smooth stones set as cores. In other places, they cut soft newly precipitated rock before it hardens, lifting blocks that are light, damp, and easily shaped. The city is therefore both built and precipitated. It is made by hands, but also by water. Yet its overall shape does not change. Each day, mineral skin forms; each day, it is scraped, cut, redirected, or harvested. Each day, the same gates are opened, the same pools kept at the same level, the same walls kept at the same thickness. The place is under permanent construction and permanent completion at once.

The built density is high. Buildings touch, overlap, lean into one another. Upper platforms rest on lower roofs. Ladders and ramps connect levels where bridges cannot fit. Nets hang over water channels. Baskets and traps are wedged into stone niches. Everywhere there are presences: human bodies moving through narrow passages, animals on ledges, birds in upper galleries, fish in the darker water below, insects in the warm mist. The city is saturated with life and use. There is little empty space. Even the walls are occupied — by nesting creatures, by hanging tools, by shells sorted into hollows, by lamps set into damp recesses.

Technology remains simple. No engines turn here. There are no complex machines, no metal frames, no great wheels. Work is done by hand, lever, counterweight, rope, and flowing water. Stone gates are lifted with ropes of braided gut. Clay plugs stop and open small channels. Copper hooks, bone needles, shell scrapers, basalt hammers, and polished stone adzes do most of the daily work. Fire is precious and careful: small hearths burn animal fat and dried fungal matter, but large wood-fires are impossible. Light comes from shallow oil lamps, their flames low and yellow, reflected endlessly in wet surfaces. In the dimness, the city glitters faintly, as though the stone itself is sweating light.

The warmth is constant. The air feels like a bathhouse, heavy and soft against the skin. It smells of wet mineral crust, warm mud, fish, shell, guano, lamp oil, and the faint sourness of crowded life. There is no chill, no frost, no season. The dimness is not the darkness of deep night but the gray-brown dimness of permanent vapor. Overhead, the sky is a muted sheet of steam and low cloud. Sunlight enters only as a diffuse pressure, barely enough to cast shadows. In inner courts and covered water-lanes, lamps burn all the time.

Water organizes everything. It is not violent here, but it is ceaseless. It seeps from the sides of terraces. It beads on ceilings and drops into shallow basins. It condenses on the cool faces of stone walls and runs down in thin threads. It wells up through porous floors in places where the basin’s hidden springs are close. The city is built to receive it, divide it, slow it, settle it, and send it onward. Channels are the true streets. Some carry clear warm water; others carry clouded mineral flow; others are dark with organic waste from fish pens or roosts. Sluice gates, clay lips, and stone weirs keep the levels exact. If one channel silts, another is opened. If one pool deepens by a finger’s width, laborers skim or redirect the flow until it returns to its old mark.

There is no plant life because the place refuses it. The water is too mineral, too warm, too restless; the surfaces are too often coated, flooded, or encrusted; the light is too weak. Nothing green takes root. Even if a seed were carried in, it would drown, salt out, or be buried by fresh crust before it could establish. The living base of the ecosystem is simpler than vegetation: bacterial films, microbial mats, pale fungal threads, and microscopic organisms coating every damp surface. These feed small animals, and the small animals feed larger ones.

Fauna is abundant to the point of crowding. The water holds pale amphipods, small crustaceans, thin-shelled snails, wriggling larvae, white grubs, little fish, eels, and blind bottom-feeders that move through the deeper channels. Crabs and isopods work the edges of pools. On wet stone, beetles and flightless insects gather. Midges rise in clouds from the shallows, their bodies darkening the air near standing water. Frogs and toads, sleek and warm-adapted, occupy the damper ledges. Bats move through the dim upper spaces, taking insects in quick passes. Birds nest on stone lips, under bridges, in the hollow towers built for them: swallows, martins, fish-catching terns, and noisy waterfowl that rest on platforms because there are no reeds to hold their nests. Their cries are part of the city’s constant sound. Their droppings whitewash many roofs and ledges, and workers harvest them carefully for use in tanning, curing, and fungal cultivation.

Animal life is not merely present; it is layered. Fish move beneath walkways. Insects film the surface of pools. Birds wheel through shafts of dim light. Crabs scrape at stone in shallow tanks. Human life is woven into this density. The inhabitants fish with hand-nets and simple weirs, collect eggs from roost towers, harvest insect larvae in woven trays, dry shellfish on heated stone, and cultivate fungal beds in warm cellars fed by animal waste and mineral drip. Nothing is wild in the sense of being untended, but nothing is fully domesticated either. The city is a managed ecology, a dense and wet communal machine of stone, water, and animals.

The sound is deafening. It is not a single roar but a stacked, saturated noise: water falling over lips, water trickling into pools, water slapping against stone, water gurgling in blocked channels. Above that are the cries of birds, the rustle of wings, the chirring and humming of insects, the splash of fish and frogs, the scraping of shell on stone, the calls of workers, the clack of bone tools, the thud of stone hammers, the creak of hide ropes, the rattle of baskets and nets. Echoes multiply in the close construction. Sound reflects from wet stone, from low ceilings, from water surfaces, from packed walls. There is no quiet district, no hour of silence. The noise has no rhythm of crescendo and decline; it remains at the same full level, a dense acoustic pressure that fills the basin.

Because of the sound, the city has developed other ways of communication. Short gestures, hand signals, touches, and body positions carry meaning. When voices are used, they are close and loud. Children learn to read the movement of water and the behavior of animals before they learn to speak clearly in the noise. The deafness of the place is not an accident; it is part of its equilibrium. Water must flow, animals must live, tools must strike, and all of it happens continuously.

The city’s rules emerge from these conditions. Nothing may be allowed to dry out completely, because drying cracks the young stone and disrupts the channels. Nothing may be allowed to accumulate beyond its appointed level, because extra mineral crust narrows the waterways. Nothing may be wasted, because there are no plants to absorb surplus and no soil to bury refuse. Waste is fed to fungal beds, fish pools, insect troughs, or salt pans. Dead animals are processed for oil, shell, bone, hide, or fertilizer. Broken stone is broken again and used as fill. Every repair replaces something in the same place. Every addition is balanced by a removal.

This is why the place never changes, despite being always under construction. The map of the city is old, but its surfaces are always young. The same terraces exist as they have for generations, but their stone is new. The same pools hold the same volume of water. The same roost towers house the same number of birds, give or take individual lives. The same amount of noise fills the basin. The same dim light presses down through warm vapor. The same workers scrape the same amount of fresh crust from the same walls. There is no history of flood here, no story of drought, no memory of a greater age, no expectation of collapse. The landscape is a stable, saturated, warm, wet equilibrium.

To stand in it is to feel surrounded on all sides. The air touches the skin like warm water. The ground is never fully solid, never fully dry. The stone is pale, rough, damp, and newly formed. The channels are crowded with moving shapes. The noise presses into the chest as much as the ears. The light is low, amber where lamps burn, gray where the vapor admits the sky. There is no horizon of green, no softness of leaves, no smell of soil. There is only wet mineral world, animal abundance, hand-made structure, and the constant, deafening labor of water maintaining itself in exactly the same place.
