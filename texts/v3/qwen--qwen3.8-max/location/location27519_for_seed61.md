## The Pocket City of Weeping Stone

Below a cold, wet plain where water gathers in shallow mineral pools and nothing green takes root, there is a hidden place beneath the surface. It is not a cave system, not a tunnel, not a natural hall that opens into grandeur. It is a pocket: one contained chamber, one wet hollow worn into the rock below the water table. You would enter it through a narrow fissure no wider than a shoulder, down a few hand-cut steps slick with condensation, and then the world would suddenly become dense, loud, and close.

The chamber is small enough to feel like the inside of a vessel. It has no horizon, no far weather, no sky. Its ceiling is low in places, vaulted in others, and everywhere it weeps. Water does not arrive here as rain. It arrives as seepage, sweat, bead, and thread. The rock above is saturated, so moisture condenses on the ceiling and upper walls, gathers into slow drops, and runs down along ancient grooves. Some drops fall freely. Others crawl over stone lips. Others are caught immediately by carved channels and led away into basins, pipes, or sounding jars. The place is wet not in the manner of a flooded ruin, but in the manner of a living surface that has never been dry.

Yet it is not flooded. Water is present, frequent, and carefully distributed, but it does not overwhelm the built places. The floor is a patchwork of raised stone walkways, shallow pools, covered drains, and smooth stepping platforms. The lower level is mostly water, dark and cool, broken by stone rims and the bases of stacked constructions. Above it, the walls are inhabited.

There is no plant life here. No leaf, stem, root, vine, grass, moss, or seed exists in the pocket city. Nothing photosynthesizes. Nothing blooms. The world is mineral and wet: blackened limestone, gray basalt, pale calcite crusts, rust-streaked seams of iron, clay pressed into joints, and occasional thin veins of dull copper. Where life exists, it is not green. It is bacterial film, chalky microbial mats, and pale fungal growths that spread over wet stone like frozen lace. The fungi are not cultivated as crops so much as tolerated, harvested when thick enough, and treated as part of the architecture of damp. Some grow in shelves from cracks. Some hang as dry-looking skins in the drier upper niches. Some glow faintly, providing most of the dim light.

The light is weak and blue-gray, never bright enough to cast hard shadows. It comes from three sources: the faint phosphorescence of fungal threads, the cold glimmer of mineral films on wet walls, and the occasional small lamp made in a shallow stone dish. These lamps are rare. They burn rendered fat from the few creatures that live in the deeper water, and because such fuel is precious, they are used only for fine work. Most of the city exists in a permanent dusk. Water reflects the glow in trembling patches. Stone absorbs it. The eye learns to navigate by sheen, edge, and memory rather than by clear sight.
