The place is a high, hot, wet city without tools, a basin of living architecture at an elevation where the sky should be cold but is instead thick with steam. It spreads openly across a broad uplifted floor, regional in extent, not a single vista but a whole territory of terraces, pools, towers, canals, and vegetated roofs. The light is ordinary. That is one of its most disturbing qualities: plain white daylight, honest shadows, no theatrical glow, no color shifted toward dream. Everything is seen clearly, and yet the place does not obey ordinary seeing.

It lies far above the lower world, near the level where clouds usually form beneath the land rather than over it. The horizon is severe and luminous, the air thin, but the heat rising from below fills the basin with a dense, humid warmth. Water is everywhere, not as weather but as condition. The rocks weep. The ground is saturated just under its surface. Vapor condenses on stone, on leaves, on the inner walls of hollow stems, and returns as warm drip. There are marshes at the edges of black rock, shallow sheets of water over polished basalt, hot springs opening in quiet bowls, mineral terraces lip after lip like vast stairs, and curtains of moisture hanging in the air where rising steam meets cooler upper currents. The wetness is not merely surface damp; it is structural. The basin feels as if water is held just beneath every skin of stone and plant, ready to bead, seep, or tremble.

The terrain has long since settled into itself. There is no raw violence here, no fresh collapse, no sense of recent catastrophe. The landforms are established, weathered into balance. Cliffs and rims have been softened by ages of condensation, thermal flow, and mineral deposition. Channels are worn smooth. Pool lips are rounded. The terraces have the patience of things that have existed in the same state for immense spans of time. Yet the scale remains open and sweeping: one terrace leads to another, one district of pools to another district of towers, one green ridge of root-work to another, until the whole basin becomes a continuous urban landscape. It is not a wilderness dotted with strange growth. It is organized. It is dense. It has the saturated complexity of a city.

But it is a city built without making.

No devices exist here. No cut stone, no metal, no fastenings, no machines, no instruments, no evidence of tool-use. Nothing has been fabricated. Everything has grown, accreted, grafted, precipitated, braided, thickened, or been deposited by water and living tissue. The urban pattern is unmistakable: avenues, blocks, terraces, plazas, arches, bridges, reservoirs, walls, stairs, vaults, elevated walkways, submerged passages. But all of it is biological and mineral in origin. Streets are canals of moving hot water. Walls are living root systems fused into tough living partitions. Towers are chimneys of pale silica and calcium-rich deposits, raised by centuries of mineral-laden springs and capped by crowns of broad, steaming leaves. Bridges are woven trunks and aerial roots, self-braided over generations until they became stable enough to bear the weight of water, moss, and hanging gardens. Aqueducts are hollow reeds and tubular plant stems, some as wide as chambers, carrying water across open spaces without a single joint made by a hand.

The density is layered and near-touching. Below the surface level are root vaults: cool, dim, saturated chambers where water moves through tangled living lattices. At ground level, marsh platforms and peat-like terraces support thick stands of reeds, broad-leaved heat-plants, moss carpets, and fungal cords. Above that, columnar vegetation rises in crowded tiers, its branches and stems forming canopies that function as roofs. Higher still, condensation gathers on the undersides of leaf-ceilings and falls back through gaps in controlled threads. There are few empty volumes. Air itself feels occupied by vapor, sound, and suspended droplets. Every surface either grows, weeps, reflects, resounds, or supports another surface. The place is lush to the point of pressure.

The vegetation is exuberant but not wild in the ordinary sense. It has become urban through pattern. Plants grow into forms that repeat with architectural regularity: pillars at intervals, arches where two growth fronts meet, flat mats that serve as floors, bowl-shaped leaves that serve as basins, vertical membranes that serve as walls. Some species exist only as living infrastructure. Their roots bind terraces. Their stems conduct water. Their leaves shed condensation into specific channels. Their seed cases are hollow and resonant. There are no flowers designed for animal visitation, because there are no animals. No insects, no birds, no fish, no crawling or swimming or flying fauna of any kind. The pools contain no shells, no bones, no tracks, no bites, no burrows. The silence of animal life is total, and that absence is one of the basin’s deepest facts.

Reproduction happens by water, heat, and sound. Spores drift in steam. Buoyant seed-knots travel along canals. Some plants release generative capsules when the surrounding roar reaches a certain pressure. Others shed microscopic propagules into condensing fog, letting them settle on wet stone or living bark. Fungal networks spread through the deeper structures, binding root-work, dissolving dead tissue, redistributing nutrients into mineral solution. Microbial mats, not animal in nature, color some surfaces with dark green, rust, or pale film, especially where warm water slows. Decay is not eaten by creatures; it is softened, mineralized, absorbed, folded back into the system. The ecology is closed, botanical, fungal, microbial, hydraulic. It is rich without being faunal. It is alive without movement of beasts.

The heat is constant and intimate. It does not burn like fire; it presses like a wet hand. The temperature is high enough that evaporation is continuous, but the water table never falls. The basin maintains its wetness by internal circulation: hot water rises, spreads, cools just enough to condense on upper surfaces, returns as drip, sinks into root masses, is reheated below, and rises again. The process has no apparent beginning or end. There is no drought here, no flood, no seasonal swing. The place has achieved a saturated equilibrium. It is always equally wet, equally hot, equally full.

Sound is the ruling power of the basin. It is deafening, but not chaotic. It is a sustained, enormous chord made of many fixed components: the deep boom of thermal vents opening into water, the rush of sheets flowing over terrace lips, the hiss of steam through narrow reed-throats, the hollow percussion of droplets striking resonant leaf-bowls, the grinding whisper of mineral surfaces vibrating against one another, the low pressure of air moving through vast hollow stems
