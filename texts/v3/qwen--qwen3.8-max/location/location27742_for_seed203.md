Below the frozen surface, there is a bright cold hollow so large that it has its own weather.

Above, the land might look sparse: a pale crust of frost, thin mats of low leaves pressed flat against the ground, colorless and still. But those leaves are not merely growing there. They are collecting light. Each one is a shallow lens, cold and slightly convex, angled toward the sky. Each stem descending into the ground is a cable. Daylight enters the surface plant-life in tiny increments, is caught by glossy leaf-faces, and is drawn downward through translucent fibers until it emerges below as a clear, hard radiance. The world under the surface is therefore not lit by sun in any direct way, yet it is bright — bright with a borrowed, filtered, cold light that comes down through living veins.

The entrance, if one can call it that, is a recent wound. The land has only newly opened. There are fresh fractures in the roof of the hollow: raw slabs of frozen soil, cracked mineral strata, and shattered root-masses hanging in sharp-edged suspension. Nothing has yet been rounded by long weather. Broken root-ends still weep a slow, glassy sap. Cleft surfaces have the pale, unweathered look of things exposed only days or seasons ago. The whole place feels recently emerged, as if some vast interior chamber has just broken through its ceiling and is still settling into being.

Below that wound lies the vault.

It is not a cave in the ordinary sense. It is a living interior. The terrain is made almost entirely of plant matter, though not plant matter as soil, mulch, or forest floor. Here, plants are substrate, architecture, bridge, wall, tower, membrane, and sky-filter. The ground beneath your feet, where there is ground, is a woven skin of roots, compressed fibers, pale bark, interlocked stems, and cold leaves. It gives slightly under pressure, then holds. In places it is firm as wood; in others it is springy, airy, barely substantial. There is very little mineral stone. Where stone appears, it is an intruder: a fallen roof-block, a sharp fragment caught in root-netting, a cold pebble of frost-riven quartz embraced and slowly overgrown.

And yet the space is not dense. That is one of its strangest qualities.

The plant-life fills the volume as structure, not as mass. It is lace, scaffolding, hollow tube, thread, arch, and suspended net. Immense root-columns rise and descend like slow lightning frozen into fiber. Some are translucent, some pale as milk, some gray-green with a thin frost of living moss. Many are hollow. Some are open tubes wide enough to walk through; others are slender filaments strung across distances so large that they vanish into brightness. Between these presences lies an almost shocking amount of empty air. The world is mostly absence. You can see for kilometers through gaps in the living lattice. The brightness fills the emptiness, but does not thicken it. The landscape feels vast, clear, and nearly hollow.

It is cold. Not the absolute cold of deep ice, but a persistent, biting chill that keeps every surface crisp. Breath whitens. Frost forms on fine root-hairs. Sap moves slowly, thickening into bright beads. Water does not flow generously here; it appears as dampness, condensation, intermittent moisture, and small temporary accumulations. Mist gathers along the undersides of leaf-platforms. Droplets bead on cold membranes, tremble, and crawl along capillary fibers until they reach a knot, cup, or bladder where they are stored. Sometimes there is a brief rain inside the hollow: not rain from a sky, but a shedding of frozen spore-dust and melt-beads from high plant-clouds, a dry, glittering precipitation that whispers against the lattice. Pockets of water collect in curled leaves, sealed seed-shells, and swollen root-joints. Some freeze at the edges. Some remain liquid because of dissolved sugars and the slow warmth of living tissue. The place is damp, but not wet. It is a cold, bright humidity held in suspension.

The brightness is one of the defining facts of the landscape. It comes through the light-conducting plants in shifting intensities. When the surface above is clear, the vault fills with a blue-white radiance, sharp and almost glacial. When the surface is overcast, the light becomes softer, paler, more diffuse. At night, or during long surface storms, the deeper parts of the plant-life glow with their own phosphorescence: a cold greenish luminescence in the sap, a faint aurora along the inner walls of hollow stems, a shimmering in the spore-haze. Shadows are clean and thin. There is little warmth in the light. It illuminates without comforting. It makes every edge visible and leaves everything cold.

Because the hollow is so vast, it has atmosphere in the fullest sense. Air moves through it in currents too large to feel all at once. The light-columns, warmed faintly by the daylight they carry, create slow vertical drafts. Cold air falls from higher roof-fractures. Moisture condenses, releases tiny amounts of heat, and alters the flow. Spore-clouds gather like weather. Frost-smoke drifts in long streamers. There are storm-cells here, though they are unlike surface storms. They are mostly silent. A squall may pass through as a moving wall of pale green dust, glittering ice crystals, and swaying fiber-threads, yet produce no roar, only a faint hiss, a soft granular whisper, and the almost soundless creak of living structures under strain.

The turbulence is real, but quiet. The place is unstable in a slow, visual way. Bridges of root-fiber shift their tension as air currents change. Hanging platforms rise and fall by millimeters, then meters. Vast curtains of woven leaf turn in the dark-light. Seed-bladders drift loose from high nurseries and travel across the emptiness like pale lanterns. Sometimes a newly formed span snaps, not with a crash but with a dull thud swallowed immediately by the fibrous air. Sometimes a broken section falls away, opening a new route; sometimes a storm of drifting spores settles and begins to adhere, starting a new membrane where there had been only void. Creation and destruction are ongoing, but they do not feel violent. They feel like the breathing of an enormous organism too large to hear itself.

Sound behaves strangely here. The living lattice absorbs echo. Hollow stems carry some noises over long distances, but only certain ones: a drip, a tapped fiber, a small sharp crack. Most sound becomes intimate. A footfall is muffled. A voice does not travel far. The turbulence overhead may be immense, yet it arrives as a visual event more than an auditory one. The landscape is mostly quiet. Not dead quiet — there are tiny sounds: the tick of freezing droplets, the faint creak of tensioned root, the soft pop of a seed-case opening, the whisper of frost sliding down a leaf. But these sounds are sparse, isolated, and small against the scale of the place. The silence is not peaceful exactly; it is spacious.

Ecologically, the place is almost entirely plant. There is little animal life. The rare fauna are more like occasional interruptions than populations. A pale beetle may crawl along a frost-rimed ridge of bark, its shell dull and cold. A root-moth, almost colorless, rests with wings folded like paper against a glowing stem. Translucent worms live inside the water pockets, feeding on microbial films that may themselves be only borderline between plant and fungus. Something like a springtail leaps in the mossy condensation zones, but rarely. There are no herds, no flocks, no swarms. Animals here are events: a glimpse, a movement at the edge of vision, a tiny disturbance in the otherwise vegetable order. Most pollination and seed-dispersal is done by wind, turbulence, and the internal weather of the hollow. Some plants release spores in precise pulses; others shed buoyant seeds into updrafts, letting the bright emptiness carry them for days.

The plants themselves are adapted to the paradox of the place: bright but cold, alive but empty, damp but not wet, below the surface yet dependent on distant daylight. Their tissues are efficient and austere. Many are translucent to pass light. Many are hollow to reduce weight.
