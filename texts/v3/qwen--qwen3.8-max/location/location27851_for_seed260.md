## The High Sill of the Unfinished Mountains

It is a high place with no memory of water.

Not dry in the way of cracked mud, cracked lips, or empty wells. Dry as absence. Dry as a rule. There is no rain here, no dew, no mist, no ice, no hidden damp in the stone. The air is cool and thin, mineral-sharp, and when it enters the lungs it feels like breathing powdered glass cooled to a pleasant, dangerous temperature. Nothing glistens with moisture. Nothing darkens after a storm. There has never been a storm of that kind.

The land stands at extreme elevation, so high that the sky has begun to thin into black even in what passes for day. Light arrives dimmed, not because of cloud but because the atmosphere itself seems tired of carrying it. A pale, diffuse illumination settles over the region like ash from a cold fire. The sun, when visible, is a dull coin behind smoked glass. Shadows are soft-edged and uncertain, sometimes lagging slightly behind the objects that cast them.

This is an open landscape, broad enough to swallow distances, but it is not crowded. It extends in long mineral plains, broken terraces, low scarps, and wide basins where raw geology lies exposed like work abandoned mid-day. The ground is made of new-looking stone: dull basalt, pale feldspar, sheets of brittle mica, seams of native copper, black volcanic glass, and powdery pale dust that is not soil. Nothing has been rounded by long erosion. The edges are still too sure, too sharp, too undecided. The mountains nearby are not old mountains. They are emerging, or perhaps only attempting to become mountains. Their slopes are unfinished. Their ridges look provisional, as if the world has placed them there to see whether they will hold.

Yet the place is lush, if one accepts a strange definition of lush.

In sheltered folds, behind wind-broken fins of stone, inside shallow craters and along the leeward sides of emerging ridges, growth spreads in thick, layered profusion. There are no leaves in any ordinary sense, but there are fronds of dark glassy fiber that flex with glacial patience. There are mosses of copper-green filament that form deep cushions several meters across. There are hollow stalks like organ pipes, some thin as reeds, others as tall as houses, growing in dense clusters that resemble frozen thickets. There are crusts of granular lichen in colors that should not be vivid in such dim light: muted violet, metallic olive, a green so cold it seems to emit its own faint shine.

These growths are sparse across the land as a whole. Between them lie kilometers of bare mineral ground, open stretches where the eye finds only dust, fractured stone, and the strange optical stutters of a place that has begun to disagree with itself. But where life exists, it exists richly. It piles itself in tiers, threads itself through cracks, hangs in dry tassels from stone lips, spreads in carpets that swallow the foot. It is lush without water, green without sap, alive without the usual chemistry.

The vegetation does not drink. It accumulates. It feeds on gradients: temperature differences, static charge, airborne mineral grains, faint radiation, pressure changes, and above all sound. Sound is the hidden nutrient here. Sound is the weather. Sound is the architect.

The landscape is deafening.

At first, the noise seems like a physical assault. It is a vast, low, layered roar, not unlike the sound one might imagine inside a planet’s skull. It comes from everywhere and from no single direction. It rises through the soles of the feet. It presses against the chest. It fills the inner ear until balance becomes a matter of will. There is no animal life to make such noise, no insect swarm, no bird cry, no throat, no wing, no footfall. The land is utterly devoid of fauna. No creature with eyes, nerves, hunger, or fear belongs here. There are no tracks, no bones, no shells, no eggs, no burrows. Even the idea of animal life seems inappropriate, as if movement by muscle would be a violation of local law.

The sound comes from geology and atmosphere in unstable relationship. Wind passes through porous stone and hollow growths, drawing out tones too large to be heard as notes. Newly forming strata grind against one another at depths the eye cannot reach, sending vibration upward through the ground. The hollow stalks resonate with one another in slow chords. Dust fields tremble. Reality itself seems to hum, like a struck vessel too large to comprehend.

And yet the noise is slow.

That is one of the hardest things to understand. It is deafening, but not frantic. It does not rush. It does not attack in bursts. It changes over hours, days, years, centuries. A single harmonic may take a human lifetime to swell, shift, and fade. The roar has tides, but tides measured in geological patience. The land is loud in the way a glacier might be loud if glaciers could scream: massively, slowly, without urgency.

Because of the sound, the world here is beginning to fail in its consistency.

The weirdness is not decorative. It is structural. The place has reached a point where sound, elevation, dryness, and unfinished geology have worn through the ordinary skin of reality. Horizons fold. A ridge seen from one angle may appear farther away than it did from the last step, even though the traveler has been walking toward it. Distances respond poorly to intention. Sometimes a nearby stone spire remains distant for hours; sometimes a far mountain seems close enough to touch until one turns away and finds it has moved behind the eyes.

Echoes arrive before their causes. A footfall may produce a soft crack that returns from nowhere a moment too soon. If a stone is struck, the sound may come from the ground, from the sky, or from inside the body of the person who struck it. Words spoken aloud are dangerous, not because they attract anything, but because they join the architecture. A spoken sentence can become a faint path. A shouted word can create a temporary doorway. A repeated phrase can harden into a wall.

There are rules here, though no one has written them.

Do not make sharp sounds near the hollow stalks. They remember them.

Do not sleep in the center of an open flat. The noise thins there, and when the noise thins, the ground becomes less certain.

Do not follow a repeated shape too far. If the same black rock, the same notch in a ridge, the same pale spire appears three times, it is not the landscape repeating itself. It is the landscape failing to decide what it is.

Do not trust silence. Silence is not peace. Silence is a hole.

Built things are scattered across the region, small and rare. They are not cities, not roads, not monuments. They are marks of primitive, careful presence. Low rings of fitted stone, no mortar used, sit in places where the sound is slightly less crushing. Some are shelters, barely high enough for a crouching body. Others are not shelters at all but acoustic devices: curved walls meant to split the roar into tolerable parts, shallow basins of stacked stone that collect vibration instead of water, rows of upright slabs with notches carved into their tops.

There are occasional artifacts: chipped obsidian blades with edges still bright, cordage twisted from tough stalk-fiber, bowls carved from pale pumice-like stone, needles made from thornlike plant spines, small hammered rings of copper or copper-like metal. The technology is simple. Handworked. Functional. There is no sense of machines, no engine, no complex mechanism. But there is intelligence in the placement of things. The scattered structures are tuned as much as built. They seem designed to survive the sound by agreeing with it.

Whoever made these things did not try to conquer the place. They listened. They adjusted. They left little behind, and what they left is already being absorbed by the land. Mosses grow over the walls. Dust fills the interiors. Stone rings sink slowly into the ground as if the earth is learning to swallow them.

The vegetation has its own ecology, though it is an ecology without animals. Nothing grazes. Nothing hunts. Nothing nests in the common sense. Reproduction occurs through spore-dust, fragmentation, resonance, and slow crystalline growth. When a frond dies, it does not rot. It desiccates into brittle fiber, then shatters into glittering particles that may be carried a short distance by the slow movement of air. Sometimes the particles settle in a place where the sound is favorable, and a new growth begins. Sometimes they simply remain where they fall, becoming part of the mineral crust.

There is no decay smell. No sweetness. No fungus of the ordinary kind. The scent is cool, metallic, faintly electric, like a stone room after lightning. The air carries almost no odor, and what it carries feels more like taste than smell: copper, cold dust, a hint of ozone, a mineral bitterness at the back of the throat.

Light behaves poorly.

Because the illumination is dim, the colors seem muted at first, but after a while the eye begins to separate them. The black glass-fronds are not truly black; they are deep greenish-brown with veins of dull gold. The copper moss shifts between olive and verdigris depending on the angle. The pale dust fields are not white but faintly lilac, as if the ground has absorbed a bruise from the sky. In the deepest quiet places, if quiet can be said to exist here, some of the lichen gives off a weak phosphorescence, not enough to see by, only enough to suggest that the land may be watching itself.

There are no stars in the usual sense, though the high sky sometimes shows points of cold light through its blackness. They do not twinkle. They seem fixed, almost painted, and occasionally one of them will slide a short distance sideways and then stop, as if correcting its position.

The mountains at the edges are the most unsettling features. They are enormous, but they do not feel permanent. Their peaks are jagged, raw, unfinished, some splitting into multiple summits that overlap visually in ways that defy simple perspective. A single peak may appear to have three shadows. A cliff face may seem to be both approaching and receding. In certain lights, entire slopes appear translucent, as though the stone has not fully committed to opacity.

This is not erosion in the familiar sense. The land is not being worn down. It is being made, or perhaps being imagined into solidity. The dust has not yet traveled far. The scree has not yet settled into stable aprons. The rock faces still look surprised by their own existence. Some formations resemble half-formed thoughts: columns that begin with certainty and end in vague powder, arches with no far support, terraces that stop abruptly where the ground simply loses conviction.

The sound may be doing the building.

In places where the roar focuses, growth is thickest and stone seems more solid. In places where the roar thins, the landscape becomes uncertain. Open flats there are not empty because nothing has grown; they are empty because the place has not yet decided whether to be flat, basin, wall, or sky. Travelers who might pass through would learn to avoid those zones, not because of beasts or storms, but because a person who walks into an undecided place may become undecided too.

There are paths, faintly.

They are not worn by many feet. They are suggestions. A line of small cairns, no higher than knees, spaced far apart. A scratch of powdered stone where something has been dragged once and never again. A row of stalks bent in the same direction, not by wind but by long exposure to a repeating tone. These paths do not follow the shortest distance between points. They follow stable harmonics. They go where the world is most likely to remain consistent.

Sometimes, in the dimness, one can see structures that are not there.

Not ghosts. Not hallucinations. More like drafts. A shelter appears on a ridge, then is revealed to be a rock formation. A wall becomes a line of shadow. A field of standing stones resolves into hollow stalks. The place contains too many possible versions of itself, and the eye must choose one. Usually the chosen version lasts only until the next slow shift in the sound.

The built remains are often oriented toward these shifts. A doorway may face not the brightest direction, not the warmest, not the most sheltered, but the direction from which a particular tone arrives once every several years. A circle of stones may align with a peak that is only visible when the light bends wrongly. A primitive tool, half-buried in dust, may point toward a path that no longer exists or has not yet begun.

The temperature remains cool throughout what passes for day and night. There is no true warmth, no baking heat despite the dryness. Stone is always cold to the touch. Metal becomes painful after long contact. The air does not freeze the skin, but it numbs intention. Movement becomes slower. Thought becomes more deliberate. The body, if brought here, would feel less alive—not dying, but reduced. As if the cold and the noise and the thin light were gradually editing away everything unnecessary.

No animal life could survive such editing. Not merely because there is no water, though that alone would be enough. Not merely because there is no ordinary food, though the vegetation is inedible in any common sense. It is because animal life requires rapid change: pursuit, flight, hunger, sleep, fear, repair. This place operates at another speed. Its processes are too slow for nerves, too loud for sensory systems, too unstable for bodies that depend on consistent space. Plants, minerals, stones, and vibrations can exist here because they do not need to decide quickly what they are.

Even the spores seem patient. They drift in long veils, not falling so much as losing interest in height.
