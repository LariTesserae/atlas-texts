The place is an urban highland of wet stone, warm metal, and innumerable animals.

It lies high enough that clouds often form below its lower terraces, yet the air is not cold. A steady warmth rises through the rock from deep seams of heated ground, through bath-warm springs, through the slow exhalation of buried machinery. The light is ordinary: white sun, blue-gray cloud, clean shadows. When the sun breaks through, every wet surface brightens at once, and the whole horizon becomes a glittering field of drips, puddles, copper pipes, and moving wings.

There is no plant life here. No grass, no trees, no moss, no weeds splitting the pavement. The world is mineral, fungal, and animal. The rocks are coated not with leaves but with living crusts: black bacterial films that feed on iron, pale fungal felts that thread through cracks, chalky microbial mats that bead water into tiny lenses, brittle salt efflorescences that bloom after long damp spells. These growths are not green. They are rust-colored, ash-gray, bone-white, bluish black, and sometimes a dull metallic violet when minerals concentrate in shallow pools. They smell of warm dust, wet stone, faint ammonia, and old rain.

Water is everywhere, though not as rivers or green banks. It arrives as mist, condensation, frequent drizzle, and sudden warm showers that seem to be generated by the landscape itself. The plateau is so vast that it makes its own weather. Clouds gather over its western districts, spill rain into one set of terraces, and leave the eastern towers in dry sunlight for hours. In some quarters the air is always thick with drifting vapor; in others, droplets form on every rail, cable, and cornice, running down walls in thin shining threads. Many cliffs and buildings weep constantly. Porous stone draws moisture upward through capillary veins. Metal surfaces sweat. Even in the absence of rain, the place remains wet. Water stands in broad shallow basins, runs along ceramic gutters, beads on hot iron, collects in the hollows worn by countless feet and claws, and falls in slow dripping curtains from aqueducts.

The terrain has been settled for so long that erosion and construction have become difficult to separate. Terraces rise in broad sweeps, their edges rounded by weather and use. Stairs are hollowed in the center. Ramparts have softened into ridges. Retaining walls bulge gently where mineral-rich water has deposited new crusts over old masonry. Nothing looks newly made; nothing looks abandoned. The whole landscape has the calm of a long-established condition, as if this wet, warm, high city has existed for ages without needing to become anything else.

It is urban in every sense. Streets, canals, viaducts, reservoirs, stairways, tunnels, and elevated rail lines overlap in dense layers. There are districts built into the rock, districts built on top of older districts, and districts suspended from great iron frames and stone buttresses. Aqueducts cross one another at different heights. Broad ceramic pipes run beside narrow service alleys. Industrial chimneys release slow plumes of steam. Condenser towers rise like clustered hives, their surfaces darkened by moisture and stained with mineral streaks. Cable lines sag between towers, not always still: some carry tramcars, some support maintenance platforms, some are thick with roosting animals. The built environment is not sleek. It is layered, stained, repaired, patched, encrusted, and continuously used.

The technology is industrial but patient. Engines turn slowly. Waterwheels the size of buildings rotate in warm canals, their wooden or metal blades polished by constant flow. Turbines hum in submerged galleries where the city’s runoff is gathered and returned. Steam capstans move with long, sighing intervals. Electric lines crackle faintly in the mist, insulated with ceramic knobs and guarded by mesh, powering pumps, arc lamps, incubators, lifts, and filtration plants. Factories do not belch black smoke so much as breathe vapor, heat, and mineral smell. They refine brines, dry fungal cultures, render animal fats, press shed shells into lime, charge batteries from thermal gradients, and raise colonies of mineral-secreting creatures whose secretions become new walls. Production is continuous but unhurried. The machines have the rhythm of weather.

Because there are no plants, the ecology rests on water, warmth, minerals, fungi, bacteria, and animal labor. The primary living films are scraped, grazed, filtered, cultivated, and recycled. Warm springs carry dissolved salts and gases. Condensation brings atmospheric dust and nutrients. Animal waste, shed skin, broken eggs, molted shells, and carcasses are rapidly taken apart by beetles, larvae, microbial mats, and slow creeping scavengers. Nothing remains dead for long. Death becomes calcium, phosphate, chitin, oil, and new crust. The city’s infrastructure participates in this cycle: gutters distribute nutrient-rich water to grazing galleries; heated floors encourage fungal growth; reservoirs culture drifting food bodies; electric fences keep certain herds moving in patterns that prevent overgrazing.

The fauna is overwhelming.

Every available volume is occupied. The air is crowded with winged forms: mist moths with broad damp-absorbent wings, flue swifts that ride warm vents, glossy beetles that hum between cable lines, membranous gliders that drop from tower to tower on thermals rising from heated stone. The walls are furred with clinging bodies. Ledge after ledge is packed with roosting creatures, their shapes repeating in tiers like roof tiles. Crabs and crab-like grazers move across wet surfaces, scraping bacterial film with hard mouthparts. Long eel-shaped animals occupy pipes and downspouts, their bodies flexing in the dark as they filter water or hunt larvae. In broad reservoirs, slow filterers glide through warm murk, mouths open, straining microbial clouds. On the highest terraces, larger animals stand motionless in the drizzle, their hides slick and dark, waiting for mist-borne prey or simply regulating their temperature against the warm stone.

Some fauna are small enough to form living films themselves. Others are large enough to change the flow of a street when they move. The density is not panic; it is saturation. There are bodies in the gutters, bodies under the stairs, bodies on the rails, bodies in the condenser shafts, bodies pressed into crevices, bodies drifting in the water, bodies sleeping in warm electrical cabinets. Wherever a space opens, something fills it. Wherever a surface stays damp, something grazes it. Wherever warmth gathers, something rests there.

The animals are not separate from the urban order. They are part of its maintenance. Grazers keep the weeping rock from becoming clogged with microbial overgrowth. Burrowers aerate compacted mineral beds. Mason colonies deposit calcium carbonate or silica around provided armatures, slowly thickening walls and sealing leaks. Moths and flies carry spores and nutrient dust from one fungal district to another. Predators keep grazing populations within bounds, but their movements too are channeled by the architecture: hunting corridors, feeding basins, breeding stacks, molting galleries. Some creatures seem to have adapted to the machinery so thoroughly that their life cycles require it. Their eggs are warmed by pipes. Their larvae develop in turbine outflows. Their migrations follow the schedule of sluice gates and seasonal condensation tides.

There may once have been human builders, or there may be human maintainers still, but they are not the dominant presence. The city feels made for many bodies, and those bodies are mostly not human. If people exist here, they move through the landscape as minor figures: engineers in oilskin, tenders of valves, observers of slow animal traffic, silhouettes on high catwalks dwarfed by wings, steam, and stone.

The sound is loud.

It is loud in the way of wet places multiplied by industry and life. Dripping becomes a continuous hiss after rain. Gutters gurgle and rush. Water strikes metal, stone, stretched membranes, and the backs of animals. Turbines produce a low, pervasive moan that can be felt in the teeth. Steam valves release with sudden soft thumps. Electric equipment buzzes and ticks, especially when mist gathers on insulators. The fauna add a vast layered noise: clicking mandibles, scraping shells, wingbeats like shaken cloth, low calls echoing in reservoirs, high calls answered from towers, the dry rattle of claws on stone, the wet slither of bodies in channels. When a roost takes flight all at once, the air itself seems to thicken with sound. When a herd moves through a lower street, the noise is not a stampede but a dense, patient shuffling, like rain moving over gravel.

Yet despite the loudness, the place is slow.

Change happens across long durations. Weather systems cross the city in hours. Breeding tides unfold over days or weeks. Mineral crusts thicken by imperceptible layers. Machines wear smooth, are re-faced, and wear smooth again. Animal populations rise and fall in cycles tied to condensation, temperature, and nutrient flow. Erosion does not destroy the city; it refines it. New construction is accretive, often biological: a wall grows because a colony has been encouraged to build upon it; a terrace widens because sediment and shell waste have been spread and cemented; a canal deepens because generations of filter-feeders have kept it clear. There is no sense of frantic expansion. The place has reached a kind of balance. It does not rush. It persists.

The atmosphere is dense with presences rather than simply with vapor. Fog, spores, insects, heat, smell, sound, and architecture all occupy the same volume. Standing in one of its mid-level plazas, one might see a dozen vertical layers at once: flooded channels below, fungal crusts on the stone, grazing crabs along the ledges, eels visible through grated drains, roosting birds or bird-like creatures on cornices, moths drifting in the warm air, cables and pipes overhead, condenser towers beyond, and farther still, the hazy edge of the plateau where rain is falling in a gray curtain while sunlight remains bright nearby.

The rules of the world are shaped by this condition. Water is the primary wealth. To interrupt a flow is to starve a district. To divert condensation is to create bloom elsewhere. Surfaces must remain wet enough for microbial life but not so flooded that grazing films wash away. Heat must be distributed gently. Electricity must be insulated from moisture but not isolated from the processes it drives. Animals must be guided, not eliminated. Their numbers are immense, but they are also necessary. The city does not conquer nature; it has become a negotiated environment, an industrial wetland without plants, an urban organism in which architecture, water, fungus, machine, and fauna are bound together.

At evening, if evening brings any great change, the light remains normal: the sun lowers, clouds take on ordinary shades of gold and slate, shadows lengthen. The wet stone holds the day’s warmth. The water continues to condense, bead, run, and drip. The engines keep their low turning. The fauna settle into night roost
