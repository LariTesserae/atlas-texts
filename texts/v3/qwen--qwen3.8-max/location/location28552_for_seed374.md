It lies low, below the surrounding tablelands, in a broad basin where the ground is not so much old land as land still arriving. The air is warm and wet, not hot enough to scald, but close enough to feel like breath held in a closed room. Light comes down thin and diffuse through a permanent haze of vapor and mineral dust, so the place is dim even at what should be midday. There is no bright glare, no sharp shadows, only a soft gray-white glow resting on wet stone.

There are no plants here.

No grass, no reeds, no roots, no leaves, no wood. Nothing green or rooted takes hold. The surfaces are mineral and fungal: pale crusts of bacteria and mineral slime, rust-colored films over wet rock, soft white fungal knots in crevices, blackish threads of mold in the seams where water lingers. In some places the ground looks almost alive with these growths, but it is not plant-life. It is simpler, older, more patient. The world here has skipped the green stage and gone straight to stone, water, rot, and animal abundance.

Water is everywhere near the surface. It does not flood the basin so much as inhabit it. The ground weeps. Stone faces shine with condensation. Shallow channels run between platforms and walls. Pools form in every depression and are deliberately kept, lined, or redirected. The atmosphere itself seems to sweat; warm moisture settles on cooler rock and drips steadily from ledges, eaves, and bridge undersides. In places the water wells up from below, warm and mineral-rich, smelling faintly of iron, salt, chalk, and warm mud. In others it condenses from the air, collecting in clay gutters and stone troughs until it spills over with a constant, maddeningly even sound.

The terrain is young. It has the raw, unfinished look of geology still coming into being. Soft mineral stone is forming wherever the water slows: pale crusts, crumbly terraces, rounded lips of fresh tufa, brittle shelves that crackle underfoot but harden where they are left undisturbed. Mud is still settling. Clay is still being rearranged by seepage. Nothing here has the worn, settled patience of an ancient landscape. It is embryonic ground, newly made, constantly being added to, but never allowed to mature. The basin seems always to be at the first moment of becoming land, even though that moment never passes.

And yet it is densely built.

A settlement spreads across the basin, low and broad, fitted into the wet mineral ground as if it grew there with the stone. It is not a city of towers or wide dry streets. It is an urban wet-work, a layered town of platforms, causeways, sluices, fish pens, stepped pools, fungal troughs, retaining walls, footbridges, ramps, and covered walkways. Every district is crossed by water. Every building is raised slightly above the damp or built around it. The architecture is thick and close: roofs nearly touch, walkways pass over one another, stone piers crowd the channels, and beneath the main levels there are lower decks, crawlspaces, drying racks, animal pens, and half-submerged work ledges.

The density is not only architectural. The place is crowded with presences. Wherever there is water, there is life in it. Wherever there is stone, something clings to it. Wherever there is shelter, something nests in it. The basin feels saturated not just with moisture but with bodies: human workers, children, elders, animals, birds, insects, fish, amphibians, and the soft pale shapes of fungal growth in the shaded troughs. Nothing stands alone. Every surface is claimed, every gap used, every volume occupied.

The people here build with what the basin gives them. There is no timber. No wooden beams, no planks, no carved forests. Instead they use soft new stone, hardened mud, fired clay, shells, bone, crustacean carapaces, woven fungal matting, stretched hides, sinew cords, and occasional bits of native copper hammered into hooks, awls, or pins. Their technology is simple and manual. Stone hammers shape the softer mineral shelves. Bone tools pierce and sew. Shell scrapers clean hides and fungal sheets. Clay is pressed into pipes, lamps, troughs, and sluice gates, then baked in stone-lined heat pits warmed by the basin’s own hot seepage. Nothing is delicate. Nothing is unnecessary. Everything is made to be repaired by hand.

Their construction is inseparable from water management. The town is built as much to guide flow as to shelter bodies. Channels carry warm water through the districts. Small stone plugs and clay gates direct it into settling pools, work basins, fish enclosures, or fungal beds. Condensation walls are set at angles to collect moisture from the air. Drip-lines run along undersides of bridges so that no falling water is wasted. Fish weirs sit in the lower channels, their mouths woven from fungal cord and reinforced with bone. Steam vents are capped with hollowed stones so their warmth can be used for cooking or drying. The whole settlement is an infrastructure of wetness, carefully organized but still primitive in its means.

Because the geology is still forming, the city is always under construction in the same places. New mineral crust grows over stakes, steps, and abandoned tools. Workers chip it away, shape it, or let it thicken into fresh platforms. A wall left alone for a while becomes furred with pale stone. A forgotten rope stiffens into a mineral cable. A broken ledge may be rebuilt not by moving material in, but by encouraging the water to deposit new stone around a frame. The town is always being made, but it does not expand. It maintains itself into the same shape. Repair and growth balance. Wear and accretion balance. Use and replacement balance. The place is full of activity, yet it does not become anything else.

The ecology is strange only in its absence of green things; otherwise it follows a harsh, practical logic. The base of life is microbial and fungal. Bacterial films coat the wet stone. Mineral-rich water supports chemosynthetic growth in the warmer channels. Fungi feed on dead matter, shed skin, insect husks, fish waste, and the remains of older fungal beds. From these simple layers comes a dense animal world. Insects hatch in enormous numbers from the water: midges, small flies, caddis-like larvae, beetles, and soft-bodied things that shimmer in the dim air. Their larvae graze on microbial films; their adults become food for everything else.

Fish move through the channels in thick shoals: small, quick, pale-bellied creatures adapted to warm mineral water, along with longer, darker eel-like forms that hide under ledges. Crustaceans crowd the pools and weir baskets, pale shrimp and squat crayfish picking over surfaces. Snails and small mussels cling to submerged stone, filtering the water. Amphibians are abundant: frogs and toad-like animals squat on wet platforms, their throats working, their calls adding to the noise. Birds nest on stone shelves and under constructed eaves: swift, sharp-winged flyers that take insects from the air, and heavier waterbirds that feed on fish, snails, and scraps. Bats or bat-like creatures roost in the darker gaps and crevices, emerging into the dimness to hunt swarms.

Human life fits into this food web rather than rising above it. The people harvest fish and crustaceans, collect eggs, catch insects in fine fungal nets, cultivate edible fungal brackets on damp shelves, and scrape microbial curd from controlled beds. They dry fish and fungal cakes on warm stone racks. They render animal fat into lamp oil. They keep small penned animals near the fish works: birds or broad-billed waterfowl that can live on scraps and invertebrates, not plant feed. Nothing is wasted. Bones become tools. Shells become scrapers and cups. Hides become coverings. Fungal mats become rope, lining, insulation, and crude fabric. The settlement is fed by water, heat, mineral biology, and careful hand-labor.

The atmosphere is thick, warm, and close. Moisture beads on skin. Clothes, if woven from fungal fiber or hide, never fully dry. The smell is not foul but intense: warm stone, wet clay, fish, fungal earth, mineral salts, animal bodies, and the faint alkaline sharpness of fresh tufa. The dim light gives no sense of passing hours. The haze softens distances, making the settlement seem larger and less defined at its edges, as if the town could continue indefinitely into the gray warm air. In fact, the basin itself is wide enough to hold several districts, each visible only as a darker mass of construction across the water.
