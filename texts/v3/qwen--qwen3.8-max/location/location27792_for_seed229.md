There is a small, high basin set into the shoulder of a dry mineral mountain, a place no larger than a cathedral courtyard and a few adjoining terraces. It is not a valley cut by water, because water has no business here. No stream threads it, no pool lies in its hollows, no dew beads on its stones. The air is warm, thin, and utterly dry, and the dust underfoot behaves like powdered bone or warmed flour: it lifts in faint curls, settles on skin as a fine mineral film, and never clings with dampness.

From the outside, the basin looks like little more than a shallow bowl of dark rock and pale dust, half-hidden by high haze. Its rim is low in places, broken by a few narrow clefts, but the cliffs curve enough to make the space feel enclosed, almost architectural. It has the scale of a room made for something larger than a person but smaller than a city: a grove, a shrine, an amphitheater, a weathered hall with the sky for a ceiling. Beyond the rim lie wider slopes, salt-white mineral fields, and wind-scoured uplands, but the basin itself is a bounded place, a distinct interior.

It is high enough that the light is changed. The sun, when it appears, is not bright but smeared—a copper disk behind veils of high dust and chemical haze. The sky is not blue so much as dusky amber, bruised violet, or a greenish gray, depending on what the wind has carried in. Shadows are soft and strange. Even at midday the basin feels dim, as if lit by late evening or by a sun that has forgotten how to be direct. Yet the place is warm. The stone stores heat. The floor gives off a low, persistent warmth from unseen mineral veins below, and the air inside the bowl is often several degrees warmer than the open slopes outside. It is a warm dry chamber, not stifling, but alive with a faint pressure against the skin, like the inside of a kiln after the fire has been drawn.

The terrain is old and settled. Wind has worked it for ages, not with violence that leaves raw scars, but with long practice. The rocks are smooth in some places, faceted in others, carved into grooves, flutes, and shallow sockets. There are no fresh talus slopes or jagged new breaks; everything has the look of having been in this state for a long time. The erosion is mature, balanced, almost deliberate. Stone benches rise in arcs. Low ridges curve like ribs. Hollows have been polished into resonating bowls. Some of the rock is basalt-dark, some is reddish, some is pale with mineral bloom. In places the ground is a fine powder; in others it is a mosaic of small, wind-fitted stones. Nothing here is wet. Nothing here is mud, mossy with damp, or softened by rain. The dryness is total, but it is not dead.

That is the first strange thing.

The basin is lush.

Not lush in the way of a riverbank or rainforest, not lush with wet green succulence. It is lush with dry, waxy, resinous abundance. Vegetation stands in scattered islands across the bowl, widely separated by open dust and stone, so that at first glance the place seems sparse. But each island is dense, extravagant, overgrown in itself. The plants are not numerous everywhere; they are intensely present where they are present. They cluster in sheltered arcs, root into cracks, climb over one another in slow tangled gestures, and hang from ledges in long fibrous curtains. Between them there is much empty space, but within each thicket there is a crowding of stems, leaves, flowers, fruits, fibers, and living architecture.

The dominant forms might be called candle-trees, though they are not trees in any ordinary sense. Their trunks are built of layered bark like dark wax, banded and smooth, warm to the touch. They do not bleed sap but resin: thick, amber, slow, faintly luminous in the dim light. Their leaves are broad, stiff, and glossy, almost black-green above and pale metallic beneath. The undersides catch the weak light and throw it back in dull flashes when the wind turns them. Veins run through the leaves in lines of dull gold or copper, and at certain moments those veins glow faintly, as if some charge is passing through them. The canopies are not tall, but they are thick. They fold inward around their own cores, forming shaded interiors hung with aerial roots, dry vines, and curled leaf-sheaths. Inside these canopies, the air is stiller, warmer, and louder with insect-like sound.

Other plants grow lower and stranger. There are horn-ferns with fronds of bronze-colored filament that uncurl and recoil in response to static in the air. There are bellows-mosses, if moss is the word, forming spongy mats of dry fiber that compress and expand slightly when the wind passes over them, exhaling fine clouds of spores or charged dust. There are organ-reeds rooted in stone crevices: hollow, jointed stems of varying length and thickness, open at the ends, tuned by centuries of wind. There are kite-vines that trail long, papery seed-cases into the air, letting turbulence carry them upward before releasing them. There are fat wax-blossoms growing in sheltered nooks, their petals thick and dark, their centers full of oily pollen rather than nectar. Some flowers open with a small audible pop. Some hum when approached. Some close slowly when touched, not from weakness but from a reflexive sealing of their internal chemistry.
