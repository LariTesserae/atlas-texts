The basin is inhabited the way a warm engine is inhabited: by many small tenants that have learned to live in its noise. Nothing here is large. Nothing has room for largeness. What lives there lives close to stone, iron, leaf, and water, and it lives by adhesion.

The first residents are not easily seen as individuals. They are films and felts: green algae on vertical rock, blackish crusts where the water is heavy with minerals, orange threads blooming near rusted fittings, pale slime in the least-lit corners. These are the basin’s skin. They make every surface slightly soft, slightly slippery, slightly alive. They take the mineral threads the water carries and turn them into food, into texture, into the first edible layer over barren rock. Where a pipe has stood long enough to sweat, they have already arrived. Where a leaf lies still too long, they are the first to claim it. They are not beautiful in any ornamental sense; they are foundational. Without them, nothing else would have a place to begin.

Over them come the mosses. Moss is the basin’s dominant flesh. It does not grow as single plants so much as neighborhoods, cushions, mats, green upholstery pressed against every stable surface. Some mosses are fine and dense as felt; others are wiry and dark, or bright as wet glass. They hold droplets until the drops grow heavy enough to fall. They hold heat against roots. They hold dust, spores, dead midges, flakes of rust, and the powder of old leaves. When a stem dies, moss begins to move over it before it has truly decayed. When a pipe drips in one place long enough, moss builds a soft green mound beneath it, and the mound becomes a small country of liverwort, algae, and mineral crust. Moss here is not a ground cover. It is architecture, insulation, nursery, and grave.

The ferns live by opportunism. They unfold from cracks where steam warms the rock, their young fronds pale and tightly curled until the humidity opens them. They do not ask for soil. They ask for a fissure, a seam, a bracket, a ledge where condensation gathers. Their roots are thin and stubborn. They take hold in ash, in grit, in the felt of algae that coats iron. Their leaves are broad and shining, always wet on the underside, always catching the drip of some higher surface. Sedges and dwarf rush make tight tussocks around pipe feet and concrete pads. Cushion plants sit on hummocks looking soft until touched, then reveal themselves as dense as packed wool. Something vine-like climbs the warm iron legs of the structures, not quickly, not greedily, but with a patient insistence that eventually makes metal disappear under green.

The vegetation is crowded because the conditions are crowded. Every surface offers something: warmth, moisture, minerals, shade, a drip, a draft, a place for spores to rest. Nothing spreads in open lawns. Everything stacks. A dead leaf becomes a platform for fungi. A fallen stem becomes a bridge for roots. A rusted drum becomes a cliff face furred with algae. A cable becomes a perch. A valve becomes a trellis. A cracked concrete pad becomes a garden of moss islands separated by shining water. The plants do not seem to compete in the usual way; they share surfaces, ride on one another, use one another’s deaths as footholds. The basin is too young for a mature ecology, but it is already dense with first arrangements.

Beneath the green there are the decomposers, though they are not always visible. White threads move through wet ash. Small fungi appear as cups or brackets on dead stems. Slime molds slide invisibly through the felt of algae until one day they fruit in tiny pale bodies on the underside of a leaf. There are bacterial films that smell of hot metal and wet stone. They work quickly here because of the warmth. A leaf that dies is not long preserved; it blackens, softens, and is furred over by new growth. The basin is always digesting itself into ground. What looks like ruin is often digestion. What looks like neglect is often fertility beginning.

Animal life is minor, but it is exact.

The insects are the most visible of the small animals. Dark midges rise in thin columns above the warm water, their bodies so fine they seem made of smoke and vibration. They are born in the films of algae, in the shallow pools, in the beads that gather on cold metal. When they rise, they do not swarm with joy so much as necessity: they are looking for mates, for warmth, for the next film of moisture. Springtails leap in the green crusts, tiny and quick, disappearing into algae when a shadow passes. Beetles with dull shells move over rusted iron, their backs beaded with condensation, their legs careful on the slippery surfaces. They are not glossy or colorful. They have the basin’s palette: black, rust, wet green, mineral gray. Their bodies seem to have accepted that shine attracts too much attention. They live low, slow, and close to food.

In the narrow channels, there may be larvae no larger than eyelashes, building cases from rust flakes and mineral grains. They move like little moving stones, dragging their armored homes through water no deeper than a finger. Tiny worms work through the wet powder between gravel, invisible until a drip disturbs them. Mites live in the moss as if in a forest, moving through its miniature canopy. Spiders, few but present, stretch webs between brackets and cables. The webs are constantly beaded with moisture, and because of that they catch more than midges; they catch light. In hard daylight they flash briefly into visibility, then disappear again, as if the basin had momentarily shown one of its nervous systems.

The frogs are the closest thing the basin has to faces.

They are small, pale, and few. They live in and around the largest warm pool, where the water is slow enough to stand and the heat is steady enough to keep them from becoming sluggish. Their color is not the bright green of lowland frogs but a washed mineral pallor: bone-white, ash-green, faintly translucent at the edges of their limbs. They sit on wet concrete, on the rims of shallow pools, on broad leaves that bend under them but do not break. They do not move often. They blink slowly when steam curls near them. Their eyes are dark and reflective, adapted to glare and mist. When the turbine changes pitch, their throats seem to pulse in sympathy, not because they choose to but because the vibration enters everything.

They do not call much. The basin is too loud for ordinary amphibian voices. Instead they make small sounds when they make sounds at all: a dry tick, a low throat-click, a breath released. A stranger might never hear them. But if one watches them long enough, one sees that they are attending to the noise. They turn their heads toward a sudden hiss. They settle when it fades. They feed on midges and other small insects that come too close to the water. They are not numerous. Perhaps they arrived by accident, carried upward in some flood, some storm, some unlikely journey, and found the basin too warm, too wet, too full of food to leave despite the noise. They have the look of creatures who have stopped waiting for quiet.

Birds are rare. Sometimes one circles high above the rim, looks down into the glitter and roar, and turns away. The basin is too wet, too loud, too enclosed, too strange in its smell and sound. It offers no perch that is not slick, no nest site that is not steaming, no silence in which to rest. Occasionally a feather may fall from a passing wing and land on moss. Within hours it will be darkened, beaded, and slowly absorbed into the general wetness. The basin does not preserve such things. It takes them apart.

There are no large animals. No deer, no mountain goat, no fox, no browsing creature with hooves or paws. The place would not support them, and it would not hide them. The rim is too sharp, the ground too broken, the air too thin, the noise too constant. More importantly, the basin does not offer the kind of life that large animals need: broad food, open movement, escape routes, shelter from wind. It offers surfaces. It offers warmth in small seams. It offers water in beads and films. Its bounty is microscopic and adhesive. What lives there must be able to live close to those conditions.

Yet the basin is not empty of larger presences, if one stretches the meaning of “live.”

The machines are not animals, but they are not merely objects either. They have become participants. They make heat. They make water. They make shade. They make vibration. They make the rhythms by which many smaller lives arrange themselves. The turbine’s slow flywheel is the basin’s most obvious pulse. The pumps are its intermittent muscles. The condenser racks are its lungs or kidneys, pulling water from air, separating wetness from breath. The valves are throats that sigh and close. The pipes are veins laid over rock, sweating, ticking, carrying warmth from one part of the basin to another. The generator coughs and hums. The pressure tanks hold their breath in painted numbers half lost under grime. The one yellow bulb, burning pointlessly in daylight, is like a small persistent organ of sight, keeping watch over nothing in particular.

These machines are inhabited as much as they are operators. Moss grows on their warm sides. Beetles cross their flanges. Ferns root in their joints. Algae films their cooler surfaces. Condensation beads on them and drips onto leaves below, creating little gardens wherever the drip is regular. A pump is not ruined by green growth; it has become a trellis. A cable is not degraded by moss; it has become a branch. A collapsed gantry is not merely broken; it has become a shelter for roots. The machines have been drawn into the basin’s ecology. They are not alive in the biological sense, but they are alive in the sense that their behavior matters to living things. When a valve opens, the moss drinks. When a pump thuds, the water moves. When the turbine rises in pitch, the frogs blink. When a condenser cools, droplets form and fall like rain. In this place, to operate is to participate.

And then there are the keepers.

They are not large. The basin does not permit largeness. They are no bigger than the frogs, and often flatter, lower, harder to see. At first they might be mistaken for clumps of root, knots of moss, or shadows under pipes. They move only when movement is necessary, and even then they move in pauses, as if timing themselves to the drip and the valves. They are not human, or if they once had something to do with human shape, the basin has worn that shape down to its own scale. They have bodies compacted from what the place provides: felted algae, tough root fiber, scales of rust, thin wire, skin like wet leaf. Their color is green-black, rust-brown, mineral gray. Their fingers and toes are long, padded, and careful, made for gripping wet iron and slick stone. Their eyes are dark and wide, suited to glare, mist, and the flat white light that comes when cloud covers the rim.

They are the basin’s gardeners, though they would not understand the word.

They do not plant in rows. They do not clear land. They adjust. They move a frond away from a moving wheel. They scrape moss from a gauge face when it grows too thick to read. They guide a thread of water into a channel that has begun to dry. They loosen a valve that has become stiff with crust. They clear condenser fins when algae begins to bridge them. They tie loose cables with strips of root. They carry small tools: a bent wire, a shard of ceramic, a hollow reed, a piece of shell or bone polished by water. They do not build new machines. They maintain the fragments that remain. They do not seem to understand the whole system any more than the frogs do. They understand local signs. A change in the sound of a drip. A new smell of hot metal. A patch of steam where there should be none. A pipe sweating too heavily on one side. A cistern filling too slowly.

They live in the spaces between things: under the loose door of the valve house, inside a hollow pipe where the air is warm and still, behind the engine shed where the wall keeps heat, beneath the cracked concrete pad where roots and cables meet. They do not make houses in the ordinary sense. They make nests of moss and insulation, of shed leaves and rust flakes, tucked into cavities where the noise is muffled by stone. They sleep, if they sleep, in intervals. They wake when a valve sighs. They become active when condensation thickens. They seem to feed on what the basin offers: algae scraped from warm stone, the soft growth that forms on iron, midge larvae from shallow pools, mineral films, droplets condensed from the air. Sometimes they sit very still on a warm flange, their bodies spread flat, as if drinking light through their greenish skins. They are not delicate. They are tough, damp, and patient. They can remain motionless for so long that moss grows on their shoulders and a beetle crosses them without noticing.

They communicate by pressure and rhythm. Sound in the basin is too constant for speech, too loud for subtlety, too reflective for distance. So they tap on pipes. They click stones. They wait for a pump to thud, then tap a pattern into the vibration while the sound carries. They press their palms to iron and feel the difference between a healthy hum and a failing one. They know the cistern’s hollow drum, the turbine’s deep tooth-noise, the high thin whistle of a vent about to release. Their language is not made of words so much as intervals: a pause, a tap, a scrape, a returning tap. If they have names, they are probably pressures. If they have stories, they are probably cycles. If they have memory, it is stored in surfaces: this valve stuck before, this channel overflowed, this rock fell, this pipe warmed early, this moss was good for binding, this pool held frogs.

There are few of them. The basin cannot support many. Each seems to have a district: a spring, a pump, a row of condenser racks, a section of rail, a broken gantry, a cluster of pressure tanks. They do not appear to have leaders. They do not gather in crowds. They meet when cycles overlap, when a valve opens at the same hour, when a drip becomes heavy enough to require shared attention. Then they work side by side without ceremony, clearing a blockage, easing a jam, guiding water, scraping crust. Afterward they separate. They are not affectionate in any visible way, but they are not hostile either. They tolerate one another as the basin tolerates its contradictions: closely, warmly, with enough space to keep from tearing.

They have a strange relation to the frogs. They do not eat them. They do not avoid them. They sometimes sit near them, motionless, as if the frogs were colleagues in the work of being still. When a frog leaps, a keeper may turn its head slowly and watch, then return to whatever small task it was performing. Beetles walk over keepers as if they were rocks. Midges rise around them. Steam curls past their faces. They do not seem to regard themselves as separate from the basin. They do not look at the rim with longing. They do not appear to imagine elsewhere. Their world is the basin’s width, the basin’s noise, the basin’s heat. That is not a limitation to them. It is the shape of existence.

If they have any reverence, it is directed not upward but inward, toward the system that sustains them. They tend the machines not because they worship them, but because the machines make the basin possible. They tend the water because water is the basin’s blood. They tend the plants because plants hold the gravel together. They tend the heat because without heat the basin would be only a cold wound in the sky. Their work is not worship. It is maintenance raised to the level of instinct, or perhaps instinct lowered to the level of maintenance. They are the basin’s immune system, its small hands, its local attention.

The basin itself may be the largest living thing there.

Not as a creature with a single body, but as a process that has become dense enough to feel like a body. It sweats. It breathes. It heats and cools. It condenses. It releases. It hums. It drips. It digests. It repairs. It has rhythms: the sigh of the valve, the thud of the pump, the rising and falling pitch of the turbine, the slow filling of the cistern, the thickening of mist near the rim, the local drizzle that falls without a cloud. It has moods, if moods can be made of pressure and sound: bright and hard under direct sun, silver and flat under cloud, inward and close when the mist thickens, almost tense when a release valve prepares to open. It is young, but its youth does not make it hasty. It is still learning its own channels. It is still deciding which surfaces will hold water, which roots will bind the gravel, which machines will remain useful, which stones will fall.

Everything that lives there is part of that learning.

The algae prepare the surfaces. The mosses hold the moisture. The ferns open the cracks. The insects move through the films and crusts. The frogs wait in the warm pool and keep the insect life from becoming too careless. The machines make heat, water, and rhythm. The keepers adjust what can be adjusted. The mountain provides broken rock, altitude, and pressure. The sky provides light, cloud, and occasional rain. The basin takes all of it and turns it into a closed, loud, wet room of living.

Whoever lives there is plural.

It is the green film on the pipe. It is the moss over the dead stem. It is the midge column above the pool. It is the beetle crossing the rust. It is the pale frog blinking in the steam. It is the spider’s beaded web between cables. It is the turbine turning with enormous slowness. It is the valve that sighs as if remembering how to breathe. It is the small keeper pressing its hand to iron, listening for the change in pitch. It is the basin’s own slow circulation, becoming visible in droplets, roots, rust, and sound.

They do not live there by conquering the place. They live there by entering it. They live by accepting heat, noise, moisture, mineral taste, thin air, and the nearness of everything else. They live in the contradiction: a marsh in the sky, a factory becoming geology, a roar that is also patience, a young mountain already making room for moss. They live close enough that their bodies take on the basin’s qualities. They become wet, loud, warm, dense, and patient.

If one stood in the center of the basin and asked who lived there, the answer would not come as a face. It would come as a pressure on the skin: the heat rising through rock, the mist thickening on the rim, the drip striking iron, the turbine’s throb in the teeth, the sudden sigh of a valve, the green moss moving over rust, the small pale frog blinking, the keeper becoming still as a stone. All of that would be the answer.

The basin lives.

Not with one life, but with many small lives bound together by water and noise. Not with a single will, but with a thousand local adjustments. Not with speed, but with the terrible, gentle persistence of a place that is only beginning to become itself.
