Underwater, the landscape does not whisper. It keeps a working voice: the low thrum of a turning turbine, the knock of rain on the surface skin, the groan of pressure in old iron, the hollow percussion of a warning bell, the rasping passage of crustaceans over metal, the sudden flash-school of fish changing direction as one body. Whatever lives in that high sea lives inside sound. It lives inside current, too, and inside the slow, patient pressure of a world that has no shore to return to.

Whoever lives there is therefore not one creature and not one kind of life. The place is inhabited in layers. The surface is inhabited; the middle water is inhabited; the drowned stone floor is inhabited; the dry chambers sealed inside flooded works are inhabited; the machines themselves, insofar as they still act, still respond, still make heat and motion, are inhabitants as well. If the sea has a people, it has many. If it has a single native, that native is not a body but a relationship: air borrowed from towers, heat stolen from engines, food raised from weed and iron, speech carried through water by metal.
