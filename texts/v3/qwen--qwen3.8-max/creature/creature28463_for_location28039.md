Whoever lives there is first of all someone who has learned to inhabit a world not yet settled into ease.

They are not people shaped by comfort, nor by abundance, nor by the soft accumulation of easy centuries. They are shaped by altitude, by cold, by stone that still moves, by water that must be caught drop by drop, and by distances so vast that even a city can feel like a landscape thinking to itself. Their lives are full, but not loud. They are numerous in presence, though not crowded. To live there is to be always aware of pressure and patience: pressure of wind, pressure of cold, pressure of stone overhead and underfoot; patience in work, in speech, in building, in waiting for weather to pass.

The people themselves look as if they have been fitted to the place by slow correction rather than by dramatic design. They are often compact and sturdy, with deep chests and unhurried breath, because the air is thin and rewards efficiency more than exertion. Their faces are marked early by exposure: wind-darkened cheeks, cracked lips, fine lines around eyes accustomed to glare off pale granite and ice-rimed walls. Their hands are their truest record. Knuckles thickened, palms callused, nails short and hard, skin roughened by wool, rope, cold water, and stone dust. Those hands move with great care. Nothing is dropped casually. Nothing is swung without reason. In a place where a dropped tool can crack, a slipped foot can loosen scree, and a careless fire can consume a season’s fuel, economy of motion becomes a moral habit.

They walk differently from lowland people. They do not stride as if the ground were a road laid down for them. They place their feet. They read surfaces without seeming to look: the wet sheen on a slab, the loose glitter of fresh gravel, the slight bulge of frost-heaved paving, the darker line where meltwater has seeped and refrozen. Children learn this before they learn many words. They learn to stop when the wind changes. They learn to listen after cold nights for the small wrong sounds in rock. They learn that speed is often not strength but risk.

Their clothing is layered, repaired, and practical to the point of tenderness. Wool is the chief wealth, because wool can be grown on animals that eat what little the plateau offers. It is felted, woven, oiled, patched, resewn, and passed down. Outer garments are dense and roughened against wind; inner layers are softer, worn next to the skin until they thin. Hides are used where strength matters: across shoulders, at knees, on boots, in aprons, in doorway hangings. Nothing is discarded merely because it is old. A garment may carry the history of several bodies before it becomes a draft-stopper, a wrapping cloth, or finally fuel for a brief flame. Fastenings are bone, carved stone, twisted sinew, or rare soft metal worn so thin it has become an heirloom more than a decoration.

They smell of the place: lanolin, cold stone, faint dung smoke, wet rope, animal warmth, and the mineral sharpness of newly broken rock. In cold weather, their breath hangs around them like a second life. In fog, their shoulders darken at the edges with moisture. In sun, they seem suddenly vivid, the weave of their clothes throwing small shadows, their eyes narrowing against a brightness that has no haze to soften it.

Their voices are quiet. Not because they lack feeling, but because thin air and stone walls teach a different economy of sound. They speak in registers that carry without tearing: low calls, short phrases, warnings that do not waste breath. They also have a language of gestures, because distances and wind can defeat speech. A lifted hand, a pointed stick, a folded hide, a certain arrangement of stones on a parapet—these can say as much as words. Runners and watchers use signs across terraces. Herders use clicks, low whistles, and the pressure of their bodies more than shouts. When someone laughs, it tends to be brief and warm, quickly swallowed by wool and stone. When someone grieves, the grief is often quiet too, held in the body like cold held in a north wall.

They are not one simple tribe or nation, but a web of households, districts, crafts, and obligations stretched across the plateau. Different lives occupy different parts of the city-country, and each part produces its own kind of person.

On the outer margins, where cloud banks drag themselves over the walls and fog settles into slow white pools, live the fog-tenders. These are the people who know dampness as a crop. They tend broad angled pans, grooved roofs, gutters cut into living rock, and stone surfaces roughened to catch condensation. Their hands are often cool and chapped, their sleeves damp at the cuff. They know the direction of cloud drift by habit and by the feel of moisture on their faces. They clean fine grit from channels, adjust coverings, and mark the first bead of gather with the same seriousness that lower farmers mark rain. They are patient people, because fog cannot be hurried. It must be received, not seized.

Near the cisterns and ice stores are the water-counters and ice-keepers. They are careful to the point of reverence. They measure levels with notched rods, remember which tank was full at the last thaw, and know the sound of a vessel filling by pitch alone. They guard against contamination, loss, and waste. In their presence, a spilled cup feels almost like an oath broken. They are not cruel, but they are severe in small matters, because small matters are where survival leaks away. They teach children to drink slowly, to rinse sparingly, to return wash-water to troughs or damp-keeping beds. Their authority is quiet but real. A water-keeper’s word can stop a construction or redirect a household’s labor, because without counted water nothing else endures.

Along the terraced herd lanes live those whose lives are braided with animals. They rise before full light, because animals wake with cold and hunger. They know the broad-hoofed woolly herds not as possessions alone, but as slow co-inhabitants of the city. They know individual beasts by stance, breath, coat pattern, and the sound of hooves on stone. They know which animals are calm in fog, which are nervous after rockfall, which mothers will guard their young too fiercely in narrow lanes. Their clothes carry the smell of beasts, and they do not mind it. To them, animal warmth is one of the city’s oldest architecture. They place pens against sleeping walls, guide dung collection, comb wool at shedding time, and keep the lanes clear because a blocked lane becomes a panicked one. They are often broad-shouldered, unhurried, and strong in the practical way of people who must move alongside animals rather than force them.

Higher up, on watch-cairns and roof platforms, are the watchers. They observe weather, rock faces, approaching beasts, distant smoke, and the behavior of birds. Their eyes are trained to notice change in large quiet landscapes: a new crack line on a cliff, a dark streak where water has begun to seep, a raptor circling differently than usual, a cloud bank lifting too quickly. They spend long hours in cold and wind, wrapped in hides, often almost still. They are not lonely people so much as people comfortable with distance. They carry messages by signal and memory. They know which sightlines belong to which district, and which cairn must be kept clear so that a line of warning can pass across the plateau before night.

Among the broken slopes and new scree fields are the stone-listeners, masons, and danger-markers. These are perhaps the most essential of all, though they do not think of themselves as elevated. They walk after frost nights. They tap walls. They inspect plaster seals placed over cracks. They listen for the faint tick of splitting rock, the dull settling that means a face has shifted. They know which benches are stable and which overhangs are treacherous. They understand that the city is built on land still in the act of becoming land. They do not speak of stone as dead. To them it has moods, seasons, weaknesses, and timing. They mark dangerous places simply: a line of pigment, a wedge, a stack of fresh chips, a rope barred with bone. They also know when to abandon a place. This is not considered cowardice. It is considered sense.

In the dense inner districts live the wool-workers, rope-makers, hide-workers, bone-carvers, tool-menders, and fire-keepers. Their quarters are less exposed but no less busy. Wool is carded, spun, felted, and woven into everything from blankets to wind-flaps. Rope is twisted from sinew, wool, and tough grasses where they can be gathered. Bone is shaped into needles, toggles, picks, levers, scrapers, and pins. Hides are stretched, scraped, smoked, and resewn. Fire is small, banked, and constantly attended. These people often smell of grease, ash, and worked fiber. Their hands are quick. Their workspaces are crowded with useful objects hanging from hooks or set into niches, because flat surfaces are precious and wind can carry loose things away.

There are also traders and runners who move between the plateau and lower lands. They are tougher than they look. They know weather windows, safe passes, places where scree can slide after sudden thaw, and points where wind funnels with enough force to stagger a loaded person. They bring what the high city lacks: fuel, grain, timber in rare small pieces, salt, medicinal herbs, soft clay, sometimes metal. They take down wool, hides, bone, dried meat, worked stone, and perhaps small amounts of copper or bronze. They are often good at languages and quick at arithmetic, because trade at such heights cannot afford misunderstanding. Lowland visitors tend to move too fast, breathe too hard, and speak too loudly. The high city slows them down whether they wish it or not.

The shrines in wall hollows are kept by no single priestly class, though certain people are trusted with memory. These are the keepers of counts and signs: those who remember when the cisterns last overflowed, which winter birds came late, what marks were left on old cracks, which families contributed labor to a wall, which children were born in a fog season. Their knowledge is practical and sacred at once. They do not stand apart from work. They mend nets, tend lamps, or watch children while speaking the old sequences aloud. In a place without roaring machines or written abundance, memory is a kind of infrastructure. If a gutter is recut wrongly, if a lane is blocked, if a wall is raised over an old seep-line, the consequences may not appear for years. Therefore someone must remember.

Household life is close, warm by effort, and organized around shared surfaces. Families often live in clusters rather than isolated units: rooms arranged around a sheltered yard, a passage, or a thick-walled animal court. Sleeping places are set near the warmest walls, sometimes beside pens so that body heat is doubled. Beds are platforms of stone or packed gravel, layered with hides, wool, and dried grass where grass can be spared. Children sleep bundled together. Infants are kept against adult bodies under wraps. At night, the sound of breathing is important. It tells that all are well. It tells that the cold has not entered too deeply.

Food is plain, valued, and rarely wasted. Much of it comes from animals: milk, curds, dried cheese, fat, dried meat, and occasionally blood drawn with care from living beasts when other food is scarce. Where grain exists, it is often traded and treated as precious: ground into dense cakes, boiled into porridge, or stored sealed against bad seasons. Hardy herbs, moss-scrapings, edible roots, and lichen preparations add small variation, though none of this is abundant. Eggs from birds may be taken cautiously. Insects during the thaw are not scorned, especially by children, but they are seasonal accidents more than staples. Cooking uses little fuel. Drying, freezing, fermenting, and cold storage do much of the work that fire would do elsewhere. A hot drink is a kindness. A full hearth is an event.

Children grow up useful early, but not without play. Their games imitate the city’s labor: stacking stones, herding pebbles through lanes, counting drops, imitating bird calls, racing messages along parapets. They learn to carry small loads before they learn to argue well. They also learn to be still. A child who can sit quietly and notice which icicle has shifted, which hide flap has loosened, or which animal is breathing strangely is already valuable. They are praised less for brilliance than for attentiveness. This does not mean they are unloved. It means love there is often shown through preparation: a boot mended before dawn, a sleeve lengthened, a portion saved, a story repeated until the child knows which cracks to fear and which winds to trust.

Elders remain integrated. They may no longer haul stone or run messages, but they watch, advise, mend, remember, and teach. Their knowledge is seasonal and local: the way a certain corner ices over, the year a tank failed, how a wall should be thickened where fog comes sideways. They sit in sun patches when the light allows, wrapped in old wool, faces like carved weather. Their hands may be stiff, but their eyes remain active. In such a place, old age is not merely decline. It is archive.

The animals are not secondary citizens of this city-country. They are co-inhabitants with their own rhythms, needs, and dignity.

The great woolly herd beasts are the most visible. They move through lanes and terraces with a slow, sure-footed gravity, broad hooves finding purchase where a human foot might slip. Their coats are thick and layered: coarse outer hair shedding fog and snow, dense underwool holding warmth. Their muzzles are wide and patient, their breathing deep, their eyes calm but watchful. They are not entirely tame in the lowland sense. They tolerate human proximity because generations of mutual use have made it sensible. Humans provide shelter, guidance, protection from predators, and sometimes fodder in hard times. The beasts provide wool, milk, dung, heat, meat in extremity, and the living presence that makes stone passages less desolate.

Their movement through the city is ceremonial without being ornamental. Gates are opened for them. Lanes are kept clear. Children step aside. Herders guide them with body weight and small sounds, not force. In fog, their shapes appear suddenly: dark massed bodies, breath hanging, wool darkened at the edges with moisture. In sun, their coats glow where the light catches. Their dung is gathered with care, dried, stacked, and counted. To waste dung is almost as serious as wasting water, because dung is heat, and heat is life. Young animals are sheltered in undercrofts during the worst cold. Their cries are high and plaintive, and adults respond quickly, because the loss of a young beast is a loss carried for years.

Smaller lives fill the city’s pores. Marmot-like creatures occupy rubble banks and cavities under retaining walls. They emerge into weak sun, sit upright, and watch with blunt alertness. They are tolerated even where they dig, because they are part of the living scatter of the plateau and because their presence can indicate stable rubble and safe warmth. Pika-like animals gather dried vegetation into crevices, their tiny calls sharp and sudden. They compete sometimes with human stores, but they are also reminders that even in austerity, life accumulates. Children may trap them if necessary, but not carelessly. Their little hay caches are recognized as a parallel labor to the city’s own.

Birds are everywhere in their seasons. Pale raptors ride the uplift along the rim, silent and immense, watching for small movement. Dark swift-winged birds hunt insects over melt pools in the brief thaw. Ravens inspect all things: newly stacked stones, opened caches, hides hung to dry, the faces of people. They are clever enough to be regarded with wary respect. Small seed-eaters and insect-eaters cluster around pens, grain stores, and roof edges. Their sounds soften the city. People often leave scraps and sheltered niches for them, not from sentiment alone but because birds also signal change. If certain birds vanish from a wall, something is wrong. If others return, the season has turned.

Insects arrive explosively when wet-thaw comes. For a short time, damp stone hums. Flies gather at seeps, beetles move under slabs, midges rise from shallow water, and mosquitoes find any exposed skin. This abundance is brief. Frost returns, and the insect world collapses into silence. Yet during that flare, birds grow busier, amphibians stir in pools that do not freeze solid, and the city feels suddenly young in a different way—not only newly lifted, but newly awake.

Even the cisterns are not empty. They hold small aquatic lives: larvae, crustaceans, cold-water invertebrates, the faint green breath of algae on shaded walls. These lives matter because they keep water systems from becoming sterile mystery. They are signs of health or imbalance. A water-keeper who sees the wrong film on a tank surface knows to inspect the catchment. A sudden absence of tiny lives may mean contamination, freezing, or blocked flow. In this way, even invisible creatures participate in the city’s survival.

What lives there, then, is not merely human or animal. It is also a web of stored and waiting lives. Grain in sealed rooms lives as potential. Wool in bundles lives as future warmth. Ice in shaded undercrofts lives as future water. Dried dung lives as future heat. Seeds, if any are kept, live as delayed promise. Even stone, in the understanding of the inhabitants, is not inert. It may not be alive as flesh is alive, but it acts. It sheds. It cracks. It holds cold. It gives back sun. It accepts or refuses the works built upon it. To live there is to be in negotiation with all of this.

The social order reflects that negotiation. Authority is not flamboyant. It belongs to those who can be trusted with scarce things: water, fire, animals, stone, memory. Leadership is often situational. A fog-wright may direct work during cloud season. A stone-listener may command during inspection after hard frosts. A herder may have final say when lanes are crowded. An elder may settle a dispute over gutter rights because she remembers three generations of use. Decisions are made slowly, because mistakes are expensive. A badly placed wall can fail. A blocked lane can crush someone. A wasted cistern can doom a district. Therefore custom is strong. Custom is not mere tradition there; it is accumulated correction.

Their laws are simple and severe in the places where they must be. Waste water, and you owe labor to repair the surfaces that gather it. Block a herd lane, and you must help move the animals safely, perhaps for a full season. Build without witnesses or helpers, and your wall may be taken down by common judgment. Misreport a cistern level, and you lose trust in matters of measure. Fire negligence is among the gravest faults. Fuel is too scarce, and wind too ready. Punishments tend toward restitution rather than spectacle. The community cannot afford to destroy useful hands. It can afford to make them remember.

Their art is subtle and functional. They do not carve grand statues, because labor is too precious and stone too stubborn. But they shape door lintels so that the hand falls naturally upon them. They select stone colors for contrast at dangerous corners: pale granite where a step must be seen, dark basalt where a wall must absorb sun. They weave patterns into wool that identify household, district, or task. They decorate shrines with small objects: a perfect bone needle, a shard of old ice kept in a sealed jar, a feather, a copper shaving, a painted mark. Beauty there is not separated from use. A well-cut drip channel is admired. A rope coiled so it will not freeze into a knot is praised. A doorway that keeps wind out while letting a person pass with a load is spoken of with satisfaction.

Their music, when it appears, is modest. A bone flute in the evening. A low hum while carding wool. The rhythm of stone being worked, which becomes its own cadence. Sometimes children sing counting games for water or freeze-cycles. Sometimes a throat-song, brief and resonant, rises from a sheltered court and stops before it becomes too much. They do not fill silence unnecessarily. Silence is part of the acoustic weather. It carries information. It lets distant sounds arrive. A city that never stops making noise would be deaf to rockfall, to animal distress, to the first crack of ice failing.

Their feelings are no less intense for being restrained. They love their children fiercely, because children are future labor and future memory. They mourn their dead quietly, often in ways tied to stone and weather. Some may place the dead in high niches where wind and birds finish what life began. Others may lay bodies beneath scree on safe slopes, marked by simple cairns. Still others may keep small bones in wall shrines. In all cases, there is a sense that the dead remain part of the city’s structure, not as ghosts but as precedent. They were here. They built this. They marked that crack. They remembered this tank. Their names may be few, but their works remain.
