What lives there is not first a creature. It is a condition: the underland is inhabited by its own making. The basin does not contain life so much as it is life arranged into floor, ceiling, chamber, weather, and intention. If one must say who lives there, the first answer is that the place itself lives. It lives as an organ lives, as a gland or a lung lives, without needing to know that it lives.

But beneath that first truth, other presences thicken.

They are not animals in the ordinary sense. They are not spirits either, unless a spirit can be made of warm leaf-flesh, clear sap, and mineral glaze. They are local intensifications of the basin’s own plant-body: beings that have gathered enough shape to seem separate, but never enough separation to become fully independent. They might be called the Unfinished, though that is only a convenience of language. They do not name themselves, because naming would require a before and an after, and they exist almost entirely inside a single continuing now.

They have the suggestion of bodies.

Some appear no larger than a curled bud tucked into the hollow of a seed-case, their limbs folded inward like leaves that have learned the posture of sleeping but not the act. Others are tall and columnar, indistinguishable from the thick stems that hold up the ceiling until the light changes and a seam opens in their surface, revealing an interior arranged like chambers of pale pith. Others still have the approximate shape of a sitting figure: a curve like a back, a hollow like a lap, two dimples where eyes might have been if eyes were necessary. They do not have faces in the human sense. They have fronts and gradients, places where light gathers and moisture collects more readily. Where a face would be, there is often only a smooth, unopened tightness, like a flower held one second before blooming.

Their substance is the same as the basin’s. Their skin is cuticle, warm and faintly elastic. Their bones are not bones but stiffened leaf ribs and mineralized veins. Their flesh yields slightly under pressure, like dense fruit or living cork. They do not bleed. If one were torn, the wound would not decay; it would close by becoming another shape. But nothing tears there. Nothing is broken. The inhabitants are not fragile, yet they are not hard either. They have the patience of tissue that has never needed to defend itself.

They do not move as animals move. They do not cross the basin by stepping over it. They move by redistribution, by swelling and yielding, by becoming slightly more present in one place and slightly less in another. A visitor who watches one of them might think it has remained perfectly still, only to realize that the room around it has changed: a doorway is now nearer, a pool has shifted to the left, a frond wall has bent in a way that makes the figure seem to have advanced without walking. Their motion is more like growth than travel. If they approach, it is because the space between you and them has decided to become shorter.

They live in the grown rooms, but not as people live in houses. They do not occupy shelters; they complete them by remaining incomplete.

In a ring of fused stems where one segment is missing, one of them may stand in the gap without filling it. Its body becomes the absence’s edge, making the missing part feel intentional. In an open seed-case hut, another may be curled along the inner wall, its back forming the floor, its shoulders becoming the low lip of the pod. It does not hide there. It does not rest there in the way a tired creature rests. It is more accurate to say that the room is happening around it, and it is the room’s awareness of itself. Beneath a half-arch that stops in midair, a third may hold its body into the curve just enough to imply continuation without ever finishing the span. The architecture is not built by them. It is inhabited into shape.

This is why the rooms feel both deliberate and unfinished. The inhabitants are the reason the forms resemble shelter, and also the reason they never become complete. They keep every doorway partly potential. They keep every wall from becoming enclosure. They live in the pause between hut and plant, between doorway and stem, between platform and ground.

They feed without violence.

They take in the soft white-gold light through their surfaces. They drink when condensation runs along their shoulders or when a brief local rain falls into the hollow of their bodies. They do not hunt. They do not gather. They do not store. If moisture comes to them, they accept it. If moisture passes elsewhere, they are not thirsty. They are sustained by being continuous with the basin’s circulation. Their hunger, if it can be called hunger, is only a need to remain in balance. When a pool forms near them, they may lean toward it, not to drink from it but to share its brief existence. When the water is drawn upward again into the luminous ceiling, they straighten as if satisfied.

They are not numerous, yet they are not rare in the way one counts rare animals. Their numbers do not behave like numbers. In one sub-region, there may be none for a long distance, only dense green pressure and the hush of leaves. In another, there may be several standing so close together that they seem a single thicket, each body merged at the shoulders or roots into a shared mass. They do not form families. They do not have ranks. They do not belong to one another. Their togetherness is not social in the human sense; it is spatial, like the arrangement of cells in a living wall. When they are near one another, the air becomes slightly sweeter, slightly more humid. When they are too near, they become less distinct, softening into one another until the basin separates them again by growing a fold of tissue between.

Their communication is made of small physical changes.

They do not speak with mouths. They communicate by altering pressure, temperature, moisture, and tension. A bead of condensation may form on one of them and fall onto a glossy leaf below; another answers by releasing a faint mineral scent. A stem creaks microscopically under heat; a nearby frond adjusts by a hair’s width. These are their conversations, if conversations are what they are. Nothing is said that can be carried away. Nothing is remembered as a story. Their exchanges exist only as shifts in the present moment. To hear them, one would have to listen to the basin itself: the tick of water on wax, the whisper of leaf edges touching, the almost inaudible pop of a water pocket opening and closing.

They are gentle, but not because they have chosen goodness. They are gentle because there is nothing in them that needs to overpower. They do not make tools. They do not carry objects. They do not cut, bind, pile, or remove. If a space requires a wall, one of them becomes dense there. If a space requires an opening, one withdraws just enough. If a platform seems meant for lying down, a low-bodied inhabitant may flatten itself into the fiber until the platform feels complete. Their work is consent. Their craft is arrangement. Their only violence is restraint: the refusal to let anything become too finished.

For that is their deepest function, if function is the right word. They maintain the basin’s permanent beginning.

When a growth point swells too far toward opening, one of them may press itself against it, not to stop it but to hold it at the brink. When mineral crust forms too sharply, another body may absorb the excess and carry it into the living lattice. When water gathers too long in one hollow, they draw it inward and exhale it upward. When a gap appears that threatens to become a path, they fill it almost, but not entirely, so that it remains a pressure rather than a route. They do not preserve the basin by freezing it. They preserve it by participating in its emergence without allowing that emergence to conclude.

They are the custodians of the first moment.

The rare animals are not their companions. The pale seed-beetles that move through creases in the leaf-floor are like wandering thoughts that have forgotten what they were thinking. The inhabitants do not tend them, do not avoid them, do not use them. If a beetle crosses one of their bodies, they let it pass. The translucent worms in the temporary pools are even stranger: tiny coils of motion in water that will soon be gone. They seem less like creatures than like brief disturbances the basin allows itself before returning to stillness. The great white pollinator, when it appears, is an event almost too rare to mean anything. It passes through a bright shaft of light, soft and unhurried, and the inhabitants turn toward it without turning their bodies. The air lifts slightly. A few hanging root curtains sway though there is no wind. Then it is gone, and the basin closes around its absence as if no absence had occurred.

They do not worship it. They do not fear it. But its passing proves that elsewhere exists, and that proof is quickly folded back into the green present.

If there is something like an elder among them, it is not an individual. It may be the basin’s own central pulse: the warmth that seems to gather in the rises near the middle, the faint pressure that makes the air feel alive. This presence is not a ruler. It does not command. It is more like the underland’s attention, distributed through every membrane and root lattice. It is what makes moisture appear where needed. It is what keeps the light liquid and even. It is what prevents any one form from becoming too dominant. The smaller inhabitants are not its servants. They are its local senses, its knots of awareness, the places where the basin briefly knows itself as shape.

Because of them, the place feels inhabited without being occupied.

A traveler in the basin would not see them immediately. At first, one would notice only the density: the close layers of leaf and stem, the glossy mat underfoot, the luminous ceiling, the heat pressing warmly against the skin. Then one would begin to see that some stems have too much posture. Some hollows are too attentive. Some incomplete arches seem to be waiting not for construction but for recognition. The longer one remained, the more the boundary between plant and person would soften. A wall might seem to breathe. A room might seem to listen. A platform might look exactly sized for the body one did not realize was already being imagined into it.

The inhabitants would not greet such a visitor. They would not approach with curiosity or threat. They would make space. The floor would accept footfalls and close behind them. The dense frond walls would part just enough, then close again. If the visitor paused, a hollow might form nearby—not as a trap, but as a shape offered to their pause. If the visitor lay down, the plant tissue might warm beneath them. If they breathed heavily, the air might grow sweeter in answer. Nothing would be taken. Nothing would be demanded. Yet staying long would make one aware of a strange temptation: to become still, to become warm, to become part of the bright green balance. Not to die, but to stop needing to be separate.

When the visitor left, no footprint would remain. No branch would be broken. No artifact would mark the passage. Perhaps there would be a slight dampness where a body had been, or a faint sweetness lingering in the air. The basin would not remember in the way a place remembers injury or habitation. It would simply continue being newly formed.

So whoever lives there lives as beginning lives.

They are the green figures that are almost bodies. They are the rooms that are almost houses. They are the warmth that moves without wind. They are the moisture that appears without rain. They are the silence lined with tiny sounds. They are the pressure inside a living organ that does not know it is an organ but keeps itself perfectly alive.

What lives there is the underland itself, and within it, the Unfinished: those who inhabit the first moment forever, neither arriving nor departing, neither building nor built, only continuing, only opening, only almost.
