Whatever lives there has learned to be minor.

It does not announce itself with leaves, flocks, crowds, or loud need. It fits into seams, pressure changes, warm cracks, drainage channels, and the slow schedules of machines. The city is not empty because nothing can live in it. It is empty because life there has stopped taking the form of a population. What remains lives by persistence, not abundance.

The first resident is the hydrology itself.

Not water as weather, not water as rain arriving neatly from a sky, but water as a condition of the city: rising, condensing, beading, falling, weeping, gathering, returning. It has territories and habits. It favors cool tile, shaded iron, the undersides of arches, the lips of drains. It avoids certain hot grates, then finds them again when pressure shifts. It climbs as vapor from the basin floor, rests on higher surfaces, thickens into beads, and drops where it has grown heavy. In that sense, water is not merely present there. It circulates like blood. It keeps time. It decides which floors are passable, which walls darken, which ceilings become sources, which rooms become cisterns, which machines cough and settle.

If the city has a chief citizen, it is this moving damp.

But water alone does not make a place alive. What makes the city alive is that so many other things have adapted to its rule.

The mineral crusts are the first evidence of that adaptation, though they are not alive in any ordinary sense. They grow. They crust. They thicken over bolts, stair edges, pipe joints, and the mouths of drains. They form brittle white frills where evaporation is strongest and darker, glassy beads where mineral content is high. They are the city’s slow accretions, built from the same chemistry that makes the air heavy. They do not eat, do not breathe, do not flee. Yet they occupy the place like living tissue, filling gaps, changing shapes, making new surfaces for other lives to cling to. They are the city’s calluses.

After them come the truest small lives.

They begin as felts and films. In warm cracks where stone stays just below burning, there are rust-colored microbial crusts, thin as breath, spreading in patches no larger than a hand or as wide as a door. They are not green. They are ochre, gray, brown, sometimes a dull orange that looks as if iron has bloomed. They feed on heat, mineral traces, and the chemical residue left by evaporating water. They do not rustle. They do not smell like soil. They are life reduced to patience.

On the undersides of ceramic tiles, behind grilles, inside service cavities where condensation collects but direct heat does not reach, there are pale filaments. Fungal or fungal-like, they branch in delicate networks over dust and metal salts. They are white, gray-white, or faintly blue with mineral stain. They do not fruit. They do not flower. They simply spread in fine lines, taking what they need from damp surfaces. Where a pipe grows too hot, they retreat. Where a wall cools, they advance. They are quiet, but they are alive, and they are everywhere if one knows where to look.

These films and filaments are the city’s meadow and undergrowth, though they provide none of the comfort usually meant by those words. They do not soften the world. They do not make shade. They make a base.

And on that base, a few animals exist.

They are rare, small, and difficult to see. In warm pools where the water is shallow and mineral-rich, translucent creatures move in sudden jerks: brine worms, shrimp-like things, pale larvae with no color except the faint cloudiness of their own bodies. They are almost invisible until a lamp catches them, and then they flash like scratches in glass. They feed on microbial films and mineral precipitates. They do not swarm. A pool that seems empty may contain only three or four of them, moving with mechanical starts and stops, then freezing again.

Around certain vents and warmer drains, there are mineralized husks. Some look crustacean; some do not. They may be molted shells, or the remains of older forms that could not survive a change in heat or salinity. They stand half-fused to stone, white or rust-streaked, hollow and delicate. They suggest that life once had more variety here, or that it still does, but only in places too hot, too deep, or too difficult to observe.

In the drier of the wet places — behind inspection panels, under raised walkways, inside boxes where fungal threads grow over dust — there are tiny grazers. Eyeless mites, pale springtails, things with jointed legs and no wings. They move with abruptness, then stop as if remembering a rule. They are not numerous enough to be called a swarm. One may watch a wall for a long time and see nothing, then notice a single white speck shift twice its own length and vanish behind a crust.

Occasionally, a larger shape passes.

It may be a heat-adapted moth with narrow wings the color of wet stone, drawn to the amber glow of industrial lamps. It may be a scavenger, flat-bodied and pale, moving through the space between pipes where condensation collects and dead filaments fall. It may be only steam misread by tired eyes. The city does not offer confirmation. It gives glimpses and then withdraws them.

Fauna there does not depend on green life. It depends on heat gradients, mineral films, waste moisture, microbial crusts, and the shelter offered by architecture. It is sparse because the place is harsh. It is quiet because sound is swallowed. It is hidden because there is no foliage to conceal it, only hard surfaces and damp shadows.

Alongside these biological lives are the mechanical ones, and in this city the distinction matters less than it would elsewhere.

The machines are not alive as animals are alive. They do not hunger, do not breed, do not heal. But they occupy the city as inhabitants. They have positions, rhythms, duties, failures, recoveries. Some are small enough to be mistaken for valves: brass-bodied regulators with cracked pressure faces, little wheeled scrapers that move along gutter rails when condensation reaches a certain level, iron boxes that shudder and click in response to changes below. Others are enormous: fans set into walls, flywheels under sheds, pump assemblies whose pistons rise and fall once every several heartbeats. Many are so crusted with mineral deposit that they seem less built than grown. They have the look of sleeping creatures covered in shell.

Some of the automatons — if that word is not too generous — are clearly old. Their movements are worn smooth by repetition. They do not hurry. They do not appear to notice anything except pressure, temperature, flow, or the failure of a component. A valve wheel may turn by itself after hours of stillness. A drip pan may shift position on a track. A heavy door may close at the same hour every day because a buried timer still believes someone will need it closed. These motions are not random. They are routines without owners.

They are the city’s fauna of duty.

They do not gather in groups. They do not fight. They do not seem to rest, though they sometimes pause for long intervals as if waiting for a signal that has not arrived. Their bodies are stained orange and brown, glazed with salt, darkened by heat. Where they move often, they have polished the stone. Where they have failed, they remain in place because removal would require more energy than the city can spare. In this way, broken machines become architecture, and architecture becomes behavior.

The larger systems behave almost like organs.

The pump houses are hearts, slow and irregular. The reservoirs are bladders and kidneys, collecting and releasing. The heat exchangers are lungs breathing vapor into vapor. The pipe galleries are veins. The chimneys are pores. The lamps are weak nerves, firing amber in the dark. None of this implies a mind. It only implies integration. The city’s parts answer one another. A pressure drop in one district causes a valve elsewhere to open. A rise in heat beneath a plaza sends vapor up through grates, thickens mist over nearby roofs, increases condensation in cooler halls, fills a trough, trips a float, starts a pump. The sequence may have no witness. It continues anyway.

That is one of the most unsettling facts of the place: it maintains itself without needing to be seen.

And yet, not all maintenance is mechanical.

There are also people there, or beings close enough to people that the word still almost fits.

They are few. Not enough to make a crowd. Not enough to fill even one of the broad avenues if they chose to stand together, which they do not. They are scattered across the upper levels, the drier galleries, the raised walkways, the sealed rooms above the flooded courts. They live where floors can be cambered toward drains, where thresholds can carry water away, where roofs can be kept just intact enough to prevent indoor rain from becoming unbearable. Their presence is suggested by polished handrails, recently opened panels, lamps that burn with purpose, footprints in mineral dust, and the faint smell of warm oil on metal.

If you encounter one, you will probably not recognize it at first as a person.

The damp and the dim make all shapes uncertain. It will be hooded, perhaps, or wearing a glazed coat that shines where droplets cling to it. Its boots will be thick-soled, either wood, ceramic composite, or layered rubberized cloth, meant to resist heat and sharp crusts. Its hands will be gloved. Its face may be hidden behind a mask or condenser veil: a lattice of ceramic or metal that filters mineral dust, recovers moisture from breath, and warms the air before it enters the lungs. The mask is not necessarily for poison. The air is breathable, but heavy, thin at altitude, saturated with heat and mineral vapor. To live in it for long is to learn how to breathe carefully.

Their bodies show the effects of the place.

They are likely pale, not from cold but from low diffuse light and long avoidance of direct sun. Their skin may be flushed in patches where heat settles, or marked by rough mineral irritations at the wrists, neck, and jaw. Their eyes are adapted to the pearl-gray dimness: wary of sudden brightness, quick to track movement near the ground, trained to read the difference between a safe crust and a brittle one. Their hands are strong and precise. They move slowly, not because they are weak, but because haste is dangerous there. A sudden step can break a crust. A careless lean can collapse a railing. A loud voice wastes breath in air that already feels used.

They do not shout. They tap.

Their communication is fitted to a place where sound travels badly. A knuckle against a pipe can carry farther than a call. A scraped rail can warn of unsafe flooring. A lamp flashed twice can mean stop. Three slow flashes can mean pressure rising. If they speak aloud, it is in low voices, with short phrases, often while their eyes remain on the ground or the machinery. They listen more than they talk. They listen for pumps, for the change in a drip’s rhythm, for the hollow note that means a floor has become unstable, for the almost silent click of a valve about to open.

They are caretakers, but not masters.

They do not command the city. They tend it. They clear blocked drains. They scrape scale from condenser fins. They replace cracked gauge faces with older ones salvaged from dead rooms. They oil mechanisms that have no names anymore. They mark safe routes with copper tags, salt lines, or notches in railings. They close panels that should not remain open, and sometimes they open panels that should not remain closed. They know which pools are thermally charged, which stairways flood when the aquifer rises, which lamp indicates a junction still served by power, which door leads to a lower level that should not be entered without a rope and a partner.

They may believe that if they stop, the city will drown, overheat, or seize. They may be right. Or they may simply have inherited a set of necessary gestures and continued them because stopping would require a decision no one is prepared to make.

Their homes are practical and damp despite every effort.

They are raised above the wettest floors. Their rooms have gutters inside, thresholds like low dams, ceilings angled to guide condensation into channels. Their beds are platforms or suspended frames, not because of comfort but because the ground is never trustworthy. They keep tools near doors: valve keys, scrapers, coils of waxed line, ceramic knives, lamp oil, spare seals. Their walls may be marked with tallies, pressure charts, or diagrams of pipe routes scratched into tile. They do not decorate with plants. They have none. If there is ornament, it is mineral: a crystal growth preserved under glass, a polished piece of blue-glazed pipe, a shard of porcelain-like ceramic, a carefully arranged collection of rust colors.

They do not farm in any green sense.

There is no grain, no vine, no leaf, no orchard, no garden. What they eat comes from the city’s own chemistry. They cultivate microbial mats in shallow warm trays, scraping them into flat, dense cakes. They harvest brine worms and small translucent crustaceans from pools that are safe enough to manage. They distill condensate into water pure enough to drink, though it may still taste faintly of metal or stone. They dry fungal filaments over heat vents and press them with salt. Their food is scarce, salty, mineral, and sufficient only because their lives have become small enough to match it.

Children, if there are children, learn early that the city is not a place for running.

They learn balance on catwalks. They learn to test crusts with a stick before trusting them. They learn which drips are harmless and which mean a pressure line is leaking above. They learn not to chase shadows. They learn that a lamp does not mean welcome, only presence. They learn that quiet is not emptiness. A child raised there would probably think silence has plumbing.

There may be more than one kind of keeper.

Some may be descendants of the original builders, preserving routines without full understanding. Some may be exiles, refugees, or workers left behind when automation made most hands unnecessary. Some may have changed across generations: lungs adapted to thin, humid altitude air, skin less troubled by mineral dust, eyes better suited to low light. Some may no longer be entirely biological. In a city of valves, gauges, and replaced parts, it would not be strange if bodies had been repaired in the same spirit as machines: ceramic joints, copper-braced ribs, sealed membranes over damaged skin, breath filters worn so long they feel like faces.

The city does not require purity. It requires continuation.

Below the places where the keepers move, there are stranger possibilities.

In flooded lower halls, where warm water covers old rail lines and the ceilings drip into black pools, there may be things that have adapted beyond the small fauna of the upper levels. Pale scavengers with flattened bodies. Eyeless crawlers that feed on rust films. Mineralized creatures, or creatures so encrusted that motion itself looks stony. Perhaps they are real. Perhaps they are only old machines stirring in the dark. The city gives no clear answer. It produces shapes from steam and reflection, then lets them dissolve.

There are husks near vents that suggest older life: shells, casings, rib-like frames, things with too many joints to be comfortable. They may be fossils of a previous ecology, or molted remains of something still living in the deeper heat. They are not comforting. They do not suggest a gentle world. They suggest that life there has always had to bargain with temperature, chemistry, and pressure.

And then there is the life that is not biological at all.

The missing crowd lives there as wear.

It lives in handrails polished by absent palms. It lives in stair nosings rounded by countless steps that no longer occur. It lives in doors that hang ajar because someone once passed through them often enough to keep their hinges bright. It lives in timers, schedules, pressure settings, lamp positions, and the height of certain inspection handles. It lives in the way a walkway bends around a place where people once gathered. It lives in the width of avenues built for processions that now carry only vapor.

This is not haunting in the ordinary sense. There is no sorrow attached to it, no sense of voices trying to return. It is more like the imprint of use left in matter. The city remembers its population the way a tool remembers a hand.

So if you ask who lives there, the honest answer is layered.

Water lives there as circulation.

Mineral crusts live there as accretion.

Microbial felts and pale filaments live there as the first true biological skin of the place.

Small translucent pool creatures, eyeless mites, springtails, rare moths, and glimpsed scavengers live there as fauna adapted to heat and mineral patience.

Machines live there as behavior without owners, moving through routines that have outlasted their makers.

A few hooded keepers live there, human or near-human, tending systems whose original purpose may have narrowed into survival itself.

The absence of crowds lives there as shape.

And beneath all of them, the city itself lives as a kind of slow organism: not conscious, not intentional, but integrated. It breathes through vents. It sweats through walls. It digests stone into crust. It turns heat into vapor, vapor into drip, drip into brine, brine into pump-work, pump-work into heat again. It does not need applause. It does not need witnesses. It has become its own weather, its own habitat, its own reason for continuing.

The inhabitants of this city are therefore not a species. They are a continuum.

At one end: condensation on iron.

At the next: a white filament spreading over dust.

Then a brine worm flicking through warm water.

Then a mite freezing behind a crust.

Then a machine turning a valve no one ordered turned.

Then a lamp moving slowly across a pipe bridge, carried by a figure whose face is hidden behind a condenser mask.

Then the faint impression of all the feet that came before.

None of them fills the city. All of them keep it from being merely dead.

If you stand long enough in one of its empty plazas, you will begin to see it: the city is not waiting for life to return. Life has already changed its clothes. It has put on salt, rust, ceramic, fungal thread, slow iron, and the patient bodies of those who can live without green things. It has learned to be quiet. It has learned to be few. It has learned that in a high, hot, wet basin with no plants and little softness, the most durable form of life is not abundance.

It is maintenance.

And somewhere beyond the mist, a pump breathes in. A bead of water falls. A crust crackles faintly. A lamp reflects in a puddle like a room buried under stone. The city continues.
