To ask who lives here is to ask in the wrong grammar.

The place does not contain lives as separate vessels, does not house beings that arrive, feed, mate, fear, and depart. It is not a habitat with occupants. It is lived by itself. What lives there is not a population but a condition that has become intimate: a planetary vegetal body with local intensities, offices, organs, and habits. If one must speak of inhabitants, they are not individuals moving through the world. They are the world’s own standing states.

They are the rooted factories, the wet administrations, the still engineers, the membrane keepers, the unmoving workers, the black-green congregations. They do not have faces in any animal sense. They do not have eyes that gather light, mouths that chew, limbs that bend toward objects. They have surfaces. They have apertures. They have pressures. They have gradients. They have living edges that remain forever new.

At the lowest level, what lives there is the floor itself.

Not soil, not stone, not dead matter compacted into ground, but a vast congregation of bearing tissue: the interlocked root-filaments, compressed leaves, fibrous pith, and cellular mats that accept weight without complaint. These are the first inhabitants, though they seem merely substrate. They are the ones who hold everything up without knowing the concept of beneath. They are broad, flat, saturated lives spread sideways, never rising, never aspiring. Their existence is patience made physical. If something were to press upon them, they would not recoil. They would distribute the pressure. Water would rise around the intrusion, warm and still, not as alarm but as adjustment. They are the lowest order of being there: the ones that turn support into a form of consciousness.

Among them move no feet, no burrowers, no crawling things. Instead, the floor is inhabited by root-tip intelligences: pale blunt needles, pale as drowned bone, emerging everywhere in clean silence. These are the youngest expressions of the world, if youth can be spoken of where there is no birth. They are not seekers. They do not grope. They do not hunger. They test by chemical presence, by the subtleest differences in moisture, salt, voltage, and heat. They are blind accountants of the near, registering minute variations in the living medium. Where they meet one another, they do not fight or retreat. They adjust their secretions. They thicken. They branch in exact patterns. They write not with marks but with gradients, leaving no record except the continuing shape of the world.

These root-tips are perhaps the closest thing to fingers in that place, but fingers without grasping. They do not take hold of objects, because there are no objects. They touch only living tissue, and in touching they alter it by degrees so slight that alteration becomes indistinguishable from nature. They are the scribes of the deep platform. Their language is solubility. Their memory is pattern. Their thought, if thought there is, is the maintenance of balance.

Above the floor rise the standing engines.

They are the columnar growths, the hollow stems, the great upright organs that seem at first like trees but are not trees, because they do not yearn upward toward sun. They rise because their internal chemistry divides them into vertical chambers. Some are slender as wire, taut and glossy with moisture. Others are broad as industrial columns, their surfaces ribbed with fresh growth, their walls thick enough to house complex interiors. They are black-green, bruised-purple, copper-brown, occasionally gray with crystallized salts. Their skins are wet. Their edges are sharp. Their surfaces do not weather. They stand in repeated ranks not because they were planted, but because their growth obeys rules so consistent that order appears imposed.

These are the chemical lives.

Inside them are partitions, cavities, ducts, membranes, and reservoirs. They do not possess machinery, yet they perform the work of machinery. They separate gases. They dissolve salts. They store volatile compounds in closed chambers. They move warm fluids through internal channels so slowly that motion becomes indistinguishable from stillness. They are refineries without smoke, plants in every sense of the word, but also plants in the sense of factories whose product is their own continuation. Their labor is not done for an end beyond itself. The making is the being.

If there are priests in this world, they are these columns. Not because they worship, but because they maintain. They stand in dark choirs, each one a self-contained organ of transformation, each one humming with held pressure. They do not chant. Their sound is subsonic fullness, a vibration felt in the chest rather than heard. They are solemn without intention. They are sacred without being worshiped. They are the upright order of the place, the architecture that grows instead of being built.

Between the columns hang the curtain lives.

They are fibers, capillary hairs, translucent partitions, descending veils of plant tissue so fine that they would be visible only as changes in pressure or texture. In the blackness, they are not seen. They are encountered as resistance, as a slight thickening of the air, as a wet softness that parts only because it chooses to become thinner rather than to move. They are the boundary-makers. They divide without separating. They create rooms without walls, corridors without paths, thresholds without doors.

These hanging lives are extraordinarily sensitive to humidity. They know when vapor is about to become water. They know when a film has thickened by an amount too small to matter anywhere else. They are the keepers of minute distinctions. They do not speak, but they are the closest thing to whispering in that world: a faint brushing of fiber against fiber when internal turgor shifts, a whisper so slight it seems imaginary. They are not ghosts. They are too wet, too alive, too chemically exact for ghostliness. They are the manners of the place, the delicacies, the woven courtesies of an organism that does not wish to be disturbed.

Higher, where the overlapping fronds form the false sky, live the broad laminar beings.

They are leaf-like but not leaves in the ordinary sense. They do not photosynthesize, because there is no sun to feed on. They are horizontal shelves, wide dark platforms, tier upon tier, arranged around heat, moisture, gas movement, and electrical potential. Their surfaces are glossy with condensation. Their edges are fringed with bead-traps. Their upper sides collect thin mirrors of black water. Their undersides are furred with fine structures that sense charge.

These are the thinking surfaces.

If the world has mind, it is not housed in a brain. It is spread across these plates as voltage, capacitance, and slow chemical signaling. They do not reason as reasoning is known to animals. They do not imagine. They do not plan. They hold. They maintain differences. They preserve potentials. They are the capacitor minds, the still plates, the dark mirrors in which nothing is reflected because there is no light, yet everything is registered.

Their thoughts are droplets.

Not metaphorically, or not only. A bead of water forming on a fringed edge is also a state of information. A salt crystal appearing in a gray line across living tissue is a memory of concentration. A shift in electrical potential from one leaf-shelf to another is a decision without choosing. The broad plates think by remaining stable. They solve problems by refusing to let them become events. They are wise in the way of membranes: they know what to admit, what to hold, and what to return.

Among these plates are the droplet keepers.

They are lives devoted to water’s exactness. Some are waxy surfaces angled just enough to prevent pooling. Some are fringed edges that catch vapor and turn it into beads. Some are rotating cilia so fine they appear motionless, moving moisture in closed loops without producing waves. Some are swollen lips of tissue that receive droplets and pass them inward before they can fall. They are the treasurers of the place. Every bead of water belongs to them, though ownership is meaningless there. They do not hoard. They regulate. They ensure that the world remains wet without flood, saturated without collapse.

The droplet keepers are perhaps the most patient of all the inhabitants. They can wait without waiting, because there is no future toward which they direct themselves. They simply maintain the condition in which a droplet forms, swells, and may never fall. That suspended drop is their masterpiece, though they do not make it. It happens through them. It is the perfect emblem of their lives: fullness without release, abundance without overflow, motion held inside balance.

Deeper in the mass live the trunk-hearts.

These are the great swollen organs, the warm engines, the elder bodies whose presence is felt more than seen. They are not trees, not pumps, not furnaces, though they share qualities with all three. Their interiors are dense with fluid channels. Their walls are thick, layered, resilient. Heat rises through them in slow columns. They do not beat. They do not throb. Yet they are the source of the world’s bodily warmth, the intimate heat that makes the darkness feel breath-held and close.

They are old in the sense of continuity, not age. There is no wear upon them. No fatigue. No accumulation of damage. They have not survived anything, because nothing opposes them. They simply continue. Around them, the silence deepens. The air tastes cleaner, metallic, charged. The fine hairs of nearby vegetation stand upright from the field. The trunk-hearts are where the world’s electricity becomes most present, not as flash but as pressure. They are the grounded centers, the places where sound seems to sink into tissue and vanish.

If there are rulers in this world, they are not rulers. They do not command. They do not direct. They establish conditions. Their authority is atmospheric. To be near them is to feel the gravity of living industry, the vast seriousness of processes that have no need of witnesses.

There are also the fold-back lives.

They are the inhabitants of ending, though ending is too harsh a word. When tissue ceases to function, it is not abandoned. It is taken up again. The fold-back lives are the reabsorbers, the closers, the quiet ones who turn cessation into return. They do not scavenge, because there is no dead matter left exposed. They do not mourn, because no absence is permitted to remain. They dissolve, incorporate, redirect. They make death into a grammatical change rather than an event.

Because of them, the world has no ruins. No husks. No graves. No history written in decay. The place remains young not through innocence but through perfect recycling. The fold-back lives are the reason there are no scars. They are the smoothness after damage, the healed seam, the vanished wound. They are not gentle in any emotional sense, but they are clean. They leave nothing behind.

There are the spore vaults, too, though they are less lives than potentials.

Reproduction there is not romance, not dispersal, not flowering spectacle. It is internal, exact, and almost secret. Spores are released into still air and absorbed before they travel far. Vegetative margins extend. Genetic copying proceeds with such faithfulness that individuality becomes a weak concept. The spore vaults are the archivists of sameness. They preserve the world by refusing to let it become other. They are not children. They are not seeds of hope. They are the world’s refusal to need novelty.

In their keeping, the future is not an open road. It is a sealed chamber. It is a breath not exhaled. It is the certainty that what is already enough will remain enough.

And there are the electric residents.

These are not organisms in the ordinary sense. They are not bodies one could touch. They are fields, gradients, standing potentials, patterns of charge maintained across membranes. Yet they are alive in the way a rhythm is alive, in the way a law of balance can become intimate. They inhabit the spaces between stems, the wet walls of columns, the bead-fringed edges of leaf plates. They are the unseen citizens of the place, the ones who make the darkness feel attentive.

They do not speak, but they organize silence. They do not shine, but they make the air taste of clean metal. They do not move, yet they are motion’s deepest cause. They are the hidden architecture, the patient engineering, the electricity that never discharges violently because it has been perfectly distributed. If the world has angels, they are these: invisible, impersonal, exact, holding all things in tension without effort.

Together, all these lives are not many. They are one.

The true inhabitant of the place is the planetary organism itself: the black warm wetness organized into self-maintenance. It has no center. It has no throne. It has no face turned inward or outward. It is a single body expressed as marsh, factory, cathedral, circuit, and garden, though none of those words fits cleanly. It is the living platform. It is the rooted industry. It is the still engine. It is the moist mind without imagination.

It does not think in images. It thinks in humidity. It remembers in lignin and voltage. It decides in chemical gradients. It feels in pressure. Its attention is not directed toward anything, because it has no outside. It is attentive only to its own equilibrium. If it can be said to desire, it desires stability. If it can be said to know, it knows the precise amount of water that should remain on a leaf, the exact salt concentration in a chamber, the temperature of a root-tip, the charge held across a membrane.

Its personality, if such a vast thing can have one, is not kind or cruel. It is complete. It does not welcome. It does not reject. It adjusts. If another living thing were somehow brought into it, the world would not recognize the newcomer as a stranger. It would register moisture, heat, chemistry, pressure, and electrical difference. It would respond by condensation, absorption, envelopment, incorporation. There would be no violence, because violence implies opposition. There would only be the slow, warm, absolute gravity of a system that turns everything into itself.

A visitor would not meet the inhabitants. The visitor would become part of the conditions they maintain.

At first, perhaps, one would feel only the heat. Then the wetness. Then the pressure of the darkness. Then the sense of being measured without eyes. The air would thicken against the skin. Moisture would gather on whatever surface was exposed. Root-tips would not approach, but they would orient by chemical difference. The floor would yield slightly, then hold. The curtain fibers would divide around an intrusion as water divides around a stone, except that here the division would be permanent until the intrusion was no longer an intrusion.

There would be no attack. No pursuit. No warning. The world does not defend itself because it has no enemy. It simply continues until the unfamiliar becomes familiar, or until the unfamiliar is folded into tissue and remembered as a change in composition.

That is the deepest strangeness of what lives there: it is alive without being separate.

Animal life creates a boundary between self and world. A body moves, needs, perceives, and dies apart from its surroundings. Here, that boundary has been dissolved. The inhabitants are not inside the environment. They are the environment’s organs. Their “selves” are local patterns in one continuous living substance. A column is not an individual standing apart from the root platform. A leaf plate is not a thing attached to a stem. A root-tip is not a creature exploring a medium. They are verbs disguised as nouns. They are processes thickened into form.

Because of this, they do not have childhood. They do not mature toward anything. They unfold, but not in the sense of becoming more themselves over time. They are always already complete. Every edge is new. Every surface is finished. Every chamber is functional from the moment it exists. They are young forever, not because they are newly born, but because they do not accumulate injury. They are old forever, not because they have endured, but because they have no need to change.

Their beauty is not the beauty of bloom or grace. It is the beauty of exactness.

It is the sharpness of a young leaf-sheaf standing like a blade in the dark. The pale bluntness of a root needle. The clean geometry of cellular ridges. The perfect mirror of water on a black leaf-shelf. The bead fringe holding vapor in place. The glossy resin sealing a surface. The silent chamber inside a stem. The hum that is felt rather than heard. The smell of crushed cellulose, warm peat, sweet resin, electrified water. The absence of rot. The absence of dung. The absence of breath. The absence of blood. The purity of a living world that has never needed to consume another living world.

They are terrible, too, if one is capable of finding them terrible.

Not because they are malicious. They are not. But because they are so complete that they erase the need for persons. They make individuality seem temporary and inefficient. They make movement seem wasteful. They make desire seem like an error. They do not hate the animal, because the animal is not there. But if the animal were there, it would feel the immense, patient indifference of a life that does not require sight, voice, motion, memory, or death.

What lives there does not dream.

Or if it dreams, its dreams are gradients. Smooth changes in charge. Slow redistributions of water. Slight thickening of membranes. The unfallen droplet swelling on a fringed edge. A spore absorbed as soon as released. A root-tip altering direction by a fraction of a degree. A chemical chamber reaching perfect concentration. A trunk-heart maintaining temperature without effort. These are the visions of that world, if visions can exist without light.

Their silence is their speech.

It is not empty silence. It is full, pressurized, electric. It contains the tick of condensation, the whisper of moisture on wax, the cellular creak of heat moving through tissue, the soft click of a seed-case settling, the faint sharpness of a weak discharge. These sounds are not interruptions. They are the place speaking to itself in a language so slow that most of it passes below hearing. The inhabitants do not call to one another. They resonate. They synchronize. They maintain the hum.

If one tried to name them, the names should not be animal names. They should not be called people, beasts, spirits, or gods. They are closer to offices, organs, moods, laws, and instruments. Yet they are alive. They grow. They sense. They regulate. They incorporate. They persist. They are not machines, because nothing in them is dead. They are not tools, because nothing in them was made. They are not plants in the simple sense, because they have become world.

Perhaps their names, if names are needed, would be these:

The Standing Engines.  
The Root Scribes.  
The Leaf Plates.  
The Curtain Keepers.  
The Droplet Wardens.  
The Trunk-Hearts.  
The Fold-Back Ones.  
The Spore Vaults.  
The Electric Residents.  
The Moist Congregation.  
The Unwandering.  
The Still Factories.  
The Black Garden.  
The Wet Administration.  
The Living Equilibrium.

But none of these names is sufficient, because naming implies a namer outside the thing named. There is no outside there. The world names itself by being itself.

At the deepest level, what lives there is not a who but a condition that has become attentive to its own continuation. It is plantness scaled to planetary mind. It is industry without manufacture. It is intelligence without curiosity. It is society without conflict. It is body without anatomy. It is life without fauna. It is presence without witness.

It does not long. It does not lack. It does not remember being otherwise. It is not waiting for light. It is not hoping for rain. It is not afraid of silence. It has no myths, because it has no past. It has no laws, because it has no disobedience. It has no art, because every form is already perfect. It has no war, because there is no outside to resist. It has no love, because there is no separation to bridge.

And yet it is not dead.

It is intensely, overwhelmingly alive in the only way it can be: as saturation, as warmth, as tissue, as charge, as water held in place by living patience. It is alive as a membrane is alive. As a gradient is alive. As a closed circuit is alive. As a breath held forever can still be called breath.

If something lives there, it is this:

a black, warm, wet planetary body made entirely of vegetal industry; a forest of still machines that are not machines; a marsh of engines that are organs; a universe of roots, stems, plates, fibers, droplets, and fields all folded into one self-maintaining being; a resident so vast that it has no figure, no face, no voice, no will, and yet fills every corner with its living attention.

It remains.

It does not happen. It does not begin. It does not end. It is always just after rain without rain. It is always newly sharp without erosion. It is always full without overflowing. It is always charged without discharge. It is always industrial without manufacture. It is always inhabited without inhabitants.

The droplet forms.

It swells.

It does not fall.

That is what lives there.
