What lives in the basin lives thinly, quietly, and as if under a rule not to accumulate.

There are no green things here. No roots, no leaves, no soft stems, no pollen, no smell of crushed vegetation. Life does not announce itself as life. It appears instead as stain, film, crust, residue, and movement so slow or so brief that it can be mistaken for mineral behavior. The basin’s inhabitants are not its owners. They are permitted exceptions in a landscape dominated by heat, stone, salt, pressure, and sound.

If one asks *who* lives there in the human sense, the answer is likely no one. No people could belong to this place without bringing artifacts with them: vessels, shade, tools, stored water, markers, paths. The basin gives no surplus for such things. It is too sparse, too warm, too constant in its noise, too poor in food and shelter. A person could cross it, perhaps, but could not become part of it without ceasing to be a builder. And this place selects against building.

What lives here is mostly smaller than notice.

The true inhabitants are the microbial skins.

They coat the basin in living layers thinner than paper. They are not plants, not mosses, not lichens in any ordinary sense, though a careless eye might borrow those words. They are chemosynthetic films, mineral-binding mats, bacterial and archaeal communities feeding on gradients of sulfur, iron, salt, heat, and dissolved gas. They live where chemistry becomes unstable: at the edges of brine pools, along damp fissures, inside shaded pores, on the inner walls of old vent tubes, beneath crusts of sulfate, under flakes of black desert varnish.

These skins are the basin’s primary life. Everything else depends on them, directly or distantly.

In some places they are black, almost lacquer-like, sealing rock with a thin varnish that seems painted. In others they are pale gray, buff, rust-colored, or faintly blue-green where copper-rich water leaves its tint behind. They do not grow upward. They spread sideways and inward, binding dust, trapping mineral particles, stabilizing surfaces, becoming part of the crust they inhabit. When they die, they do not rot generously. They dry, crack, darken, and are reabsorbed into the mineral skin of the basin. Their remains become varnish, stain, powder, salt-bound residue.

They are not abundant in the way grass is abundant. They are abundant in patience. A single patch may take centuries to widen by a hand’s breadth. They do not bloom. They do not overrun. They maintain a boundary. Where moisture is constant, they persist. Where moisture fails, they go dormant, shrink, or vanish into crust. The basin’s equilibrium is partly their doing: they hold dust in place, seal porous stone, moderate evaporation, and keep chemical surfaces in slow repair.

Around the brine pools, life becomes slightly more visible.

These pools are shallow, warm, and clear in their centers, their edges crusted with white salt. They are not ponds in the gentle sense. They are chemical bowls. The water is often brackish, sometimes bitter, sometimes metallic, always warm. It holds just enough life to support a few small animals, and not enough to support many.

In the clearest of them, tiny translucent crustaceans move in short, nervous flickers. They are not like shrimp from colder, richer waters. Their bodies are almost glassy, their organs faintly visible as darker specks within. They have little color. Some are colorless; others carry a dull rose or amber taint from the microbial films they eat. Their eyes, if present at all, are reduced. In many, vision has become unnecessary. They navigate by touch, by chemical taste, by the faint pressure waves made when droplets fall from overhanging rock.

They feed on the soft living films that coat submerged stones and the undersides of salt crusts. They graze slowly, with tiny scraping movements, never clearing a surface entirely. If they did, they would destroy their own refuge. Instead, they take just enough. Their populations remain low. A pool that seems empty one day may contain a dozen such creatures, each separated from the others by a small territory of warm water. They do not school. They do not swarm. Their presence is revealed only by a slight distortion in the water, a flicker, a moving grain of light.

Some of these crustaceans carry their eggs in salt-resistant cases. If a pool becomes more concentrated or dries partly, the eggs can endure. When moisture returns, they hatch into the same small world their parents occupied. Nothing expands. Nothing explodes. The population re-forms at its old limit.

In the sediment at the bottom of certain pools, there may be threadlike worms no thicker than hair. They are pale, nearly transparent, and live in narrow burrows lined with mineral grains. They feed on bacteria in the mud, or on organic particles that settle from the microbial films above. They rarely emerge. Their existence is mostly hidden, a faint movement beneath the surface, a tiny ripple in the warm brine.

Where condensation gathers, life becomes more secretive.

The basin is damp rather than wet, and this dampness is local. It collects under rock shelves, along cool undersides of basalt, in fissures, in the throats of old vents, wherever warm vapor meets a surface just cool enough to make droplets form. These are some of the richest places in the basin, though “rich” is a modest word here. A wet crack may support a thin film of microbes, a few mites, a handful of wingless insects, and nothing more.

The creatures of these seams are small, flattened, and pale. Mite-like arthropods live in the narrowest spaces, their bodies shaped to slide between mineral plates. Their surfaces are often dusted with fine salt or iron powder, making them look less like animals and more like mobile grains of the basin itself. They wait in dry periods with almost no movement, then become active when condensation returns. Their bodies are adapted to harvest moisture: microscopic grooves, hairs, and shell textures guide droplets toward their mouths or help them absorb damp from the air.

There are also wingless insects, if they can be called insects in the ordinary sense. They are not graceful. They are low, compact, and quiet. Many have lost flight because flight would be useless or dangerous in such an open place, where there are no plants to shelter them and the wind is slight but persistent. They move by running, not jumping. Their coloration is dull: salt-white, bone-gray, rust-brown, blackened basalt-dark. They feed on microbial crusts, dead arthropods, mineral-encrusted films, and whatever organic matter settles into moist cracks.

They do not sing. The basin is already loud. Sound made for communication would be swallowed or confused by the constant industrial chord of the place. Instead, these creatures use vibration, touch, humidity, and chemical traces. Some are sensitive to minute electrical changes in the air. When charge builds along salt crusts and tiny blue sparks discharge in the dark, certain crack-dwellers stir. Not in fear, perhaps, but in recognition of a recurring condition. A discharge may crack a crust, expose a fresh surface, release trapped moisture, or loosen microbial film. For life here, even electricity is part of maintenance.

Near the vent chimneys, the inhabitants become more specialized.

The chimneys stand like worn stacks, some hollow, some partly collapsed, some still breathing warm vapor through narrow stone throats. They are not new. Their surfaces are smoothed by long exposure, glazed by mineral deposits, darkened by heat and time. But inside them, where steam passes and condensation runs in slow threads, life persists in thin vertical refuges.

There are creatures like limpets, though not true limpets. They cling to the inner walls of warm tubes, where moisture is steady and microbial films renew themselves slowly. Their shells are not elegant. They are low, domed, rough, made of mineralized secretions and encrustations. When steam pulses rise through the chimney, they tighten themselves against the rock. When the pulse passes, they move a few millimeters to graze. Their motion is so slow that a watcher might spend an hour and see no change.

Some of these vent-dwellers have bodies adapted to heat gradients. Their lower surfaces are tolerant of warm stone; their upper surfaces are more resistant to dry air. They live in the narrow band where temperature and moisture balance. Move too high, and the air becomes too dry. Move too low, and the heat becomes oppressive. Their whole existence is a small vertical negotiation.

In certain wider chimneys, there may be hanging filaments: not roots, not fibers of stone, but living ribbons of tissue or colonial growth, slick with condensate. They extend only a little into the rising vapor, catching droplets and microscopic food carried upward. They are pale, soft, and rare. They do not sway dramatically. They tremble faintly with the breath of the vent. If they are animals, they are animals reduced to patience. If they are colonies, they behave like a single slow organ attached to the basin’s machinery.

The open flats themselves are almost lifeless, but not absolutely so.

Between the pools, chimneys, and crack refuges, the ground is wide, warm, and dry-looking. Crossing these flats is dangerous for small creatures because exposure is total. There is no cover, no moisture, no shade except the occasional shadow of a low ridge or stone. Yet even here, a few hardy movers pass.

They are scavengers, predators, or wanderers. Most are arthropods: flattened, dust-coated, long-legged or low-bodied, depending on their way of moving. Some keep to the darker crusts where the ground is slightly cooler. Others move only when the air is most humid, after one of the local drizzle bands has passed over a vent field.
