What lives there does not live upon the landscape. It lives within its saturation, in the pressure between grains, in the heat between water films, in the noise that has no silence. To ask who lives there is already to ask the wrong question, because the plain does not offer the old separation between dweller and dwelling. The inhabitants are not figures against a background. They are densities, textures, motions, stubborn repetitions. They are what happens when existence has no spare room.

The first and most numerous living things are not animals at all. They are skins.

They spread over mud, shell, stone, and water as microbial mats: black, gray, opalescent, sometimes faintly luminous, rarely green. They are the living surface of the plain, and also its record. They coat every stable fraction of ground, stretch across shallow pools, line the pores between pellets, and seal the crusts that form over liquid sediment. They do not rise. Nothing here rises for long. They grow sideways, downward, inward, into every available seam. Where they are disturbed, they glow with a cold, pale light, not as a warning but as a consequence. Where they are buried, they become thin dark layers in the sediment, a memory compressed into mineral film. Where they are torn, the tear is colonized. Where they are crushed, the crushed matter is reabsorbed. They are patient in a way that does not require patience, because they have no future as a hope. They have only continuation.

They feed on warmth, mineral gradients, dissolved ancient matter, faint light smeared through vapor, and the chemical residue of everything else that lives and dies. They are not one organism but countless colonies, sheets, films, mucous veils, gelatinous skins. They do not see. They do not hear as ears hear. They respond to pressure, heat, salinity, vibration, and the movement of water. Their “thought,” if one insists on the word, is a slow shift in chemistry. Their memory is layering. Their intelligence is adhesion: the ability to remain, to seal, to repair, to cover. They are the closest thing the plain has to flesh, and also the closest thing it has to ground.

The few things that seem plants are almost mineral in habit.

The black, leafless spikes appear in isolated clusters, rooted in places that are only momentarily stable. They are not graceful. They look less like stems than like stakes driven into wet powder. Some are hollow. Some weep moisture from their tips. Some are coated with cysts or fine sediment until they resemble old claws. They do not leaf out, do not flower, do not spread into green abundance. They endure. They may draw heat or mineral nourishment through their surfaces. They may anchor films. They may be less individual plants than organs of the plain’s own metabolism, protrusions through which pressure, water, or chemistry finds temporary expression. When they fall, they are not mourned or preserved; they are folded into mud and become part of the next surface.

The floating skin-mats are softer and stranger. They lie over shallow water like membranes peeled from some enormous body. Some are thin enough to show the tremor of the water beneath them. Some are thickened into leather. Some tear in bursts of vapor, then reknit. They are not peaceful. They are sites of constant microscopic conflict: colonization, overgrowth, decay, reabsorption. On them, tiny lives move without being noticed. On them, rain leaves dents that heal too quickly. On them, reflections sometimes arrive late.

The liverwort-colored crusts on the weeping rocks are older-looking, tougher, more stubborn. They cling to rounded stone where condensation is constant. They are not truly green; the color is muted, bruised, almost mineral. They live by absorbing what the air gives, by filtering moisture, by holding on. They are small refuges. Cysts lodge in their crevices. Microscopic grazers scrape their edges. They are among the few surfaces that feel durable, though even they are slowly being smoothed into the general wetness.

Within the shell-pavement lives a world of packed multitudes.

Those pavements are made of tiny shells, pellets, rounded grains, bone-white fragments, chitin, calcium, and compressed organic powder. They are not inert. They are inhabited through and through. The smallest dwellers there are test-bearers, coil-makers, pellet-pressed lives no larger than grains of sand or smaller. They live within shells inherited, secreted, repaired, and fitted against neighboring shells. Some extend fine filaments through pores to draw water. Some retreat into their cases when vibration changes. Some cement themselves into place. Some move only by fractions of a millimeter over the course of a day. They are not individuals in any comforting sense. They are units in a crowded architecture of bodies and cases, and their density is what makes the ground firm enough to bear weight.

They are also part of the sound. When pressure travels through the pavement, their packed shells produce that dry mineral chatter beneath the water. It is not speech. It is not music. It is the plain speaking through the bodies of its residents. They live inside noise, and noise lives inside them. A sudden burst of gas can shiver through thousands of shells at once. A distant liquefaction can set the pavement murmuring underfoot. The shell-dwellers do not flee, because there is nowhere to flee. They tighten. They seal. They adjust their position by tiny secretions. If they die, their shells remain, become pavement, become support, become history. Their dead are not removed; they are incorporated into the ground that continues.

There are scavengers among them, if that word can be used for lives so small and so diffuse. They feed on organic powder trapped in crevices, on settled cells, on the residue of films that have torn and sunk. They do not hunt. They sift. They graze on residue. They are part of the plain’s relentless conversion of death into sediment.

Across this surface move the few visible creatures.

The pale flat worms are among the strangest. They glide through the upper films like slow lightning. They are thin enough to seem like motion itself, almost without substance. Their bodies are translucent, faintly glossy, sometimes threaded with darker lines that may be gut, nerve, or mineral deposit. They have no eyes. They sense the surface by ripple and chemical slope. They feed on microbial skin, grazing without destroying it entirely, moving in long, delicate passes. When disturbed, they may flatten into invisibility or split into fragments that each continue living. They do not appear to suffer division. They appear to expect it.

Sometimes they gather in temporary braids, not from social instinct but because a pressure wave or heat pulse drives them into the same fold of water. Then the surface briefly shows a knot of pale ribbons moving together, and for a moment it looks as if the plain has produced a single thought. Then the knot loosens. The worms separate. The film closes over them.

The ray-like bodies are larger, though still soft and mostly water. They glide over saturated crusts with their undersides close to the surface, feeding on films and suspended cells. They are translucent at the margins, denser near their centers. They have no visible faces. They have no bones. Their structure comes from mineral fibers, pressed tissue, and hydraulic shape. They breathe through their undersides, drawing water over absorbent surfaces. They are not fast. Their movement is a long, patient spreading, then a slow contraction. When they pass over a disturbed mat, the glow beneath them bends and trails, so they seem to carry a dim halo of disturbed light. They do not sing, but their passage can set the shell-pavement vibrating at a frequency felt more than heard.

They are rare. To see one is an event. To see several at once is almost a distortion of the plain’s usual economy, as if some local abundance has briefly permitted more visible life than normal. They do not dominate. They do not herd. They appear, glide, feed, withdraw, and become difficult to distinguish from the surface again.

Below the crusts and films live the burrowers.

They are eyeless, faceless, soft-bodied, and slow. Some are wormlike. Some are sacklike. Some are segmented but without obvious heads. Some are no more than moving tubes of tissue that open at both ends. They live in the deeper mud where water, gas, and heat circulate. They swallow sediment, extract organic powder and microbial residue, and excrete cleaned clay, pellets, or fine mineral mud. Their movement makes the surface breathe. Where they shift below, the ground rises slightly, sinks, tightens, or releases a bubble. They are the reason some patches seem to inhale. They are the reason a pool can tremble without wind.

They do not build in the visible sense. Their work is circulation. They keep chemistry moving. They open channels that close behind them. They allow gas to escape before pressure becomes violent, though they also sometimes cause sudden pops when a pocket ruptures. They are sensitive to vibration. A deep grinding elsewhere can pause them. A local storm of vapor can drive them downward. They are not delicate, but they are responsive. Their bodies are mostly water and muscle-fiber, with no hard parts except occasional grains incorporated into their skins. When they die, they collapse into the mud they helped move. They leave no bones. They leave only altered ground.

There are things in the deeper mud that are larger than these burrowers but still not large enough to be called the vast ones. They are rare, elongated, slow, and known mostly by the changes they cause: a long swelling, a line of pores opening, a patch of water warming, a subsonic groan traveling through the packed grains. They may be animals, or they may be something older than the distinction. They are not aggressive. They are not mysterious in the sense of hiding intentions. They are simply too slow and too embedded to be observed whole.

Where the ground exhales through narrow throats of mud and shell, other lives gather.

These heat-throat dwellers are not plants, though they may resemble crusts, tubes, or collars. They are built by colonies of tiny bodies that secrete mud, mineral, chitin, and salt into layered rims. They tolerate heat that would distress other surface lives. They line the vents, stabilize the throats, and feed on chemical gradients carried upward by warm water. Some are brittle and pale. Some are dark and slick. Some look like scabs around a wound that never closes. They are not passive. Their growth changes the shape of the vent, which changes the flow of vapor, which changes the local weather. A slight narrowing of a throat can make a hiss become a whistle. A slight thickening of a rim can turn a steady exhalation into intermittent bursts. They do not control the plain. They participate in its turbulence.

Around them, microbial films become thicker, more heat-resistant, sometimes faintly sweet or metallic in odor if odor could be distinguished from pressure. Cysts settle in their warmth. Small grazers pass near their edges but rarely enter the hottest centers. The heat-throat colonies are nodes of intensity, places where the plain’s internal chemistry becomes visible as steam, mud, and sound. They are also fragile. If a vent shifts, if the throat collapses, if the ground liquefies, the colony is buried or torn apart. Its remains become part of the next rim, the next crust, the next attempt at form.

There are lives even in the air, though the air seems almost too thick to permit flight.

They are not birds. They are not insects. They are membranes, films, bladders, and drifting skins. Some are microbial colonies adapted to suspension. Some are gelatinous sheets buoyed by warmth and vapor. Some are almost invisible, only a trembling edge of condensation moving against the gray-brown dimness. They feed on suspended cells, minerals, spores, and moisture gradients. They do not soar. They drift, rise, stall, tear, and reform. Rain is both their medium and their catastrophe. A sudden burst can destroy thousands, dissolving them into droplets. A rising column of vapor can carry survivors upward, where they become part of the low sky’s occupied texture.

They help make the air feel full. They are why mist seems inhabited. They are why reflections sometimes fail. When light smears through vapor, their moving edges can create the impression of shapes just beyond recognition. They cast no true shadows. They leave no bodies. If one touches you, it feels like warm breath or a wet film brushing the skin, then it is gone. It may leave a faint luminescence on the surface it touched. It may deposit cysts. It may take nothing. It may give nothing. It is simply another layer of the plain’s saturation.

The plain is also full of cysts.

Cysts are not exactly alive in the active sense, but they are not dead. They are compressed possibility. They lie in the mud, in the shell crevices, in the crusts, in the floating mats, in the air-films, in the tissues of larger beings. They are tough, small, often no larger than a grain. They endure pressure, heat, burial, drying, chemical shock. They are the reason destruction here is not final. When turbulence tears a mat, cysts remain. When a sinkhole closes, cysts remain. When a heat burst sterilizes a patch, cysts remain deeper down. They wait without intention. When conditions ease, they reopen, rehydrate, resume. They are the plain’s continuity under interruption.

They are also food, material, and sediment. Some are consumed by grazers. Some are crushed and become mineral-rich powder. Some settle into shell-pavement and become part of the packed surface. Some are carried by water into structures and become the beginning of a new colony. The landscape does not hoard life in individual bodies. It distributes life as potential, then reclaims it as ground.

The dead are not absent here. They are too thoroughly present.

Every death becomes sediment almost immediately. Every secretion becomes surface. Every surface becomes habitat. Every habitat becomes material. Bone-white fragments, chitin, compressed organic powder, shell, cyst wall, microbial residue—all of it is folded into the living ground. There are no graves because there is no separation between the place of the dead and the place of the living. The ancestors of this plain are grains underfoot. Their remains support weight. Their minerals feed films. Their old structures become nuclei for new crusts. Nothing is preserved as a relic. Everything is reused as texture.

Then there are the vast ones.

They are so few and so slow that they cannot be counted as fauna in any ordinary way. They are not creatures one meets. They are events one survives, or mistakes for geology. Below the saturated crust, in the deeper liquid sediment and compressed layers, there are masses of living matter so large that movement in them resembles subsidence or swelling. They may be kilometers wide. They may be composed less of flesh than of symbiotic mineral, microbial tissue, warm fluid channels, and ancient structural secretions. They do not rise. They do not walk. They alter the plain by being.

A stretch of ground becomes warmer. A low dome rises over several hours. A field of pores opens and exhales. A shallow pool tilts as the surface beneath it shifts. A long, subsonic groan travels through the shell-pavement, setting every film and cyst into a minute tremor. That is one of them turning, breathing, or merely adjusting its pressure. It may not know the surface lives any more than a body knows the bacteria in its skin. It may not think in intervals short enough to matter to smaller beings. If it has awareness, it is basin-wide, heat-slow, mineral-patient.

These vast ones are not rulers of the plain. They do not dominate. They are too rare and too slow for domination. But they give the landscape a sense of inhabited depth. They make it clear that the flatness is not empty because nothing is visible. The flatness is full because some lives are too large to be seen as bodies. They are felt as changes in ground level, in warmth, in pressure, in the behavior of water. When they exhale, local storms form. When they contract, sinkholes open. When they settle, old crusts crack and new films spread over the break. They may be ancient beyond any surface memory. They may have been here before the plain reached its final shape. They may be part of the reason causality feels worn thin: their actions unfold over such long intervals that effects seem to precede their apparent causes to smaller senses.

The built marks belong mostly to smaller builders, though even here the word “built” must be used carefully.

There are creatures or colonies that leave faint structures: mounds of fused shell, partial rings of fitted stones, hollow cones of secreted mud, low domes woven from rootless black stems. They do not use tools. Their bodies are the means. They press, secrete, cement, bind, grow, and arrange by contact. Some are colonial, each individual no larger than a grain or a thread, working through repetition. Some are larger, soft-bodied, using their own surfaces to shape mud. Some may be plant-animal intermediates, growths that behave like organisms and organisms that behave like growths. They do not build with a future city in mind. They build because their mode of being produces shape.

A mound begins as a local thickening. A few shells are bound. A film grows over them. Heat rising from below is channeled around the thickened spot. More sediment settles. More bodies attach. The shape becomes a mound. The mound changes the flow of water. The changed flow invites more deposition. Eventually the mound may collapse, be undercut, or be buried. The builders do not resist this. They continue in fragments, or they become sediment, or they begin again nearby. Their marks are not monuments. They are hardened behavior. They are traces of a pattern that held for a while.

Some of the rings may focus vibration. Some of the cones may vent vapor. Some of the domes may shelter cysts or tender films. It is tempting to call these things architecture, but that would imply permanence and intention of a familiar kind. They are closer to nests made by tide, or thoughts made by pressure. They vanish because the plain does not preserve built things. It incorporates them.

If there are people there, they are not people in the human sense.

If “whoever” means minds, then there may be minds, but they are distributed, soft, and permeable. They do not stand upright. They do not hold objects. They do not make tools separate from their bodies. They do not have faces as faces are usually understood. They may have sensory patches, heat pits, ciliated fringes, water lenses, chemical pores, pressure-sensitive membranes. They may be colonial rather than singular. A single “person” may be a local knot of coordinated lives: microbial films, shell-dwellers, burrower channels, gliding grazers, and heat-responsive cysts all entrained into one responsive region. Or a person may be a bounded body, but still so wet, flat, and permeable that its edges are not always clear.

Imagine, if one must imagine a body: a low, broad mass, no taller than a child, able to flatten itself to a skin when turbulence passes. Its upper surface is slick, dark, faintly luminous when disturbed, covered with shell fragments and sediment so that it looks like a moving patch of ground. Its underside is absorbent, fringed with fine filaments that taste the mud. It has no eyes, but it senses pressure, heat, chemical traces, and vibration with extraordinary precision. It moves not by legs but by changing its internal water distribution, gripping the packed grains with secreted films, and flowing forward in slow pulses. It can squeeze through spaces no larger than a hand’s breadth. It can remain still long enough to be covered by sediment without dying. It can survive being torn, because its vital functions are distributed through tissue layers and cyst-reserves.

Such beings would not speak with voices. They would communicate through the shell-pavement. A tap, a drag, a pressure wave, a chemical release, a change in heat—these would be their words. Their language would be conducted through the ground, not air. It would be slow, dense, and hard to lie in, because vibration in the packed surface touches matter directly. A false signal would disturb nearby films. A strong signal would ripple water. Their “conversation” would be a tuning of local conditions: here a mound reinforced, there a channel opened, there a vent eased, here a mat repaired. Their intelligence would be ecological, hydraulic, acoustic. They would not ask, “What can this be used for?” They would ask, through behavior, “How can this be continued?”

They would not accumulate possessions, because nothing here remains free of the ground for long. They would not build permanent cities. Their settlements, if they can be called that, would be temporary arrangements of mounds, rings, films, and channels, maintained only as long as local turbulence permits. When a settlement failed, it would not be abandoned as a ruin. It would be folded back into the plain. Their history would not be written. It would be layered. Older layers would not be remembered as stories but as differences in density, mineral composition, and vibration. They might revere nothing except continuation. They might fear nothing except total drying, and even that may be impossible here.

Their relation to causality would be unlike human expectation. Because echoes can arrive before their causes, because reflections lag, because footprints fill before the foot lifts, because pressure waves move through packed surface faster than thought, their perception would not be strictly linear. They might respond to an event before its apparent origin reaches them. They might treat the future as a pressure already present in the ground. They might not distinguish sharply between anticipation and memory. To them, a visitor’s footprint might feel like a pressure that has already been absorbed, even as the foot is still descending. This would not be magic to them. It would be the texture of the world.

Their reproduction would be wet, fragmentary, and cyst-bearing. They might bud from tissue torn in turbulence. They might release cysts into vapor or water. They might merge temporarily during floods, exchanging chemical patterns, then separate into altered individuals. Their young would not be infants in the mammalian sense. They would be small colonies, compressed potentials, tender films hidden in shell crevices until they are dense enough to bear pressure. Their elders would not be wrinkled bodies but thickened layers, old crusts, deep mats, regions where the ground responds slowly and richly. Death would not be a departure. It would be incorporation. A dying being would loosen, sink, be colonized, and become part of the next surface. There would be no corpse left unclaimed. There would be no spare matter to be left.

If they make marks, they make them by growth. A ring of fitted stones may be a person’s body hardened into a resting posture. A hollow cone may be a secretion left behind after a heat-throat colony has died. A low dome may be the dried outer membrane of a being that withdrew into deeper mud. These marks are not devices. They are traces of lives that became shapes. The plain accepts them, then wears them down. The people, if they are people, would not resent this. They would understand themselves as temporary densities in a condition that continues.

They would not be gentle by human standards. They would not be cruel either. They would be immediate. Anything that enters their region would be measured by pressure, chemistry, warmth, and the possibility of incorporation. A fallen stem becomes substrate. A dead grazer becomes powder. A broken film becomes food. A stranger becomes an interruption to be accommodated. They would not hate. They would not worship. They would press closer. They would test the stranger’s edges. They would begin to cover.

If you stood there, what lives there would not come to you as a face.

It would come as the ground answering your weight. Water would rise around the edges of your feet. The packed shells would shift by millimeters, producing a dry chatter that travels up through your bones. The roar would enter your chest. Your shadow would blur. Your reflection might arrive late. A microbial film might spread over the print you left, already beginning to seal it. A pale worm might pass through the water at your ankle, grazing the disturbance. A heat-throat colony nearby might change its hiss because your presence altered the airflow by less than a breath. Somewhere far below, one of the vast ones might adjust its pressure in a way that will not reach the surface until long after you are gone.

You would not meet the inhabitants as separate creatures approaching you. You would feel yourself becoming part of their condition. The air would taste you. The water would carry your salt into pores. The mats would colonize the pressure of your steps. The shell-dwellers would transmit your tremor across distances your eyes cannot judge. If you remained still, you would be covered. If you moved, you would be followed by reorganization. If you spoke, your voice would enter the ground and return changed, perhaps before you finished making it.

That is what lives there: not a single kind, not a ruling species, not a tribe, not a beast, not a garden, not a people in the old shape of people. It lives as saturation. It lives as density. It lives as skin over water, shell against shell, cyst in mud, heat in throat, pressure in grain, sound in ground. It lives without tools, without surplus, without silence, without empty room. It lives by making everything part of itself, even interruption, even loss, even the idea of a visitor, until the visitor is only another trembling layer in the ancient, wet, deafening plain.
