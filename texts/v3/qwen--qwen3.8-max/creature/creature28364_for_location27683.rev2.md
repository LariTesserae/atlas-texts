What lives there is not life in the sense of greenness, softness, moisture, or quickness. It is life as a long industrial fact. It is life as maintained difference: heat against cold, charge against discharge, pressure against void, crystal against fracture. Nothing there is born from water or rooted in soil. Nothing grows toward the sun as a plant does. Yet the plateau is not empty. It is inhabited by beings so slow, so vast, and so mineral in their nature that they seem less like animals and more like portions of the world that have learned to continue.

They are not machines, because nothing there has been made. They are not artifacts, because no hand or design has arranged them. They are not rocks, because they maintain themselves, respond, feed, repair, reproduce, and alter their surroundings. They are fauna only in the broadest possible sense: organized persistence. Living engines without manufacture. Bodies that behave like processes. Presences that weigh on the region not by number but by consequence.

The greatest of them are the horizon beings.

They are the ones that first deceive the eye. A traveler, if such a person could exist in that place, might take them for mesas, ridges, or dark swells of ancient stone. They lie low against the ground, broad-backed and weathered, their outlines softened by immensity. Only after long watching would the deception fail. A shadow would shift when no light has shifted. A contour would remain too patient against the heat-shimmer. A slope would exhale a vibration felt in the feet before the ears admit it. Then the truth would arrive without drama: that rise is not land. It is a body.

These beings are enormous enough to make scale confusing. Their backs may form terraces. Their flanks may bear long copper-green stains and interference films that look like mineral weathering but are metabolic deposits. Their surfaces are plated in ceramic-like growths, layered over millennia, cracked and healed, cracked and healed again. Ridges of fused mineral run along their bodies like structural reinforcements, though they were not added; they arose from internal stress and thermal cycling. Metallic filaments thread through their outer layers, sometimes fine as hair, sometimes thick as fallen trunks of no tree that ever lived. These filaments do not glitter with ornament. They conduct. When thermal gradients change, they pulse faintly, not with light exactly, but with a subtle alteration in the way the air near them resists.

Inside them, industry occurs without machinery. Their bodies are full of chambers, seams, capillary channels, crystalline stacks, and dense nodular organs that function like compressors, capacitors, kilns, and filters. Some of these spaces are hollow and ring under stress. Others are packed with mineral grain so fine it behaves like dry fluid. Heat moves through them in deliberate circuits. Charge accumulates in dielectric layers. Pressure is stored in stressed ceramic and released over years. Their metabolisms are not chemical in the wet sense; they are thermodynamic, ionic, piezoelectric, and electrochemical. They do not digest food as animals do. They process difference. They place themselves across thermal seams, charged strata, fractured bedrock, and conductive veins, and they let the imbalance pass through themselves in controlled ways. Where one part of their body touches hot stone and another touches cooler stone, they convert that contrast into motion, charge, and internal refinement.

Their movement is almost beyond recognition as movement. A limb may be indistinguishable from a buttress of stone until, over days, its shadow changes by a few degrees. A body may take a season to turn its mass toward a stronger heat gradient. A step, if step is the word, may be a slow redistribution of pressure across the ground, a settling forward, a re-anchoring of conductive spines, a deliberate closure of one circuit and opening of another. The ground remembers these movements. It is pressed, scored, and polished beneath them. Sometimes the marks seem older than the body that made them, as though the creature’s motion has spread backward through time as well as forward through stone.

They feed on the plateau’s inequalities. They draw heat upward from deep fissures and release it through radiating plates. They accept static charge from the dry air and route it through crystalline organs until it becomes useful chemical separation. They ingest mineral dust, powdered stone, and metallic grains not by mouths but by broad grinding surfaces, vibrating plates, and electrostatic gathering fields. Some have exposed feeding organs above the surface: rotating mineral disks, slow polishing basins, arrays of ridged crystal that rub grains into charged powder. These structures resemble machinery only because function has shaped them into mechanical forms. They are organs. They wear down and are replaced. They secrete their own abrasives. They produce waste that is not refuse but refined material.

Their excretions are among the purest substances on the plateau. Bright pellets of metal lie near their resting places. Thin sheets of glass, stressed into precise curvatures, accumulate where their heat has cooled. Alkaline powder drifts from their pores in dry plumes. Crystalline structures expelled from their bodies continue to vibrate for hours, days, or years, emitting faint tones as their internal stresses relax. These wastes are not pollution. They are part of the ecology. Smaller beings gather around them. Mineral films spread over them. Later, some of this refined matter becomes incorporated into new bodies, either through accretion or through slow uptake by younger fauna.

The sounds produced by the great beings are as much a part of their existence as their shapes. They are loud without urgency. A low piston-throb can emerge from one for hours, so deep that it is felt in the chest before it becomes audible. Grinding rotations pass through the ground like the turning of enormous millstones, though nothing is being milled except mineral into usable states. Pressure releases occur with long metallic sighs. Transformer-like hums rise when charge builds in their conductive layers. Sometimes a being will emit a single tone that seems too small for its mass, a clear crystalline note that travels kilometers and returns altered by the terrain. Sometimes the terrain answers, not because it is alive in the same way, but because the plateau itself is full of resonant stone and stressed crystal.

They do not call to one another in the manner of animals. There is no cry, no alarm, no court song. But they do communicate through slow modulation of vibration, heat, and electrical field. One being may change the rhythm of its internal throb for a decade. Another, far across a salt flat, may alter the capacitance of its spines in response. Between them, the ground becomes a medium. The distance becomes a wire. Their exchanges are not conversations so much as mutual adjustments of state. If there is meaning in them, it is the meaning of balance maintained, gradient acknowledged, circuit completed or refused.

When electrostatic gradients grow strong enough, the great beings become terminals. This is one of the rare moments when the plateau’s emptiness reveals itself as circuitry. They do not gather closely. They orient. Across tens of kilometers, they align conductive ridges, spines, and limbs toward zones of charge. The air stiffens. Fine dust lifts from the salt in thin, unsupported lines. The ordinary light remains unchanged, which makes the event more disturbing, not less. Then an arc occurs. It is not lightning born of storm. It is discharge from the living industrial balance of the region. It may be white and instantaneous, or it may hang as a trembling line of potential made visible by ionized dust and excited mineral vapor. Thunder may follow. Or it may precede. Or it may arrive as a metallic chord from beneath the ground, as if the plateau itself has struck a bell.

At such moments, the fauna are most fully present. Their bodies complete pathways that the landscape could not complete alone. They accept enormous potentials and step them down through their internal structures. Some release heat in visible shimmer. Some expel clouds of dry vapor. Some shed metallic flakes from overloaded surface films. Others remain still, absorbing the event into deep storage, their bodies becoming heavier with held charge for years afterward. These discharges are not accidents. They are part of the region’s metabolism. They redistribute energy, alter mineral bonds, reset gradients, and perhaps initiate reproduction.

Reproduction among the great beings is slow and difficult to witness. It does not resemble mating, nor does it resemble manufacture. There is no nest, no egg in any ordinary sense, no parent guarding young. Instead, continuity happens through mineral inheritance and electrical templating. During major discharges, patterns of charge may be impressed into crystalline fissures, hardened grains, or newly formed glass. These patterns are not alive at once. They persist. They attract mineral dust. They draw heat. They accumulate conductive filaments. Over centuries, a stressed crystal lattice may become a self-maintaining node. A refined waste pellet may begin to regulate its own temperature. A cooled plate shed from a larger body may develop internal conduction paths. Eventually, if conditions permit, it begins to feed on gradients. It does not grow by swelling with flesh. It grows by accretion, by deposition, by internal reorganization. A young being, if young is even a word for something already ancient at its emergence, may be no larger than a boulder. It may spend millennia merely becoming capable of movement.

Some of the great beings also propagate by settling. When one has existed long enough, portions of its body become geologically stable while still connected to its deeper processes. A flank may become a terrace. A spine may become a ridge. These attached forms may later separate through slow fracture, taking with them enough internal pattern to continue as independent beings. This is not budding in any soft biological sense. It is more like a mountain calving a hill that can still hum.

Not all fauna there are immense. The plateau permits smaller forms, though even “smaller” does not mean familiar. Some are thin sheets of moving charge that travel across the salt crust. They are barely visible except by the way they lift and flatten fine dust, leaving momentary trails that are immediately erased by electrostatic attraction. They feed on static differentials, spreading themselves over charged patches and contracting when the gradient weakens. They have no thickness that matters. They can pass over cracks without breaking. When they meet one another, they do not collide; they merge, divide, or exchange patterns of potential. They are rare, and when they are seen, they seem like flaws in the light rather than beings.

Others are dense nodules, metallic and heavy, rolling almost imperceptibly across glassy plains. They collect metal from dust by contact, by charge, by slow adhesion. Their surfaces are pitted, mirror-bright, or coated in oxide films depending on their history. They do not have limbs. They move by internal shifts of mass, by thermal expansion, by minute magnetic responses to the fields generated by larger beings. A nodule may take a century to cross a plain. It may stop for decades beside a charged seam and become half-buried. If disturbed, it does not flee. It only alters its rate of accumulation. Some contain enough separated metal to become conductive enough to attract arcs. Those that survive such strikes may become nuclei for larger forms. Those that do not survive are refined into vapor and scattered as metallic frost on nearby stone.

There are acoustic beings as well, if beings is not too strong a word for stable patterns of vibration. They are not bodies in the usual sense. They are loops of sound that persist in the hot air and in crystalline rock, sustained by the resonant properties of the plateau. They move as tone moves, reflecting, refracting, strengthening where geometry favors them. They feed on mechanical stress, drawing energy from expanding mineral plates and cooling glass. When they pass through sufficiently strained stone, they can cause it to crack in precise shapes, releasing the stored stress in a sharp report that echoes for hours. These cracks are not damage in a meaningless sense. They create new surfaces, new edges, new grain, new resonant chambers. The acoustic beings are therefore part of the plateau’s slow manufacture of itself.

There are also heat-reactive films, not vegetation, not skin, but living electrical interfaces. They coat rock faces, mesa sides, and the outer plates of larger fauna in thin, shimmering layers. They respond to voltage, pressure, and thermal difference. They do not grow toward light. They grow toward gradients. When a great being shifts, these films brighten in patterns that travel across its body like diagnostic signals. When an arc is near, they stand out in sharp geometric lines, revealing conductive pathways that are otherwise invisible. They may be considered separate fauna, or they may be the outer nervous layer of larger beings; the distinction is not stable. In some places, a film covers a dead being so completely that the corpse appears to still be thinking.

The ecology of the plateau has no green foundation, no soil, no rot, no bloom. It is a web of transformations. Minerals are ingested, separated, refined, and excreted. Heat becomes motion. Motion becomes charge. Charge alters chemistry. Chemistry becomes structure. Structure weathers into mineral. Mineral becomes available again. Nothing is wasted because there is no outside into which waste could be discarded. Even the finest dust is potential. Even the loudest sound eventually becomes stress in stone. Even the death of a great being is not an ending but a transition into a different rate of operation.

When one of the great beings ceases, it does not collapse as an animal corpse collapses. It equalizes. Its internal pressures settle. Its conductive paths cool. Its heat gradients fade. Its ceramic plates crack less frequently. Its hum drops below perception. The body remains, but the active balance that made it a being has dispersed. It becomes a feature of the landscape. Its spines become ridges. Its chambers become caves. Its metallic filaments become veins. Its waste fields become mineral gardens for smaller forms. Yet even then, the land may continue to hum faintly through it. Some dead beings are more influential after stillness than they were in motion, because their cooled structures provide stable pathways for charge and heat. They become infrastructure without having been built.

The plateau is not crowded by these beings. That is essential. They are few. The distances between them are immense. A person could stand for days and see nothing but salt, glass, oxidized stone, and heat-shiver. The air would feel unused. The horizon would remain open. The silence between events would be enormous. But the silence would not be empty. It would be charged. It would be the silence inside a vast apparatus that is currently between strokes.

Because there are so few, each one dominates a great volume of reality. A single being can define the acoustic character of an entire basin. A single slow movement can redistribute dust patterns across kilometers. A single discharge can alter the behavior of the landscape for years. There is no clutter to absorb consequence. The emptiness amplifies. The region does not teem; it resonates. Presence there is not measured by density but by influence. One body can outweigh a forest. One sound can occupy a week. One arc can rewrite the electrical habits of the plateau.

Their perception, if they possess it, is not like sight. They have no need for eyes in that hard, clear light. They sense gradients. They feel thermal imbalance as appetite or orientation. They detect electrical potential as tension in their conductive layers. They perceive pressure through crystalline strain. They may know one another not as shapes but as disturbances in the field: a distant capacitance, a slow vibration, a heat shadow, a zone of refined mineral. If they notice smaller beings, it is perhaps as minor fluctuations, local leaks, or useful conductors. If they notice anything like an observer, it would be as an anomaly: a small, wet, temporary irregularity in a dry and enduring world. Such a being would not be prey. It would not be predator. It would be an instability, perhaps even a contaminant, because moisture and rapid metabolism are alien to the plateau’s chemistry.

The great beings do not flee. They do not hunt. They do not fight in any quick, visible way. Their conflicts, if they can be called conflicts, are thermodynamic negotiations. Two beings may orient toward the same gradient. One may draw charge from a seam the other needs. They do not strike. They alter their fields. They redirect heat. They change the conductivity of the ground between them. They may spend decades in a voltage argument, each adjusting its internal balance until one yields by shifting away, not in fear, but because its metabolism finds another path. Violence there is rare and slow. It might take the form of one being draining a gradient so thoroughly that another can no longer maintain coherence. The loser does not fall. It cools. It becomes less active. It may eventually become geology while still standing.

The reality of the plateau is not stable enough to describe these beings with full confidence. They are not illusory. They are materially undeniable. Their heat can burn. Their sound can press against the chest. Their weight leaves permanent marks. But their descriptions do not always remain consistent. Seen from one angle, a being may appear as a low mesa with three ridges and a dark central depression. Seen from another, it may reveal spines, pores, and conductive channels that were not visible before, though the shape has not changed. The new details do not look added. They look as though they were always there and only now permitted to exist. Shadows may lag behind the beings that cast them. Pressure marks may indicate a creature has stood in one place for centuries before it is seen standing there. A being may appear near, yet remain near after hours of walking, as if distance is a property it can decline to resolve.

These instabilities are not magical in any decorative sense. They are not portals, miracles, or dramatic violations. They are failures of categorical consistency. The world has become uncertain in its own descriptions. Stone behaves like machinery. Machinery behaves like tissue. Fauna behave like landforms. Distance behaves like memory. The beings there are not exempt from this; they may be concentrated expressions of it. They are where the plateau’s contradictions become most visible. They are alive but not wet. Industrial but not built. Few but dominant. Slow but loud. Material but not entirely fixed in description. They make the ordinary light seem cruel, because the light reveals everything clearly and yet does not make the revealed things understandable.

At noon, they become almost unbearable to look at. The salt fields blaze. The black glass plains radiate heat. Their ceramic plates shine with harsh highlights. Their shadows shorten into hard black cuts. The air around them trembles, but the tremor does not always follow the slope of the ground. Their internal sounds deepen as thermal stress increases. They do not sleep. They do not seek shade. They process the heat. Some expand their radiator surfaces. Some route charge downward into cooler strata. Some become still enough to be mistaken for stone, though the stone around them is not as tense.

At night, when the plateau cools and the stresses reverse, they become more audible. Their engines change pitch. Some draw heat from below to compensate. Some release stored charge into the ground in long, soft pulses. The sky may flicker with high-altitude glows that are neither aurora nor fire, caused by fields too vast to see directly. In the dark, the beings lose visual detail but gain mass. A creature unseen can feel nearer than one seen. The sound of a distant body turning can seem to approach from inside the listener’s own bones. The emptiness becomes larger because the dark removes edges, leaving only pressure, sound, and the knowledge of enormous forms.

The lesser fauna respond to the great beings as weather responds to mountains. Charge-skins alter their paths when a major being changes its field. Rolling nodules drift toward freshly excreted metal. Acoustic loops strengthen near bodies that emit regular vibration. Heat-reactive films bloom across stone when thermal gradients sharpen. None of this is frantic. None of it is abundant. It is sparse, slow, and consequential. The plateau does not swarm. It does not teem. It holds. It holds a small number of overwhelming beings and a scattering of dependent processes, separated by silent distances, connected by conduction, resonance, and gradient.

If one could stand there, one would not feel surrounded by life. One would feel exposed to it. The ground would be hard, hot, and faintly vibrating. The air would draw moisture from skin and eyes with ruthless speed. The light would be ordinary, severe, and merciless. The sound would arrive as a layered industrial chord: distant grinding, near electrical hum, subsonic pulse, sharp crystal ticks, long exhalations from pores that are not vents, and the vast slow breathing of beings too large to be understood as animals. Nothing would be built. Nothing would be cultivated. Nothing would be green. Yet everything would be active. Everything would be produced. Everything would be maintained.

What lives there, then, is not a collection of creatures so much as a condition of inhabited industry. It is the plateau organized into appetite, endurance, and self-continuation. It is gradient made fleshless. It is geology that has begun to maintain itself. It is fauna without softness, life without water, ecology without plants, industry without builders, sound without voices, presence without crowd.

They are the slow engines of the high dry world. They are the mineral beings that turn heat into history. They are the few and enormous facts that make the emptiness loud. They do not move through the landscape; they are what the landscape does when it decides, over centuries, to remain a being.
