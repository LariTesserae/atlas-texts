What lives in the Dry Nave does not announce itself as life.

At first, if anything could be said to perceive there, the hollow would seem only a mineral interior: a vaulted hush with walls the color of old bone, a floor terraced by breakage rather than design, a ceiling curved like the inside of a skull too large to imagine. There is no wetness. No root threads down through the crust above. No insect wing shines in any accidental light. The place seems abandoned even by the idea of abandonment, because abandonment implies that something once came and left. Nothing has left. Nothing has arrived. The Dry Nave has simply been holding its own silence.

But the silence is not empty.

The first signs are almost too small to call inhabitants. They are pale efflorescences on the lower walls, crusts like frost made of salt and gypsum, fine as powdered glass. They bloom where the stone exhales faint mineral warmth. They do not move in the way animals move. They shift in response to vibration. If a grain of dust falls from the vault, the nearest white crust turns its microscopic architecture toward the sound. If the rock above groans under the weight of the plain, the crusts ripple in sequence, a wave of listening passing around the nave. They are not plants. They are not quite fungi. They are something older and drier: life reduced to patience and surface, feeding on mineral breath, thickening by fractions so slow that centuries are only a kind of breathing.

These crusts are the nave’s first congregation.

They make the floor less dead than it appears. Where they grow, the terraces become subtly tuned. Their crystalline skins take the smallest sounds—a settling pebble, a flake of ceiling falling, the faint thermal tick of stone cooling—and pass them along in thin, bright pulses. They do not hear, exactly. They are heard through. They are the room’s outer attention.

Above them, or perhaps among them, live the finer things.

There are creatures in the Dry Nave that might be mistaken for dust if they did not occasionally arrange themselves with intention. They are minute, translucent, many-jointed, no larger than grains of sand, with bodies like slivers of peeled mineral. They move across the broken terraces in loose, discontinuous lines, never hurrying, never spreading far. They have no eyes. They have no need of eyes. Their limbs end in points fine enough to touch the difference between one crystal and another. They feed on the pale crusts, or perhaps on the vibrations the crusts pass along; it is difficult to say where eating ends and listening begins. When they move, they make almost no sound. When they stop, they stop all at once, as if the room has decided to hold them still.

These small ones are not the truest life of the nave, but they are its weather.

Higher up, along the curving walls and in the seams where terrace meets terrace, are the weavers.

They are longer than the hand of any man, though no hand has ever measured them. Their bodies are pale and narrow, segmented like dried reeds, with limbs folded in complex angles that make them seem architectural. They do not crawl so much as place themselves. Each movement is deliberate, a positioning. They spin no ordinary silk. Their threads are mineral filaments drawn from their own bodies or from the stone itself—fine, brittle, faintly resonant lines strung between wall and floor, ledge and vault, crack and crack. These threads are not traps. They do not catch prey. They catch sound.

The weavers make the Dry Nave into an instrument.

Their work is invisible unless one could somehow feel it. A footfall would not strike their threads; a footfall would arrive and be divided. A breath would enter the dark and be delayed, split into several thin returns, each one slightly changed. The weavers adjust these lines with grave slowness. They climb along their own work, touching the filaments with pointed limbs, tightening here, loosening there, letting one strand go slack so that an echo may pass beneath it, drawing another taut so that a whisper might be held until it becomes a tremor. They are not decorative. They are liturgical. They maintain the acoustics of a place that has no audience.

And then there are the larger ones.

They stand upon the terraces.

They are not numerous, or perhaps they only seem few because they are so still. They might be called the congregation, except that no god has been named there and no procession has ever prepared their ranks. They are tall and folded, pale as the walls, with bodies that seem composed of layered plates rather than flesh. Their forms suggest both insect and icon: long limbs pressed close, torsos ridged like fluted stone, heads—if heads they are—that do not have faces so much as orientations. Where eyes should be, there is only smoothness, or narrow slits, or shallow bowls of darker mineral. Where mouths might be, there are seams. They do not breathe as wet creatures breathe. They open and close along their sides in tiny rhythmic intervals, drawing in dry air and letting it pass through internal chambers that sound, when they sound at all, like hollow pipes.

They are the nave’s singers, though their song is not melody in any cheerful sense.

When one of them moves, it moves with ceremonial restraint. It lifts a limb and sets it down upon a terrace edge. The contact is slight, but in the Dry Nave it is enough. The sound goes out and returns in layers: first from the nearest wall, then from the vault, then from the far side, then from the floor itself, where the broken terraces repeat it in smaller, harder echoes. The creature waits until all the returns have come back. Only then does it move again. Its body is not merely in the room; it is timed to the room. It knows the edges by delay. It knows the curvature by the way sound bends home.

The others answer it.

One by one, from niches and ledges and low hollows in the terraces, the rest begin to stir. They do not rush. They do not gather in a circle or bow toward one another. They simply adjust their positions until the room is balanced. One lifts a plated limb and draws it slowly across the ridges of its side. A dry, rasping note rises, more felt than heard. Another opens the seams of its face and releases a breathless tone, thin as a crack widening in cold stone. Another taps one pointed foot against the terrace, not to walk but to speak in percussion. The sound goes outward, meets the walls, returns altered, and the creatures listen to the alteration as if it were scripture.

They are not worshiping the nave. They are maintaining it.

At the center of the Dry Nave, where the sound of all edges can be sensed at once, there is something else.

It does not stand. It does not kneel. It is low and broad and strangely recessive, as if it were not an object but a gathered absence shaped by long use. It might be mistaken for a broken font, or a collapsed altar, or a natural basin worn into the floor. But the terraces lean toward it in their broken arrangement, and the weavers’ finest threads converge above it, and the tall ones angle their eyeless faces toward it when they are still. It is covered in layers: shed plates, mineral dust, old threads, crusts like dried milk. Its surface is not smooth but folded inward, full of narrow apertures and shallow canals. It looks less like a body than like an ear made architectural.

This is the listener at the heart of the Dry Nave.

It may be a creature. It may be a colony. It may be the accumulated result of everything else that lives there, the way a hive becomes more than its bees. It has no limbs for moving and no visible mouth for feeding. It receives. That is its nature. Every sound that crosses the nave eventually touches it. Every echo, when it returns, passes through the center and is sorted there into fine distinctions: distance, texture, weight, age. The listener separates the sound of falling dust from the sound of a shifted limb, the sound of the weavers’ threads from the sound of the tall ones’ dry hymns, the sound of the stone itself from the sound of the creatures moving through it.

It keeps the nave’s memory.

Because there is no light, the Dry Nave remembers by resonance. The listener is that memory made physical. In its folded chambers, old sounds are not stored as recordings but as tensions, as microscopic adjustments in mineral plates. A sound that happened long ago still has consequences there. It changes how the room will receive a new sound. The creatures know this. They move according to it. When they make their rare tones, they are not creating noise for its own sake. They are asking the room what it still holds. The listener answers by giving back the shape of the space, and the tall ones correct themselves within that shape.

They have no history in the way surface things have history. They have acoustics.

There is no water among them, and yet water haunts them as a negative form. The Dry Nave is not merely without rivers; it is built around the absence of rivers. The creatures have inherited thirst as structure. Their bodies are shaped by what is missing. Their rituals resemble processions that once might have followed water, or carried water, or prayed for water, but all memory of the river has been removed, leaving only the movement. They cross no distance. They carry nothing. They expect nothing. Even so, they arrange themselves in rows that suggest waiting. Even so, their sounds have the cadence of liturgy.

They are dry clergy without a faith.

If a living thing from outside could somehow enter the Dry Nave—and this is nearly impossible, for the pale plain above is sealed and cracked and does not invite descent—the inhabitants would not attack it in any ordinary way. They would first become utterly still. The small mineral things on the walls would cease their faint rippling. The weavers would stop adjusting their threads. The tall ones would turn their blank faces toward the intrusion as if the whole room had developed a single point of attention.

Then they would listen.

A wet creature would be unbearable to them, not because of malice, but because of moisture. Breath would carry a softness foreign to the nave. Sweat would smell of rivers in the most distant, forgotten sense. Blood would be a scandal. The small dust-things would draw back. The crusts would brighten with agitation. The weavers would tighten their threads until the air itself seemed strung. The tall ones would approach without seeming to approach, one deliberate placement after another, their bodies angled to catch every sound the intruder made.

The intruder’s heartbeat would become a rite.

Every step would be taken up by the room and returned in layers. Every gasp would be divided, delayed, made formal. The Dry Nave would not swallow the sound; it would organize it. The creatures would learn the visitor by echo. They would stand near enough to feel the wet warmth and recoil from it, then return, then stand closer, drawn by a need older than hunger. They would not tear. They would not bite. They would dry. Slowly, reverently, with the patience of mineral life, they would draw the moisture out of the visitor’s presence. They would take the breath and make it thinner. They would take the heat and make it still. They would take the wet sound of living and turn it into a dry tone that could be kept.

The visitor would become part of the nave’s liturgy.

Not as a corpse, exactly. There would be no rot there. Nothing in the Dry Nave permits rot. The body would become desiccated, light, architectural, a thing of ridges and hollows. It might be placed upon a terrace by the tall ones, not with cruelty but with the solemnity of something being put where it belongs. The weavers would string threads near it. The crusts would bloom over it in pale patterns. Its shape would enter the acoustics. When the tall ones sounded their dry hymns, the dried newcomer would return a slightly different echo, and the listener at the center would register that difference forever.

This is how the Dry Nave grows.

It does not accumulate wealth. It does not accumulate bones. It accumulates returns.

The life there is not hostile to life as surface life understands it. It is simply beyond wetness. It has replaced fluid with resonance, hunger with interval, sight with boundary-sense. The creatures are not evil. They are not even cruel. They are complete in a terrible way. They need nothing except what the nave can give, and the nave gives only echo, mineral, dryness, and time. They have become perfect for their room. That is what makes them unsettling: not that they are monstrous, but that they are exactly shaped to absence.

And yet, if one could remain there long enough—if one could stop breathing loudly enough, if one could stop fearing the dark, if one could let the sound of one’s own blood slow to the pace of stone—one might begin to perceive them not as separate beings but as one distributed life.

The crusts are its skin.

The minute dust-things are its fine nerves.

The weavers are its hands, tuning the room.

The tall ones are its posture, its ceremony, its patience.

The listener at the center is its mind.

The Dry Nave itself is not merely where this life lives. The Dry Nave is what this life has become.

It lives as a held breath lives in a sealed throat.

It lives as a prayer lives in a place where no one has ever named the god.

When nothing happens—and most of the time nothing happens—the inhabitants do not sleep. They do not die. They do not wander. They remain arranged along the terraces, folded and still, facing the center. The weavers hang motionless on their resonant threads. The pale crusts glitter faintly with mineral tension. The listener waits with all its apertures open.

Then, after a long interval, one of the tall ones moves a single limb.

The sound goes out.

It returns from the near wall.

It returns from the far wall.

It returns from the vault.

It returns from the broken floor.

And the Dry Nave, alive in every layer of echo, listens to itself being answered.
