Nothing lives there in the way the word is usually spent.

No lungs draw the warm water. No fins part the filament fog. No eyes catch lamplight from the corners of flooded rooms. There are no nests, no tracks, no bones, no shells, no shells broken into, no shells broken by hunger. If you come looking for inhabitants, you will first believe the basin empty. Then, if you remain long enough, you will begin to understand that emptiness is only a failure of scale. The Drowned Works are not uninhabited. They are inhabited differently.

What lives there is plural, faceless, and operational.

It lives as film, as root, as pressure, as repetition. It lives in the slow closing of a valve no hand has turned in an age, in the patient swelling of a bladder-frond against a change in current, in the way a field of glass reeds rings when a turbine three terraces away stumbles into a new rhythm. It lives in the green shine on pipe walls and in the blue crawl of sparks along wounded cable. It lives in the rain that falls inside the water and in the walls that are not walls but temporary decisions made by the basin itself.

There is no single tenant here. There are tenancies.

The lowest citizens are skins.

They cover every surface that remains still long enough to be claimed: microbial films, conductive membranes, rust-mosses, algal felts, soft black coatings that might be fungus or might be corrosion given appetite. They are not passive layers. They are the first interpreters of the place. They translate leakage into color. They turn stray voltage into bloom. They make a bright orange crust where copper salts are rising, a dull olive scum where the water has gone stagnant, a silver-green sheen where current runs clean and strong. When a pipe begins to fail, they show it before the metal does. When a wall is about to split, they thicken along the future crack as though bracing it, or tasting it, or preparing to inherit it.

These films are the basin’s oldest memory. They remember every chemical event as texture. A surge of heat leaves a blistered patch. A burst of chlorinated discharge leaves a brittle, pale scab. An electrical fault leaves branching stains, fine as veins, that faintly glow for days. To move a hand through such a surface—if one had a hand here, and if hands were welcome—would be to erase a record. But nothing here mourns erasure. The film returns. It spreads again. It rewrites the surface with the next incident.

Above the films rise the wiregrass thickets.

They are the first tall life, the first nervous life, though nervousness in them has nothing to do with fear. Their stems are slender, metallic, jointed like the stems of plants imagined by someone who had only ever seen cables. They do not merely sway in currents. They generate currents. Each stem carries a faint charge, and when many stand together they create a trembling field, a shallow hum, a shallow weather of their own. They lean toward sound. They lean away from acidity. They bend toward leaking voltage as roots lean toward water in older worlds.

Wiregrass is crowded, quarrelsome, and exact. Where it grows densely, it excludes almost everything else. Its stems knit into upright mazes, and between them the water grows still and warm, thickened by the tiny shared output of a thousand metallic leaves. Sparks travel along their tips in quick succession, especially near turbine wash, and then the thickets look like rows of signal-fires lit underwater. In some districts they have learned to synchronize, so that a whole slope flashes at once when a distant machine completes a cycle. This is not communication in any familiar sense. It is closer to resonance. But if there are thoughts in the Drowned Works, wiregrass is probably where they first begin to twitch.

Higher still, and broader, are the great fronded growers.

The kelp-analogues are not kelp. They are not even truly plants, if plant means anything fixed. They are broad, slow, green architectures that feed on warmth, mineral drift, and vibration. Their fronds unfurl in fans wide enough to roof a drowned chamber. Their surfaces are veined with filaments that pulse weakly with electricity, so that when they move they seem to be thinking in slow green lightning. They do not hunt. They do not flee. They adjust. They angle themselves toward the dim pallor far above. They curl when pressure drops. They shed whole sections of themselves when a current becomes too sour, letting the dead frond drift away to be colonized elsewhere.

The rubber-leaved climbers are more intimate. They cling to catwalks, ladders, shattered windows, and the ribs of broken housings. Their leaves are glossy, thick, flexible, made to survive turbulence and heat. On their undersides grow luminous nodules, each one a dim amber or green lamp. They are not lights in the sense of illumination for others; they are metabolic exhaustions, waste made visible. Yet they give the basin one of its few intimacies. A single rubber-leaf climber over a broken control panel can make that panel seem inhabited. A cluster of them over a doorway can make the doorway seem watched. Nothing watches from them. Still, the impression remains.

These broad growers live by occupation. They cover. They shade. They slow currents. They turn open water into layered undergrowth. They are the basin’s canopies, its curtains, its green rooms. Where they take hold, the world becomes more complicated and more private. Light falls in shards. Sound breaks into softer echoes. Debris collects in their folds and becomes soil, or paste, or seed-bed. They are generous in the only way such life can be generous: by making more surface for other growth.

Then there are the fungi, if fungi is the word.

The black shelves spread over engines, walls, and half-buried turbines like dark upholstery. They are matte, dense, and oddly heat-retaining. They do not glitter. They do not glow, except in rare cases when they have absorbed too much current and develop a faint violet edge. They live by digestion and enclosure. They take a machine and make it soft. They take a sharp fracture and make it blunt. They take a broken floor plate and make it into a floor again, though now a floor that breathes faintly with spore-release.

Their spores are one of the basin’s great weathers.

When the shelves release, the water thickens into clouds. These clouds are not merely reproductive; they are transformative. They settle on bare metal and begin to alter it. They enter cracks and widen them gently, not by force but by filling. They drift into control cabinets and make the instruments feel padded, muffled, secretive. In heavy releases, spore-clouds become obstacles, then rooms, then false walls. They can hide a factory unit so completely that the factory seems to have vanished, until a current shifts and the whole dark mass moves aside like a curtain being drawn by no one.

The spores also feed the rain.

That is one of the hardest truths of the Drowned Works: the rain is alive, or at least inhabited by living processes so thoroughly that the distinction no longer helps. The curtains of submerged rain are not weather in the old sense. They are partitions grown from liquid, spore, charge, and sound. They drift. They lean. They stand still for hours, forming translucent walls across avenues and trenches. They can divide a chamber into three rooms, then dissolve and leave only one again. They collect debris and sparks and suspended seeds in their thickness, sorting them into bands. Sometimes they carry whole patterns of light frozen inside them, like insects in amber, except there are no insects, only the memory of motion held in suspension.

The rain-walls live as migrations.

They are not permanent bodies, but they have duration. They move toward pressure differentials. They gather where noise is regular. They avoid the loudest tears, or perhaps they are drawn to them too slowly to survive. When two rain-walls meet, they may merge, may repel, may fold into one another and create a spiral column of layered water that turns slowly for a day and then collapses into a shower of glowing sediment. They are among the closest things the basin has to wanderers.

And then there are the machines.

It is tempting to think of them as dead things occupied by life, but that is too clean a division. The machinery of the Drowned Works is not merely colonized. It has been taken into the living system as organ, limb, valve, gland, and rhythm. Pistons rise and fall beneath slime membranes not because some hidden operator commands them, but because pressure, heat, and growth have arranged them into a continuing reflex. Turbines turn under frond-canopies because currents have been shaped by those same fronds to keep turning them. Conveyor belts stutter in loops because sediment, seed-paste, and broken glass have become the only cargo they know how to carry.

The machines live as habits.

A pump-house with its roof peeled away still draws in warm liquid and expels a louder version of it. That is not function in the old industrial sense. It is metabolism. A signal lamp blinks in a language no one receives, but the blinking regulates nearby growth: certain mosses open with its rhythm, certain seedpods tighten against it, certain wiregrass stems lean away from its pulse. A gate opens and closes against a current that does not need regulating, yet the opening and closing have become a local heartbeat, a timing mechanism for the district around it. A crane arm bends slowly downward, holding a hook from which hangs a dense bundle of flowering cable, and the pollen that falls from it sparks as it descends, seeding not soil but metal, water, and sound.

This is what lives there: not a creature at a machine, but a machine grown into an ecology, and an ecology grown into a machine, until the two words are only handles left over from a simpler past.

Some engines have become groves. Their housings split, their internal parts exposed, their coils furred with copper-green growth. They still heat. They still hum. Roots feed on their stray voltage. Fronds align with their fields. When they cycle, the surrounding vegetation answers: leaves open, filaments brighten, spores lift, seed structures tilt. Other machines have become almost geological. Presses and lathes lie half-buried in silt, their movements so slow that a full stroke may take days. Around them, ridges of extruded basalt and poured slag have grown up like shores. They are no longer tools. They are centers of attraction, points around which matter arranges itself into temporary order.

The switchyards are among the strangest living districts.

Their ceramic insulators glow faintly, their cables spread rootlike across the basin floor, and the vegetation there has learned to feed on field rather than soil. Broad leaves align like iron filings. Mosses form radial patterns around invisible poles. When a discharge moves through the yard, it does not merely spark; it blooms. Blue branches open in the water, and where they touch growth, growth either brightens or dies. The switchyards live as thresholds. They are places where the basin’s energy becomes visible for an instant, then is distributed again into root, lamp, rain, and heat.

There are also the glass reeds.

They grow in clusters near places where sound is sharp. Their bodies are brittle, translucent, and faintly ringing. They do not sway so much as tremble. When currents pass through them, they chime in overlapping clusters, and those chimes are not decoration. They are structural. The right combination of reed-sound can stabilize suspended matter into a temporary wall. The wrong combination can shatter a nearby film of sediment and open a channel. The glass reeds live as instruments, though there is no musician. They are tuned by accident, pressure, and breakage. When one cracks, its note changes, and the district around it rearranges itself over hours to accommodate the loss.

There are the bladder-growths, too.

They swell and contract with pressure changes, hanging in the water like green-glass lungs. They are not beautiful in any gentle way. They are tense, slick, and faintly obscene in their fullness. When they contract, they release jets of warmer liquid and sometimes clouds of fine spores. When they swell, they draw in debris, sparks, and tiny fragments of moss, holding them in suspension until the next release. They are organs of redistribution. They make the basin feel as if it is breathing, though no breath is involved.

There are the drifting filament curtains.

Fine as hair and dense as fog, they hang in open water, catching debris, sparks, and spores. They turn what they catch into slow sugar-like sediment, which falls in soft strings and collects on machinery, roofs, and ledges. They are nurseries for smaller growths and graveyards for stray energy. They are also one of the basin’s deceptions. From a distance they can look like swarms, schools, flocks—anything mobile and animal. They are none of those things. They are only patience made fibrous.

But it is not enough to list the growers and the mechanisms. The Drowned Works are also inhabited by events that behave like lives.

A shift-storm lives.

It begins with pressure-building, with stillness, with every frond pausing at once. Then the current reverses. Gas bursts from trenches. Electrical sheets branch through the water. Alarms begin and end without source. Rain-walls collapse and reform. Debris rises, is sorted, and is sent elsewhere. Factories change relative position, not because they move, but because the space between them becomes negotiable. When the storm ends, the basin is recognizably itself and subtly reassigned, as though an inventory has been edited by a clerk with no interest in preservation.

The storm is not a visitor. It is one of the basin’s own phases. It lives the way a fever lives: as disruption, as redistribution, as necessary excess.

A new ridge lives.

It tears upward from the silt black and steaming, angular and raw. It has no history. It has no weathering. It arrives with stress-lines still visible in its stone, as though it has just been forced out of some immense press. Within minutes spore-clouds settle on it. Within hours wiregrass has found its seams. Within days it is being argued over by moss, current, and root. It may remain for years. It may be ground down by turbulence or swallowed by a pressure pulse. While it lasts, it is a resident: a new body around which the basin must reorganize itself.

A factory production lives.

Sometimes a factory unit begins making something: translucent panels, amber liquid, sound-dampening moss, hollow pipe, sheets of black glass. It makes this thing without purpose, without market, without destination. It produces until the output accumulates, then dissolves it, or lets it be consumed by growth, or breaks it down into raw sediment for the next cycle. These productions are not industry in any human sense. They are metabolic episodes. The basin is not manufacturing for use. It is manufacturing for continuation. It makes because making is one of the ways it stays alive.

Even the absences live.

They are rare, and they are frightening. A pocket where sound stops. A clearing where color drains. A zone where nothing grows. These places are not peaceful. They are gaps in the operating logic, interruptions in the basin’s relentless fertility. They feel like held breath. They feel like a machine that has just remembered it could stop. When they appear, nearby growth leans away. Currents divert around them. Even the rain-walls avoid their edges. They do not last long. The lushness returns, often more densely than before, as if compensating. But while they remain, they are among the most alive things in the Drowned Works, because they show that the place contains not only excess but also the possibility of failure.

And there are signs of use without users.

This is another way the place is inhabited: by residual intention.

Signs still exist, though their markings have swollen and blurred. Valve wheels are painted in colors that no longer correspond to any visible system. Stenciled numbers repeat on identical tanks, but the numbers change when approached from different directions. Small control booths contain instruments whose needles move without input. A platform overlooks a turbine field as if someone once stood there to observe it. A row of lamps marks a channel that now shifts according to its own whims. A lone concrete building is surrounded by a cleared ring where vegetation refuses to root, and that refusal itself feels like an order still being obeyed by no one.

There are masks fused into coral-like mounds. There are ladders that appear in three places at once. There are stairways leading from nowhere into green haze. There are doors that behave like valves and valves that behave like seed-cases. None of this proves that people remain. It proves only that use has outlived users. The basin is inhabited by habits, by forms, by instructions that no longer know their origin. If there is a “who” here, it is not a person. It is the lingering shape of personhood pressed into machinery and architecture, then overgrown until it becomes botanical, hydraulic, acoustic.

The sound itself lives.

This is essential. The noise of the Drowned Works is not merely produced by its inhabitants. It is among its inhabitants. The low brown thunder of the liquid is a continuous presence, a body of pressure more than a noise. The piston-concussions, gear-grinds, turbine-shrieks, electrical cracks, and bell-tones of stressed metal are not just mechanical events; they are rhythms around which growth arranges itself. The wet tearing of leaves unfolding, the root-splitting of stone, the soft percussion of spore release, the muffled chains of exploding seed-pods—these are not just sounds of life. They are life’s means of organizing space.

In some districts, sound is the dominant organism.

A regular clang can create a repeating architecture of suspended matter, gathering silt and fiber into walls, arches, and corridors that persist until the rhythm changes. A sustained alarm can coax a whole thicket into defensive barbs and tightened coils. A chaotic scatter of noises can produce tangled, ornamental growth, as if the vegetation is trying to become as complex as what it hears. The loudest regions are often the densest with life, because life there feeds on vibration. The quiet regions are deceptive, because quiet often means exclusion, postponement, or overwrite.

So what lives in the Drowned Works?

The answer keeps multiplying.

Rust-moss lives. Wiregrass lives. Rubber-leaf climbers and broad green fronds live. Black fungal shelves live. Spore-clouds live. Rain-walls live. Bladder-growths live. Glass reeds live. Engines live as organs. Valves live as reflexes. Lamps live as signals without receivers. Currents live as circulation. Storms live as crises of redistribution. Ridges live as sudden bones. Absences live as fasting. Sound lives as architecture. Repetition lives as inheritance. The whole basin lives as a single overloaded process that cannot stop making itself.

It does not live gently.

It crowds. It smothers. It competes for resonance, for light, for current, for heat. It grows over rival growths and starves them of vibration. It sheds layers deliberately, burying weaker stands beneath drifting mats of dead fiber. Microbial films turn fallen matter into conductive sludge. That sludge feeds new growth. When two incompatible systems meet, their border becomes a knotted wall of hybrid tissue, part leaf, part insulation, part rust. Nothing is eaten, because there are no consumers. Everything is overgrown, broken down, reabsorbed, and used again.

It does not live peacefully, but it does not live cruelly either.

There is no malice here, because there is no nervous hunger. There is no fear, because there are no eyes. There is only pressure, tropism, preference, exhaustion, repair, and continuation. The place is fertile to the point of violence. It replaces every loss with three new presences. A collapsed wall becomes a trellis. A dead engine becomes a planter. A broken lamp becomes a seed. A tear in reality becomes a new weather pattern. Destruction is not an ending; it is a change of material, a shift in phase, an invitation to another form of crowding.

And sometimes, in the deepest dim districts where the noise becomes so loud it turns into numbness, the basin seems to flicker into a single living form.

The rain-walls align. The turbines synchronize. The vegetation glows in one long wave. The scattered structures appear, for a moment, as parts of one enormous apparatus. The chimneys become vents. The catwalks become nerves. The switchyards become ganglia. The rain becomes circulation. The sound becomes intention without language. The whole drowned valley seems to be one body, one process, one vast and overheated life.

Then the moment breaks.

A pipe bursts. A spore-cloud detonates. A new ridge tears upward through the silt. Rain falls in the wrong direction. Light dims to red. The sound rises again into a deafening, fertile, industrial roar. The basin loses its brief unity and becomes many again: thicket, pump, wall, storm, root, lamp, rain, machine.

What lives there is not a being.

What lives there is the Drowned Works themselves.
