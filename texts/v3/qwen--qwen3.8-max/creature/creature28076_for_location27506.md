What lives there is not, at first, a *who* in the ordinary sense.

Nothing with a heartbeat lives in that hollow. Nothing with wings, shells, fangs, moist mouths, quick eyes, or the small restless appetites of animals. There are no flies drawn to the amber lamps, no spiders stitching webs between pipes, no beetles moving through the grit, no larvae in the black water. Even the pool, which in another place would gather life around its edges, remains animal-less: a warm, still lens without fin, feeler, shell, or track. The absence is not incidental. It feels structural, as if the hollow has been tuned to a kind of existence that excludes nerves and blood.

But the place is not empty.

What lives there is slower than animal life, stranger than hunger, and more distributed than a body. It lives in films, threads, crusts, weather, and maintenance. It lives in the town’s own feedback. If there is a resident, it is not a creature standing inside the settlement. It is the settlement as a living arrangement.

The first inhabitants are the films.

They are the dark, green-black skins that cling to seep-stained walls and to the shaded sides of brick and ceramic. Under lamplight they look like shadow given just enough substance to be wet. When the air is dry, they become dull and faintly powdery; when condensation returns, they darken and take on a glassy, saturated color, as if the stone has suddenly remembered being underwater. They are not moss, not algae, not lichen in any familiar sense, though they carry the memory of all three. They are microbial and mineral, living on iron salts, sulfur traces, heat, and the thin chemical wealth carried by sweating rock.

They do not form fields. They form patches, stains, veils. A film may spread slowly across a wall where a crack weeps reliably, then pause for a long time when the seep changes course. It may bloom faintly after a heat pulse, when moisture thickens in the air and the machines breathe out vapor. Where the films are thick, they make the walls slick and dark; where they die, they shrink into brittle membranes that flake away and are lifted by thermal currents. No mouth grazes them. No pest strips them back. Their limits are chemical, not ecological. They stop where the minerals stop, where the heat becomes too harsh, or where the water forgets them.
