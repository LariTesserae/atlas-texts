What lives there lives by agreement with stone.

The first inhabitants one notices are the people, but they do not announce themselves as people from the wetter lower world would expect to. They are sparse, deliberate, and so attuned to the high mineral country that a settlement can seem empty until a doorway shifts its angle, a wall-panel turns out to be a cloak, or a low figure rises from a sun-warmed apron with the unhurried economy of something that has been conserving heat and charge for hours. They are not tall. Height is a disadvantage on an exposed roof-continent where wind, dust, and static punish anything that rises too confidently. Their bodies tend toward compactness: broad feet, dense limbs, low centers of gravity, hands made careful by generations of fine maintenance. Their skin is dry in a way that goes beyond climate. It has a matte, almost fired quality, smooth or faintly textured, often dusted in iron-pale powder even when freshly cleaned. It does not sweat. It does not glisten. It sheds warmth slowly and takes on the temperature of the surrounding stone.

A visitor from a lower, damper world might at first mistake them for human only in proportion and habit. The hands are recognizably hands. The faces are faces. Doorways are made for shoulders. Streets are made for feet. Yet the resemblance is like that of a ceramic copy of an older mold. These inhabitants have been edited by the plateau, or have edited themselves, until they suit it. Their eyes are protected by inner membranes or narrow lids that close against dust and glare. Their lips are thin and sealed; speech among them is quiet, consonantal, full of pauses. They do not shout unless there is danger, and even then the sound is short, efficient, grounded. Their voices carry strangely in the thin air, seeming to arrive from a little distance even when the speaker stands close. They smell of warm mineral, metal dust, heated fiber, and the faint sharpness of static, not of sweat or breath or wet cloth.

They do not drink as lower peoples drink. The idea of carrying free liquid inside the body belongs to another kind of world. Their biochemistry is built around ionic gradients, bound salts, heat differentials, and electroactive films. They eat, if the word still applies, but their food is not green and was never alive in the leafy sense. They consume pressed mineral cakes, refined sulfate crusts, charged dust wafers, dense pastes made from cultivated electrochemical skins, and the shed products of fauna: shell scrapings, membrane residues, calcified plates, nutrient-rich powders separated by charge. Eating is slow. It has more in common with recharging or annealing than with feasting. A worker may sit for a long time with a thin plate of sun-fed crust resting against the tongue, letting its energy unspool through the body’s lattice-like interior. Meals are quiet, often solitary or companionable without ceremony. No one licks their fingers. No one expects moisture. No one craves the coolness of water, because water has never been part of their ordinary desire.

Their clothing follows the same logic. They wear layered wraps, close-fitting garments of woven mineral fiber, felted metallic thread, and softened shed faunal plates. Nothing flutters uselessly. Loose fabric raises dust, catches charge, and invites small accidents. Cloaks are often slightly conductive, threaded with fine dark veins that bleed static harmlessly to the ground. Boots are thick-soled but sensitive, designed to feel the temperature of paving and the faint vibration of buried machinery. Gloves are common among workers, not for warmth but for insulation, precision, and protection against abrasive surfaces. Jewelry, when worn, is functional or commemorative: a polished bead from a heat battery, a ring of shell-plate, a small grounding charm that is actually a calibrated resistor. The most beautiful personal objects are those that have survived long use without becoming dangerous.

Their towns and cities are not merely places where they sleep. They are instruments of living. A house there is a thermal regulator, a dust trap, a grounding point, a shelter for small symbiotic fauna, and a repository of memory. Entrances are recessed and angled against wind. Before entering, one brushes off dust, touches a grounding rail, and waits for any accumulated charge to equalize. Interior floors are often slightly sunken, so that fine powder settles into channels rather than living spaces. Walls are thick and warm. Windows are narrow, glazed with translucent mineral sheets or fitted with adjustable louvers. Light is admitted carefully. Heat is stored deliberately. At night, when the plateau gives back the day’s warmth, the houses breathe out through high vents. In the day, they hold their coolness close. Courtyards are enclosed and often contain not gardens—there are no gardens—but arranged fields of conductive plate, heat sinks, small static shrines, and sometimes resident animals that clean surfaces or regulate leakage.

The people move through these spaces with a practiced softness. They do not rush. Rushing is not merely discouraged; it is understood as clumsy, wasteful, and sometimes dangerous. A running child raises dust. A hurried worker may strike a brittle panel, disturb a charge line, startle a nearby herd, or crack a surface that has been stabilizing for decades. Speed is associated with lower countries where water repairs quickly and vegetation covers mistakes. Here, mistakes remain visible. A cracked insulator may leak for years. A disturbed dust field may take a season to settle. A broken migration path may force a city to alter its outer wall over generations. So the inhabitants cultivate slowness the way other peoples cultivate courage or piety. Their dignity lies in restraint.

Their work is mostly maintenance, but maintenance in this place is not a lesser activity. It is the central art. There are film-keepers who cultivate the dark electrochemical skins that grow on sunlit rock, spreading mineral inoculants, shading certain panels, exposing others, harvesting the thinnest layers and leaving enough for the living stone to continue feeding. There are charge-tenders who walk the fin arrays at dawn and dusk, listening to the hum of collectors, replacing brittle insulators, grounding dangerous accumulations before they arc. There are dust-masons who understand how wind sorts grains, how streets can be aligned to let powder pass without burying doorways, how courtyards can be shaped so that dust becomes a resource rather than a nuisance. There are heat-wardens who manage kilns, thermal batteries, and the long slow engines that turn day into night into usable force. There are shellwrights who follow the great plated herds at a respectful distance, marking shed plates, predicting molts, negotiating the movement of settlements around the animals’ ancient courses.

These roles blur. A film-keeper must understand weather. A charge-tender must know animal behavior, because a herd passing over exposed conductive seams can alter local potentials. A dust-mason must consult rail-listeners, because buried tracks expand, settle, and shift with thermal cycles. The inhabitants are engineers, but their engineering resembles stewardship. They do not impose a plan upon the land so much as discover what the land will tolerate and then refine it over long time. Their machines are not new in the bright, brittle sense. They are repaired, re-fitted, partly buried, re-housed, and integrated into walls until they seem to have grown there. An engine may stand in a courtyard for so many generations that children believe it is part of the foundation. A transformer yard may become a shrine because three trades have used it, altered it, and left small commemorative plates bolted to its sides.

The fauna are not separate from their lives. The animals are co-inhabitants, coworkers, materials, calendars, weather signs, and sometimes neighbors. The great low creatures of the open plains—the broad, segmented, terrace-backed grazers of mineral film—are not domesticated in any simple sense. They are too vast, too slow, too deeply woven into the plateau’s own processes to be owned. The people know their routes, their seasons, the particular sound their mouth-plates make against stone, the way dust gathers along their flanks after a thermal wind. Settlements may orient themselves to the shelter of a herd’s habitual path, not by driving the animals but by placing walls where the beasts already break the wind. When a creature sheds a plate, it is a civic event. Shellwrights approach with insulated tools and soft sledges. The plate is measured, blessed in a practical way, and carried back to be used in roofing, wall facing, insulation, or ceremonial paneling. Nothing is taken from the living animal by force. To injure one would be not only cruelty but stupidity: it would disturb a pattern far older than the city.

Children learn the herds before they learn to read. They learn to identify a distant herd by the shape of its dust haze, to know which routes bring them close after static storms, and which must never be blocked. They learn that the animals are not scenic. They are infrastructure. A herd compacts ground. Its passage polishes stone. Its grazing keeps certain electrochemical films from overbuilding and shorting exposed seams. Its shed plates provide material harder than wood and lighter than solid stone. In return, the people clear dangerous obstructions from migration lines, mark unstable ground with low cairns, and sometimes divert young animals from approaching active kilns or charge yards. This is not worship. It is negotiation.

In the air, the veil-gliders belong to everyone and no one. The thin membrane creatures are so nearly invisible that their presence is known by distortion, by faint rasps, by the way distance shimmers above a tableland. The inhabitants read them the way lower peoples might read clouds. A loose veil of gliders over a plain suggests rising thermals. A tight high gathering can mean charge accumulation. If they descend in long drifting arcs along a ridge, conductive minerals are likely exposed there. People do not hunt them, except in rare licensed cases when their shed lattice parts fall to the ground. Those parts are precious: light, strong, electrostatically active, useful in filters and fine separators. Children sometimes make small practice kites from damaged membrane scraps, but even play is taught to be quiet. A fluttering toy can confuse local fauna or attract an arc. So the kites are grounded with fine wire, flown only in approved yards, and taken down before the heat shifts.

Within cities, the boundary between animal, machine, and building becomes even thinner. The polished beetles that clean machinery are not pets, but they are welcomed. They move over gears, ducts, and sealed motors, consuming oily residues and dust-caked films that human hands cannot easily reach. Workers leave small trays of residue for them, not as feeding exactly, but as maintenance by invitation. The flat coin-like creatures that live in walls are more difficult to classify. They move in geometric patterns across warm interior surfaces, apparently regulating electrical leakage. A household may know its wall-coins the way another household would know its cats: not by names, but by behavior. If the patterns become irregular, someone calls a charge-tender. If the wall-coins cluster near a corner, a leak is developing. If they vanish, something worse may be happening: a fracture, a dead short, a toxic mineral seam exposed behind plaster. Their presence is considered good. To kill them without cause is a serious breach.

Duct-filterers attach themselves to exhausts and industrial vents, living on waste heat and airborne particulates. They are large, sedentary, often mistaken for parts of the building until they shift slightly during cleaning cycles. Rail-burrowers nest beneath track beds and conveyor trenches. Their tunnels aerate the ground and help prevent collapse, though they must be monitored, because too much burrowing near a foundation can have the opposite effect. The inhabitants do not see these creatures as infestation. They see them as junior partners in a dry ecology. A city that has no wall-coins is suspect. A factory with no duct-filterers is probably wasting energy. A rail line with no burrower sign may be too sterile, too brittle, too new.

The people’s social life reflects this entanglement. Households are often small and dispersed. Crowding is uncomfortable not merely psychologically but physically: too many bodies in one place alter local charge, raise dust, and strain heat systems. Yet isolation is not total. Settlements are linked by rail, cable, signal, and long routes of foot traffic. Hospitality is formal, careful, and highly valued. A traveler arriving from a distant mesa must be allowed to ground, brush, rest, and acclimate before questioning. To demand news immediately of a dust-covered stranger would be rude. To offer a warmed plate, a place beside a heat battery, and a small portion of charge-rich crust is the ordinary kindness of the high country. Guests from outside, especially from lower wetter regions, are treated with courteous bewilderment. They leak. They breathe too loudly. They carry fluids in containers and seem distressed by the dryness. The inhabitants will accommodate them, but never quite understand them.

Their language has many words for things that elsewhere would require phrases. They have names for dust by grain, by charge, by source, by behavior in wind. They have terms for heat that is stored, heat that is moving, heat that is dangerous, heat that is welcome, heat that has gone stale. They have words for static that crawls, static that waits, static that sings in metal, static that will strike, and static that can be harvested. They have few words for rain, and those are borrowed, abstract, or technical. When they speak of the lower world, they often use metaphors of corrosion, weight, and noise. To them, water is not refreshing; it is chaotic solvent. It dissolves surfaces, weakens structures, encourages uncontrolled growth, and erases careful charge gradients. They can understand that life elsewhere depends on it, but the idea remains faintly horrifying, like living inside a slow leak.

Their ethics are practical and material. The worst casual offense is to make unnecessary dust. The next is to store charge without proper ground. To obstruct a known migration route is not merely illegal but foolish; the city will pay for it eventually. To waste heat is poor form. To abandon a machine before exhausting its possibilities is wasteful. To repair something badly is worse than leaving it alone, because a bad repair creates hidden failure. Truthfulness is associated with stability: a person’s word should be like a well-laid plate, seated evenly and unlikely to shift. Deceit is described in terms of hairline cracks, hidden arcs, dust under foundations. Praise is often understated. A master craftsperson may be called “quiet-handed.” A good child is “low-dust.” A trusted leader is one who “does not surprise the walls.”

Their sense of time is long. They measure days by heat cycles, but years by larger patterns: herd circuits, mesa retreat, the slow advance of a dune field toward a rail line, the lifespan of a heat battery, the interval between major static storms. They know that their cities will not remain exactly as they are. They expect to repair, uncover, re-bury, re-face, and adjust. A building is never finished; it is merely in a stable phase. A factory’s waste mound becomes a hill, then a platform, then a district. A road built by ancestors may now run half-submerged, its upper edge used as a walkway during high dust season. No one laments this. The world is not falling into ruin. It is accumulating.

Children are taught by apprenticeship more than lecture. They learn to brush dust without scattering it. They learn to place a hand on a wall and feel whether it is warm from the sun, warm from a duct, or warm from an electrical fault. They learn to listen to machines: the tick of cooling metal, the soft click of a relay, the almost inaudible rasp of a filterer adjusting itself in a vent. They learn to walk in a way that minimizes disturbance. Play is not absent, but it is patterned. They build small towers of insulating blocks and discharge them safely. They race polished beads along grounded tracks. They mimic veil-gliders with stiff cloaks, learning to read thermals by the lift of fabric. They tell stories about the first settlements, the first herds, the first great arc that taught the founders where not to build. Their heroes are rarely warriors. They are surveyors, repairers, negotiators, and people who noticed a small irregularity before it became disaster.

Love among them is quiet. It expresses itself in maintenance: in warming a bed-plate before another sleeps, in polishing a worn rail, in saving a fine insulating bead for someone’s tool, in walking beside another without raising dust. Public embraces are rare, not because feeling is absent, but because bodies carry charge and heat, and intimacy is treated with the same care as any delicate system. A household may consist of two people, five people, one person with several symbiotic wall colonies, or a workshop collective that has lived together so long its members share routines more than blood. There is no single proper form. The test is whether the arrangement remains stable, warm, and low in friction.

Their rituals are restrained but not cold. Fire is rare, so when it appears it is solemn. Kiln-openings are ceremonies. The first arc of a new collector array is watched in silence. Grounding chains at major shrines are touched by travelers before long journeys. At street corners, small shrines of fitted stone and shell hold offerings not of food or flowers but of carefully chosen materials: a pinch of conductive powder, a shard of intact insulator, a shell-plate worn smooth by a grandmother’s hand. Prayer, if it is prayer, is often a request for continuity: that the charge remain balanced, that the dust pass over, that the herds continue, that the walls hold.

Death is handled without burial, because there is no soil in the ordinary sense, and no decay of the wet kind. The dead are dried, separated with respect, and incorporated. Not crudely, not as mere material, but as continuation. A person’s personal plates may become part of a family wall. Their charge, if stored in capacitors at the end, may be released into a public battery. Their name may be added to a maintenance litany recited when a district performs its yearly inspection. In some settlements, an elder who has spent a lifetime tending a particular engine will have a small portion of that engine named after them. In others, the threshold of a house is made from a commemorative plate bearing the touch-wear of generations. To be remembered is not to be preserved unchanged. It is to remain useful.

This does not make them grim. They laugh, though often softly. They enjoy long evenings when the stone releases heat and the air above the courtyards shimmers with invisible warmth. They tell jokes about lower-world visitors who try to wash things with nonexistent water. They tell jokes about engineers who fall in love with their own machines. They enjoy the satisfaction of a perfectly seated panel, the arrival of a herd near enough to shelter a district for a season, the blue hour when the sky deepens and the static collectors begin to sing. They take pleasure in surfaces: the gloss of a well-used rail, the dull bloom of a sulfate crust, the pearl-dark sheen of faunal plate, the almost invisible movement of wall-coins in lamplight. Their beauty is tactile and patient. It is not the beauty of softness, but of fit.

There are conflicts, of course. Sparse peoples are not automatically peaceful. Disputes arise over migration routes, over charge rights, over whether a new factory will disturb a film field, over whether a town should expand into a basin or retreat from a dune. But conflict here tends to be procedural, slow, and mediated by experts. A disagreement may take years to resolve because the consequences must be modeled across decades. The question is rarely who wants something most, but what arrangement will least disturb the long balance. If someone is reckless, they may be assigned to dust duty, to grounding watch, or to a remote survey post where speed is impossible and attention is forced. Exile is severe, because the open plateau is not empty in the way an unprepared person might imagine. It is empty only of easy support. To be cast beyond the settlements is to be removed from the web of maintenance that keeps life possible.

Outsiders sometimes arrive with ambitions of extraction. They see the salt ridges, conductive veins, shell deposits, and thermal gradients as wealth waiting to be seized. The inhabitants are not naive. They know what their country contains. But they resist the logic of rapid taking. They will trade. They will guide. They will sell refined plates, insulating powders, charge-storing shells, heat batteries, and finely worked ceramic. They will not permit strip-working of crusts, disruption of herds, or ungrounded machinery. Visitors who wish to build must learn the rules: no casual fire, no wet processes, no dust storms of one’s own making, no obstruction of animal routes. Many outsiders find the place unbearable. The dryness feels absolute. The silence presses. The absence of green becomes psychologically loud. They miss the sound of leaves, the smell of rain, the reassurance of visible water. The inhabitants watch them leave without malice. To them, the lower world is too wet to think clearly in.

Yet the plateau is not hostile. It is exacting. It rewards attention. Under the ordinary sun, at midday, one can stand on a paved apron and see the whole world spread in mineral clarity: the terraced mesa, the wind-aligned gravel fields, the far ridge where a static storm flickers without thunder, the slow-moving herd like a line of walking architecture, the veil of gliders overhead, the city behind with its low roofs, its humming ducts, its wall-coins shifting in geometric patience. The inhabitants move through this scene as if they were not separate from it. Their footsteps are quiet. Their tools are grounded. Their voices are low. Their bodies hold the warmth of the stone.

If one asks who lives there, the simplest answer is: the dry people. But the fuller answer is larger. What lives there is a negotiated multitude: people, herds, gliders, wall-fauna, beetles, filterers, burrowers, electrochemical films, heat engines, static collectors, and cities that have become almost geological. They all share the same conditions and the same discipline. They do not demand that the world be wet, green, soft, or quick. They ask for gradient, surface, warmth, charge, and time. In return, they maintain the balance with care.

And when the thermal winds rise and the air begins to tighten with charge, the whole inhabited world becomes visible in a single quiet alignment. Collectors unfold. Herds turn by imperceptible degrees. Veil-gliders gather overhead like living distortions of distance. Wall-coins trace faster patterns. Beetles retreat into machine housings. People pause, touch grounding rails, and listen. No one runs. No one shouts. The city accepts the charge. The plateau continues. What lives there lives on, warm and dry under ordinary light, in the long patience of a world that has learned to be full without water.
