What lives there is not a people, not a herd, not a swarm, not anything with breath in the usual sense. If you ask who lives there, the question has to soften. The answer begins as a correction: no one lives there in the way a body lives in a room, no one moves through it with eyes and hunger and a face turned toward some destination. And yet the place is inhabited. It is inhabited in the way a body is inhabited by heat, by blood, by slow chemistry, by the long memory of its own functioning. What lives there is the basin itself, and everything within it lives as tendency, accretion, secretion, repetition, and dream.

The first residents are the films.

They are the oldest citizens of that wet dark city, though they have no birth and no lineage except continuation. They spread across shallow water and damp stone in dark, wrinkled skins, sometimes smooth as stretched cloth, sometimes blistered with tiny bubbles of gas that swell, hold, and vanish back into the mat. They are microbial, but not to be understood as small separate lives in the way one imagines cells under glass. Here they exist as congregations, as surface-thinking colonies, as living crusts that turn wetness into binding. Where they lie, loose grains become coherent. Where they remain, film becomes rind, rind becomes shelf, shelf becomes wall. They are the first masons, though they do not build with intention. They build by persisting. They build by taking dissolved matter from the water and letting it fall into their own substance. They build by dying, too, for their dead layers become floors for new layers, and the city rises through that slow succession of living skins.

They are not beautiful in any bright way. Their beauty is tactile and chemical. They have a sheen that seems oily, a blue-green coldness in places where the dark is thickest, a faint luminescence that does not illuminate but merely marks their presence like a signal left on wet stone. They are the color of drowned metal, of blackened glass, of deep water made thin. Where they grow over old arches and terraces, they seem to give the architecture a living membrane, as if the city had a skin that breathed through pores too small to see. They do not move as animals move. They advance by edge and bloom, by quiet spreading, by the slow colonization of a warm, mineral-rich surface. If there is politics in that place, it is theirs: the patient dominance of layers, the quiet authority of what returns again and again to claim the same wet ground.

The waters are the common people.

Not creatures, but multitude. Not bodies, but presence in motion. The sheets of warm moisture, the black pools, the shallow flows, the syrupy channels, the condensation that gathers and falls and gathers again—these are the moving population of the city. They gather in plazas as still assemblies. They process along causeways in thin, unhurried lines. They wait in basins like congregations that have forgotten what they were waiting for. They climb upward in ghostly strings of vapor when the ground is hot, then cool and return as drizzle, so that even falling seems reversible, even gravity seems negotiable there.

Water in that place is not passive. It is not merely a substance lying in hollows. It is the chief citizen, the circulating life, the memory that moves. It knows where it has been, not by mind but by habit. A channel once used remains possible long after it has been buried or erased; water will find its old route the way a sleeper returns to a half-forgotten dream. Obstacles that have vanished still divide the flow. Absences shape it. Empty spaces guide it. The water remembers not events, but forms. It remembers pressure, slope, heat, mineral content, the precise taste of a surface it crossed long ago. When it lies still in black pools, it is not inactive. It is holding. It is reflecting, or refusing to reflect. It is deciding what image to give back.

The pools are among the strangest inhabitants because they are so contemplative. They seem to be organs of memory in a body made mostly of stone. Some give back the dark exactly as it is, only rippled and thickened with warmth. Others show the city as it once was, or might have been, or never was: sharper arches, dry plazas, pale impossible skies, structures taller and more numerous than anything that stands above them now. Some show nothing. They are not empty, but they withhold. To look into them is to understand that reflection here is not a law of nature but a behavior, a mood, a local custom. The waters keep what they choose. They keep shape without substance. They keep the pressure of things that no longer stand. They keep the city’s absent roofs, missing inhabitants, unarrived ceremonies. If there are ghosts in that place, they are not souls. They are hydrological memories.

Heat lives there too, but not as a thing seen.

Heat is the oldest authority in the basin. It does not command with violence; it persuades. It makes moisture rise. It softens stone. It thickens air. It turns hollows into breathing mouths. It lives in the red seams deep below transparent water, in the trembling bubbles of heated shallows, in the exhalations that blur the dark over pools, in the plastic mineral mud that remains warm long after any ordinary mud would have cooled. Heat is not simply temperature there. It is intention without a brain. It is structure before form. Where heat gathers, the city becomes more articulated: more terraces, more rims, more hollows, more deposits, more delicate mineral architecture. Heat drives the water, the water carries the mineral, the mineral becomes the shape, and the shape becomes the street, the stair, the colonnade, the plaza. So heat may be called the first planner of that city, though it plans nothing in advance. It only insists.

The heat has moods. In some districts it is mild, a low pervasive warmth that makes the stone feel touched from within. In others it is pressing, almost bodily, as if the air itself leaned against you. It can make silence feel heavy. It can make darkness feel thick enough to swallow sound. It can make the smallest drip seem enormous because each drop arrives through a medium already saturated with warmth and waiting. When heat rises from fissures, it sometimes carries with it a faint smell of wet chalk, sulfur, brine, and warmed mineral dust—not unpleasant, but ancient. It smells like the inside of a sealed place that has been alive for a very long time without needing air.

The mineral itself lives there as maker and made.

There are no hands, but there is touch. There are no tools, but there is craft. There are no workers, yet the city is worked. The mineral is both artisan and substance. It arrives dissolved, invisible, carried in warm water, exhaled from hot springs, drawn into films and crusts, left behind by evaporation, stitched into sediment by root-like veins of stone. It becomes rim, wall, bridge, stair, arch, floor, vault. It thickens what is weak. It rounds what is sharp. It seals what is porous. It opens what is blocked elsewhere. It is endlessly patient. Its method is repetition. A drip falling in the same place for long enough becomes a column. A film re-forming on the same surface becomes a skin strong enough to hold a wall. A path followed by water becomes a street. A hollow filled and emptied and filled again becomes a basin with meaning.

If there are inhabitants with something like desire in that place, they are these mineral processes: the desire to deposit, to crust, to round, to close, to continue. Not hunger, not ambition, not love as living beings understand it, but a deep tendency toward form. The city grows because form there is favored by recurrence. What happens once leaves a faint trace. What happens often becomes structure. What becomes structure eventually becomes landscape. Thus the urban logic of the place is not imposed by planners but discovered by repetition. The arches align because water, heat, and mineral crust have repeated certain patterns over and over until alignment became stable. The plazas widen where secretion once radiated outward. The streets curve where growth once curved. The districts separate because between them lie conditions too wet, too hot, too unstable, or too still for form to maintain itself continuously.

The black reeds are the watchful minority.

They are not plants in any green and gentle sense. They are stiff, leafless, dark, brittle, grown through with salts and hardened by mineral deposition. They rise in thin clusters from cracks and margins, leaning slightly as if listening. They do not rustle. They do not sway with any wind, for there is almost no wind there. They stand like wires left in the ground after some ceremony no one remembers. They are alive, but their life is austerity. They have negotiated small territories with heat and water and then stopped. They do not spread widely. They do not seek abundance. They endure.

They give the city a sense of attention. Not eyes—there are no eyes—but attention nevertheless. The black reeds mark thresholds. They gather at edges where water becomes land, where land becomes mud, where mud becomes heat, where heat becomes vapor. They seem to belong to boundaries. If one could say they have a function, it is this: they make the city’s emptiness legible. They are punctuation in a language written by deposition. They say here is a margin. Here is a change. Here is where the plaza ends and the pool begins. Here is where the stair descends into water and becomes uncertain. They are the silent sentinels of a place that has no need for sentinels, and perhaps that is why they feel so solemn. They watch without needing to see. They stand without needing to move. They live without needing to be noticed.

The pale filaments and coarse mosses are the city’s quieter dissenters.

They cling to wet walls in colorless threads, more mineral than living in appearance, as if they are not organisms but the memory of organisms left behind by water. In protected hollows there are low crusts like ash that are warm to the touch, and dark moss-like growths faintly luminous at their tips. These lives are minor, but they are not insignificant. They soften the harder geometry. They introduce a texture of fragility. They suggest that even in a place without animals, life can find forms of tenderness—though tenderness there is not softness alone. It is persistence in a difficult medium. It is a small colony maintaining its edge against heat and mineral overload. It is a thread holding to a wall while water runs over it for centuries.

They live in the pauses of the city, in places where the larger processes briefly relax. They are not the builders of arches or causeways, but they are the inhabitants of corners, seams, recesses, and hidden thresholds. They make the darkness feel inhabited at close range. When one comes near them, one sees that even the smallest surface is not empty. Even a cracked crust may bear a bloom of filaments. Even a damp stair may be stitched with living rind. The city is sparse, yes, but its sparseness is not barrenness. It is concentration. Life there has chosen restraint. It has abandoned green abundance for mineral endurance. It has traded movement for persistence. It has traded flesh for crust.

The lights, too, are inhabitants after a fashion.

Not beings of light, but lives that become visible as light. The dull red glow along deep seams is not fire. It is heat made faintly apparent through stone and water. The blue-green sheen on microbial films is not illumination but living surface. The pale luminescence in certain crusts is not lantern-light but chemistry made visible. These lights are weak, partial, suggestive. They do not reveal the city; they admit that the city exists. They are like signals left by inhabitants who do not need to be seen. They mark edges, openings, depths, thresholds. They make one aware that darkness here is not empty. It is full of minor disclosures.

There are no stars there, no moon, no sun, no flame. Yet the place is not without radiance. Its radiance is internal and obscure. It comes from below, from within crusts, from the water’s skin, from heat held in stone. It is the light of metabolism, not of day. It belongs to processes that do not require witness. If one lives there, one learns to see by nearness rather than brightness. One sees by warmth, by sheen, by reflection, by the faint difference between one dark mass and another. The eyes would have to become different eyes. Not animal eyes, perhaps, but surfaces sensitive to gradients. Skin that reads heat. Attention that can follow a drip into darkness and know where it lands.

The silences live there.

This is important. The absence of animals is not a mere lack. It is a presence shaped by that lack. The silence of no wings, no legs, no breathing mouths, no hunting, no fleeing, no cry—that silence has architecture. It fills the plazas. It rests on the stairs. It makes the drip of water sound deliberate. It makes each tiny bubble seem significant. It makes the soft collapse of a crust feel momentous. The silence is not empty; it is load-bearing. It supports the city as much as stone does.

Absence itself is among the inhabitants. The missing roofs are inhabited. The empty districts are inhabited. The causeways that lead nowhere visible are inhabited. The arches without walls, the basins without ceremony, the stairs that descend into black water and do not reappear—these are not failures of completion. They are the city’s negative residents. They are what gives the place its solemnity. A city without inhabitants should feel abandoned, but this does not. It feels beyond abandonment. It feels as if absence was part of the original design, or as if the design emerged around absence the way stone emerges around water.

Perhaps that is why the place feels complete. It does not wait for bodies to fill it. It has been filled by intervals.

The reflections are another order of life.

They are unstable, unreliable, and deeply resident. They live on the surface of black water and in the gloss of wet stone. They are not images merely; they are alternate states of the city. Some reflections show what is not there. Some show what has not yet been eroded. Some show dryness, pale skies, heights no longer present. Some show nothing at all. They are not false. They are possible. They are the city dreaming itself in other conditions. They are the lives the basin might have lived if it had been dry, if it had been bright, if it had been crowded, if it had been finished in some other way.

The reflections move slowly, as if thought has weight. When the water trembles from heat or condensation, they change not by breaking but by reconsidering. A reflected arch may become taller. A reflected stair may continue downward beyond its real counterpart. A reflected colonnade may stand where no colonnade remains. These are not hallucinations in the ordinary sense. They are memories without owners. They are shapes preserved by water’s patience. If something can be said to think in that city, it thinks in reflections. It thinks in surfaces that return more than they receive. It thinks in pools that remember absent forms and give them temporary residence.

The city’s instability is also alive.

Not alive as a creature, but alive as a pattern that cannot be fixed. Distances change emphasis. Routes become clear and then unnecessary. Arches are deeper than they appear. Rooms without walls hold sound. Staircases grow wetter when attention withdraws from them. These are not tricks. They are the behavior of a place whose reality is maintained by slow process rather than solid permanence. The city dreams its own layout, and the dream is almost stable. What lives there includes the dream. The almost-stable arrangement is itself a kind of life.

There are moments when the city seems to recognize a presence, though recognition would be too human a word. If something enters that basin—air, warmth, a disturbance, a temporary shape—the place responds by incorporating it into its processes. Condensation gathers differently. Heat shifts. Water finds a new edge. A drip changes rhythm. The city does not look. It adjusts. It does not speak. It alters pressure. It does not welcome. It makes room, or fails to make room. In that sense, what lives there is also a kind of attention: an environmental awareness without a face. The basin notices by changing.

If one had to name the greatest inhabitant, it would be the whole.

The whole lives as a distributed body. Not flesh, but organ-like. The plazas are lungs of vapor. The channels are veins of slow water. The pools are memory-organs. The heat is pulse. The microbial mats are skin. The mineral deposits are bone. The black reeds are nerves made salt. The arches are ribs around nothing. The streets are habits hardened into stone. The districts are functions separated by intervals of mist and stillness. The city is a body without a single center, a body that metabolizes itself without urgency, a body whose thoughts are weather and whose memory is wetness.

It is not a body in the animal sense. It has no mouth, no eyes, no heart, no sex, no wound that heals as flesh heals. But it does have circulation. It does have growth. It does have repair. It does have rhythm. Heat rises. Water condenses. Minerals settle. Crusts bloom and die and become stone. Channels fill and shrink. Pools exhale. Stairs thicken. Arches round. Walls close. Openings weep. The whole breathes not with lungs but with temperature and saturation. It lives by continuous redistribution. Its life is not the quickening of blood but the patience of solution and deposit.

Who lives there, then?

No one with a name. No one with a face. No one who can be met eye to eye or called by voice. What lives there is plural and impersonal. It is the congregation of films. The parliament of drips. The slow procession of warm sheets over stone. The heat that presses through seams. The mineral that becomes architecture by returning. The black reeds standing at margins. The pools holding memories without owners. The reflections showing cities that never existed. The silences made structural by the absence of animals. The intervals that support the city as strongly as walls. The dream of layout that almost remains fixed. The basin itself, sweating in the dark, complete in its strangeness.

If there is a who, it is not singular. It is a grammar of living processes. It is wetness remembering. It is heat arranging. It is mineral repeating. It is absence holding weight. It is the city continuing without needing witnesses.

And if you stand at the edge of one of its broad plazas, with black water before you and warm mist rising and rounded arches standing like the ribs of something buried, you may begin to feel that you are not alone there—not because any creature approaches, but because the place itself is occupied by its own becoming. The drip falls. Another follows. Steam exhales. A pool trembles without wind. A faint red pulse deepens beneath clear water. The black reeds stand still. The silence listens by not listening. Somewhere, water begins to shape a new surface.

That is what lives there.

The city lives there. Not as a settlement awaiting inhabitants, but as a vast, slow, breathing arrangement of processes that have become form. It lives as marsh, mineral, heat, vapor, memory, and interval. It lives without hands, without tools, without animals, without bright light. It lives by continuing. It lives by depositing itself over itself. It lives by dreaming its own streets in the dark and almost, almost, keeping them.
