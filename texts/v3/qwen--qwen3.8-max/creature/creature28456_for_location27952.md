What lives there is not one people, not one species, not even one kind of life in the way a surface mind would expect. It is a commonwealth of dry forms, a settled population so deeply fitted to its place that the first mistake would be to separate the inhabitants from the city, the city from the fungal plains, the plains from the stone overhead, and the sound from all of them.

They are not human. Nothing about them is human except, perhaps, the impression they give of having become native to a world completely. They are pale, for the most part: bone-white, ash, wax, pale olive, dull amber, the blue-black of old carbonate where it takes a living film. Many are small. Many are no larger than a finger, a hand, the span of a forearm. Some of the older tower-dwellers grow longer, but none are huge in the way of solitary beasts. Their size is modest because their strength is collective, and their time is long.

Their bodies are dry to the outside. They do not glisten. They do not sweat. They are sealed in wax, in chalky bloom, in fine mineral dust that settles on them as if they had been standing in a storm of powdered stone. Under that dust they are alive, but their life is hidden. The water that sustains them is bound inside tissue, inside eggs, inside slow chemical processes. It does not bead, does not run, does not gather in drops. To touch one would be like touching a warm pebble wrapped in silk, or a dry seed with a heart still capable of swelling. They do not thirst as surface creatures thirst. They do not seek pools, because there are none. They carry their small inner dampness as a sealed inheritance.

Most are blind, or nearly so. Eyes have become faint patches that can tell only whether a glow is stronger or weaker. Some have no eyes at all. Their heads are crowned instead with finer instruments: feathery antennae, stiff sensory hairs, membranes set into joints and thoraxes, organs that read vibration the way surface animals read light. They know one another by touch, by scent, by the tiny percussion of limbs on stone and wall. They know the city as a field of tremors: avenues polished smooth by traffic hum with a different signature than untouched dust; hollow towers return sound in long, slow notes; a fungal grove gives off a faint crackle when spore cases open; the air passages in the stone breathe at intervals so slow that only a whole generation can learn their rhythm.

They are luminous, but weakly. Some carry cold light in their bodies: a pale blue-green spark along the seams of the shell, a ghostly wash across the abdomen, a flicker in the larval walls where they work. This light is not used as a lamp. It is more like a signal half-buried in the body, visible only when the creature is still or disturbed. When great numbers rest together on fungal caps, their combined glow can make a district seem lit from within, but it never becomes bright. It remains a dimness with presence, a living shadow.

If one must divide them into kinds, human words would be rough. They are not castes in the simple sense, because several forms live together, and because the boundary between creature and city is soft. But there are recurrent lives.

The builders are the first and last citizens. In their earliest form they are larvae: soft, pale, eyeless, slow, their bodies segmented and patient. They do not carry blocks. They do not quarry stone. They build grain by grain. They take dust into their mouths, mix it with silk and body salts and waxes, and lay it down in thin beads, ridges, and sheets. Their secretions harden into a substance like pale ceramic, like chalk, like old bone. They work in darkness, by touch and vibration, following inherited shapes that are not plans but impulses refined over uncounted generations. A tower wall is not made in a season. It is made by millions of such gestures, each one small, each one added to the last. The larvae are the true architects. They do not know the tower as a whole. They know only the next grain, the next seam, the next curve.

When they become adults, many take harder forms: flattened beetles, pale and polished, their shells dusted with mineral bloom. They move along walls and avenues, testing surfaces with forelegs and mouthparts. They do not supervise in any human sense, but they continue the work. They smooth worn places. They close cracks. They add reinforcement where vibration has grown too harsh. Their bodies become part of the maintenance of the city. Some die in the walls, and when they do, their dried shells are not removed. They are covered, bound, and incorporated. Death becomes material.

The gardeners are another great kind. They resemble wingless moths more than beetles: thick-bodied, ash-colored, soft in outline, their backs and limbs fringed with fine hairs that catch spores. They tend the fungal columns and low thickets. They climb the broad caps and press their mouthparts into the waxen surfaces, not to wound but to stimulate. They prune dead tissue by eating it. They carry spores from grove to grove without knowing they are carrying them. Their bodies are living sowers. When they rest in multitudes on fungal caps, they look like a second skin of dust and pale fur. They are slow, and their movements have a ceremonial quality only because they are so repeated. They do not farm as surface peoples farm. They do not plant rows or dig soil. They participate in growth by being part of it.

Their light is often visible. When a gardener moves across a fungal cap, the disturbed surface may answer with a weak silver-green glow. The creature itself may pulse faintly along its back, especially during spore seasons. From a distance, a grove full of gardeners can seem like a field of cold stars lying low under stone.

Then there are the resonants, the sound-makers. They are the reason the country is deafening. Some are crickets with long antennae and hardened legs made for stridulation. Some are beetles whose thoraxes have become resonating chambers. Some are thin, dry fliers whose wings produce not music but a continuous sifting noise, like sand falling through stone. They gather in hollow towers, along vents, on fluted ridges, in courts where air gathers. They do not sing for pleasure alone, if they sing for pleasure at all. Their sound is the nervous system of the city.

A change in air current begins as a shift in pitch. A disturbance on an avenue becomes a ripple of clicks. A new construction site is marked by repeated dry trills that guide larvae and carriers. A predator moving through a district creates gaps in the sound, and those gaps tell more than any alarm. The resonants coordinate the city without commanding it. Their music is not ornament. It is orientation. In some districts, the towers have been shaped by generations of builders to amplify certain notes, so that the resident insects produce not random noise but slow chords. These chords change over days, weeks, seasons. They are the audible state of the place.
