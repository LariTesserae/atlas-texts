What lives there is not one creature, but a sealed multitude.

The first inhabitants are the plants, if “inhabitant” can mean something rooted and patient rather than something that walks. They are the oldest citizens of the hollow: broad-leaved things with swollen bladders of internal moisture, gray-green rosettes that press themselves into cracks like hands braced against stone, resin-dark vines that climb in slow spirals and make the walls shine with their varnished leaves. They do not behave like ordinary greenery. Nothing about them is soft or careless. They grow with the deliberation of keepers, each leaf a closed vessel, each stem a treasury. They are tended, pruned, trained, and honored, but they are not merely cultivated. They belong to the basin as ribs belong to a body.

Among them live the people of the hollow.

They have no single name that would translate cleanly into the speech of lower places. If one had to name them, one might call them the Hollow-born, or the Sealed Ones, because their whole life is shaped by the principle that nothing precious should be opened to the air. They are small-boned but dense in their movements, with hands made quick by fine work and feet made sensitive by generations of walking bare on stone, root, and packed dust. Their skin carries a faint sheen, not from sweat but from the oils and waxes they rub into it to preserve its warmth and keep their bodies from losing what little moisture they hold. Their hair is dust-colored, ash-brown, rust-black, sometimes pale as salt, and often bound close or covered with woven caps to keep out the fine mineral air. Their eyes are large and patient, accustomed to the amber gloom, and they blink slowly, as if even sight here must not be wasteful.

They move without hurry. That is not laziness. It is economy. Every gesture has been refined by an environment where excess is impossible. They bend rather than reach. They carry rather than drag. They stop to listen before they step. When they speak, their voices are low and resonant, shaped to travel through dense air and narrow lanes without becoming shrill. They have learned to make sound carry without spending breath recklessly. Their laughter is a soft percussive exhalation, their grief a quiet pressing of lips together. They do not weep openly; tears would be too intimate, too alarming, almost a breach of the body’s sealed law. When sorrow comes, they touch one another’s wrists or foreheads and remain still for a time.

Their clothing is layered but light, woven from tough plant fibers and softened with oil until it becomes supple and faintly glossy. They wear wraps, aprons, hoods, and many small pouches, because everyone in the hollow carries things: sealing wax, fiber cord, a polished awl, a stopped bead of resin, a pinch of dry seed, a scrap of bark cloth. Their garments rustle faintly as they move, a dry whispering that blends with the sound of leaves. Ornament is modest but meaningful: polished bone beads, chips of salt crystal, beetle-shell buttons, tiny carved stoppers worn at the throat. A person’s adornment often tells which terrace they keep, which work they have mastered, or which household has trusted them with a vessel.

Their bodies have become suited to the place. They do not sweat freely. Their breath is measured. They can go long hours with little intake and then restore themselves slowly from thick, oily plant pulps and sealed draughts drawn through narrow tubes. They have an instinctive awareness of containment. They know when a leaf is beginning to lose its internal seal, when a stopper has dried too much, when a roof skin is letting warmth escape, when another body is fevered or depleted. Much of their medicine is not healing in the dramatic sense but maintenance: rubbing oils into cracked skin, adjusting the temperature of a sleeping niche, pressing warm poultices of resinous pulp over small wounds, teaching children not to gasp unnecessarily, not to run until they are breathless, not to open what can remain closed.

They live by roles, but not rigid ones. There are Leaf-Tenders, who know the temperaments of the fat-leaved plants and can tell by touch which leaves are full, which are resting, which must be shaded, which may be tapped. There are Lamp-Keepers, who measure oil with grave care and light the niche lamps at thresholds, stair-turns, and crowded corners. There are Wind-Listeners, who press their ears to ceramic tubes and hollow reed pipes, judging how the air is moving and adjusting roof vanes or wall slits by minute degrees. There are Dust-Sweepers, who gather mineral powder and leaf-crumb and fold it into beds where it can become ground again. There are Rope-Menders, Roof-Weavers, Stone-Tuners, Seed-Counters, Resin-Gatherers, and Mosaic-Walkers who know every uneven path by the feel of it underfoot. No one does only one thing for all their life, but most people become known for one particular patience.

Their homes are not separate from the living system; they are knots within it. A house is part shelter, part trellis, part storeroom, part instrument. Families do not exactly own them in the way lowland peoples own land. They keep them. They inherit responsibilities more than properties. A household may be entrusted with a certain stair, a certain wind-catcher, a certain old succulent trunk whose base has grown around the corner of a wall. To live there is to maintain. A child learns early that a loose rope is not a small thing, that a cracked cup must be re-sealed, that a plant pressing too close to a doorway must be guided, not hacked away. Repair is their deepest art. Their architecture grows by adjustment, not conquest.

They eat sparingly, but not poorly. Their food is rich in oil, dense pulp, nut-like seeds, fibrous roots, and slow-fermented plant pastes kept in sealed jars. Nothing is wet in the way lower foods are wet. There are no soups, no runny sauces, no bowls of liquid exposed to air. Meals are shaped into cakes, pastes, wrapped portions, or dry loaves bound with fiber. Fruit, if it can be called fruit, is resinous and heavy, more like warm wax than flesh, and it is cut with obsidian-dark blades into careful pieces. Some plants yield a thick edible oil that hardens into pale flakes when cooled. Others provide a bland, satisfying pith that must be chewed slowly. The flavor profile of the hollow is warm, oily, faintly mineral, sometimes aromatic with resin. To an outsider it might seem austere. To the Hollow-born it tastes like safety.

Their most sacred acts involve sealing. A new vessel is not celebrated when it is first opened, but when it is first made secure. A child may receive, at an early age, a small stopped bottle carved from stone or fired clay, containing a drop of resin or oil, and the lesson that goes with it: what is kept is not hoarded but protected. Lovers may exchange sealed beads rather than rings. A guest is honored by being allowed to touch the stopper of a household vessel, not by being offered a drink. To spill anything would be more than waste; it would be a kind of violence. The people have a gesture for averting such harm: a hand closed over the mouth, then pressed to the heart, then laid flat against the nearest wall, as if returning the body’s intention to the basin.

They are not grim. Their lives are full of pleasure, but it is pleasure of a close, warm kind: the satisfaction of a well-fitted joint, the glow of a lamp in a dark passage, the smell of heated resin, the sound of a seedpod striking another in a slow wind, the feel of a polished stair underfoot, the comfort of another body nearby in a lane so narrow that shoulders almost brush. They enjoy storytelling, but their stories are often rhythmic and repetitive, built like work songs. They enjoy music, but their instruments are small and integrated with the settlement: tuned stones, stretched fiber strings, hollow tubes, bells made from dried husks, drums of thin wood and beetle-shell. They sing with closed mouths more often than open ones, humming into the warm air so that the sound seems to come from the walls themselves.

The animals are also inhabitants, not ornaments.

The beetles are among the most important. They move through the lower beds with dull, patient industry, their feet ticking faintly against stone and dried leaf. Their shells are not beautiful in a showy way; they are matte, dark, sometimes faintly coppered at the edges, worn-looking as old tools. They consume dead plant matter, dried droppings, and the crumbled residue of the hollow’s life, reducing it to fibrous pellets that the people gather for fuel and plaster. Children learn not to crush them. A beetle moving through a lane is given room. If one enters a house, it is guided out with a leaf or a scrap of bark, because its work belongs elsewhere.

The moths are more quiet. They rest beneath eaves in clusters, wings folded like folded cloths, opening and closing by slow degrees as if they too are breathing with the basin. Their color is dust, ash, pale rust, sometimes a faint mineral blue at the wing roots. They feed on resin dust and fine pollen, and their bodies carry a dry scent like warmed wax. When they die, their bodies are not swept away carelessly; they are gathered and worked into caulking or placed in shrine niches with seed heads and bone slivers. Some people say the moths remember the mountain’s old breath, though no one can say what that means exactly. It is the kind of phrase that belongs to a place where memory is long and explanation is unnecessary.

The hoppers are the musicians of the lower terraces. Long-bodied and alert, they cling to stems and produce a dry, metallic stridulation, one note answering another until the whole hollow seems threaded with fine sound. The people listen to them not only for pleasure but for information. If the hoppers’ pitch changes, it may mean a shift in warmth, a change in the dust-haze, or a draft finding a new path through the roofs. Wind-Listeners sometimes tune ceramic pipes to match the hoppers’ notes, because a settlement that sounds right is a settlement that breathes right.

The lizards belong to the warm walls. Their scales are patterned like the mineral ground, and they move with such care that they seem to be thinking through each placement of claw and belly. They are not feared and not fed like pets. They are recognized as indicators. Where they choose to rest tells the people which stones hold heat most evenly. When they retreat early into crevices, the basin may be preparing for a colder breath from the rim. When they gather near the small mineral chimneys that exhale sulfurous warmth, the Leaf-Tenders know which rooted plants will thrive there. A lizard sleeping above a doorway is considered a good sign, though no one would say it brings luck. It simply means the wall is sound, warm, and settled.

The roof creatures—small, dense-furred, short-tailed beings somewhere between rodent and climber—are the hollow’s restless hands. They run along beams, slip through masonry gaps, carry away fibers, hoard seeds, and scratch softly in the upper dark. They can be mischievous in a minor way, untying what has been loosely tied, nesting in baskets meant for storage, loosening a rope that should have been snug. But the people tolerate them, because they also move seeds through the terraces, aerate the roof gardens, and keep certain insects from settling too thickly in the eaves. In the evening, their tiny claws make a sound like rain, though rain is unknown. The Hollow-born have a word for that sound, a word with no equivalent elsewhere: the soft busy patter of life continuing above one’s head.

The birds are the loudest of the basin’s voices. They nest high in the tangle of vines, pipes, and roof crossings, and their feathers are tight and sleek in dust-brown, ash-gray, and rust. They are not graceful in the way of watered-world birds. They are compact, watchful, and economical in motion. Their calls cut through the amber air: short knocks, drawn whistles, sharp double cries. They mark time more reliably than the sun, which is often dimmed by haze. Certain calls mean waking, certain calls mean settling, certain calls mean dispute over a perch, certain calls mean that the upper air is shifting. Families often learn the calls of their local birds as intimately as other peoples learn the names of relatives. A child can tell you whether the cry overhead comes from the old vine-mother near the eastern stair or from the young pair nesting in the reed pipes above the plaza.

The people do not rule over these creatures. They arrange life so that each kind has its place. That is their understanding of order: not mastery, but placement. They clear one path so a beetle can cross another. They leave a gap in a wall for roof creatures to pass. They prune a vine so a bird can reach its nest without fighting the leaves. They dim a lamp if moths gather too thickly near it. They do not speak of this as virtue. It is simply how the hollow remains whole.

Even the settlement itself seems alive.

The walls hum when the wind is right. The stair tones change underfoot. The rope bridges creak with long, slow thoughtfulness. The ceramic wind-catchers whistle faintly. Hanging seedpods knock together in dry percussion. Leaves rasp against leaves with a woody sound unlike any wet forest. Voices carry, soften, return. The basin is a sounding bowl, and the people live inside it as if inside an instrument that must be kept in tune. They understand that sound here does what water does elsewhere: it gathers the place together. It makes the hollow feel continuous. It tells them where they are. It reminds them that they are not alone.

Their rituals are built around that continuity. At certain hours, when the wind passes through the upper pipes in a particular pattern, the Lamp-Keepers light the niche lamps one by one until the terraces glow with small orange flames. The people pause where they are. They touch wrists if they are near one another. They do not pray for rain. They do not lament the dryness. They sing, or hum, a short resonant phrase that seems to answer the wind-note. This is not worship of the basin so much as participation in it. They are saying, in their way: we are still here, the seals are holding, the warmth remains, the sound is true.

There are shrines everywhere, but they are hard to distinguish from practical objects. A niche holding polished bone, salt crystal, dried seed heads, and a stoppered bottle may also be a place where a household keeps its most important tools. A carved stone lip on a wall may be both a rope-guide and a memorial. A step worn into a particular curve may mark generations of feet and also serve as a tonal marker in a night-song. The sacred and the useful are not separated in the hollow. To repair a roof is a devotional act. To tune a pipe is a communal duty. To fold a dead leaf into the dust-bed is a small completion.

Their dead are treated with the same sealed care. Bodies are not left to moist decay, because moist decay does not belong here. They are washed in oils, wrapped in fiber cloth, and placed in dry niches where the warm air draws all remaining fluid inward and away. Over time they become light, quiet, mineral in a sense, part of the architecture of memory. Their bones may be polished and set into walls, not as relics of horror but as continuations of presence. A person who has lived within the hollow does not fully leave it. They become part of its grammar: a name in a song, a repair pattern on a wall, a particular way of tying cord, a tone underfoot on a stair.

Children grow up close to everything. They learn the basin by touch before they learn it by explanation. They know which stones are warm in the morning, which leaves must not be bent, which ropes are safe to climb, which lamps mark a turn in the dark, which birds will scold if approached too quickly. They are not shielded from death, because death here is not an intrusion but a folding-in. They are taught that nothing should be wasted, but not in a harsh voice. The teaching is quiet: pick up the fallen fiber, save the cracked bead, return the husk, seal the jar, mend the mat, listen before you strike. They become adults when they can maintain something without being reminded.

There is a feeling, in their presence, of a people who have forgotten nothing and demanded little. They are not ignorant of the world below the mountain. Sometimes a watcher climbs to the rim and looks out into the haze, where lower lands become distance and absence. They do not long for it. They understand, with a certainty passed down through repair and song, that the hollow is not a refuge from life but a form of life so finished that it no longer needs to reach outward. The outside may have rivers, storms, floods, wet green chaos, open spillage, and violent change. Here there is sealed persistence. Here, richness means enough held well.

If one asks what truly lives in the basin, the answer keeps expanding.

The plants live. The animals live. The people live. The lamps live in their small way, consuming oil and giving warmth. The wind lives as it passes through pipes and becomes voice. The dust lives as it settles and is gathered and becomes ground. The sound lives as it binds terrace to terrace. The old stone lives in its slow holding of heat. Even the dryness lives, because it is not emptiness but discipline. It shapes every body, every gesture, every roof, every song.

And perhaps the hollow itself lives, in the way that very old things live when all their parts have learned to move together. It breathes through mineral vents. It feeds through sealed leaves. It remembers through repair. It speaks through wind-notes and bird calls and the soft scrape of feet on stone. It does not think as a person thinks, but it has a kind of judgment: it knows what fits. It knows when a wall has been patched too thickly, when a vine has grown too far over a wind path, when a lamp is burning too high, when a song has drifted out of alignment with the evening pipes. The people feel this not as punishment but as pressure, a subtle wrongness in the air, and they respond by adjusting, reducing, smoothing, listening.

At the center of the basin, where three terraces meet, life gathers most visibly. Children sit on low walls with their feet tucked under them, carving small stoppers from bone or wood. An elder runs a hand along a rope, testing its tension by sound as much as feel. A Leaf-Tender presses her palm to a swollen leaf and nods, satisfied that it remains sealed. Two roof creatures argue softly in the eaves over a stolen fiber. A bird gives its clear double cry, and someone below answers with a short hum. A lamp is lit in a niche beside a stair, its orange flame steady in the thick air. A beetle crosses the packed mosaic floor with patient ticking feet. Dust rises a little in the warmth and then settles. Nothing spills. Nothing breaks open without intention.

That is who lives there.

Not merely a people, not merely a garden, not merely a town of stone and resin and rope, but a complete way of being alive. They are the children of the exhausted mountain, the inheritors of its final patience. They have made a world from warmth, restraint, and resonance. They live close, they live sealed, they live loudly in the small dark, and they do not ask for more. In the amber gloom of the hollow, surrounded by waxed leaves, warm stone, animal voices, and the low continual song of wind, they have become what the place needed them to become: life that has learned to remain where nothing flows.
