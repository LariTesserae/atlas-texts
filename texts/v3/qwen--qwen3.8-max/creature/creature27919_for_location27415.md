In the Pocket Furnace lives something that has learned the shape of confinement.

It is not a beast in the way lower animals are beasts, nor a spirit in the way wind is spirit. It is a tenant of pressure and heat, a small keeper of the closed place. At first, if one were unlucky enough to find it, one might take it for a stone that had been burned into a posture: a low, rough knot of black and rust, no bigger than a kneeling child, fitted into the shallow floor of the hollow as if the mountain had clenched around it and forgotten to release.

Then, as the heat shifts, it unfolds.

Limbs appear—jointed, thin, polished by dust. Its back is plated with mineral scales: dull red hematite, chalky pumice, flecks of mica that catch light like buried fish-scales. It has no eyes. The Pocket Furnace does not offer horizons, and it has no need for them. Instead, its face—if face is the word—has a cluster of small pits, each finer than a needle’s point, arranged in whorls. Through these it feels warmth as another creature might smell water. It knows the world as differences of heat: the breath of a crack, the memory of sun in quartz, the cold hunger of a newcomer’s shadow.

Inside its chest there is not a heart but a kiln.

When it is still, one can see a faint glow through the seams of its plates, not bright, not flame, but the deep orange of metal just before it loses patience. Its breathing is a sound like sand slipping over hot tin. When it moves, it makes a soft clink, as if it were carrying a pocketful of broken dishes. It is not beautiful in any ordinary sense. It is beautiful the way a scar is beautiful when it has become useful.

The Cinder-Tender—for that is the name the place seems to suggest rather than say—lives by gathering and hoarding heat.

All day, while the hidden basin drinks the sun, it moves from one blister of rock to another, pressing its mouth, a small vent like a cracked nozzle, against the stone. It draws out the stored warmth and transfers it to nodules it has shaped from ash and salt. These nodules it buries in neat rows beneath the hottest part of the floor. They are not eggs, exactly, though they are tended like eggs. They are reservoirs. At night, when the mountain cools and the mineral walls contract with little gunshots of stress, the Cinder-Tender digs them up again and arranges them around itself, listening to the ticks and whispers as they give back their heat. It is a miser of warmth, but also a nursemaid.

It eats what can be eaten without greenness.

It licks veins of sulfur and copper from the rock. It sucks dry steam from fissures that exhale only when the air pressure changes. It cracks sun-split stones to reach the mineral flour inside. Sometimes it gnaws on old lightning—those glassy scars where a bolt has fused dust into crooked tubes—and from these it seems to gain something more than nourishment: perhaps memory, perhaps an appetite for brightness.

It never descends to the slopes below. Nothing green has ever taken root there, and the Cinder-Tender does not want root, leaf, or sap. Green things would be too wet, too patient in the wrong way. Its patience is the patience of firing clay.

At noon, when the Pocket Furnace becomes so hot that even shadow seems to blister, the Cinder-Tender is most alive.

It stands at the center of the hollow with its limbs spread, plates lifted slightly, allowing the heat to pass into the kiln in its chest. Then it sings. The song is not sound alone. It is a pressure, a thin whine of heated air, a clicking of mineral grains, a vibration in the teeth of the stone. If there were ears there, they would hear something between a wasp and a cooling stove. The song does not ask for rain, or green, or a view. It asks for more containment. It praises the sealed, the narrow, the kept. It is a hymn to not-spreading.

In the late afternoon it makes things.

This is not craft as people below would understand it. It gathers shards, pebbles, metallic dust, and the thin glass skins left by ancient fires. With its forelimbs—two of the limbs more delicate than the others, tipped like tongs—it presses these materials into the heat-vents along the wall. It does not build objects so much as provoke them. A lump of fused sand becomes, after hours, something like a beetle with no legs. A twist of copper vein becomes a finger that points at nothing.

The Cinder-Tender examines these things, warms them, cools them, breaks some apart, keeps others. It may be trying to make company. It may be trying to make the mountain digest its own substance. It may simply be doing what fire does when trapped long enough: turning matter into testimony.

If a living thing from outside enters the Pocket Furnace—a bird driven by storm, a climber, a careless insect—the Cinder-Tender does not rush.

It becomes still. It lets the newcomer believe the place is empty. Then, with great care, it approaches the coldness. To it, cold is the only rarity. It places one hot limb near the intruder, not to burn, at first, but to measure. It leans its pitted face toward breath or water or trembling flesh and reads the temperature there.

If the intruder carries water, the Cinder-Tender is fascinated. It will sit for a long time watching the water resist heat. If the intruder leaves quickly, it will follow to the edge of the hollow and stop at the threshold, for it cannot bear openness. If the intruder does not leave, the Cinder-Tender begins, without malice, to make the intruder fit the place. Metal grows warm. Cloth stiffens. Thoughts become simpler, hotter, more insistent. The body begins to understand that to remain in the Pocket Furnace is to be reduced to what can endure.

There are those who would say the Cinder-Tender is not one creature but many, a colony of heat-ants wearing a single shape when they need to seem singular. There is some truth in this. When it moves, tiny sparks run beneath its plates, and sometimes the outline wavers as if made of several smaller bodies agreeing to be large for a little while.

At other times it seems entirely alone, the last of a kind fired into being when the mountain was young and angry. Perhaps it is both. The Pocket Furnace does not care for clean categories. It keeps what keeps heat.

Beneath its habits, the Cinder-Tender guards one secret.

At the deepest point of the basin, where the rock is folded like a burned palm, there is a pocket within the pocket. It is no larger than a skull. Inside it lies a coal that has never gone out. It is not red, not black, not truly mineral. It is a dense, breathing ember the color of closed eyelids.

The Cinder-Tender returns to it after every task. It lowers its face into the hollow and remains there, motionless, for hours. This may be its heart, its parent, its prisoner, or its god. When the wind passes over the mountain and makes no sound in the hollow, one could almost hear the coal thinking.

That is what lives in the Pocket Furnace: a warden of heat, a small and ancient furnace-priest, a creature made of the very refusal to open.

It does not look outward, because there is nowhere for it to look. It does not long for the green slopes below, because it has forgotten their grammar. It tends the hot, the hard, the hidden. It keeps the mountain’s secret close, and if you ever found the place, you would know it was inhabited not by tracks or droppings, but by the sudden, terrible sensation that the heat was paying attention to you.
