What lives there is not a simple fauna arranged upon a landscape. It is a population of moving processes, each creature a valve, a grinder, a carrier, a filter, a conductor, or a momentary awareness inside the plateau’s vast dry industry. The inhabitants do not merely endure the absence of ordinary water; they are made from that absence. Their bodies are sealed, varnished, and mineralized. Their fluids are oils, resins, waxes, electrolyte gels, and salt-rich secretions. Their senses are tuned not to softness or dampness but to vibration, pressure, heat gradients, and electric fields. To be alive there is to be part of the roar.

The first creatures one would notice on the open crust are the plinthbacks. They are low, broad, armored grazers, moving in slow herds across the living root-mat like dark knots of stone. Their backs are plated with overlapping scales of mineralized cellulose, built from the salts and fibers they ingest. Their feet are wide and padded with dense fibrous cushions, spreading their weight so they do not break the brittle surface. Their mouths are broad grinding discs, rasping the photosynthetic crust in measured sweeps, cropping it without killing it, encouraging new tissue to spread. As they feed, they produce a deep, chest-born resonance, a sound felt more than heard: a slow knocking that travels through the root-felt and announces the herd to anything with sensitive feet.

Plinthbacks are not peaceful in any soft sense. They compete for crust, press shoulder-plates against one another, and grind their armored ridges until sparks of mineral dust fly. Yet their violence is slow, economical, and part of the plateau’s maintenance. Where they pass, the ground is trimmed, aerated by their footfalls, and enriched by their dry waste. Their young are born with pale, flexible plates that harden within hours as they absorb mineral powder from the herd’s dust. In pollen squalls, the herds turn their backs to the wind, lock their plates, and become temporary mounds, while yellow clouds roar over them.

Above the herds, the air is never empty. It belongs to the glass-midges: translucent, stiff-winged insects that click through the bright air in swarms thousands of meters wide. Their bodies are almost clear, their wings veined like fragments of leaf-glass. They feed on airborne spores, pollen, powdered resin, and charged particulate. Each wingbeat makes a tiny brittle click, and when a swarm is dense enough, the combined sound becomes a high, dry hiss beneath the tower-roar. The midges are not merely food; they are aerial filtration. They clean the sky of excess particulate, concentrate static charge, and when they die, they fall as glittering grains that the crust below absorbs. Sometimes an electrostatic pulse passes through a swarm, lighting it for an instant with a blue-white lattice. Then the air carries the sharp scent of ozone and hot resin.

The pollinators are larger, stranger, and more essential. They are the arc-brushes: heavy, wax-armored creatures whose bodies bristle with metallic-looking filaments. They move among the tower crowns and vent-fields, gathering charge as they go. Their bristles stand out in charged halos, and their eyes, if one can call them eyes, are dark resin lenses sensitive to ultraviolet flicker and electric gradients. When an arc-brush approaches a receptive floral vent, it does not simply enter. It taps, withdraws, trembles, and tests the voltage. If the tower is ready, the creature touches a conductive spine and a bright snap leaps between them. That discharge opens a resinous nectar bladder, releases a packet of dry pollen, or triggers a seed-capsule to flex. The arc-brush feeds on the oily nectar, becomes dusted with pollen, and carries charge to the next structure. Some are killed by mistimed arcs; their bodies fall as blackened, conductive husks, quickly claimed by scavengers.

Inside the great towers live smaller beings so tightly woven into the plant’s operation that they seem less like animals than mobile tissues of the machine. These are the vent-dwellers. They are flat, heat-hard, quick-moving creatures, no larger than a hand, with fringed limbs and reflective shells. They live among the internal conduits, pressure chambers, and heat-exchange vanes, feeding on chemical films, excess salts, resin smut, and waste products that would otherwise clog the tower’s passages. When a conduit narrows, they gather and eat it open. When a membrane overheats, they spread across it and shed insulating flakes. When internal pressure shifts, they emerge from vents in coordinated clouds, ticking against the tower’s outer ribs. A tower without vent-dwellers runs poorly; a tower with too many becomes choked. Their populations are regulated by heat, voltage, and the hunger of aerial predators.

On the pale ground between towers, the seed-jacks dart and scatter. They are nervous, many-jointed creatures with hard, polished shells and lateral pouches lined with wax. They collect the oily seeds dropped or blasted from the towers, stuffing them into their pouches until their bodies rattle. They are not careful gardeners. They are startled dispersers. When a tower discharges, when pollen thunder builds, when a knell-hunter strikes nearby, seed-jacks bolt in ragged paths. Seeds spill from their pouches, are kicked into crust cracks, or are cached in shallow depressions and forgotten. In this way, the towers spread without building anything. The seed-jacks’ panic is part of the planting mechanism. Their shells reflect the bright sky, making them look like moving chips of light.

Higher up, riding the thermal pulses that rise from the towers, are the wind-rays. They are broad, dry-winged gliders, their membranes made of layered fiber and translucent scale. They rarely flap. They adjust the charge along their wing edges, tilt into rising heat, and spiral upward in slow, precise arcs. They feed on glass-midges, seed-jacks, vent-dweller clouds, and resin exudates that harden slowly from tower wounds. Their mouths are hooked and dry, their senses are tuned to electrostatic fields, and their bodies carry enough insulation to survive close to charged crowns. At rest, they fold themselves against tower ribs and look like shed bark panels. In storms, they rise into the particulate clouds and trail charged filaments, hunting the turbulence. Their presence adds a papery crackle to the air: wing-membranes flexing, static snapping along their edges.

Rarest among the airborne forms are the spore-sieves. They are vast, slow, drifting filterers, buoyed by sealed internal bladders filled with gases generated through anhydrous chemical reactions. Their bodies are pale and thin-walled, ribbed with dark cellulose hoops, and they trail long electrostatic filaments through pollen clouds. They do not swim in any liquid sense; they comb the dry air, drawing spores and fine particulate toward filtering fringes. They are so light that a strong gust can carry them for hundreds of kilometers. They make almost no sound except a low creaking of their ribs under pressure. When they die, they descend slowly, collapse into brittle husks, and become feeding grounds for peel-broods and seed-jacks. To see one drifting above a tower cluster is to see the plateau’s production made visible: a living balloon moving through its own weather.

The scavengers keep the world clean. They are the peel-broods: low, many-legged, heavily armored creatures with mouthparts capable of rasping bark, seed-shells, chitin, bone-analogues, and desiccated flesh. They do not bury the dead. They disassemble them. Their bodies exude dry resinous compounds that loosen fibers and dissolve surface oils. They move with patient, clicking determination over fallen panels, broken capsules, shattered leaves, and carcasses. Where they finish, only polished mineral fragments remain. They are not gentle, and they are not sentimental. If one of their own cracks under a passing herd, the others consume it before the dust settles. Through them, every shed thing returns to the crust without wet decay.

Below the crust, in the compacted root-felt and buried rhizome networks, live the root-tappers. They are pale, blind, slow creatures, part worm-like, part jointed, with needle-mouths that can pierce living root tissue without killing it. They draw electrolyte gels and salt-rich fluids, and in return they excrete mineral grains that help harden the surface above. They are among the plateau’s hidden regulators. Their movement creates a faint subsonic hum, and sensitive animals can tell by the ground’s vibration whether the root-tappers are active nearby. During great electrostatic redistributions, they rise closer to the surface, drawn by charge gradients, and the crust seems to breathe with them.

Predation exists, and it is adapted to the noise. The knell-hunters are the plateau’s acoustic ambushers. They are lean, plated, long-limbed creatures with insulated feet and resonant chambers in their throats. They do not chase prey across open ground for long; the bright distances are too exposed. Instead they wait near tower bases, seed-scatter fields, or grazing routes, timing their attacks to the loudest sounds: a seed-capsule clash, a spore-blast, a thunderclap of static. Some can even imitate small mechanical noises, producing clicks or groans that draw curious prey closer. Their strikes are fast. They pierce, inject a paralyzing resin, and consume the victim’s oils and gels. They are sparse, because the plateau’s production is vast but its large bodies are widely spaced. A knell-hunter may control several kilometers of open crust, listening to the land as if it were a machine whose faults can be exploited.

If one asks not merely what lives there but who lives there, the answer becomes stranger. There are beings who move with enough intention, memory, and coordination to seem like persons, though they are nothing like human beings. They might be called the Dry Cantors, though that is an outsider’s name; if they name themselves, the name is probably a pattern of knocks, static pulses, and low vibration rather than a spoken word.

The Dry Cantors are tall, narrow, multi-limbed, with hollow frames and bodies plated in reflective scale. Their limbs end not in simple hands but in bundles of fine, insulated filaments capable of touching, conducting, isolating, or discharging electricity. Their senses are distributed across their bodies: tympanic membranes along their ribs, heat pits near their shoulders, electrostatic spines along their backs, and resin-lensed optical organs that see brightness and flicker better than shape. They do not build. They do not carry tools. They do not make permanent marks. Their entire way of being is based on temporary presence: standing in the right place, touching the right membrane, discharging at the right moment, moving before the pattern closes.

They live in small bands or alone, following routes determined by tower hums, charge gradients, seed-release cycles, and the movements of herds. They feed on oily nectar, resin beads, seed kernels, electrolyte gels, and shed vent-dweller bodies. They do not farm, because the plateau farms itself. They do not domesticate, because the animals are already bound into the system. Their relation to the landscape is closer to that of a nerve ending than a farmer. They notice what is changing. They move toward imbalance. They can stand within a discharge path and draw excess voltage through their bodies into the root-crust, their spines glowing blue while the ground around them cracks faintly with heat. They can tap on hollow tower ribs and listen to the returning vibrations, detecting blockages or pressure shifts. They can gather near an overcharged crown and, by coordinating their own small discharges, trigger a controlled release that prevents a larger rupture.

Their communication is industrial and musical at once. A conversation among Dry Cantors might consist of three knocks on a cellulose ridge, a long scrape along a fluted trunk, a burst of static from shoulder-spines, and a sudden stillness. Their language is not stored in objects. It is carried in rhythm, voltage, and bodily memory. They teach their young by placing them in resonant positions: here, where the wind sings through a tower vent; there, where the plinthback calls rise through the crust; there, where the arc-brushes flash at dusk. Their children learn the plateau as a sequence of pressures and intervals. Nothing is written. Nothing is built. Yet their knowledge is precise.

Their young hatch from hard resin-shelled cases laid in natural fissures, with no nest made and no place kept. The young are dry almost immediately, already plated in thin reflective scales, already sensitive to charge. They learn by following resonant trails, by imitating discharges, by pressing their filament-bundles to vibrating stems until they understand which rhythms mean stability and which mean danger. When the Dry Cantors die, their bodies are taken by peel-broods or shattered into scale-dust by wind; nothing is buried, nothing preserved, nothing set apart as a relic.

They are not gentle either. If a vent is blocked and cannot be cleared by touch, they may tear away the obstruction with their filament-bundles, even if living tissue is damaged, because the tower’s failure would harm more than one creature. If a predator presses too close to a gathering, they can focus a static discharge into a blinding blue crack, driving it back. If resources shift, they abandon routes without mourning them. Their ethics, if they have such a thing, are the ethics of a self-running factory: keep the flow open, keep the charge balanced, keep the production moving. They do not own the plateau. They belong to its operation.

At night, if night comes to this high dry world, the inhabitants do not fall silent. The towers cool and contract, knocking and groaning. The plinthbacks settle into loose circles, their plates ticking as they lose heat. The glass-midges thin into high, faint streams. The arc-brushes roost in sheltered vents, their bristles still sparking weakly. The wind-rays fold against dark tower ribs. The Dry Cantors may become more active, because charge redistributes as temperatures fall, and the plateau needs more careful attendance. Their static pulses move through the dark like small blue thoughts. The root-tappers hum underfoot. The knell-hunters listen. The peel-broods continue their slow work. The world remains awake, loud, and dry.

During storms, all these lives intensify. A pollen front can turn the bright air into a yellow roar. The herds lock into defensive mounds. The glass-midges are swept into dense clouds and eaten by filter-feeders. The arc-brushes retreat to leeward vents. The seed-jacks flee in all directions, scattering the tower’s output across kilometers. The wind-rays rise into the turbulence, hunting the chaos. The vent-dwellers emerge in pressure-triggered clouds, becoming food and maintenance in the same moment. The Dry Cantors move quickly between towers, touching, discharging, listening, their bodies flickering with borrowed voltage. The plateau’s production becomes visible as weather, and every inhabitant becomes part of that weather.

What lives there, then, is not a single creature and not merely a list of animals. It is a dry industrial ecology in which bodies are machines and machines are bodies. The towers are the largest inhabitants, but they are not alone. The herds trim the living ground. The swarms filter the sky. The pollinators carry charge and seed. The vent-dwellers clean the towers from within. The seed-jacks scatter the future. The gliders patrol the thermals. The scavengers erase death. The root-tappers regulate what lies beneath. The predators enforce tension. And the Dry Cantors, if they are truly persons, are the plateau’s rarest product: awareness without ownership, intelligence without artifacts, mind without a city.

To stand among them would be to understand that the place is not empty. It is overproduced, overcharged, over-loud, and ancient. Nothing there is soft in the way wet worlds are soft. Nothing is quiet. Nothing is still. Every inhabitant is a function with eyes, a hunger with a rhythm, a tool that grows and dies and is recycled. The plateau lives as one vast, sparse, roaring system, and whatever lives within it lives by becoming part of that roar.
