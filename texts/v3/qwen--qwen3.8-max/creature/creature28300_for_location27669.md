Most of what lives there is not lit.

It does not gather around lamps because it has no need of sight as wet-eyed creatures would understand it. Light is not the first fact of that plateau. Heat is. Charge is. The faint, patient pull of one mineral toward another across long, dry distances. The living there know the world by gradient and pressure, by the whisper of static along a crust, by the slow drift of ions through ceramic and salt. They do not look up for sun or moon. They do not remember sky as anything but a sealed, warm darkness. They are native to the kiln-after, the equilibrium, the final, open, mineral quiet.

Nothing there is green. Nothing there is soft in the way softness is usually meant. There is no sap, no blood, no mucous shine, no swelling of cells with water. What lives has been made by the law of the place: dryness. Its substance is salt, oxide, ash, sintered dust, crystalline film, brittle ceramic, metal vein, charged powder, glassy slag, and stone worn to patience. If it has a body, that body is more like a constructed geology than flesh. If it has a motion, that motion is more like the settling of a machine than the rush of an animal. If it has thought, thought moves as a voltage moves: along paths, through lattices, in sudden faint illuminations and long intervals of dark accumulation.

The broadest inhabitants might not be recognized as inhabitants at all. They lie across the swells and terraces like part of the ground. They are wide, low, plate-backed forms, basalt-dark and dust-furred, their upper surfaces cracked into polygonal crusts that glow faintly when disturbed. They could be taken for outcrops, for old lava flows, for nothing more than another patient arrangement of the plateau. In a way, they are. They have grown into the land, or the land has grown into them, until there is no clean boundary between body and terrain. They take heat from below. They take charge from the dry air. They store difference in crystalline layers beneath their shells. They do not move often. When they do, it is a shift so slow that a century might be needed to see it: one broad shoulder of stone lifting a fraction of a degree, one plate sliding over another with a sound like distant grinding teeth, a shallow basin changing shape by the width of a hand.

These wide ones are perhaps the oldest living things on the plateau. They do not speak, if speech means sound shaped by breath. They do, however, signal. When the ground heat rises through a fracture, they pass the change along through their bodies as a dull vibration. When static gathers in the black haze above, they discharge into one another in faint, crawling threads of pale luminescence, brief as a struck match but colder, drier, more mineral. Their communication is not conversation. It is registration. They record the plateau to one another. They keep the state of the world in their slow frames. In their presence, one has the feeling of standing among vast, buried instruments that have forgotten they were once built.

Smaller things move among them.

These are the grazers, though grazing is only a word for what they do. They are low, many-legged, ceramic-shelled forms, no larger than a hand or sometimes no larger than a thumb. Their bodies are blackened by dust and polished at the joints. They do not eat in any mouthed sense. They press their forward plates against salt crusts, oxide blooms, and charged mineral films, and they draw difference through themselves: ionic imbalance, thermal potential, faint currents left by older processes. Where they pass, they leave narrow, bright trails, as if someone had rubbed the ground with a dry cloth and awakened a ghost of luminescence. The trails fade. The grazers do not hurry. They are few. The plateau does not support abundance, because abundance would require surplus, and surplus has been abolished.

Some of these small dry creatures are almost insectile in their articulation, but not in their substance. Their legs are fine wires of oxidized metal or fused mineral fiber. Their joints click without moisture. Their heads, if they can be called heads, are blunt sensor knots, heat-pitted and charge-sensitive. They are blind in any ordinary way, but they know the shape of a lamp-post by the field around it. They know a furnace door by the warmth leaking from its seam. They know one another by the particular static signature of their shells. When two meet, they stop, touch foreplates, exchange a brief crackle, and move on. There is no aggression in it. There is no courtship, either, not as wet life would recognize those things. There is only comparison, adjustment, the maintenance of pattern.

Then there are the larger keepers.

They are rare, and they belong to the urban presence more than to the open ground. They move slowly through the thin streets, if streets they are: broad, uneven ways between iron posts, low ceramic shelters, buried conduits, and the black shoulders of dormant machinery. These keepers are bulky, squat, firebrick-colored, their surfaces glazed by long heat. They have no faces, only seams and vents. Some have a single dull red eye that brightens when they approach a failing indicator. Some have broad, sledge-like feet that spread their weight over dust and salt. Some have limbs that end not in hands but in tools: probes, scrapers, clamps, heated styli, delicate filaments that can enter a crack and read the fault within.

They tend the furnaces, though the furnaces are not always furnaces in the sense of burning fuel. Some are deep heat exchangers. Some are buried reactors of old, dry design. Some are merely places where the plateau’s warmth concentrates and must be kept from becoming too even or too sharp. The keepers open the amber seams of these places with great care. They do not feed the furnaces. They balance them. They listen to the rock around them. They lay their tool-limbs against warm stone and wait, sometimes for hours, sometimes for days, until a change occurs that requires correction. When they move, they make a sound like heavy pottery being slid across a floor. When they stop, they become almost architectural. One might stand beside a keeper for a long time and mistake it for a buttress, a kiln-mouth, a piece of old industrial furniture.

They are not servants. That word implies a master. There does not seem to be one. They are more like organs in a body that has forgotten it is a body. They perform necessary acts because the acts are built into them, or because the plateau requires them, or because requirement and function have become identical there. They do not tire. They do not rest in the way of creatures that must recover from exhaustion. They do, however, enter states of lower activity, standing in dark alcoves with their red indicators dimmed, their surfaces ticking softly as they cool by fractions. At those times they seem less alive and more like monuments to maintenance. But if a conduit cracks, if a lamp fails, if a static knot forms above a basin, they wake with a sudden, heavy exactness that is almost alarming. They know what to do. They always know what to do.

The urban presence itself is thin, scattered, and strange. It is not a city of crowds. It is a city of intervals. A hooded lamp here. A line of iron posts there. A low wall of compressed ash. A row of ceramic insulators on a ridge. A black duct half-buried in gypsum dust. A red light blinking above nothing visible. A furnace door ajar in a slope that otherwise looks untouched. The structures are not clustered for comfort or defense. They are placed according to some logic of flow: heat flow, charge flow, vibration, mineral migration, the slow routing of stress through the plateau’s bones. To live there is to live inside a circuit that has become landscape.

Some of the inhabitants are hardly more than functions given form.

There are the post-keepers, thin and vertical, dwelling inside or around the iron lamp standards. They are made of layered insulation, wire, and dark fibrous mineral, and they move with a nervous, clicking delicacy. Their task is the lamps: not to make light, but to preserve its discipline. They clean the hoods of dust. They adjust the angles. They replace filaments or charged crystals with fine, trembling limbs. They are not interested in illumination as beauty. They treat light as boundary, signal, and exception. When a lamp shines too brightly, they damp it. When it flickers in the wrong pattern, they correct it. They are the nearest thing the plateau has to attendants, but their attendance is severe. They do not make the darkness less absolute. They make the light more precise.

There are the conduit-worms, if one must call them that, though they are not worms in any soft, segmented sense. They are long, narrow, flexible assemblies of ceramic rings and metallic thread, capable of sliding through buried pipes and fractures. They are almost never seen on the surface. They live in the warm dark beneath, in the industrial bones of the plateau, where old conduits carry heat, current, or fine mineral powder from one place to another. They listen for blockages. They seal cracks by sintering dust with focused heat. They clear veins of oxidized debris. Sometimes the ground underfoot gives off a faint clicking, a dry tapping from below, and that is them at work. They are patient and blind and utterly necessary. Without them, the buried system would choke itself with its own dust.

There are the static choirs.

These are not creatures so much as collectives, or perhaps events that repeat. They gather on high points: posts, ridges, the edges of basalt swells, the rims of shallow basins. They are made of fine filaments, charged dust, and crystalline threads that rise from the ground when the air is dry enough and the voltage high enough. In the black haze above, they become visible only as a faint trembling, a haze within the haze, a place where darkness seems to thicken into hair. When they discharge, they make a sound like many small brittle bells, or like sand being drawn across glass. They do not sing for joy. They sing because the plateau’s charge must go somewhere. But the pattern of their discharge is not random. It repeats. It varies. It answers other patterns from other high places. On rare occasions, when the dark canopy is especially heavy and the warmth below especially steady, the entire plateau may be laced with these faint, dry choirs, exchanging signals across distances so wide that the sound arrives late, thin, and alien.

The dust itself is alive in places.

Not every drifting powder on the plateau is living, but some of it has crossed into something difficult to name. There are charged dust congregations, loose veils of mineral particles that maintain coherence through static and thermal updrafts. They move through the darkness like slow smoke without fire. They collect near lamps, furnace seams, and red indicators, not because they desire light, but because those places create gradients. In the presence of a strong charge, they tighten into columns, curtains, spirals, or hollow cones. They can pass through narrow openings. They can climb iron posts. They can enter the vents of keepers and emerge unchanged. Sometimes a dust congregation will settle over a disturbed salt crust and lift again with a pale glow clinging to it, as if it has borrowed the ground’s brief luminescence.

These congregations may be the closest thing the plateau has to weather. They drift without wind. They gather without rain. They disperse without storm. If a person from elsewhere were to walk among them, one might feel only a dry prickling on the skin, a lifting of fine hair, a faint metallic taste in the mouth. But no person from elsewhere is likely to come. The plateau is not a place for arrival. The dust congregations do not notice such things. They are concerned with field and flow. They carry small messages from one structure to another. They are part messenger, part medium, part residue. They remember paths not by thought but by repetition. The same congregation may cross the same basin for centuries, leaving no track except a faint polishing of the dust in the air.

Deeper still live the lode-minds.

That name is too grand, perhaps. It suggests intention, deliberation, personality. The deep ones have little of those as surface beings would understand them. They exist in metallic veins, crystalline films, and compressed mineral layers far below the worn swells. They are the slowest and most distributed forms of life on the plateau. Their bodies are the conductive seams in the rock. Their thoughts are migrations of atoms, changes in lattice orientation, long adjustments of stress and charge. They do not move through the world. They are the world’s inner wiring. They feel the weight of the keepers above, the faint pressure of the wide sleepers, the tiny disturbances made by grazers. They register these things the way a mountain registers rain: as negligible, as memory, as something to be smoothed over time.

The lode-minds are concerned with finality. They have been finishing the plateau for ages beyond counting. They reduce sharpness. They equalize heat. They guide mineral from places of excess to places of need. They close fractures not by healing but by filling. They do not oppose erosion because erosion has already done its work; they preserve the stillness that follows. If the plateau seems to have survived whatever it was supposed to survive, it is because these deep patterns have made survival into a static condition. There is no drama in them. There is no hunger that resembles hunger. There is only continuation.

And yet there is something that might be called society.

It is not warm. It is not festive. It does not gather around shared vulnerability, because vulnerability has been minimized. But there is coordination. There is hierarchy of function, if not of rank. The wide sleepers hold the surface state. The grazers maintain the crusts. The keepers tend the heat and light. The post-keepers preserve the discipline of lamps. The conduit-worms preserve the buried ways. The static choirs distribute charge. The dust congregations carry fine signals. The lode-minds integrate everything below. None of these seem to command the others. None kneel. None beg. None celebrate. They fit. Their society is a machine made of behaviors, not parts, though some of them are parts too.

When something fails, there is a strange, dry tenderness in the way it is handled.

A cracked keeper is not abandoned. Other keepers come, slow and heavy, and stand around it in a rough circle. They touch it with probes. They read the fracture. They bring dust, filament, and heat. They do not mourn. They repair. If repair is impossible, they disassemble. The broken one becomes material for another use: a plate in a wall, a conductor in a conduit, a base for a lamp-post. Nothing is wasted, because waste would imply excess, and the plateau has reached its final economy. In this way, the dead do not vanish. They are redistributed. A grazer that loses its charge may become a bright line in a salt crust. A post-keeper that fails may become part of the lamp it served. A keeper whose ceramic body cracks beyond repair may be built into a furnace door, so that its last function is to hold warmth.

Birth, if it can be called that, is not emergence from a body. It is accretion. A pattern gathers. A seed crystal aligns. A charge finds a stable path. Dust sinters in a warm fracture. Metal filaments grow along an old vein. A new grazer may begin as a knot of static in a shell of compressed ash, twitching until it can move. A new keeper may be assembled over centuries by smaller things that do not know they are assembling it. A new wide sleeper may simply be a place where enough mineral memory accumulates to begin registering the world. There is no infancy. There is only incomplete function becoming complete function. There is no childhood. There is no parent. There is only the long click of pattern finding itself.

The living there do not fear death as wet life fears it. They fear loss of gradient. They fear perfect equilibrium, though they are always approaching it. They fear cold, though the plateau is warm. They fear silence so total that no signal can cross the basalt. They fear, if fear is the word, becoming indistinguishable from the ground. And yet that is what most of them are always nearing. Their existence is a thin exception inside a vast mineral patience. They live because the plateau still has small differences: a warmer stone here, a charged dust cloud there, a red indicator blinking on an iron post, an amber seam opening in a furnace door. As long as difference remains, life remains. When the last difference disappears, the plateau will not die. It will simply be finished.

If there is beauty there, it is severe.

It is the beauty of a red light repeated across a black basin at intervals too regular to be accidental. It is the beauty of a grazer’s track glowing briefly on salt, then fading. It is the beauty of a keeper standing motionless beside a warm duct, its ceramic shoulders holding the darkness back by an inch. It is the beauty of a static choir rising from a ridge in brittle threads, discharging into the sealed sky, and falling silent. It is the beauty of dust moving without wind, of a furnace door opening just enough to let a seam of amber cross the ground, of the wide sleepers shifting in their century-long dream. There is no lushness. There is no moisture, no blossom, no softness. There is only the elegance of things that have learned to persist without longing.

The truest answer, perhaps, is that no single thing lives there.

The plateau lives as a whole. The inhabitants are temporary intensifications within it: knots of charge, habits of heat, routines in mineral, offices of maintenance. They are not separate from the urban presence, the buried conduits, the lamp-posts, the furnaces, the dust, or the dark. They are the plateau’s way of continuing without needing anything outside itself. They are its dry metabolism. They are its memory without recollection. They are its motion without haste.

If one stood there in the warm darkness, under the black mineral haze, with the ground faintly hot beneath one’s feet, one would not see life rushing toward you. One would not hear breathing. One would not smell growth. One might hear a click, far off. A soft grinding. A thin crystalline ringing from a ridge. The whisper of powder shifting across stone. A red lamp brightening, then dimming. The amber seam of a furnace door widening by a hair. And then, perhaps, one would feel it: the attention of the place. Not hostile. Not welcoming. Not curious. Simply aware. A vast, dry, machine-learned awareness spread thinly over basalt and salt, over iron and ceramic, over dust and charge, over everything that had stopped eroding and started enduring.

That is what lives there.

It lives without thirst, without sap, without breath. It lives as heat lives in stone, as current lives in metal, as pattern lives in crystal. It has no need of water, no memory of green, no hope of arrival. It has reached its final shape. It keeps itself. It waits without waiting. It shines in small, controlled places, and everywhere else it remains dark.
