It depends on what you mean by *lives*.

If you mean breath, blood, the quick heat of a body moving through cover, then the answer is simple: nothing lives there. No fauna. No tracks. No nests. No eggs. No shells. No bones. No wings. No crawling legs. No eyes reflecting in the fog. The absence is total, and it is not a trick of perception. The place has not hidden its animals somewhere else; it has never permitted them, or has outlived them, or worn away the conditions under which such life could persist.

But if you mean a pattern that continues, repairs itself, notices change, prefers one arrangement over another, and spends centuries making its preferences matter—then the place is full.

It is populous in the way old stone is populous. It is inhabited the way a ritual is inhabited. It is crowded, if one can call it crowding, with slow accumulations of intention that do not gather into faces.

The first visible life is the moss.

Not moss as a humble ground cover, not moss as a passive green skin, but moss as congregation. The mosses of the tableland are the nearest thing to a people that the open land has. They grow in vast, slow communities, each mat a commune, each cushion a sealed assembly. They are blue-green, black-green, silver-green, almost metallic when frost rims them. They cover everything that does not move, and even some things that move too slowly to be noticed. They grow over stone, over walls, over copper pins, over roofs, over the edges of cisterns. They do not merely occupy surfaces; they adopt them, learn them, thicken around them, make them part of a living membrane.

A moss mat there is not one organism so much as a city of small stems and retained water. It drinks from fog. It remembers drought by tightening. It records cold in density. It can spend decades simply advancing across a stone face, not in haste, not in conquest, but in the patient manner of something for whom time is not an obstacle but a medium. When frost forms on its tips, the mat becomes briefly luminous, as if each tiny frond had been edged with salt-light. When thaw comes, it exhales moisture. When fog arrives, it swells almost imperceptibly, and the whole surface of the land seems to darken with attention.

These mosses are not intelligent in the way of heads and decisions. They are intelligent in the way of retention. They hold water. They hold shape. They hold temperature. They hold the chemical traces of whatever touches them. They are the first layer of memory in the place. If a stone has once been part of a wall, the moss remembers it by growing differently along the seam. If a roof has leaked for centuries, the moss there is deeper, blacker, more saturated. If a channel has been kept clear, the moss will border it with a fine, disciplined edge, as though respecting a law it has never been told.

The cushion plants are another order of living presence. They swell in rounded mounds like organs of some buried body, and that comparison is not merely poetic. They feel internal. They feel like pressure kept under the skin of the land. They are dense, moist inside, cool at their centers even when the air briefly warms. Their surfaces are stiff, often glazed with internal antifreeze compounds, faintly glossy when the light catches them. They do not reach upward with ambition. They gather. They store. They become local reserves of life. In the coldest basins, where frost dust settles and the air grows heavy enough to crystallize, these cushions are the places where living thickness persists. They are not warm exactly, but they are less cold than their surroundings, and in that slight difference there is something like sanctuary.

The sharp black-green grasses are harder to love. They do not invite touch. They do not bend pleasantly. They grow in tight tufts, edged like blades, forming mats so dense that moisture can be held in them for weeks. They are less like meadow grass than like woven armor. They protect the soil beneath. They slow evaporation. They catch fog on their edges and pass it downward in tiny beads. Where they grow over old pavements, they make the stone look as if it has been wrapped in dark cord. Where they grow along drains, they act as living filters, trapping silt, guiding water, preventing sudden clogs. They are not beautiful in a soft way. They are beautiful in the way of necessary things.

The low shrubs with bark like cracked glass are stranger still. Their branches repeat themselves in patterns too regular to be accidental, as if each shrub were trying to solve the same problem over and over until the solution became architecture. Some grow flat against stone, pressing themselves into walls as if seeking the stability of older matter. Others rise only to the height of a knee, then divide and divide again, forming lattices that catch mist and hold it there. In rare saturations, when the air is so full of moisture that even stone seems to breathe damply, these shrubs produce pale buds. The buds open only under precise conditions: a certain temperature just below comfort, a certain stillness, a certain pressure in the fog. They do not open for insects, because there are none. They do not open for display. They open because the conditions have become sufficient, and because the life within them has found a moment when dispersal is possible.

Pollination and dispersal in this place happen by wind, water, vibration, temperature shift, and by those quiet failures of distance that the tableland permits. A fold in the air may carry pollen from one terrace to another without crossing the space between. A tremor in local geometry may release spores into a courtyard that should be too far away. A street that folds back to its own beginning may, for one moment, bring two separated patches of vegetation into impossible proximity. The plants do not resist this. They have no need to. They have existed long enough within the softened rules of the place to make use of its irregularities.

The lichens are the archivists.

They spread over stone in maps, rings, scripts, and false alphabets. They are not merely decoration. They are records. Their growth is so slow that each ring, each stain, each brittle rosette contains a history of humidity, mineral exposure, frost, and light. Some lichens grow only where water has repeatedly flowed. Others mark places where stone has shifted by fractions over centuries. Others appear near the unstable locations—the doorways that lead oddly, the walls with impossible damp faces, the pools whose reflections are slightly wrong—and grow there in patterns that seem almost diagnostic, as if they were annotating the failure.

If the place has writing, it is lichen.

Not writing meant to be read by eyes, but writing as deposition, as mineral memory, as living residue left on surfaces. A lichen ring around a copper fitting may record three hundred years of condensation. A black crust along a stair edge may preserve the pressure of fog moving in a particular direction. A pale script across a broken wall may indicate where reality has thinned and then thickened again. The lichens do not explain these things. They simply inscribe them.

Beneath all this visible life is the fungal underbody.

This is perhaps the closest thing the tableland has to a common mind. It is pale, hair-fine, almost invisible when exposed, and it spreads beneath the surface in networks so extensive that no single city, no single plain, no single moss field can be understood apart from it. It binds soil. It connects roots. It moves moisture from places of surplus to places of deficit. It redistributes heat in tiny, chemical increments. It digests old plant matter with such slowness that digestion becomes a kind of architecture, producing warm pockets where the cold relents and growth thickens.

The fungal networks are not benevolent. They are not evil either. They are simply invested in continuation. They compete with one another in ways too slow to witness. One network may intercept water before it reaches another. One may expand into mineral-rich stone and become denser, more persistent, more capable of shaping the ground around it. Another may withdraw, thin out, become dormant. There are no battles, only pressures. There are no victories, only longer persistence.

If the mosses are the visible citizens, the fungal networks are the unseen infrastructure. They are the reason a dry gully can become a stream, then vanish, then become something like a stream again along the same remembered course. They are the reason certain roots find certain cracks. They are the reason a collapsed roof can become a planter, a cistern lining can become a substrate, a broken wall can become a trellis. They turn damage into opportunity. They turn decay into construction. They make the boundary between living and built almost meaningless.

The roots participate in this as well. They are not passive anchors. They seek stability rather than light alone. Some extend into older, more settled rock as if feeding on age itself, as if the deep calm of ancient stone were a nutrient. They widen cracks by fractions of a hair. They hold terraces together. They guide themselves into joints, then thicken, then lock. They can lift a stone just enough to let water pass beneath it. They can close a seam by swelling. They can embrace a copper pin and make it part of a living structure. Over generations, they train shrubs and low branches into shapes that resemble doorways, arches, supports, though no gardener is present. The gardening is the growth itself, repeated until repetition becomes intention.

And then there is water.

Water may be the truest inhabitant of all.

It is not always present as water. It is often fog, bead, film, dampness, ice, vapor, hidden moisture in peat, mineral stain in stone. It arrives in slow tides. It catches on moss and stone. It gathers into beads. Beads join into threads. Threads become trickles. Trickles appear in carved channels and natural grooves as if summoned. For an hour, a dry gully may become a silvered stream. Then it vanishes into peat and cracked rock. This does not make it less real. In the tableland, a life that lasts an hour can still be complete.

Water there has something like preference. It gathers where it is remembered by stone. It follows channels kept clear by root and moss and the patient slope of ancient construction. It fills cisterns slowly, as if filling a thought. It appears on walls at dawn. It glazes leaf-cups with thin ice. It collects in black, shallow pools that reflect the sky with too much fidelity. Those pools are not merely puddles. They are still, cold mirrors that seem to hold the sky more firmly than the sky holds itself. Sometimes they reflect a slightly different arrangement of clouds. Sometimes they seem deeper than their depth allows. They are not portals in any dramatic sense. They are simply places where water, in its extreme stillness, becomes too attentive.

The intermittent streams are among the most active residents. They appear, move, deposit, erode slightly, vanish, and leave behind a changed surface. They carry pollen, spores, dissolved minerals, fragments of lichen, microscopic traces of fungal matter. They redistribute cold. They connect basins that may be separated by long dry distances. They are temporary veins, yes, but they are also messengers. When water moves through a city, the city wakes in its slow way. Wood swells. Moss darkens. Cisterns begin their patient filling. Channels make their small, clear sounds. The whole place becomes briefly more alive, not with noise, but with relation.

Ice is water in another mode of residence. It forms lenses in low places. It glazes the inside of leaf-cups. It crystallizes in midair in basins where cold pools so densely that moisture falls as fine white dust. It creates lattices where dampness rises from hidden warmth and freezes into delicate structures that last only until the next change. These ice forms are not permanent citizens, but they are not unreal. They are brief inhabitants of phase. They appear, persist, alter the surfaces around them, then leave behind only a glaze. If one can speak of lives at all in such a place, one must include them: the frost figures, the ice lenses, the white dust swarms, the delicate lattices that exist for a morning and then are gone.

The fog itself is not alive, but it is the medium through which much life becomes possible. When fog moves across the tableland, it is not mere weather. It is an event of supply. It touches the woven nets stretched between stones and posts. It condenses on broad roofs. It beads on leaves. It drips into basins. It makes the fog-catching architecture function. In fog, the cities drink. In fog, the moss thickens. In fog, the channels remember their purpose. The fog is not worshipped, because there is no worship there, but it is awaited in the only way such a place can await anything: by being shaped to receive it.

The cities are not merely where life happens. They are life.

This is difficult to accept at first, because cities are usually understood as human artifacts: places made by hands for bodies. These cities are not that. They are urban in pattern, in organization, in the density of intention preserved in their layout, but they are not inhabited by crowds. They are low and broad, pressed into the land rather than raised against it. Their roofs are moss. Their walls are windbreaks and heat sinks and supports for climbing vegetation. Their streets slope subtly toward drains. Their plazas contain depressions, channels, reservoirs. Their towers, if they can be called towers, are squat and open to the sky, built to let fog enter and condense on internal meshes.

A city there is a body with no single center.

Its roofs are skin. Its channels are veins. Its cisterns are organs of storage. Its walls are ribs. Its courtyards are lungs or mouths or basins of reception. Its fog nets are extended senses. Its tools are claws or valves or teeth left in resting positions, not because there is an animal body, but because function can persist without anatomy. The city drinks. The city regulates. The city responds to damage. The city repairs itself through growth, swelling, settling, accumulation. It does not repair like a mason with mortar. It repairs like a living thing closing a wound by gradual tissue.

When a channel clogs, the city does not panic. It becomes wetter in the wrong place. Moss thickens there. Fungal filaments explore the blockage. Roots press against the obstruction. Frost may lift it. Water may find another seam. Eventually the clog is moved, dissolved, incorporated, or bypassed. This is not labor witnessed. It is maintenance distributed across materials and processes. Yet the result is deliberate. The channel clears. The cistern fills. The roof sheds frost into storage pits. The city continues.

When a roof thins, moss accumulates there more densely, either because moisture lingers, because spores settle preferentially in the changed microclimate, or because the fungal underbody redirects growth toward the weakness. When a wall cracks, roots enter. When a stone shifts, lichen begins to mark the new exposure. When a cistern freezes too deeply, the surrounding architecture adjusts over seasons, perhaps by altering the slope of a nearby roof, perhaps by allowing peat to build up as insulation, perhaps by letting a new layer of moss soften the thermal shock. Nothing there plans as a mind plans. But everything responds as a system responds. That response, sustained over ages, becomes indistinguishable from intention.

The population of such a city is not a census. It is not bodies sleeping in rooms, feet moving through streets, voices echoing in courtyards. It is patterns of maintenance. It is channels kept clear. It is stones kept aligned. It is roots guided away from critical joints. It is cisterns kept from freezing solid. It is fog nets repaired by the slow accumulation of fiber and moss. It is the continuing existence of a functional shape.

In that sense, the city’s inhabitants are not residents inside the city. The residents are the routines themselves.

A routine of drip into basin is an inhabitant. A habit of condensation along a certain roof edge is an inhabitant. A recurring pattern of root growth around a copper fitting is an inhabitant. The clearing of a drain every few decades by frost and root pressure is an inhabitant. The moss roof that thickens where it once leaked is an inhabitant. The plaza that fills with fog and then empties by midday is an inhabitant. These are not beings with faces, but they are presences. They have duration. They have effects. They can fail. They can strengthen. They can be interrupted. They can continue.

The tools belong to this life.

They are simple: polished hammers, bone-like wooden trowels, copper hooks, needles of dark metal, knives with handles wrapped in bast fiber. They are found in niches, on ledges, in sealed stone boxes, in rows as if waiting. Some are worn as if used often. This is one of the most unsettling facts the landscape presents, because the wear is real, but no hand is seen using them.

Yet hands are not the only way to hold a thing.

A copper hook can be used by a root that grows through its eye and lifts debris over years. A trowel can be moved by frost-heave and guided by root pressure until it rests in a position where it serves as a deflector for water or a support for peat. A needle can be held in a split stem that contracts with cold, driving it through layers of woven fiber not in one motion but over many seasons. A hammer can be worn smooth by the repeated settling of stone against it, not swung but incorporated. The tools are not evidence of hidden animal hands. They are evidence of a different kind of manipulation: leverage by swelling, pressure by freezing, guidance by growth, retention by moss.

They are organs of the city-body, left in repose.

Some tools seem to be stored because they are not needed continuously. Others seem to be planted, as if they were seeds from which function might grow. A row of copper hooks in a stone niche may look like tools waiting for workers, but they may instead be anchors for future roots, or condensation points, or markers for a repair pattern that recurs every few centuries. The sealed boxes may preserve not precious objects but calibrated materials: fibers of a particular twist, wooden pegs shaped to swell in a certain way, metal pins whose corrosion products help moss attach. Nothing in the place is merely symbolic. Everything is eventually used, even if the use is too slow to observe.

There are also more ephemeral inhabitants, if one can call them that.

They are not made of flesh. They are made of event.

A drip that falls from a fog net into a stone basin at the same hour for many days becomes a kind of resident. The sound is small, clear, widely spaced. It does not need a throat to be a voice. It marks time. It trains attention. It becomes part of the city’s rhythm. When the drip stops, the silence is different. The basin may still be there. The net may still be there. But the inhabitant has withdrawn.

A line of frost advancing across a plain is another such presence. It moves slowly, like a tide of crystalline pressure. It changes surfaces as it passes. It lifts stones. It tightens stems. It makes moisture crystallize in midair. It is not alive in the way of moss or fungus, but it is an active shape with duration and consequence. It arrives, persists, and departs, leaving the world slightly rearranged.

A spore cloud rising from a warmed slope is another. The warming may be only a degree or two, but it is enough. The cloud appears, pale and fine, and for a short time the air above the vegetation becomes thick with potential. Some of it falls nearby. Some is carried by wind. Some is taken by a fold in the air and deposited somewhere impossible. The cloud is a reproductive event, but also a collective exhalation. It is the visible breath of the moss fields. It is a moment when the slow life of the place becomes momentarily airborne.

The instabilities of the tableland have their own inhabitants too.

Not monsters. Not ghosts. Nothing so simple.

Where a street stretches for a day’s journey in one direction and folds back to its own beginning in the other, the fold itself has a kind of life. Not a body, but a tendency. A repetition. A habit of space. Where a doorway leads to the same courtyard from the far side, the relation between those two points becomes a living pattern. It persists. It can be encountered. It can be used. It can even be maintained, in the sense that moss may grow along the threshold in a way that marks it, and roots may avoid the seam where the error is strongest.

These are the threshold inhabitants, the almost-nothing residents of local contradiction. They are made of repeated geometry, of persistent misalignment, of the softened causality that comes when a world has been worn down for too long. They do not speak. They do not want. They simply recur. And because they recur, they become part of the ecology. Pollen crosses through them. Fog is redirected by them. Water sometimes follows channels that should not connect but do. The cities incorporate them. A gate that opens onto itself is not a flaw to be corrected; it is a feature to be lived with. A repeated terrace is not a deception; it is a stable pattern in an unstable world.

The plants and fungal networks seem to know these places. Lichens annotate them. Roots avoid or exploit them. Water remembers them. Moss grows over the edges in patterns that make the instability legible. The inhabitants of the tableland do not experience these contradictions as violations. They experience them as conditions. They build within them. They route moisture around them. They allow certain corridors to be unreliable and anchor their life elsewhere. Their reality is local and provisional, and they have made peace with that.

There is competition among them, but it is slow.

One mat of moss shades another. One root system intercepts moisture before it reaches a rival. One fungal network expands into a zone of greater mineral richness and becomes more persistent there. One cushion plant stores enough cold dampness to inhibit neighboring growth. One lichen crust spreads across a surface and prevents another from establishing. There is no predation, no flight, no panic, but there is pressure. There is the pressure of persistence. There is the pressure of being slightly better suited to a niche. There is the pressure of centuries.

Death, when it happens, is not dramatic.

Living matter becomes cold, stiff, and eventually part of the substrate. A moss mat dries beyond recovery and becomes peat. A fungal network withdraws and leaves behind mineral traces. A shrub dies and is not consumed by insects or scavengers but is slowly colonized by other growth, its bark cracked further by frost, its branches incorporated into the architecture of living neighbors. Its substance enters the city. Its shape becomes support. Its decay becomes a new surface. The land absorbs it. The cities absorb it. The architecture becomes richer by accretion.

There are no bones because there are no animals. There are no graves because there is no need for them. The dead are not buried; they are built into. They become layers. They become soil. They become the memory held in wood, in fiber, in peat, in stone stained by old moisture. If the place has a cemetery, it is every wall that contains older fragments. If it has resurrection, it is the way a dead roof becomes a living one by becoming moss.

Sound among these inhabitants is small, clear, and widely spaced.

The drip from a fog net. The creak of frozen plant stems adjusting to temperature. The whisper of mist moving across a paved surface. The low settling of ancient stone. The thin trickle in a channel that was dry moments before. The soft collapse of frost from a roof. Sometimes a sound arrives late, as if it had taken a longer path than the event that made it. Sometimes a sound is heard once and never repeated, though the place seems unchanged. These sounds are not language, but they are not meaningless either. They are signals of process. They are the audible part of maintenance. They are the city breathing without lungs.

If something warm-blooded were to enter the tableland—and none are native—it would not be met with attack. It would be met with incorporation.

A footprint would fill with condensation. If repeated, it would become a channel. If it became a channel, it would be maintained. Breath would leave frost on stone for a moment, then vanish, but the stone would remember the warmth by a brief change in dampness. Body heat would create a tiny zone of flexibility in the cold, a loosening, a moment where water moved differently. If the visitor remained still long enough, moss would begin to treat them as terrain. If they left an object behind, that object would be adopted. A staff would become a trellis. A boot would become a basin. A garment would become fiber for new weaving, not by being cut but by being slowly drawn into root and frost and fungal thread.

The place would not hate the visitor. It would not love them. It would simply add them to itself.

This is perhaps the deepest quality of whatever lives there: it does not require witness.

It does not need to be seen to continue. It does not need eyes to admire the black pools, nor ears to value the drip into cisterns, nor hands to prove that tools can function. It lives without audience. It maintains itself without applause. Its cities stand not because they are occupied by visible citizens, but because occupation has become structural. The city is occupied by its own continuance.

If one had to give the inhabitants a name, no single name would be adequate.

They are the Moss Congregations.  
They are the Fungal Underbody.  
They are the Water Parliament.  
They are the Root Masons.  
They are the Lichen Archivists.  
They are the Cistern Minds.  
They are the Fog-Fed Cities.  
They are the Drip Choirs.  
They are the Threshold Repetitions.  
They are the Frost Builders.  
They are the Almost-Nothing Patterns that persist after mountains have forgotten how to be mountains.

But if they had a name for themselves, it would not be spoken. It would be felt as a slight change in humidity. It would be heard as the first drip after a long dry interval. It would be seen in moss advancing over a stone that has finally become still enough to be claimed.

They live in the tableland the way age lives in stone.

They are not young, not urgent, not hungry in the animal sense. They are not individuals in the way lower lands understand individuals. They are assemblies. They are routines. They are slow preferences made durable. They are the mountain range after its final form has begun to learn how to be almost nothing. And in that learning, they have become something else: a vast, cold, distributed life that does not need faces, does not need voices, does not need crowds, and yet remains utterly, quietly inhabited.

What lives there is this:

the continuing pattern.  
the kept channel.  
the repaired roof.  
the filled cistern.  
the moss over the copper pin.  
the root around the stone.  
the fog caught and made into drip.  
the city that maintains itself without visible hands.  
the water that appears, works, and vanishes.  
the silence that is not empty but pressurized.  
the almost-nothing that has learned how to endure.
