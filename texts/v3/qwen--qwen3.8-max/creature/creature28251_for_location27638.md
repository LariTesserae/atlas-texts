What lives there does not first appear as a single creature. It appears as a pressure of residence: a wet density, a census kept in film and shell and small movement. The basin is not inhabited in the way a field is inhabited by grazing things or a house by people. It is inhabited as a wound is inhabited by healing, as a pipe is inhabited by scale, as a quiet machine is inhabited by the consequences of its own long running. Life there has settled into the damp and made the damp necessary.

The first residents are not animals in any obvious sense. They are the microbial skins and films, the thin living surfaces that turn stone, metal, and standing water into something that can be eaten. They coat the black pans in patches of rust-red, sulfur-yellow, iron-black, and pale mineral gray. Under the milky light they look like stains, but they are the base of every appetite in the hollow. They live on dissolved salts, on weak current, on warmth from buried engines, on the faint chemical wealth carried by condensation. Where water stands still long enough, they become a velvety skin. Where current disturbs them, they grow in streaks and ripples. Where electricity lingers, they thicken into bright threads, almost luminous, almost deliberate.

The fungal growths are their quieter partners. They are not soft in the way of foliage. They are not gentle. They cling to shaded walls in sheets like old skin, in crusts like ash, in pale mineral blooms that flake when touched. They spread over brick and ceramic, over dead cables and the backs of iron housings, binding dust and droplet and rust into something stable enough for small bodies to use. They smell dry and sour and chalky, a smell like wet cellars and powdered stone. They are shelter, nursery, and food. They are also weathering: their slow threads work into cracks and make the old built things more broken, more rounded, more useful to animals.

Upon these films and crusts move the grazers.

They are the most common visible life: flat, pale, patient things that suggest crabs and limpets mixed together and then worn down by the place itself. Their bodies are low, broad, and eyeless. Their shells are not bright; they are stained by copper, darkened by iron, crusted with mineral bloom until each one looks like a small piece of the basin carrying itself. The edges of their shells have been smoothed by constant contact with wet stone. The undersides are fitted with rasping mouths, hard combs that scrape bacterial film from rock, brick, and rusted metal. They feed slowly. They feed constantly. Their movement is so gradual that condensation can gather on their backs while they graze.

They do not wander randomly. They know the walls, the seepage lines, the places where mineral water beads first and where the films grow thickest after a condensation cycle. They gather in clusters, pressed close in damp crevices or arranged around the rims of shallow pools like pale dishes. Their bodies fit together without seeming to fight. When one shifts, the others adjust by a fraction of a body’s width. There are tiny negotiations among them: a pressure of shell against shell, a chemical taste left on stone, a vibration passed through metal. Nothing rises to conflict unless a feeding surface is threatened. Then the movement becomes firmer, more insistent, but still almost silent.

Their shells are habitats in themselves. Tiny organisms settle on them: white specks like moving mineral grains, threadlike hitchhikers, small cleaners that eat fungal overgrowth from the shell margins. Some young attach to the backs of larger ones, gaining movement, warmth, and access to fresh film. An old grazer can carry a whole miniature population. When it dies, its shell does not disappear. It becomes a bowl, a shelter, a surface for eggs, a scraping plate for younger mouths. In this way, even the dead remain part of the basin’s architecture.

In the shallow pans where water is thin and still, another kind of life rises from the crust: the tube-dwellers. They are segmented, patient creatures housed in cases built from whatever the basin provides. Their tubes are made of cemented grit, chips of basalt, rust beads, fragments of porcelain, tiny shards of green-glazed ceramic, and grains of pale sweating stone. Each tube is fixed to the mineral crust at an angle suited to the flow of droplets. From the open end protrudes a crown of feathered filaments, sometimes pale, sometimes faintly amber or blue, depending on what the animal has eaten. When mist thickens or water begins to crawl over the crust, the crowns spread and filter the passing moisture, catching bacteria, spores, suspended mineral particles, and the finest organic debris.

They are easily mistaken for part of the stone until disturbed. Then the crown withdraws with a soft, wet contraction, and the tube seals itself behind a tough cap or a plug of grit-bound mucus. The movement is small, but it sends a tremor through the surrounding crust. Nearby tube-dwellers respond. A whole shallow pan can close itself in a ripple of retreat, leaving only what appears to be mineral texture. Minutes later, one crown reappears, then another, until the water is again being combed by hundreds of tiny patient mouths.

In the flooded channels and drowned gutters live the long ones.

They are sleek, soft-bodied, limbless or nearly limbless, more like eels crossed with worm-salamanders than anything with a cleaner name. Their skins are slick and translucent in places, dark veins visible beneath, pulsing faintly with fluid movement. They have no obvious eyes, or only dim light-pits that register changes in the milky illumination. Instead their heads carry sensitive grooves and pits that taste current, heat, salt, metal, and charge. They move through the water with slow lateral bends, exploring cracks, slipping under grating, winding around pipes and through the hollow interiors of dead engines. They prefer the channels where water runs warm from buried machinery, because warmth carries food and loosens microbial films.

They are hunters, but not hurried ones. They wait near outflows with their mouths slightly open, filtering small things until something substantial passes. Then the strike is sudden and wet, a short whip of the body, a closure of pale jaws. They eat smaller channel creatures, larvae, shed skins, injured grazers, and the soft bodies of tube-dwellers if they can pry them from their cases. Sometimes they rest in the mouths of old pipes, their tails trailing into darker water, their bodies vibrating faintly as they sense the approach of prey. They are themselves prey for larger ambushers, and their translucence may be useful: in the dim water they become difficult to see until their dark veins betray them.

They shed their skins often. The cast skins drift in the channels or snag on rusted edges, thin and empty, already being colonized by fungal threads and bacterial films. Small animals gather to eat them. Nothing in the basin refuses such a gift.

The air has its own residents, though the air here is so heavy with moisture that flight feels less like crossing open space than swimming through a thinner pool.

The smallest aerial creatures are rust-flies: thin-legged, quick, born from oxidized films and standing damp. They skim over the black mirrors of the pools, touching the surface tension with delicate steps, making rings that tremble outward until mist swallows them. Their larvae live inside rust layers, eating the bacteria that eat the iron. The adults are brief and hungry. They cluster on wet rails and charged wires, and when a current flickers through the metal, their bodies twitch as though startled by pleasure or pain.

Larger and slower are the mineral moths, though they are not moths in any ordinary sense. Their wings are not dusty with pollen. They are scaled with tiny mineral flakes, crystalline plates that catch the weak light and shed a fine powder when the creatures move. They cluster on warm engine casings, on condenser stacks, on the rounded roof of the pump-house, their bodies pressed close in rows. When a valve releases heat or a fan coughs into motion, they rise in trembling clouds. They do not travel far. They circle the source of warmth, ride the mist, and settle again when the air cools. The powder from their wings seems to encourage condensation: droplets form more readily where it falls. In this way, even the winged things help the basin remain wet.

There are also heavier damp-winged forms, low-slung and awkward, too broad for easy flight. They move mostly by crawling, using their wings only to glide from pipe to stone or to escape a rising pulse of heat. Their bodies are furred with wet filaments that trap microbial spores and mineral grains. They smell faintly of ozone and old oil. They gather around warm vents and the mouths of ducts, where the air carries food. When they die, they do not fall cleanly. They are taken apart where they lie by mites, by small crawlers, by fungal threads, until only a dark smear and a few wing-scales remain.

On the ground and under it, the scavengers keep the basin from becoming choked with its own remains.

They are many-legged, low, and deliberate. Some resemble overgrown aquatic insects; others are closer to armored slugs with jointed limbs. Their bodies are broad enough to press against the ground, their carapaces crusted with mineral deposits until they look like moving debris. They do not hurry. They move through the grating, beneath loose plates, around boiler shells, and through the spaces behind split brick. Their mouthparts are strong and worn smooth. They crack empty shells, strip dead fungal mats, chew the bodies of dead grazers, and pick organic film from rusted surfaces.

They are the basin’s patient cleaners. Where a creature dies, they arrive in ones and twos at first, then in larger numbers if the body is big enough. They do not swarm noisily. They work in close silence, mandibles clicking softly, legs adjusting their weight on wet stone. Their presence is often known only by the slow reduction of what was dead into fragments, stains, and mineral residue. Older scavengers become so encrusted that when they stop moving they are almost indistinguishable from the rubble. Water collects on their backs. Small flies mistake them for stone. If touched, they do not flee quickly. They tighten, lower themselves, and wait.

Predators are here too, but they are not dramatic. They are fitted to the quiet.

In the pipes and flooded ducts lie the needle-jaws: flattened, eyeless hunters with long bodies and narrow heads, their scales or skin matching rusted metal so closely that they seem to be part of the pipe wall. They wait where water moves, because moving water brings prey. When a small creature passes, they strike with a sudden sideways bend, their jaws closing in a line of fine points. The kill is quick, and the water barely disturbs. Afterward the predator may remain motionless for a long time, digesting in the dark.

Inside drowned engine blocks and open-topped housings live larger ambushers. They use the machinery as a skeleton of concealment. They rest between pistons, along flywheel rims, under furred mineral lace, their bodies curved around old iron. Some are translucent enough to take on the color of whatever lies beneath them. Some can sense the weak electric fields produced by stressed wiring or living muscle. They do not chase prey through open water. They let the basin bring food to them: a grazing creature that strays too close, a long swimmer that enters the wrong cavity, a moth fallen onto the surface of a pool above them. Then there is a wet burst, a muffled disturbance, and stillness returns.

There are also hunters that use the vertical damp. They cling beneath overhanging lips of stone or to the ceilings of partial sheds, their bodies soft and suctioned, their limbs folded. They let condensation gather on their backs, becoming indistinguishable from a dripping surface. When something suitable passes below, they drop or descend with terrible softness. Their prey is smothered, dragged upward, or held until it stops moving. The event is so quiet that the only sound may be a single displaced droplet falling into a pool.

Nothing in the basin lives alone.

Even the animals carry their own smaller lives. Shell-mites move over the grazers like pale grains, cleaning fungal growth and eating organic debris from the margins of plates. Thread-parasites lie coiled in the tubes of filter-feeders, stealing food when the crown is extended. Tiny commensals live in the gill spaces of larger water-crawlers, feeding on waste and sheltering from predators. Some creatures lay their eggs on the backs of slow-moving animals because movement keeps the eggs aerated and safe from still-water decay. Others lay them inside mineral crusts, attaching them to charged surfaces where microbial-rich water continually washes over them. A single square of wet stone can contain several generations, several appetites, several quiet strategies of survival.

The machines are not separate from this. They have become organs of the living basin.

The pump that turns at intervals gives the hollow a pulse. Animals time their feeding to its rhythm. When the pump groans into motion and water shudders through the pipes, grazers emerge from crevices to catch fresh film stirred from the walls. Tube-dwellers extend their crowns. Long channel hunters move toward the outflows. When the pump stops, the water settles, the mist thickens, and the smaller fliers rise to take advantage of the changed humidity. The basin has learned the machine’s habits, or the machine’s habits have become part of the basin’s body.

Warm vents and condenser stacks are nurseries. Eggs are laid there in protected seams, where heat keeps development steady and moisture never fails. The young that hatch are pale, soft, and numerous. Many die. The survivors find surfaces to graze, cracks to hide in, currents to ride. Some never leave the warm vicinity of their birth. Others move outward along rails, cables, and gutter lines, following mineral tastes and electric gradients until they find their own territories.

Old electrified rails are migration paths. Creatures too small to cross open stone follow them because the metal carries warmth, charge, and microbial growth. Droplets leap from wire to stone when a field strengthens, and blind water-crawlers orient themselves by that trembling. A transformer flickering with blue light can trigger brief reproductive events: eggs released into warm water, swarms rising from pipes, surface-skaters spinning in tight circles. The electricity is not commanded by any visible engineer. It behaves like weather. The animals treat it as such.

At the center of the basin, where the black pool is deepest and the machinery below is most alive, there may be something that approaches a “whoever.”

It is not human. It is not a ruler. It is not even clear whether it should be spoken of as one body or as several bodies grown together over long use. The closest thing to a name would be something like the sump-keeper, though it keeps nothing intentionally.

It rests in the central pool, partly submerged, partly fused with the low engine mound that rises there like an altar of old iron and mineral crust. Its outer form is broad and domed, built from living tissue and borrowed materials: plates of mineralized shell, fragments of turbine housing, green-glazed ceramic worked into its back, copper-stained crusts, a ring of old pipe segments that have become part of its armor. From beneath this heavy dome extend pale limbs or feeding arms, folded most of the time against its body. They are not elegant. They are worn, strong, and precise. When they move, water curls around them in slow eddies.

Its face, if the front can be called a face, is eyeless and broad. It is a landscape of sensory pits, soft papillae, and wet grooves that taste heat, pressure, salt, charge, and the chemical signatures of living things. It does not watch in the way of hunting animals. It registers. It knows when the pump changes rhythm. It knows when a storm of condensation is about to fall from the upper pipes. It knows when too many bodies have gathered near the warm outflow and when a predator has moved into a channel it has not occupied before.

It breathes, or circulates water through itself, by means of internal passages that may once have been part of the machine around it. Water enters through gaps in its crust, passes through gill-like laces furred with symbiotic bacteria, and exits in slow plumes. Those plumes feed the pool’s microbial films. The faint blue-green glow around the central pool comes partly from its breath, partly from the bacteria living in its flesh. When it exhales, the surface of the pool trembles. When it inhales, small fliers lift away from the water as if pulled by a thought.
