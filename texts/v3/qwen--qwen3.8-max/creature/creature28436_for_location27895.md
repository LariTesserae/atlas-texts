What lives there first is not a creature with a clear edge.

It is the thin life of the stone itself: the rust-colored films that darken warm ledges, the white mineral crusts that bloom in brittle lace along the waterline, the slick translucent smears in the shallows, the faint orange stains that mark where microbial generations have lived and died in the same narrow place for longer than memory. These are the oldest inhabitants. They do not stand. They do not reach. They spread, thicken, thin, crack, heal, and spread again. They feed on warmth, dissolved stone, and the slow chemistry of water. In a place with no green life, they are the nearest thing to pasture, to soil, to living ground.

They are also the first food.

On them, and within them, live the smaller mouths of the basin. Most are so quiet that the eye must learn to accept them as movement at all. There are soft-bodied grazers no larger than a fingertip, flattened against wet rock, rasping bacterial film with a patience that seems almost mineral. There are pale water beetles, their shells the color of wet bone, resting just beneath the surface of still pools. They do not dart. They do not swarm. They hold themselves in place, then move one leg, then another, as if testing whether motion is necessary. In the darker basins there may be blind shrimp-like creatures, translucent and slow, with a faint rust line in their bodies where they have fed on microbial matter. They live in the deepest pockets, where the water is warmest and least disturbed. One might sit for hours and see only one of them shift.

Nothing here lives in abundance. There are no nests, no burrows, no heaps of bones or shells. The animal life is sparse and restrained, tucked into crevices, hidden under mineral skins, surviving in numbers just large enough to continue. If the basin has a body, these tiny creatures are its rarest thoughts.

There are also lives that are neither plant nor animal in any simple sense. The white crusts that seem almost fungal are not quite fungal; they are mineral accretions threaded through by living filaments. Some of the cords left by human hands may have begun as such organisms: twisted strands of fiber or sinew colonized by moisture-loving growths, then stiffened by mineral deposition until they became something between tool and stone. Even where they look dead, they are not entirely dead. In that wet warmth, the boundary between living, dwelling, and becoming stone is soft.

But if you ask who lives there in the sense of having hands, intention, and memory, then the answer begins with the people who tend the basin.

They are not numerous. They could not be. The place would not accept many. At most, a few of them exist there at one time; often there is only one. They are sometimes called the Wet-Handed by those who know of them, though they do not use that name themselves. Among themselves they have no grand title. Their identity is not spoken loudly. It is shown in posture: knees lowered, palms open, head inclined toward the sound of water.

They are human, or near enough that the difference is hard to press. Their bodies have been shaped by the basin’s conditions. They are warm-skinned, not pale and chilled as cave-dwellers are often imagined, but softened by constant moisture and gentle heat. Their skin smells of salt and warm stone. Their fingertips are ridged, stained with rust and white mineral residue, toughened by stone yet softened by water. Their knees are calloused. Their eyes are wide and patient, accustomed to dimness, able to follow the faint shine of moisture across basalt. They blink slowly. They move without splashing.

Their hair is kept short or bound close with mineral-stiffened cords, because loose strands would shed into the water and because the air is too damp for anything to remain uncluttered. They wear wraps of layered hide or felted fungal fiber, darkened by moisture until everything they own takes on the basin’s color: brown-black, rust-brown, gray, dull bone-white.
