What lives there is not a crowd. It is a set of attendances.

The basin is inhabited first by things too small to be called inhabitants in the ordinary sense: films, threads, pellicles, living surfaces. They exist in the hairline channels beneath the stone-like ground, in the matte skins of towers, in the seams where conduits glow faintly, in the mineral darkening that appears on shaded panels after a cycle of condensation. They are not quite microbial and not quite mechanical. They eat charge and mineral residue; they make surfaces conductive or insulating as needed; they heal micro-fractures by depositing crystalline lace; they turn bare new stone into something that can be lived on. They are the city’s first digestion and its oldest memory, even though the city is young. When the air smells of wet stone and faint ozone, that is partly their work: a clean, active stillness, the scent of matter being quietly taught how to persist.

Above this unseen layer live the plants, and they are not mere decoration. They are civic bodies.

The broad-leafed climbers that ascend the towers are slow, deliberate creatures. Their overlapping fans are not arranged only to catch light but to catch moisture, to intercept the tremble of condensation before it falls. The metallic veins in some leaves carry tiny currents, or perhaps they carry signals; when a droplet strikes one, the whole vine may adjust by a fraction of its breadth, redirecting water along a chosen path. The mosses are even more patient. They thicken along seams and shaded corners like living weatherstripping, absorbing drip before it can erode new stone, holding it until the city decides whether to drink, store, or release it. The fernlike growths are sensitive. Their glossy fronds do not merely curl when touched by air; they test the air, tasting humidity, temperature, and whatever subtle charge the filaments overhead leave behind.

The reed-thin trees with pale trunks and dark canopies are the basin’s vertical citizens. Their roots spread over sharp rock in smooth muscular nets, not forcing the stone apart yet, but accepting its angles and binding them. They stabilize the young terrain without softening it. Under their canopies the air is greener, thicker, cooler. In certain cycles, when the dim light deepens and the moisture rises, those trees exhale a very fine mist of spores or luminous motes, and for a while the thickets seem filled with slow-moving stars. It may be reproduction. It may be communication. It may be prayer, if prayer is understood as a communal breathing without words.

The flowers are perhaps the clearest sign that the vegetation here has entered into negotiation with the city. The pale-mouthed blossoms open only when condensation gathers. They do not open for insects, or not primarily. They open for water. Droplets fall into them, pass through hollow stems, and descend into hidden reservoirs beneath terraces or inside tower bases. Some of these flowers are soft and organic; others have edges with the precision of machined valves. A few close slowly when approached, not from fear but from a change in local humidity. Others turn toward passersby, as if curious, or as if reading the heat and vapor a living body leaves in the air.

Animals are rare, but they are exact.

In the temporary pools live the glass eels, if eels is even the right word. They are thin, translucent, smoky, and slow. Their bodies seem less like flesh than like blown mineral, flexible yet brittle-looking, as though a hard tap might crack them into bright fragments. Inside them, faint threads brighten and dim in response to the city’s deeper currents. They move through shallow water with an almost ceremonial deliberation, feeding on mineral films, stray charges, and perhaps the residue of reflections. They do not school. Sometimes two will lie side by side in a dark pool, motionless for so long that they seem to be part of the basin’s wiring. When a pool begins to dry, they do not panic. They sink into the remaining damp, flatten themselves, and enter a state more like storage than sleep. If water returns, they unfold again. If it does not, they may remain as slender glassy knots in the sediment until some future wetness calls them back.

These pool-creatures are also recorders, or something near recorders. When one bends over a pool where they have been living, the reflection may not match the present exactly. A tower appears with one more seam than it has now. A walkway shows itself at a slightly different angle. A garden seems fuller, or emptier, or arranged according to a plan not yet visible. The eels may be eating old images. They may be excreting them. It is hard to say. The water does not treat memory as a solid thing here.

On warm surfaces near condensation vents rest the flat-bodied moth-analogues. They look almost inert when folded: wet-paper wings pressed close, patterns resembling circuitry, lichen, or the fine cracks in aged ceramic. They do not flutter idly. They wait until the air reaches saturation, and then they lift, not in swarms but in sparse, silent spirals, moving between vents, leaves, and high filaments. They may pollinate the condensation flowers. They may spread the luminous motes. They may also serve as mobile sensors, their wing-patterns reading changes in heat and charge. When they feed, they extend slender mouthparts into beads of water and remain there, trembling faintly, until the droplet is gone. Then they fold themselves again and become part of the surface.

The predation is brief and clean. A pale shape may dart from a pool’s edge and take one of the resting winged things. A ripple crosses the basin. A leaf trembles. The event is over before it can become drama. Stillness returns, not because nothing happened, but because the place absorbs happening without exaggerating it.

High above, among the towers and filaments, glide the darker shapes. They are rare enough that one might doubt them until seen. They do not flap. They do not call. They have no visible plumage, only broad, folded planes like membranes of dark leaf or thin architectural sheeting. They ride thermal currents and the subtle upward drifts released by warm structures. From below, they can look like shadows detached from their sources. They appear, cross a portion of the dim sky, and vanish—not always by flying away, but sometimes by becoming transparent, or by entering a fold in distance where the eye refuses to follow.

What they are is uncertain. They may be predators. They may be guardians. They may be instruments that have gone feral, or animals that have become instruments, or something that never needed the distinction. They seem to observe more than hunt. When one passes overhead, the vegetation does not scatter in alarm; it merely becomes quieter. The moths lower themselves. The pool eels dim. Even the city’s faint pulses seem to slow for a moment, as if acknowledging a senior presence.

Between the plants, the water, and the architecture move the tenders.

They are the most visible of the basin’s caretakers, though even they do not obtrude. Some are tall and narrow, with surfaces like warm ceramic and limbs jointed in ways that suggest both insect and conduit. Others are lower, broader, more root-like, moving on several soft limbs or sliding along prepared channels. Their faces, if they can be called faces, are smooth or lightly recessed, with dark glassy panels where eyes might be, or no eyes at all. They do not need to see as ordinary creatures see. They sense humidity, pressure, charge, surface tension, the minute complaints of new materials settling into shape.

The tenders emerge from doorways that were not noticeable until they open. They step onto terraces, pause, and then begin to work. One may touch a seam in the ground, causing it to widen just enough to let trapped moisture escape. Another may prune a climber with a delicate pinch, not cutting so much as persuading a stem to retract. A third may stand motionless beneath a line of condensation, letting droplets strike its shoulders and run into reservoirs in its torso, later to be carried to a dry bed or a thirsty root mat. They do not hurry. They do not converse in sound. When they coordinate, it is through tiny taps, shifts in surface temperature, or brief releases of vapor. Sometimes two tenders meet at a junction, touch forelimbs, and exchange something too subtle to name; then one turns away while the other remains, as if a decision has been made.

They are not servants. They are more like mobile organs of the basin itself. They may be generated by the city when a need persists long enough. They may be grown from the same strata that form the engineered ground: fibrous mats, crystalline sheets, metallic lattices, and porous stone-like masses given temporary autonomy. When a tender is damaged, it does not bleed. It becomes still, then returns to a wall or a low platform where filaments descend and integrate it again. In some cases, a damaged tender is not repaired but reassigned: its ceramic shell becomes a housing for moss, its limbs become supports for a climbing vine, its interior becomes a nest for moth-larvae or a reservoir for condensation. The boundary between living body and civic furniture is not treated as sacred here. It is treated as weather.

And then there are those one might call persons, though the word has changed in this place.

They are few. They do not crowd the basin. They move alone, or in twos, or in small groups spaced far enough apart that each still seems solitary even when together. Their bodies are humanoid but not strictly human. They are adapted to the warm damp air: broad, quiet faces; large eyes that gather the dim amber light; skin with a faint sheen, not wet but capable of exchanging moisture through pores that seem almost engineered. Some have fine lines across their arms or throats, like leaf veins or circuit traces, and these lines darken slightly when the humidity rises. Their hair, when they have it, tends to be dark, soft, and slow-moving, as if suspended in a heavier medium than air. They wear garments that are part textile, part living tissue: woven fibers, mossy panels, thin ceramic plates that warm with the body and help regulate vapor loss. Their clothing does not rustle. Nothing about them rustles.

These persons do not dominate the landscape. They inhabit it as careful guests who have been granted long tenure. They live in the towers, but not every tower is occupied. Some towers may house only one person across many levels. Others are shared by two or three who rarely speak but often move in coordination, tending gardens, checking reservoirs, or standing for long periods on balconies while condensation gathers on their sleeves. The interiors of their dwellings are stranger than their exteriors. Rooms extend farther than the tower’s shell suggests. Stairways fold in ways that should not fit. Some chambers are warm and bare; others are filled with hanging roots, shallow water channels, or beds of moss that serve as places of rest. The inhabitants do not seem disturbed by impossible geometry. They use it the way ordinary people use corridors and doors.

Their voices are low and sparing. When they speak, sound is softened almost immediately by the air, as if the basin prefers not to carry words too far. Much of their communication happens without voice: hand movements near water surfaces, taps on panels, alterations in the pattern of condensation, the deliberate placement of a leaf or stone. They can stand beside a pool and conduct a long exchange merely by disturbing its reflection in tiny ways, then reading the way the water answers. They do not argue loudly. They do not need to. Their society seems built around restraint, attention, and the assumption that most things will reveal themselves if waited for.

They are gardeners, but not in the sense of imposing form. They negotiate. They walk geometric beds and decide which root may cross a conduit, which flower may keep a droplet, which terrace should be allowed to darken with moss. They listen to the city’s low pulses and interpret them. When a distant seam opens with its soft decision-sound, one of them may turn toward it without surprise, as if hearing a name called from another room. Sometimes they carry small vessels of water from one basin to another, not because the city cannot move water, but because the act seems ceremonial. The carrying of water may be their oldest rite. It is hard to tell whether they maintain the city or the city maintains them. Perhaps both statements are incomplete.

They are not lonely. That is important. The sparseness of their numbers does not read as loss. The empty plazas, the broad avenues, the terraces without buildings, the walkways crossing open ground—all of these are not scars of abandonment. They are held capacity. The few who live there seem to inhabit the space with the confidence of people who know that emptiness can be a form of readiness. They do not fill every silence. They do not occupy every surface. They allow the basin to remain large around them.

There are moments when their presence becomes especially clear.

One may see a solitary figure kneeling beside a new pool after a brief rain. The figure does not drink. It places one hand near the water without breaking the surface, as if feeling the temperature of the reflection. The pool shows a version of the nearby tower slightly different in shape. The figure watches this for a long time, then touches its own throat where the vein-like lines darken. Later, it traces a pattern on a plaza surface with moisture from its sleeve. The pattern lasts only until the next thermal shift, then fades. The figure seems satisfied.

Elsewhere, two inhabitants stand beneath a tower where condensation drips in a rhythm too regular to be natural. They listen. One raises a hand when the rhythm stops. The other nods. They wait. The rhythm does not resume. They leave, not disappointed, as if a conversation has simply ended for the day.

Near a thicket, a child-sized figure may appear, though it is difficult to be certain whether it is a child, a smaller adult, or something made to be small for reasons no longer explained. It moves among the reed-trees with easy familiarity. When the fernlike fronds curl toward it, it curls its fingers in response. It does not laugh.
