Whatever lives in the Low Ward of the Pump-Yards has first agreed to be wet. Not merely to endure damp, but to be made of it in part, to think in terms of seepage, pressure, and long patience. In the flooded lanes where black water lies finger-deep over worn basalt, life begins not as bodies but as skins: living films of green-black algae and iron-loving bacteria that coat setts, chains, and the lower courses of glazed brick. They eat rust and exhale a lacquer that keeps the metal from wasting away too quickly. Under every hanging bead of condensation, minute animalcules turn in slow spirals; when the bead falls, they are flung into another pool and become part of its taste.

From these films come the small, visible lives. Valve-spiders with copper-bright legs spin webs of non-corrosive thread between handrails and along pipe galleries; their webs thicken before a leak, and the wardens read them like weather. Lamp-moths, their wings like oiled parchment, circle the caged lamps and drink warmth from the wire cages without burning themselves. In the channels move black eels with blunt heads and bodies like flexible iron, passing through pipe-mouths to clean out sludge, leaving polished surfaces behind. Sluice-crabs carry fragments of ceramic insulator on their backs as if they were holy shards. Brick oysters cling to engine plinths, filtering steam-flecked water until their shells become glazed with a dark, metallic sheen. Condenser frogs sit on warm valves, their translucent throats pulsing in time with the engines; when an engine falters, they stop first, and that silence is often the only warning anyone needs.

Then come the green lives, though green is not always their color. Broad-leaved marsh plants grow in open courts, their leaves so large and glossy that they seem to collect the air itself. Their roots descend from pipe galleries in slow curtains, not as invaders but as invited guests, drinking condensation, holding loose tiles in place, and, in the rare dry spells, releasing stored moisture back into the basin. Mosses grow where lamps burn longest, feeding on warmth as much as light. Shelf fungi climb blackened timber in tiers, soft and porous, some pale as wet bone, others dark as engine scale. In the reed banks beyond the levees, coarse grasses and sedges lean inward, listening to the pumps. Nothing in the Low Ward grows merely for itself; every root, leaf, and spore has entered the same long negotiation with water.

Above these smaller lives move the people of the basin, if people is still the right word. They are few, no more than might fill one long hall, yet they are enough to give the place its order. They call themselves the Low Wardens, though that name sounds too formal for creatures so quiet and damp. They are the pump-born, the sluice-tended, those who have lived long enough in saturated air to have been changed by it.

Their bodies are lean and sure-footed, shaped by balance on wet stone and narrow walkways. Their skin has a faint green-bronze cast, not unhealthy but steeped, like tea left too long in the cup. Their hair lies close and dark, slicked by moisture even when they have just come from a dry room. Their eyes are large and reflective, made for the greenish half-light, and they blink slowly, as if every surface might suddenly shine. Between their fingers is a faint fullness, not quite webbing, but enough to make them strong swimmers. Their lungs are accustomed to air so thick that outsiders would feel drowned breathing it; they can also hold their breath for long stretches, moving through flooded passages with their eyes open and their mouths shut.

They dress for wetness as other people dress for winter. Their coats are waxed canvas or glazed leather, dark with use and shining at the elbows and cuffs. They wear slit-soled boots that shed water, brass-toed and softly heeled, so that they make little noise on stone. Their belts carry valve keys, oil horns, listening rods, small lamps, and folded cloths kept as dry as possible. Copper buckles, glass beads, and little ceramic charms are common, not from vanity alone but because bright, hard things resist the damp. In colder seasons, if cold can be said to exist there at all, they wrap themselves in layered wool that smells of smoke, oil, and marsh mint.

They move without haste. Hurrying in the Low Ward is a sign of failure, because failure means a broken gate, a seized valve, a flooded hall. Instead they glide along raised walkways, step from dry stone to dry stone, pole narrow boats through black streets, or stand motionless beside sluices, listening. Their faces are calm, almost sleepy, until something changes in the sound of water. Then they become precise and immediate, like surgeons or musicians.

Their homes are built above the condensation line whenever possible, in lofts and upper galleries inside the larger structures. These rooms are dry by effort and art, not by nature. Floors are heated by bricks warmed from engine flues. Walls are lined with absorbent mats, glazed tiles, and woven reed panels that can be removed and dried. Small stoves burn marsh peat and rendered fats, giving off a low, steady heat. Beds are raised platforms of timber and tile, covered with layers of felt and waxed cloth. In every home there is a lamp, a water jar, a tool rack, and a listening place where one can press an ear to the wall and hear the basin speak.

Children in the Low Ward begin their lives near water. They are born in the cistern courts, in shallow heated pools where their first breaths are taken in air already thick with steam. They are buoyed and supported until their limbs learn the basin’s balance. They do not splash when they play; splashing is waste, alarm, or folly. Instead they learn to make ripples, to read the rings spreading across black water, to understand that every touch sends a message through the whole ward. Their games involve drip-codes, valve-turning, and the patient pursuit of lamp-moths. They are taught early that water is not an enemy to be defeated but a neighbor with whom one shares walls.

Food comes from the agreement as well. The Wardens cultivate mussels and brick oysters on pump plinths, harvest eels from the channels, and grow pale tubers in warm silt beds. They raise marsh herbs on roof ledges and cultivate fungi in dark galleries where the air is thickest. Algae is pressed into green-black cakes and dried over engine heat. They brew tea from reed roots, condenser water, and a little mineral salt. On rare days when the air dries enough, they hang fish, cloth, and fungal strips from the pipe galleries, and the whole ward smells of smoke, brine, and warm metal. Such days are remembered for seasons.

Their work is not labor in the ordinary sense but attendance. Each Warden holds a station, though stations shift with need and temperament. There are sluice-tenders who stand at the rows of gates and read the pressure in the groaning timber. There are root-cutters who prune the hanging roots before they crack a joint, yet never cut too much, because roots also keep the galleries cool. There are lamp-trimmers who tend the caged lights, polishing glass and replacing wire until each halo burns true. There are gauge-listeners who spend whole nights with their ears against pipes, distinguishing a healthy hum from the hollow knock of cavitation. There are oilers, scribes, condensation-sweepers, tile-washers, and those who keep the transformer sheds free of creeping damp. No one is master of the ward; at most, one is master of a gate, a lamp, or a particular sound.

Their language reflects the place. Speech among them is low, with many pauses and soft clicks, as if words were valves being tested. Much is conveyed without voice: a finger trailed through condensation, a knock on iron, a lamp blinked three times, a ripple sent across standing water. They have dozens of terms for damp, for temperature, for the taste of water, and for the sound a pump makes just before it fails. They can say, in a phrase no louder than breath, that the third sluice is weeping iron, or that the south gallery is breathing too fast, or that the lamps are drinking too much oil because the moths are restless. Their poetry, if they have any, is mostly composed of lists: names of leaks, names of weather, names of water as it moves through different materials.

They are not cheerful in the way of dryland peoples, nor are they gloomy. They are settled. They have the calm of those who know exactly what their world requires. They do not fear flood; flood is ordinary. They fear drought, because drought means cracked seals, warped timber, silent engines, and the death of the basin.
