The soundscape is enormous, layered, and continuous: a bass floor of wind moving through silica-stiff grasses; above it, the dry ticking of capacitor reeds knocking their charged tips together; beneath that, the slow grinding of furnace grazers chewing moss into heat. There are clicks, valve-deep and metallic, made by beetles opening breathing pores in the root-felt. There are long blue cracks of static when fog touches a reed bank. There are hollow moans from the drifting filter-feeders, rising as if the sky itself were being drawn through a reed. Nothing screams. Nothing races. Even alarm here is expressed as a change in voltage, a shift in odor, a sudden alteration in the pattern of breath.

What lives there lives as a component.

The first inhabitants are not walkers but surfaces that have become alive. The cushion mosses, black lichens, pale crusts, tight rosettes, and hair-fine grasses are not background. They are the plant-floor, the living pavement, the first machine shop. They hold water, temper frost, capture light, and make the initial charge. They are so dense that an animal stepping on them does not touch stone but a compressed community: fibers, rootlets, fungal threads, water films, dormant spores, and microscopic predators packed into a handspan of cold ground. A square handspan of this ground may contain more active industry than a factory floor.

These low plants do not merely survive the climate; they regulate it. Mosses store meltwater and release it slowly, keeping the air near them humid enough for microbial life. Dark lichens absorb sunlight until thin halos of vapor rise from their surfaces in the morning. Rosettes cup warmth in their centers, protecting tiny pools and the larvae that develop inside them. The translucent sedges bend light into their stems and glow faintly at dusk, not because they are beautiful, but because their tissues are full of moving fluids and mineral salts. The grasses, stiff with silica and threaded with conductive veins, turn wind into voltage. Their roots bind the plateau into a single breathing skin.

In the damp hollows, microbial films are the metallurgists. They are the oldest workers and the least visible. Copper-green skins stretch over shallow water. Iron-red threads creep along mineral cracks. Pale sulfur crusts form around vents where subsurface gases escape in slow bubbles. These films do not merely coat the stone; they change it. They precipitate metals, fix nitrogen, digest mineral dust, produce acids, exude sugars, and lay down sheens that later become food, wiring, or shell. Where they flourish, the ground smells faintly of wet metal, cold ozone, and green sap. They are the founders of conductivity. Without them, the land would still be alive, but not awake.

Insects are the fine tools of the plateau. They come in clouds so dense that they alter the light, especially over basins where moisture gathers. Pale high-altitude flies drift in bright veils. Glass-winged midges rise and fall in static-laced columns. Armored beetles with dull brass shells crawl through the root-felt, carrying spores and conductive dust on their backs. Moths with scales that hold static charge brush over reed tips and leave behind brief sparks. Some of these insects are pollinators. Some are scavengers. Some are parasites. Some are so small that they are less like animals than like weather: living particulate, moving between plant and air and soil.

The larger beetles function as valves. They are broad, patient creatures, their shells scarred by frost and mineral grit. They move through the dense root mats, locating breathing pores in the soil and opening or closing them with their mandibles and forelimbs. They aerate microbial layers. They circulate gases trapped below the vegetation. They prevent pockets of fermentation from becoming stagnant, and they keep oxygen moving through the dense underground felt. Their work produces a low percussive beat, not loud but pervasive, a rhythm that can be felt through the soles of the feet. At dusk, many of them seal themselves into holes, becoming living plugs against the cold. At dawn, they unlock the ground again.

The furnace grazers are the most visible engines of the highland. They are massive, slow, broad-bodied herbivores, thick-furred and deep-chested, their limbs columnar, their feet padded with living felt that presses seeds into the ground as they walk. Their fur grows in overlapping weather-courses, shedding rain and rime, trapping air, hosting mosses, lichens, mites, and nesting insects. Their nostril-flaps warm thin air before it reaches their lungs. Dorsal slits open and close with their breathing, venting excess heat in thick white plumes. In the early morning, when the plateau is locked in frost, they look like stones breathing. By afternoon, when dark stone has grown hot under the sun, they stand in shallow shade and steam gently from their shoulders.

They eat the compressed vegetation of the basin floors: cushion moss, metallic grass, antifreeze-rich rosettes, and the tougher stems that grow along conductive seams. Their mouths are broad and grinding, their teeth replaceable, their jaws patient. The sound of their chewing is deep and rhythmic, like slow machinery turning under load.
