In that hollow live the Unthirsty.

Not one creature, not one keeper, not one hidden god crouched among the tiles. The place is tenanted by a congregation: a close, warm, slow commonwealth of lives that have accepted the law of the hollow and made it into flesh, shell, leaf, fiber, resin, and habit. Some are rooted. Some walk. Some climb. Some swim through dust. Some seem only half alive, or alive in more than one place, or alive because they are continually being repaired. If you ask who lives there, the answer keeps dividing and reassembling, like the mosaic underfoot.

The first inhabitants are the plants, if “plants” is even the right word for them. They are not green things merely tolerated by the architecture; they are residents. They are the oldest citizens and the most numerous. They fill the seams and shelves, spill from the vessels, braid over the walls, and press themselves into every crack where powdered brick, ash, wax, salt, shell, and shredded fiber have been laid down as soil. They do not ask for rain. They do not remember rain. Their bodies are built against loss: fat leaves sealed with wax, furred stems that hold warmth, rosettes that open only when the air is already kind, hanging ropes of jointed stem that store oil in each bead. Some are green, but many are not: gray-green, olive, bronze, dusty silver, pale as old paper, dark as fired clay. Their roots are fine and patient, threading into cords and tile joints, binding the constructed ground, stitching the hollow together as they grow.

Among them are the wax-rosettes, low and tight, their leaves thick and closed like small fists. In the warmest hours they loosen by degrees, not to release moisture but to expose a faintly shining interior where resin gathers in slow amber points. There are salt-cushions, finer than moss, mineral-threaded, springing from copper pins and hairline cracks in clay; they look like tiny tufts of gray-green fur and seem to feed on dust and the slow chemistry of metal. There are resin-vines that climb the lashed reed walls in dense ropes, their stems knotted with translucent beads that swell over long intervals and never fall until they are ready. There are oil-pods, warm to the touch, faintly luminous when the light enters them at the right angle, filled not with juice but with slow golden oils that move like thought. There are fan-leaves broad as open hands, dry on both faces, giving off a resinous scent when brushed; they do not breathe out moisture, only warmth and a faint medicinal sweetness. There are hollow reeds with jointed stems, each joint sealed by a thin membrane of wax, and in those joints small creatures nest or hide.

These plant-lives are not passive. They are slow, but they choose. They lean toward the places where the lattice admits pale light. They send roots into joints that need binding. They cover walls that have begun to crack, not by accident but as if answering a need. They drop leaves that crisp into dry flakes and are carried away by beetles and ants, becoming substrate again. When one rosette dies, it does not rot; it stiffens, lightens, opens, and becomes a little shelter for seeds or insects until it is gradually taken apart grain by grain. The plants of the hollow are both garden and gardener. They are alive, and they are also tools of maintenance. They are the first builders after the builders.

The second great tenantry is the insect-kind, though that word is too simple for them too. They are small, quiet, and exquisitely fitted to the place. The pottery beetles are among the most visible: low-bodied, deliberate creatures with shells like fragments of fired clay, each one marked by hairline crazing or a dull glaze of age. They move along ledges with almost no sound, their legs precise, their claws making only the faintest tick when they cross a tile. They feed on dead fiber, shed leaf-husks, cracked resin, and the dry remains of smaller lives. Their eggs are laid in seams of powdered brick, and when the larvae hatch they are pale and slow, already shaped like tiny artisans, eating and packing and smoothing with their bodies until they harden into adults. When a pottery beetle dies, its shell often remains for a long while, indistinguishable from a shard, until another beetle carries it away or it becomes part of a wall.

There are parchment moths that rest beneath leaves in clusters, their wings dry and thin as old skin. They do not flutter wildly; they open and close their wings in tiny increments, as if counting the warmth. They feed on resin-dust, pollen, and the fine particulate that hangs in the hollow’s air. When they move together, they make a sound like pages turning in an empty room, except there are no pages and no room, only leaves and wings. Their bodies are so light that when they die they do not fall heavily; they settle, and often remain attached to the underside of a leaf until they are slowly disassembled by ants or mites.

There are resin-feeders, small and glossy, no larger than a thumb-joint, whose entire lives revolve around the amber beads wept by certain vines. They do not pierce the plants violently. They wait until a bead has swollen enough to loosen, then gather it with their mouthparts and carry it to crevices where it is stored, shared, or smeared as a sealant. They smell faintly of heated amber and crushed leaves. Their bodies are sticky only with resin, never with wetness, and where they walk they leave tiny shining traces that harden into protective films over cracks.

The tile ants are everywhere, though never in disorderly swarms. They are the hollow’s patient laborers. They carry grains of powdered brick, flecks of copper, salt crystals, fragments of shell, and dust from seedcases. They do not merely collect; they place. They line tiny tunnels with wax. They bind loose fibers with resin. They cultivate the mineral-threaded cushions by pressing them into cracks where they will hold. Their bodies are dry and hard, their movements economical. If a joint in the wall loosens, the tile ants arrive over many hours, not as an emergency but as a routine of the place. They may not know they are repairing. They may simply be following hunger or instinct. But the effect is repair.

The thread spiders are unlike ordinary spiders. Their webs are not sticky. They are fibrous, fine, and almost textile, more like stitching than snaring. They weave between stems and pegs, making small hammocks where seeds can lodge without falling, tiny bridges for ants, veils that catch dust without trapping living things. Some of their webs are so regular they look like mending done with thread. A spider itself may sit at the center, motionless, its body flat and pale as a chip of bone. It does not kill with venom in any visible way; it waits, listens, and when a torn leaf or cracked cord needs binding, it moves with astonishing delicacy and sews the place shut.

There are lizards, or things very like lizards, though their bodies belong more to the hollow than to any ordinary reptile. They are thin, matte-scaled, and still. Their scales have the texture of painted clay: not glossy, not wet-looking, but smooth and slightly chalky, as if each one had been fired and then worn by handling. They watch from cracks and arches, heads tilted, eyes like tiny beads of dark resin. They eat resin-feeders, moths, and the smaller beetles. They do not drink. They take what they need from the bodies of their prey and from warm oils licked from seed capsules. When they move, they do so in brief, precise intervals, then freeze again. Sometimes one seems to be a painted detail on a wall until its eye shifts.

The small warm-blooded things, if one can call them that, live mostly in the upper tangle where the vegetation is thickest. They are no larger than a hand, some furred, some covered in feather-like filaments that are not quite feathers. Their bodies are round and compact, their movements quick but careful. They eat seeds, resin-flakes, oily grains, and the soft interiors of certain pods. They nest in hollow stems, in dried seedcases, in woven pockets made by thread spiders, in the abandoned shells of pottery beetles. Their eyes are bright and dry, without any shine of moisture. They groom one another with small, deliberate motions. They do not sing. A few make low, breathy notes: not melodies, but signals. One note means the air has shifted. Another means a seedcase has opened. Another means a stranger is near. They never cry out. Sound is too precious for that.

Then there are the dust-swimmers.

They are among the strangest lives in the hollow, and perhaps the most difficult to describe truthfully. They are slender, narrow-bodied, unhurried. They are not fish, yet the mind reaches for that word because they move through fine dust, pollen, and floating resin-motes as though the air were a thick medium capable of bearing them. Their bodies are smooth, their fringes long and delicate, their fins or fin-like membranes almost transparent. They do not fly. They do not fall. They swim. When they pass through a shaft of filtered light, they stir tiny trails of particulate brightness that remain for a moment before closing. They feed, if they feed at all, on floating dust, resin-spores, and perhaps on light itself, though that may be only a way of saying they gather where the light is warm. They make no sound. When they turn, the dust turns with them. When they vanish, the air forgets them. Some people looking into the hollow might swear they saw nothing but motes moving by themselves. Others would see a dozen such bodies gliding between the leaves, patient as fish in a shallow pool that is not a pool.

There are larger lives too, but few, and they remain mostly hidden. In the densest braid of vegetation, where a wall becomes a green shoulder and a stair becomes a cascade of stems, something broad and slow sometimes shifts. It may be a creature, or it may be a living mound. It has a back like a terrace, plated with old tile, bound by roots, cushioned by mineral-threaded growth. When it moves, it moves with the patience of furniture becoming landscape. It may be a tortoise-like being, or an enormous beetle, or a thing with no name outside the hollow. It eats fallen seedcases, dry husks, and the powder that gathers in basins. It sheds plates that later become part of the floor. Its breath is warm and resinous. It does not seem dangerous. It seems necessary. Around it, smaller animals rest as if it were a warm wall. If it has eyes, they are not often open. If it has thoughts, they are slow enough to be mistaken for the thoughts of the place itself.

But if one insists on asking who lives there—who, not merely what—the answer becomes more delicate.

There are figures, or appearances of figures, in the hollow. They are not easily seen, and perhaps they are not truly separate individuals. They are small: no larger than a hand, sometimes no larger than a finger. They move among the tiles and cords with a careful, practiced grace. Their bodies seem made of the place itself: lashed fiber, smoothed clay, fragments of beetle-shell, polished bone, thin copper, living cushions of mineral-threaded growth. Their faces, if they have faces, are often smooth masks with two resin-dark eyes and no mouth, or only a shallow groove where a mouth might be. They do not speak. They do not need to.
