What lives in the Weeping Yard is not, at first, a single creature. It is a plurality, a damp assembly, a parliament of small lives arranged around the needs of water.

The birds are the most visible residents. They keep to the ledges, the pipe-mouths, the upper cavities where the clay is driest and the air moves least. They are not showy birds. Their feathers are dark and close, slicked with oil until light slides off them in dull green and blackish gleams. They have strong feet and long toes, made for gripping wet lips and narrow rims, and they move with the careful, jerky attentiveness of creatures who know that every surface can betray them. Some are rail-like and pale, almost colorless in the dimness, as if the Yard has bleached them by degrees. Others are small and quick, nesting in the mouths of dry channels, dropping out sideways when startled, wings cracking open with a sound like wet cloth snapping in a closed room.

They do not sing in any ordinary sense. They cluck, rasp, whistle through flattened beaks, make soft hollow noises in their throats when unsettled. When the air shifts, their calls enter the clay pipes and are changed there, so that a bird’s simple cry may return as a low note from somewhere near the floor. Over time, the birds have learned this. They pause after calling, as if listening to the Yard answer. They are not afraid of the sound. They are part of it.

Their droppings streak the lower walls and are not allowed to remain. The water is routed over them, guided by old channels worn smooth, carrying the white and grey deposits into troughs where algae and duckweed make use of them. The birds know the rhythm of this washing. They shift when the float-valves knock. They lift their heads when a sluice opens. They roost where the keepers have permitted roosting, not because they are trained, but because the place has made other places inconvenient. Nests in the main spillways are removed. Nests in the lesser pipe mouths are tolerated. The birds accept this arrangement with the unthinking patience of animals who have never known another world.

One predator lives among them, though it is easy to miss. It might be called a heron, if one is willing to stretch the word. It is too still, too angular, too much a product of the Yard’s own shadows. Its legs are like blackened cords. Its neck folds in a way that seems mechanical until it strikes. Its feathers are the color of wet shell-ash and old rope. It stands for hours in a corner where two channels meet, and mist gathers on its back without seeming to disturb it. The smaller birds go quiet when it moves. The frogs vanish with soft clicks. The keeper does not drive it away, because it takes the weak fish, the sick frogs, the too-bold insects, and in doing so keeps the Yard from tipping into excess. It is not loved. It is necessary. When it stretches its wings, the air seems to thin around them, and even the dripping appears to pause.

Below the birds, the wetter lives begin.

In the deeper gutters, where water collects long enough to darken, there are eels or things so like eels that the difference hardly matters. They move in slow knots, their bodies thick with muscle and slick with mucus, tasting the water with their whole length. They are black-brown along the back, pale underneath, and when they turn in the channels they seem less like separate animals than like the Yard’s own sediment becoming briefly alive. They feed on organic films, on insect eggs, on the fine waste that gathers in low places. Their passage cleans what their mouths cannot reach. Sometimes two or three will lie tangled in a basin trap, moving so slowly that they appear to be carved from wet clay. Then a drip falls nearby, and they unravel all at once, sliding away into the dark with a sound like fingers drawing over glazed tile.

Frogs sit at the basin edges with the patience of small clerks. They are not brightly colored. They are grey-green, mottled, their bellies faintly luminous in the weak light. Their throats swell and subside as though they are breathing the condensation directly. They watch insects with round, wet eyes, and when they leap it is sudden and precise, a soft smack of tongue against air followed by the click of their landing. If something larger passes near, they fold themselves into the water and disappear, leaving only a ring of ripples spreading against the ceramic lip. Their voices, when they come, are low and rasping, not unpleasant, and in the cooler hours they join the drip-rhythm so completely that it becomes difficult to tell which sounds are animal and which are architectural.

Insects are everywhere, though rarely in great clouds. The Yard does not permit excess for long. Midges rise from damp corners in thin smoke-like threads. Beetles with flattened bodies work through the silt traps and under the edges of mats. Larvae wriggle in shallow films of water, their movements tiny and ceaseless. Moths with dust-colored wings gather near the lamps, and the lamps are kept so dim that the moths seem more like drifting ash than living things. The insects are not a plague; they are a crop, a low-level food, a quickening in the margins. Birds take them. Frogs take them. Fish take them when they fall into the channels. Even the spiders, living in the driest upper angles, take their share. Those spiders are pale and deliberate, their webs beaded with moisture, each strand tuned to the faintest movement of air. They repair their work after every disturbance, and the keeper does not trouble them unless a web threatens to catch where a rope must run.

Among the silt and broken shell, pale crabs move sideways through the traps. They are small, flat-shelled, and quiet except for the faint ticking of their claws against clay. They eat what other lives leave behind: shed skin, dead larvae, fragments of algae, the softening remains of things that have slipped into the channels and failed to become anything else. They are the Yard’s meticulous undertakers. Their carapaces look as if they were made from the same shell-mortar as the walls, and when they freeze, they are almost indistinguishable from the built surfaces. Sometimes a bird will watch one for a long moment and then decide against it. Sometimes a keeper will lift a crab out of a valve housing with careful fingers and set it back where it belongs. There is no cruelty in this. There is only placement.

Even the green things live there under strict conditions.

Moss grows in shallow clay collars where it is allowed to hold a little moisture without spreading. Liverwort darkens the joints between tiles. Reed-like plants stand in ceramic pots, their roots confined, their stems bent toward the weak light with a persistence that looks almost like longing. In one long trough there is a dull green skin of duckweed or something very like it, divided and skimmed by hand so that it never chokes the water beneath. These plants are not ornamental. They drink, filter, stabilize, and feed. They are also kept humble. No root is allowed to pry. No stem is allowed to thicken into wood. No fallen leaf is allowed to rot into earth. The Yard refuses soil with the same quiet firmness with which it refuses silence. Every green tenant lives in a vessel, a seam, or a controlled dampness. If a plant forgets this, it is cut back. If it persists, it is removed.

And then there is the keeper.

If a person lives in the Weeping Yard—and one may live there, or may have lived there, or may be only the remembered shape of all the hands that have maintained it—that person is not a resident in the ordinary sense. They do not inhabit the Yard as a house. They inhabit it as a duty inhabits a body.

The keeper is slight rather than large, not from starvation but because the place dislikes bulk. The air is cool, mineral, close; it preserves and thins at once. Their skin is pale at the fingertips and creased from damp. Their sleeves are tied back or worn smooth at the wrists. Their garments are layered, oiled at the shoulders, darkened by water in patches that never fully dry. They may wear a hood or a cap to keep condensation from their neck. Their shoes are soft-soled and quiet, made to grip planks rather than splash across wet tile. They move along the raised walkways with a careful, rolling gait, never hurried unless the sound of the Yard demands haste. Their face is hard to remember afterward, except for the eyes, which have grown accustomed to the grey-green light and can find meaning in a trembling drop.

They carry little: a hooked pole for clearing runnels, a stone knife for cutting weed from pot lips, a small clay lamp with a shuttered flame, a length of spare rope coiled at the belt, sometimes a wooden float or a green bronze pin wrapped in oiled cloth. Their hands are their finest tools. They touch walls with the flat of the palm not for reassurance, but to read the surface. A proper chill means condensation is forming. A dry patch means a vent may be wrong. A faint warmth means too much heat has entered, whether from body, lamp, or weather beyond the screens. The keeper knows these differences the way a musician knows strings.

They wake to changes in sound. A drip that has quickened by half a rhythm. A pipe that has begun to whistle in a key that belongs to another part of the Yard. The knock of a float-valve rising too soon. The sudden silence of birds when something has moved below the roosts. These things pull them out of sleep more surely than light does. Their bed is a raised alcove, dry as the Yard allows anything to be dry, lined with wicker, oiled hide, and worn planks. They do not sleep deeply. They sleep like a person tending a slow fire, which is perhaps what they are doing, except that the fire here is cold, and it is water.

Their work is endless but not violent. They scrape channels. They rinse traps. They move a nest that has begun too near a spillway. They skim duckweed and divide it. They rub oil into gut-skin panels until the light remains dim but even. They check rope grooves for fraying, test counterweights, ease stiff levers with patience rather than force. When a clay lip cracks, they patch it with shell-mix and tarred cord, and the patch becomes part of the Yard’s skin. They do not hide repairs. They do not pretend the place is untouched. Their labor is visible in the same way that age is visible on a face: not as damage, but as history.
