Who lives there is not easy to count, because not everything that lives there has a single body, a fixed address, or a continuous memory. The first thing to understand is that the settlement itself is the oldest inhabitant. The green-extending plain, the root-felt, the mycelial threads, the trained trunks, the compressed leaf-streets, the breathing frond-fields, the fog-combs, the cisterns, the sap-pipes, the half-living engines: all of these make up a distributed civic organism. It does not possess a face in any ordinary sense. It has pressures instead of moods, growth-habits instead of intentions, seasons instead of thoughts. Yet it is aware enough to notice when a district thickens, when a boiler is underfed, when a pool has begun reflecting the wrong sky. The smaller lives within it are not separate from it in the way citizens are separate from a city. They are closer to organs, or habits, or recurring decisions.

The people — if “people” is the right word, and the survey offices use it with many caveats — are sparse, patient, and cool in temperament. They are not numerous. The settlement does not support crowds, and they do not desire them. They are tall in some districts, low and broad in others, depending on the growth-line they come from, but most share a highland economy of movement: unhurried, deliberate, sparing of heat. Their skin tends toward muted tones: olive, sage, ash-brown, gray-green, sometimes a dark mossy black where weather has worked it. In certain families there is a faint resinous sheen at the temples or along the knuckles, as if the body has learned to seal itself against damp. Their hair is often fine and fibrous, sometimes bound close with strips of leaf-cloth, sometimes worn long enough to collect mist. Their eyes are accustomed to thin light and deceptive distance. They blink slowly. They look at far things as if they might be near, and near things as if they might be far, because in this place it is wise to do so.

They dress in layers made from the materials around them: leaf-laminate cured until it is supple, moss-felt pressed into warm linings, bark-cloth hardened for boots and shoulder guards, thin resin panels that shine like dull amber. Metal is rare and treasured. A brass clasp, an iron hook, a copper ring greened at the edges may pass through many hands and many generations. Their clothing is not decorative in a lavish sense. It is repaired, pruned, re-oiled, re-woven. A person’s coat may have been alive once; a person’s boots may still smell faintly of sap. They carry tools as often as ornaments: condensation jars, pruning knives, pressure keys, folded survey-cords, small resin lamps, whistles carved from seed-cases. Many wear a bead of water at the throat or wrist, not as jewelry but as a practical reminder of where the next drink may come from.

They call themselves by different names depending on the work at hand. In census-trunks and municipal records they are often entered as the Maintained, because they believe that nothing remains itself without care. Among fog-combers they may be called the Combed, after the mist drawn through high fringes. In engine sheds they say the Fed. In pruning districts they say the Cut-Back. None of these names is grim. They are matter-of-fact. To be maintained is not to be passive. It is to be kept in relation. A person who is not maintained, they say, becomes a district of one, and that is dangerous.

Not all of them begin life in the same way. Some are born from human bodies, though even birth here has a botanical administration. A child may be carried first in a seed-cradle, a warm leaf-basket slung near a boiler vent where the air is humid and steady. Its first measurements are taken by grafters as well as midwives. Its name is spoken into a young trunk so that the tree will hold the sound in its rings. Other inhabitants are grown directly from civic stock: root-bodies trained for specific tasks, leaf-faced attendants germinated in municipal basins, pressure-tenders whose nerves are tuned to the hum of pipes. These are not considered lesser persons, though their lives may be shorter or stranger. They are assigned duties early and spoken to gently, because a grown person who is not spoken to may begin to believe it is only infrastructure.

Some are remembered into being. This is the hardest fact for outsiders to accept, but in the settlement it is treated with the same calm as a delayed train or a leaking roof. When a district thins at dusk, or when an old stair opens onto a room that was cut down decades ago, figures may appear there. They wear the shapes of earlier inhabitants, though their clothes are made of current leaf and their voices arrive with a slight delay, like sound through dense moss. They may knock on doors that no longer exist. They may sweep floors that have become lawns. They may stand at grooved stem-lines waiting for transport that has not run in centuries. The living do not flee from them. They note their direction, offer them water if they seem dry, and assign them temporary work if they seem able. Many of the Recalled fade when the district thickens again. Some persist for a season. A few are entered into the census under the category “returned” and given a pruning schedule, because even a memory can grow unruly if left untended.

Their homes are separated by wide intervals, and this spacing shapes their minds. A household may live inside a trained tree whose trunk has been thickened over generations into a tower of load-bearing wood. Windows are set in amber resin, not glass, and glow dully when lamps are lit inside. The walls breathe faintly in changes of pressure. The roof is scaled with overlapping seed-casings that click in high wind. Roots form stairs, railings, furniture, and the frames of beds. Inside, the air is warm and smells of resin, dried leaf, and engine oil carried in from a nearby shed. Outside, the nearest neighbor may be a kilometer away across a lawn of fine root-hair or a shallow basin of dark water. Children grow up understanding that emptiness is not absence. They learn to cross open ground without fear. They learn that a house seen from a window may take much longer to reach than it appears, and that this is not a reason for panic but a reason to leave early.

Family here is less a line of blood than a cluster of maintenance. A household may include born children, grown assistants, a remembered ancestor who appears only when the cistern overflows, a boiler that must be fed at dawn, and a section of living wall that has to be sung back into shape after storms. They do not find this confusing. They find it complete. Affection is shown through care: filling a water-trough, oiling a hinge before it cracks, pruning a doorway so it does not close too fast, standing watch during dormancy intervals. Their tenderness is quiet. They do not often embrace in the open, but they will touch the dampness on one another’s sleeves, or share the first bead of condensation from a high leaf, or press their palms together until their warmth equalizes.

Their work is divided by habit more than by caste, though some roles cling to certain families or districts. The fog-combers are among the most respected. They climb the high catch-fronds on ladders of braided cane or walk among them on long stilts rooted in soft ground. Their task is to keep the mist-catching surfaces clean and angled correctly. They comb the fringes with tools made of wood and bone, loosen condensed beads into channels, and watch for blockages caused by spore mats or insect silk. Their hands are often raw and cool. They wear oiled hoods and speak little, because their work requires attention to small sounds: the tick of a bead forming, the hush of fog arriving, the slight change in pitch when a frond is too heavy with water. When a fog-comber sees a pale moth, they stop and note the direction of its flight before returning to work.

Cistern-keepers live nearer the still pools and bead-tanks. They are patient people, often older, often marked by long exposure to damp. Their fingers are darkened and wrinkled, their voices low. They listen to water as others listen to engines. They know which cistern is safe to drink from and which has begun to hold another hour. They test pools with long ladles, by taste, by reflection, by the behavior of floating pollen. If a pool shows a sky from a different time, they mark it with a ring of white resin and forbid drinking until the water remembers its own weather. They also manage the overflow, directing surplus into seed-bowls, boiler feeds, or germination basins. In condensation festivals, they become almost ceremonial, moving from trough to trough with small cups, offering the first clear taste of the new dampness to each household.

Pruner-surveyors have the most unsettled work. They carry folding rules, knotted cords, brass compasses that are not always reliable, and leaf-panels on which they draw maps that must be revised constantly. They are called when a building has migrated, when a street has folded back on itself, when a stair arrives at the wrong level, or when a door has grown where none existed before. They do not try to force the landscape back into the old plan. They measure the new condition, judge whether it is stable, and either accept it or schedule a correction. Their tools include blades as much as instruments. They prune thresholds, cut back encroaching walls, and mark growth that has exceeded civic tolerance. They are calm in the face of impossibility because their profession begins from the assumption that the map is never finished. A good surveyor can look at a two-dimensional district at dusk and say whether it will thicken by morning or needs to be roped off until it recovers depth.

Engine-feeders live with heat and noise. Their skin is often scarred, patched with resin salve, darkened by oil and steam. They work among the thumping sap-engines, the boilers, the dynamos, the presses, the vats of fermenting green liquor. Their task is not merely mechanical. They feed the machines fiber, seed-cake, sap-residue, and sometimes carefully chosen cuttings from old growth. They oil belts, clear valves, tighten cables, and also pollinate intake chambers with soft brushes made from feathered leaves. Some engines respond to rhythm; feeders tap against their housings in set patterns to keep pressure steady. Some engines recognize individual feeders by heat and vibration, and will settle when a familiar hand rests on their flank. The feeders speak to them not because they believe the machines understand every word, but because speech regulates their own breathing in the noise. A well-fed engine thumps with a kind of satisfaction. A neglected engine whines in a way that can be heard across an entire district, and that is considered a public embarrassment.

Grafters are quieter and slower. They shape the built environment. They choose which trunks will become columns, which roots will become stairs, which canes will become bridges, which leaf-panels will be encouraged to harden into walls. They use iron pins and cables where necessary, but they know that metal is only a temporary suggestion. The plant will eventually decide.
