To ask who lives there is to change what one means by “who.”

The basin has no populace in the ordinary sense. It has no crowds, no households, no named residents, no footpaths worn by daily commuting, no doors closing against the night. If a traveler stood at the rim and called down, nothing would answer with a voice. If they descended into the damp green streets, they would feel at once that the place is occupied—but occupied in the way a bell is occupied by sound, or a dry channel by the memory of water.

What lives there lives thinly, patiently, and mostly in intervals.

The first inhabitants are the mosses.

They are less individuals than communities, less plants than weathered green assemblies. They cover the terraces in dense felts, black-green when wet, duller and tighter when dry. In the lowest quarter, where water gathers and the stone stays dark, they become deep and spongy, holding rain and cloud-moisture in their bodies long after the sky has cleared. They live by retention. They take what arrives—mist, melt, dew, brief rain—and keep it close. Their lives are spread out in small stems and leaf-thin surfaces, each mat a slow reservoir.

When the cold comes, they stiffen. Frost enters them and turns their edges brittle. They do not die from this; they wait. They become quiet, glassy, reduced. Then moisture returns, and they swell again. Their green darkens. Their tissues soften. They resume. This is their way of living: not by speed, but by revival. They are citizens of interruption, at home in a place where water comes and goes and warmth never stays long enough to become generous.

Under the mosses, and among them, live the lichens.

If the mosses are the basin’s soft flesh, the lichens are its mineral skin. They live on stone that seems too bare to support anything. They crust over pale surfaces in ash and bone-white, in jade and rust, in sulfur and gray. Some grow as flat circles, some as brittle ridges, some as rosettes with edges like torn paper, some as tiny branching forms that resemble coral or dried nerve-ends. They are slow to a degree that makes patience look like ambition. A lichen may spend years widening by less than the breadth of a fingernail. Its life is a chemical negotiation with rock, air, light, and cold.

The lichens are builders without tools. They bind dust, etch surfaces, trap mineral grains, and leave behind crusts that become foundations. They live on columns and ledges, in plazas where rain pools briefly, along the worn lips of hollow chambers. Some are alive only at their edges, old dead centers still clinging to stone. When fragments break away and fall, they make a faint clicking sound, like small dry bones striking the floor. They are not decorative. They are the city’s oldest masons.

Among the lichens and mosses grow the cushion plants.

They live as tight green domes, firm to the touch, stuffed with their own old growth. Their centers hold warmth a little longer than the open stone. They collect moisture, dust, dead leaves, and tiny corpses. Inside a single cushion plant there may be an entire micro-world: a springtail sheltering from wind, a mite moving through damp threads, a seed lodged and waiting, a fungal filament working through decay. These plants are little cities inside the city. They do not spread carelessly. They compress. They endure by keeping their interior life close, by becoming dense, round, and conservative. They look humble, but they are survivors with a strategy.

Where the channels carry intermittent water, sedges live.

They bend in pale tufts, their roots finding seams in the stone. They are not lush in the lowland sense; they are lean and resilient. Their leaves are thin, their colors muted, their growth careful. They live where moisture pauses, where a thread of meltwater may darken a groove for an hour and then vanish. They take advantage of those brief deliveries. Their roots help open tiny fractures, and their dead leaves fall into cracks, becoming the beginnings of something darker and softer than stone. They are among the basin’s quiet engineers.

In the most protected niches, ferns live.

They are miniature forests, no taller than a hand, unfurling in shaded corners where condensation gathers and the wind cannot reach them. Their fronds are delicate but not tender. They grow where the air stays damper, where overhangs shield them from frost, where the stone gives back a little of the day’s cold light. They make the basin feel older still, because ferns carry an ancient shape with them. They do not belong to a garden; they belong to persistence.

And then, in severe little bursts, the flowering plants live there.

Their colors are not soft. They are dark violet, dull gold, almost-black red. They bloom close to the stone, as if trying to stay below the notice of the wind. Their flowers are small, intense, and temporary. They open when conditions allow, receive whatever visits—midge, moth, cold air, accident—and set seed in the narrow spaces between rocks. Their beauty is not ornamental. It is a risky act. To flower in such a place is to spend saved warmth, saved moisture, and stored years in a single brief gesture. The flowers do not make the basin gentle. They make its austerity visible.

The structures themselves are lived in, though they are not all alive.

The hollow columns, the curved walls, the ramp-like terraces, the tube-shaped growths—these are built by generations of organisms and physical processes. Some surfaces still carry living crusts; others are dead mineral scaffolding. Yet even the dead parts are inhabited. Lichens colonize their outer faces. Mosses settle where moisture collects. Roots enter cracks. Frost opens them further. Spores lodge in hollows. Invertebrates hide inside empty tubes. The columns are like old towers whose walls have become habitat long after their first purpose has passed. They are not monuments. They are slow accumulations that have become shelter.

Inside these structures live the smallest animals.

They are rare, hidden, and adapted to cold. Springtails move through the moss like tiny disturbances, appearing when dampness returns and vanishing when the surface dries. They are among the basin’s most ordinary signs of animal life, though ordinary here still means scarce. They feed on decay, fungal threads, algae, and the fine organic matter that collects in moss. Their leaps, when they make them, are little shocks in the stillness.

Midges gather where water beads or pools. They are slight, quick, and easily forgotten. They do not fill the air in swarms; they appear in thin numbers, enough to mark the presence of moisture. In the brief periods when rock cups fill and channels darken, their larvae may live in the cold films, feeding on microscopic growth. When the water disappears, the basin seems empty of them again. Their lives are fitted to interruption.

Spiders live in the damp crevices.

They are pale, patient, and small. They do not build grand webs across the city; they make modest snares in sheltered corners, between lichen ridges, beneath overhangs, inside hollow stems and cracked chambers. They wait. They take a midge, a springtail, whatever small life passes. Their existence is one of stillness and suddenness. They are among the basin’s few predators, and their presence makes the tiny world complete. Without them, the damp places would be merely crowded with hidden smallness; with them, there is balance, risk, and hunger.

Mites live under lichen and in cushion plants.

They are almost invisible, yet they are important. They move through the minute spaces where larger animals cannot fit, feeding on decay, fungal matter, and microscopic organic fragments. They help turn death into soil, though soil here is not a broad brown bed but a thin dark accumulation in cracks. Their lives are spent in the city’s seams. They are residents of the in-between.

In the brief wet places, other hidden lives may exist: tardigrades in water films, rotifers in rock basins, nematodes in damp organic pockets. They live in conditions that would seem too severe for life, and they do so by being small, tough, and capable of waiting. When the basin dries, some of them contract into endurance. When moisture returns, they resume. They are not dramatic. They are almost beyond notice. But they belong to the place’s deep logic: life here survives by pausing as much as by moving.

A moth may pass through.

It comes on a warmer day, if warmth can be called what barely raises the stone above chill. Its wings are dull and rapid, not showy. It searches for the small severe flowers, or perhaps only for shelter from wind. It does not stay long. It is a visitor more than a resident, a brief soft thing crossing the basin’s stone and air. Its presence makes the emptiness feel momentarily porous, as if the world outside had sent one small messenger through.

Birds may visit the rim.

A raven, perhaps, or a thin-voiced mountain fowl. They look down into the basin, hear its hollow noise, see its green terraces and pale columns, but they do not settle into it for long. It is too loud, too exposed, too poor in easy food. It offers structure and moisture, but not abundance. A bird may take a spider, pluck a midge from the air, inspect a damp crack, then leave. The basin is not a home for birds. It is a place crossed, a place noticed, a place where hunger may be briefly answered before the cold and sound drive the visitor away.

No people live there.

There is no fire, no hearth, no blanket worn smooth, no path compacted by repeated human steps. No hand has shaped the walls. No metal has cut the stone. No mind has imposed a plan from outside. If a person were to enter, they would be a guest, and an uncomfortable one. The basin does not invite occupation in the human sense. It has no door that could be shut, no roof that could be repaired, no field that could be sown. It is built, but not for our purposes. It is sheltered, but not comfortable. It is organized, but not governed.

If anything human belongs there, it is only attention.

The basin could be lived in by someone who had learned to be almost nothing: to move slowly, to make no tools, to leave no permanent mark, to listen more than speak, to accept cold as a constant companion. But even then, such a person would not be the place’s true inhabitant. They would be an anomaly, a temporary warm body among far older lives.

The larger inhabitants are not quite creatures.

Water lives there, though not as a river. It arrives in pulses. It beads on moss. It darkens seams. It fills shallow basins for an hour. It drips from overhangs. It runs in thin silver threads down the worn channels, then stops. It makes the city wake. When water comes, the basin changes sound, smell, and color. Moss swells. Lichens brighten. Channels become visible. Small animals emerge. For a little while, the place seems fuller. Then the water leaves, and the basin returns to dampness, waiting.

Frost lives there.

It enters at night and in shaded corners. It stiffens moss, glazes pools, tightens stone. It works by expansion and patience. It widens cracks by fractions. It lifts grains. It tests every hollow and joint. Frost is not evil in the basin, but it is severe. It keeps life compressed. It teaches every plant and creature that warmth is temporary and that survival means being able to endure interruption.

Cloud vapor lives there too, in its own way.

It spills over the high table and descends into the basin as gray moisture. It condenses on cold surfaces. It makes the walls weep without rain. It turns the city into a room of soft gray light and wet stone. During these times, the basin seems inhabited by weather itself. The columns disappear into mist. The terraces gleam. The air becomes close and intimate. Then the cloud lifts, the light returns, and the ordinary world reappears.

Wind lives there most loudly.

It is not alive in the fleshly sense, but it uses the basin as if it were a body. It drops into the hollows, passes through tubes, crosses cracked chambers, and turns emptiness into resonance. It moans in low throats of stone. It whistles through narrow grown pipes. It makes the columns sound like cold organs. Sometimes it swells until the whole basin seems to breathe. The loudness is not made by crowds. It is made by absence shaped into passages. Wind is the basin’s most audible resident because the basin has so few bodies to absorb it.

Sound lives there as a consequence.

It is not a creature, but it behaves like one. It moves through the streets, rises from hollows, answers itself from opposite walls, and fades into the high air. It gives the basin the feeling of being occupied. A listener could almost believe there are voices, processions, distant workers, hidden choirs. But there are only shapes and air. The sound is the city’s emptiness speaking. It is the proof that structure can be inhabited even when nothing with a face is present.

The dead also live there, in a sense.

Not as ghosts, but as material. Old growth becomes foundation. Dead lichen becomes crust. Dead moss becomes dark matter in cracks. Broken columns become shelters. Old roots leave channels. Former lives are not removed; they are incorporated. The basin does not discard its past. It grows over it. Every wall shows generations. Every hollow tube carries the memory of earlier accretions. The living do not replace the dead here; they build upon them, cling to them, take shelter in their remains.

What lives there, then, is a pattern.

It is a pattern of cold tolerance, intermittent water, slow growth, mineral patience, and resonance. It is a pattern made of moss mats and lichen crusts, cushion plants and sedges, tiny arthropods and rare visitors, hollow stone and moving air. It is not a society of individuals so much as an assembly of relationships. Moss holds water; water supports moss. Lichen prepares stone; dust lodges in lichen. Roots open cracks; frost widens them. Hollow columns catch wind; wind makes sound. Sound fills emptiness; emptiness preserves sound. Cold slows decay; decay feeds patience. Life does not conquer the basin. It endures within it.

The true citizens of the place are small economies.

A cushion plant saving warmth. A moss mat saving water. A lichen crust saving dust. A spider waiting in a damp crevice. A seed lodged in a crack. A pool lasting until noon. A flower opening for a single cold afternoon. A bird pausing at the rim. A cloud condensing on ancient stone. A frost film forming over a shallow basin. A hollow column turning wind into tone. These are the lives that belong there.

And perhaps, if one must give the basin a single inhabitant, it would be this:

Patience.

Not patience as a virtue, not patience as a feeling, but patience as a physical condition. The patience of stone worn to its final shape. The patience of moss returning after frost. The patience of lichen spreading by imperceptible degrees. The patience of water arriving only when it arrives. The patience of sound moving through empty chambers. The patience of a world that has finished becoming dramatic and now continues in detail.

That is what lives there.

Not a crowd. Not a ruler. Not a builder with hands. Not a beast with a den. Not a human with fire. But lives adapted to absence, lives made of holding on, lives that thrive by compression, by waiting, by reviving when conditions permit. The basin is inhabited by cold green persistence, by tiny hidden animals, by weather and resonance, by the slow civic order of processes that have no minds but still make form.

It is empty, but not uninhabited.

It is loud, but not voiced.

It is urban, but its citizens are not people.

What lives there lives as the final mountain still lives: quietly, anciently, in the small damp spaces between stone and sky.
