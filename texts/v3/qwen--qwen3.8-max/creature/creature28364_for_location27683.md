Nothing human lives there. Nothing green lives there. Nothing quick, soft, or wet lives there in any way that would reassure a terrestrial eye.

What lives there lives as load, as pressure, as slow conversion. The inhabitants of the plateau are not animals in the ordinary sense, though they may be called fauna because they persist, respond, maintain boundaries, and continue themselves through time. They are dry beings. Their bodies are mineral bodies: ceramic plate, metallic filament, crystalline lattice, conductive dust, sealed veins of ionic melt, membranes of charge. Their breath is not breath but the movement of anhydrous vapor through internal channels. Their warmth is not the warmth of blood but of controlled thermal gradients. Their weight is not merely physical; it is consequential. Where one of them exists, the surrounding kilometers become arranged around it.

They are few. That is the first fact one must hold. The plateau is not a swarm, not a colony, not a nest, not a hunting ground thick with tracks. It is almost empty. The distances between living beings are enormous. The air between them is thin, hot, and largely unused. A traveler could stand for hours or days and see nothing but salt-white flats, black glass, copper-stained ridges, and the hard ordinary light falling on all of it without mercy. Yet the emptiness is not absence. It is capacity. It is the silence inside a vast instrument. The beings that do exist are so large, so slow, so dense with process, that they fill the region with pressure, sound, and influence long before they fill it with body.

They do not move like animals. They move like weather deciding to become geology.

The largest of them can be mistaken for landforms. A low dark elevation on the far side of a basin may be one of them. It may seem to be a mesa with rounded shoulders, its surface weathered into ridges and plates. The eye accepts it as stone. Then something about its shadow changes. Not quickly. Not visibly in the way a limb moves. The change is more like a correction: the dark mass separates by a degree from the logic of the surrounding terrain. Its outline becomes too intentional. Its silence becomes too occupied. The observer realizes, with a coldness that has nothing to do with temperature, that the rise in the land is not a rise in the land.

These great beings are sometimes best understood as gradient-feeders. They do not graze on matter the way a herbivore grazes on grass. They graze on difference. Hot stone against cooler stone. Charged strata against discharged strata. A compressed mineral layer against a fractured void. A thermal seam beneath the plateau against a radiating surface above it. They place themselves across such differences, or perhaps they grow into such differences over centuries, and their bodies become the site where the difference is spent. Inside them, the expenditure is industrial without being mechanical in the made sense. There are expansions and compressions. There are grinding surfaces that are not gears but living mineral facets. There are conduits where superheated anhydrous vapor moves through chambers lined with crystalline tissue. There are slow electrochemical separations occurring along metallic filaments too fine to see from the ground. There are pressures that build for years and release with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat.

Their surfaces are plated in grown ceramic scales. These are not armor in any simple sense. They are metabolic surfaces, heat exchangers, electrical insulators, protective crusts, and sensory boundaries all at once. Over them lie ridges of deposited mineral: rust-red iron compounds, green copper salts, sulfurous yellows, thin films that shimmer with interference colors. In certain lights, these deposits make the creature look ancient beyond any possible age. In other lights, they make it look freshly precipitated, as though the plateau has just sweated it into being. Metallic threads run through the outer layers. When thermal currents shift inside the body, those threads pulse faintly, not with visible light so much as with a change in the air: a tightening, a prickle, a sense that the space around the creature has become electrically attentive.

The sounds from one of these beings are among the most overwhelming facts of the plateau. From a distance, one may hear nothing. Then, without a change in volume, the sound becomes bodily. It arrives in the chest before the ear accepts it. A slow piston-throb. A deep rotation of mineral against mineral. A pressure release like a vast dry sigh. A low electrical hum that makes the teeth ache and the small bones of the inner ear feel weighted. These sounds are not calls. They are not signals, at least not primarily. They are the noise of maintenance, of internal industry, of a living body converting heat into charge and charge into continuation. The plateau itself carries them badly and too well: a sound from a distant basin may press against the sternum as if produced inside the listener, while a nearby crack of cooling stone may recede into an impossible distance and continue there, growing older and stranger as it goes.

The motion of the great ones is almost beyond motion. A limb, if it can be called a limb, may take days to complete a shift. It may not lift so much as redistribute its own stress through the ground. A body may take a season to turn by an amount too small to notice, yet sufficient to alter the pattern of shadows around it. Footfalls are not steps but pressure fronts. When one of these beings changes posture, the ground several kilometers away may tremble once, faintly, as if remembering an earthquake that has not yet occurred. Dust rises without wind. Crystal fields give off brief, high sounds. Electrical films brighten on nearby rock faces. The light, which is already ordinary and severe, seems to sharpen, as though the region is increasing its own clarity around the event.

Their waste is not refuse in a simple sense. It is refined. They excrete bright pellets of metal that ring like struck bells when they settle on stone. They produce sheets of glass, sometimes thin and dark, sometimes pale and faintly conductive. They release fine alkaline powder that gathers in windless hollows. They expel crystalline structures that continue to vibrate long after being separated from the parent body. These expelled materials are not dead. They are not inert. They remain charged with pattern. Some become part of the substrate. Some are later incorporated into other beings. Some become the starting points for new forms of continuity. Nothing is discarded here, because nothing is merely waste. The plateau has no trash, no ruins, no abandoned products. It only has transformations.

The great gradient-feeders create their own local weather, though the word “weather” is almost wrong. There are no clouds. There is no rain. There is no moisture to gather, condense, or fall. Instead, their bodies produce heat-shimmer, static tension, and occasional silent glows in the air above their highest ridges. The air near them feels thickened, though it remains thin. It pulls at the skin. It raises fine hairs. It makes cloth crackle and metal objects feel awake. Sometimes a faint arc crosses from one ridge of the creature’s body to another, white and brief, so ordinary-looking that the eye accepts it as a fact of light before the mind protests. The thunder that follows may arrive late, early, or not at all. Sometimes it comes as a long metallic chord rising from below, as if the ground has decided to harmonize with the creature’s internal hum.

Other beings live more explicitly as parts of circuits. They are not builders of circuits. They do not place themselves with intention the way a technician places components. Yet their growth, orientation, and slow adjustments make the plateau legible, at moments, as an enormous living diagram. These are the terminal forms, the arc-keepers, the conductive spines that stand at nodes of potential difference. They may appear as tall, narrow interruptions on the horizon: dark shapes with long limbs or raised spines, their surfaces stained with copper and blackened by old discharges. They are not numerous. They may be separated by tens of kilometers. But when an electrostatic gradient becomes severe enough to seek release, they become relevant.

At such times, the terminal forms orient themselves. They do not rush. They do not gather in any emotional or social sense. They simply become aligned with the field. Their long dry limbs, their conductive ridges, their metallic filaments, and their raised crystalline spines complete pathways between charged regions of the land. The empty basin between them becomes the inside of a circuit. The silence becomes tense. The ordinary light seems to flatten against surfaces. Then the arc comes. It may cross the sky as a white line, brief and factual. It may leap from ground to spine, spine to spine, spine to distant body, then vanish. For an instant, the whole plateau appears as a machine whose components are alive. Then the moment closes. The thunder arrives wrong. The charge redistributes. The beings remain where they are, though their internal hums may change pitch for years afterward.

These terminal beings feed, if feeding is the word, on discharge itself. Not on the flash as spectacle, but on the sudden collapse of difference. Their bodies are layered with dielectric plates and conductive seams that can absorb, delay, divide, and store current. They may stand motionless for centuries, accumulating no visible nourishment, until a rare discharge passes through them. Afterward, they may shed thin flakes of burnt mineral, or their surfaces may bloom with new metallic films. Sometimes one of them will move a single limb a few degrees over the course of a decade, not toward food, but toward a more favorable relation with an unseen field. To watch one, if one could watch long enough, would be to watch a living instrument adjusting itself to a law that is local, inconsistent, and slowly shifting.

There are also grinding forms. They are lower and broader than the great basin beings, and they may be found on terraces, in shallow basins, or along the edges of glassy plains where fine mineral sediment collects. They resemble fields of exposed organs rather than animals. Their feeding structures are mineral disks, grown in layers, mounted on short stalks or embedded in broad ceramic bodies. These disks rotate almost imperceptibly. They polish, abrade, and separate grains of mineral dust. The action is not milling in the manufactured sense; it is digestion by surface, by vibration, by electrostatic attraction. The disks wear down and renew themselves. Their edges are not machined but secreted. Their motion is not driven by gears but by thermal stress, internal pressure, and the slow conversion of heat into mechanical repetition.

The sound around a grinding form is crystalline and relentless. Ticks. High faint rings. A continuous glassy whisper. Occasionally, one of the rotating disks will find a resonant frequency in the surrounding stone, and a nearby ridge will answer with a long clear tone. The creature, if it is one creature, feeds on the charged surfaces produced by abrasion. Freshly ground mineral carries potential. The grinding organs collect it, refine it, and incorporate it into the body’s interior chemistry. Waste is expelled as powder so fine it seems like dry smoke, or as tiny bright grains that settle into metallic lines along the ground. These lines are not roads. They are metabolic traces, old pathways of charge, the residue of feeding. Over long periods, such traces may guide other beings, not by scent or memory, but because they remain conductive, remain thermally distinct, remain usable.

Lesser fauna exist, though “lesser” does not mean small in any comforting sense. Some are thin sheets of moving charge that travel across the salt crust. They are almost invisible except by their effects: a shimmer on the surface, a lifting of fine dust, a crackle in the air, a brief brightening of metallic film. They are not solid bodies in the way a mammal is solid. They are bounded patterns of electrical life, perhaps supported by ionic layers in the crust, perhaps sustained by their own internal circulation of charge. They feed on static differentials. They may spread over a wide area and then contract suddenly when a gradient collapses, becoming for a moment a brighter line, then vanishing into the mineral skin of the plateau. They do not grow toward light. They respond to voltage, pressure, and thermal difference. If one of them passes near a foreign object, the object may become briefly charged: hair lifts, metal tingles, dry surfaces snap. They are not curious. They are not hostile. They are simply alive in a mode that has no tenderness.
