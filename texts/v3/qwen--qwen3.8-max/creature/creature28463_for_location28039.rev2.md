What lives there is not one thing.

The plateau refuses a single portrait. If you ask who lives in that high, cold, damp city-country, the first answer that comes to mind is human: people in thick hides, bent against wind, moving carefully over stone. But that answer is too small. The place is not mainly human in the way a lowland market town is human. It is inhabited in layers, in pores, in shelters, in routes, in stored heat and counted water. It is lived in by animals and birds, by insects and lichens, by ice and seepage, by custom and caution, by the memory of repairs. The city is less a container for one kind of life than a slow negotiation among many.

The people who live there are the ones who have learned to make a home out of shortage. They are not numerous, but they are everywhere in the sense that their labor has touched almost every surface. Their bodies are marked by the elevation: broad-ribbed, deep-lunged, economical in movement. They do not waste breath. They walk with a measured rhythm on broken stone, feet accustomed to uneven steps, loose scree, wet slabs, and sudden shade. Their hands are never soft. Knuckles crack in winter; fingers know rope, wool grease, stone dust, and the bite of cold metal when it is rare enough to be used. Their faces are wind-burnished, eyes narrowed against glare, skin weathered in patches where sun and frost have taken turns working on them.

They are not pale with delicacy nor dark with romance. They look used. They look attended to by hardship, not defeated by it. Their hair is tied back or covered against dust and cold. Their clothing is layered because weather in the city changes by meters and moments: a sun-warmed south wall, a fog-filled lane, a wind-cut pass, a shaded undercroft where ice persists through half the year. They wear felted wool, hides softened with fat, stitched panels that can be opened for work or sealed for sleep. Some have goggles or narrow eye-shields made of bone, wood, or thin plates of horn, because the light off fresh ice can be cruel. Their garments smell of lanolin, smoke, wet stone, and animal pens. Nothing about them is disposable. A torn sleeve is patched. A worn boot is re-soled. A rope is reused until it becomes a wick, a binding, then a fiber for chinking.

Their lives are organized around conservation. Heat, water, fuel, fodder, and shelter are too precious to be treated casually. They do not live in grand houses with many empty rooms. They live in thick-walled spaces where every niche has a purpose: a sleeping platform warmed by the body heat of animals on the other side of a stone partition, a hearth no larger than a basin, a rack of drying meat, a bundle of herbs, a jar of rendered fat, a coil of rope hung where it will not freeze into a useless knot. Their interiors are dim and close, not because they love darkness, but because openings are weaknesses against cold and wind. Light enters where it can be spared. Air is managed. Doors bend. Entrances recess. Hide curtains hang heavy. The smell of a dwelling is dense: wool, dung smoke, stone damp, milk, skin, and the faint sourness of closed warmth.

They are quiet people, but not silent. Their voices have learned the city’s volume. They speak without shouting because thin air, stone, and distance already eat noise. They have many words for things that might elsewhere be compressed into one: kinds of damp, kinds of frost, kinds of stone weakness, kinds of wind, kinds of cold. They distinguish the fog that merely passes from the fog that will bead and run. They know the difference between a crack that is old and dry and one that is new and listening. They can hear whether a cistern is filling by the pitch of its drip. They know when a herd is uneasy by the sound of hooves shifting faster than usual. Children learn these things before they learn many stories. A child here can tell you which gutter feeds which jar, which lane shelters from which wind, which stair is slick in thaw, which wall must not be leaned on after a hard freeze.

Their labor is patient and communal. No one builds alone because stone at this height punishes arrogance. Walls are raised by many hands, with ropes, levers, wedges, and long arguments about angle and drainage. Work is intermittent. A project may take years not because the people are idle, but because weather, herds, fuel, and hunger interrupt everything. They do not experience this as failure. They experience it as the shape of the world. A house is never finished; it is maintained. A cistern is never merely dug; it is deepened, cleaned, covered, inspected. A road is not a permanent line but a repeated act of clearing, stacking, and repair.

Certain people become known for particular attentions. There are those who watch stone. They go out after cold nights to inspect plaster seals on cliff faces, to listen for hollowness in benches, to note whether a scree field has shifted. They mark dangerous cracks with pigment or cord. They decide when a lower room must be abandoned, when a lane must be rerouted, when a watch-cairn needs to be moved back from a failing edge. There are those who watch water. They know the city’s tiny hydrology: which fog pans drink best, which roof channels clog, which shaded basin holds ice longest, which seep turns bitter after long dry spells. They clean gutters with bone tools and argue over the angle of a trough. There are those who watch herds, those who watch fuel stores, those who watch fodder, those who watch the weather signs along the plateau rim. No one rules everything. Authority is distributed like moisture: gathered where conditions allow, directed where need demands.

Their social world is dense but not crowded. Households know one another by sound and smell as much as by face. They know which neighbor burns dung with moss, which keeps a lamp too bright, which has a sick calf, which has a child old enough to run messages. Privacy is not solitude; it is the ability to keep your warmth, your water, and your obligations in order. Conflict exists, but it tends to be practical. A blocked herd lane is a serious matter. A wasted cistern is more than rudeness; it is harm. A fire left unguarded is a communal threat. Punishment is not theatrical. It is labor: repair the wall, re-cut the channel, carry fodder, rebuild the windbreak. Justice here is restoration because survival depends on function.

They have shrines, but their sacredness is modest. Wall hollows hold small objects: a white pebble, a knot of red cord, a carved bone bead, a scrap of wool from a favored animal, a fragment of old timber too precious to burn. They do not need grand temples. The plateau itself is overwhelming enough. They make small acknowledgments at the first heavy frost, at the return of certain birds, at the first reliable drip of a fog-gutter after drought, at the birth of calves. They may leave a drop of milk or a pinch of dried herbs at a pass before travel. They do not beg the mountain to be safe. They know it is not safe. They ask only to notice in time.

They are not joyless. Their pleasures are compressed and vivid. A patch of sun on a south-facing wall can make several people pause in the same place, not speaking, only letting warmth enter their shoulders. A brief thaw brings children out to watch water bead and run. A rare lowland caravan brings news, grain, fuel, soft metal, salt, and the smell of foreign hides. A festival, if they keep one, is not loud in the lowland sense. It is a day when more lamps burn than usual, when meat is cooked longer, when wool is dyed, when old songs are sung in voices kept deliberately low, when people visit distant kin across terraces and return before the cold tightens. Laughter exists, but it is often sudden, quick, and close to the body.

Their dead are handled with the same economy of care. There is little wood for pyres and little soil for graves in the usual way. The dead may be folded into stone, placed in dry niches, covered by cairns, or set in high crevices where frost and wind finish what flesh cannot refuse. Bones, when they are kept, are not ornaments of horror. They are continuity. A grandmother’s skill may live in the way a wall is fitted. A dead herder’s route may still be followed by animals. The city is layered with ancestors not as ghosts but as work: old floor levels, blocked doorways, tool marks under newer plaster, a cistern cut by hands no one living saw.

What lives there, then, is also the herds.

The great woolly beasts are citizens in all but name. They are not pets. They are not scenery. They are co-inhabitants whose bodies help make the city possible. Broad-hoofed, sure-footed, thick-coated, they move through outer lanes with slow certainty, their breath hanging in the cold. They know the sheltered routes. They know which walls block the worst wind. They know where the footing is stable and where a careless step will send scree sliding. In places, the architecture seems shaped around them: wide turns, low windbreaks, rubbing stones, pens set against human dwellings so heat can pass both ways.

The people depend on them for wool, milk, meat, hide, dung, bone, and warmth. But dependence is not one-way domination. The herds have moods, preferences, and memories. An old female can refuse a pass before the humans notice ice forming in its shadow. A young male can panic in a narrow lane and endanger walls, jars, and children. The herders’ work is therefore partly guidance and partly negotiation. They use voice, touch, salt, routine, and the pressure of walls more than force. They know that a frightened herd is a disaster. They know that an animal injured on broken stone may never walk properly again. They watch hooves, coats, eyes, dung, and the small tremors that mean cold is becoming too deep.

The animals’ dung is wealth. It is collected, dried, stacked, counted, traded, and protected. In a place where timber is scarce and fire cannot be fed casually, dung fuel is a form of stored summer. Children learn to recognize good drying cakes by weight and smell. Older people judge fuel stores the way others might judge grain. The smell of dung smoke is not unpleasant to them; it means warmth, cooking, and continuity. The animals also give the city its living heat. In winter, pens against houses make the difference between endurance and death. A dwelling without animal warmth is a hard dwelling. A household without access to a herd lane is poor in ways that have nothing to do with coin.

The herds also give the city its rhythm. At dawn they move outward to sparse grazing where the plateau offers it. At dusk they return, and the sound of their hooves on stone becomes one of the city’s deepest clocks. Their slow passage through lanes softens the architecture into use. Wool catches on rough edges. Muzzles press against palms. Calves stumble on new stone. Old animals stand with lowered heads while someone checks a sore or clears ice from their coats. Their eyes are not gentle in a sentimental way; they are patient, large, and indifferent to human fuss, which is perhaps why the people trust them.

What lives there includes the smaller lives in the city’s pores.

The walls are not dead stone. They are inhabited by diggers, nesters, hoarders, watchers, and thieves. Marmot-like creatures live in rubble banks and cavities under retaining walls. They emerge into weak sun and sit upright, alert, fat in the short season, vanishing at the first suggestion of frost. Their burrowing is watched carefully. Too much digging can loosen a foundation; too little means the stone is too cold, too dry, or too dangerous even for them. Their presence is a kind of report. If they move out, people notice.

Pika-like animals store dry vegetation in crevices. Their sharp calls cut across terraces and disappear. They are small, quick, and fiercely busy. Children sometimes imitate them in games, but adults leave their stores alone unless necessity is dire. To steal a pika’s winter cache is to admit that the city’s own stores have failed. Birds live in eaves, cairns, cliff niches, and the hollows of watch-towers. Pale raptors ride the uplift along the plateau rim, appearing almost motionless above the fog before dropping out of sight. Their presence often means carrion or a weak animal, and the herders read them accordingly. Dark swift-winged birds appear over melt pools, taking insects in fast passes. Ravens are constant. They inspect everything: newly stacked fuel, a forgotten strip of hide, a cracked cistern cover, a human argument. They are not sacred exactly, but they are respected. They notice what people overlook.

Small seed-eaters and insect-eaters gather around pens and stores. They are pests and companions at once. They eat what they can, but their numbers also tell where warmth and food are concentrated. In the short thaw, insects explode into being: flies over damp stone, beetles under slabs, midges near standing water, mosquitoes where melt lingers long enough. They are not loved, but they are expected. They feed birds, amphibians, and small mammals. They remind the city that even cold places have seasons of excess. When frost returns, the insect noise stops so suddenly that the silence feels newly made.

In cisterns and ponds that do not freeze solid, there are smaller lives still: larvae, crustaceans, cold-water invertebrates, and sometimes amphibians whose dark bodies seem made for absorbing weak light. These lives are not usually seen, but they matter. They keep water from becoming stagnant. They feed birds. They indicate whether a cistern is healthy. When people clean a tank, they know which creatures should be there and which should not. A cistern without small life is suspect. A cistern with too much death in it is cleaned immediately. Even the water is not imagined as pure emptiness. It is a medium with inhabitants.

What lives there includes the green world, small and stubborn.

Vegetation is not abundant, but it is not absent. It is simply slow. Lichens crust the rocks in black, gray, rust, and pale green, spreading over centuries. They are the first painters of the new stone. They make surfaces legible. People notice them because they mark stable rock, old surfaces, shaded damp, and the direction of prevailing weather. Moss gathers in seams where condensation lingers. It is soft where almost everything else is hard. It is protected where possible because it holds moisture and helps form the thin beginnings of soil. Cushion plants hug the ground, dense as stones, green only to those who kneel. Dwarf grasses and sedges appear in sheltered ledges, especially where animals have lain and enriched the ground.

Around drip-lines and cisterns, life becomes slightly richer. Algae darken stone. Low flowering plants appear in thaw. Root-tangled turf forms where water has been allowed to remain. These patches are important beyond their size. They are places where insects gather, where birds forage, where children learn the difference between useful green and careless green. The people do not garden in the lowland sense. They encourage. They clear debris. They redirect a drip. They protect a ledge from trampling. A flower here is not trivial. It is an event. It tells that the season has turned, that water has arrived, that something besides stone and cold is persisting.

There are also lives too small to see, but the people sense their effects. Films of microbial life bloom in summer melt. Rot works slowly in dung piles and stored fodder. Ice lenses are not sterile; they hold traces of organic change. Stone is altered by chemical patience. The city’s dampness is alive in that sense. Even where nothing is obvious, something is transforming mineral into soil, moisture into growth, waste into fuel, death into future use. The inhabitants do not speak of this in abstract terms. They know it as smell, as stain, as the difference between good compost and poison damp, between a healthy cistern and a sick one.

What lives there includes the city itself, if one allows a city to be a kind of body.

Not a body of flesh, but a body of habits and passages. It has circulation: herd lanes, stairways, causeways, gutters, channels, ramps, and the hidden veins of water moving from roof to jar to tank. It has organs of storage: cisterns, ice chambers, grain rooms, fuel stacks, wool caches, undercrofts, sealed chambers, pens. It has skin: walls, roofs, windbreaks, hide coverings, plastered faces, stone lids. It has senses: watchers on cairns, listeners at cliff faces, tenders of fog pans, children running messages, ravens on roof edges. It has memory: old floors, blocked doors, repaired cracks, tool marks, stories attached to particular stairs. It has wounds: rockfall scars, abandoned rooms, shifted terraces, places where the plateau has surprised it.

The city breathes, though not with lungs. Doors open and close. Hide flaps lift. Fog enters lanes and is guided into pans. Air moves through sheltered passages. Heat accumulates in sun-warmed stone and leaks away at night. The city drinks by drip and seep. It eats by storing dung, wool, milk, meat, grain, and fuel. It sheds stone, old mortar, worn ropes, broken slabs, and sometimes whole outer works. It is not eternal. It is not even stable in the way lowland cities imagine stability. It persists by being repeatedly made. Every generation repairs it into continued existence. If the people stopped maintaining it, the city would not simply decay; the plateau would begin taking it apart. Frost would open roofs. Scree would bury lanes. Meltwater would undercut walls. Wind would strip hides and scatter stores. The city lives because it is tended.

This is why the people do not see themselves as masters of the place. They are more like careful tenants of a much larger body. They live in it, but they also live under its conditions. The plateau sets the terms. It permits certain routes and denies others. It lets water gather here and not there. It makes one bench safe for three generations and then drops a block from the cliff above. It opens a fog bank and fills cisterns; it closes the sky with rime and shuts down travel. The people respond. They do not command.

There are also lives at the margins, lives that test the city’s edges. Thin predators may come in hard seasons, drawn by the smell of pens and the promise of weak animals. They are not often seen, but their signs are read: tracks in fresh snow, a disturbed cairn, a herd too nervous to graze. The city’s walls and watch-cairns exist partly because of them. The people do not hate these predators with the hatred of enemies. They fear them with the fear of neighbors who know what hunger can do. They defend their herds, but they also understand that the plateau supports lives beyond human convenience.

Lowerland visitors, when they come, live briefly in the city too. They arrive breathing hard, carrying fuel, grain, salt, soft metal, resin, cloth, or news from below. They often find the place immense and quiet in ways that unsettle them. They may talk too loudly at first, waste lamp oil, misjudge distances, or fail to hear the warning in a distant rockfall. The city teaches them quickly if they stay. They learn to move slower, to keep doors closed, to value a dry pair of boots, to understand that a cup of water is not a small thing. Some leave. Some remain. Those who remain usually do so because they have learned to love the severe clarity of the place: the blue shadows, the ordinary light, the satisfaction of a well-seated stone.

What lives there is also time, in a sense. Not time as an abstraction, but time as accumulated practice. The freeze-thaw cycle organizes days. The snowline moves and returns. Birds appear and disappear. Cisterns fill and empty. Wool is shed, gathered, twisted, and worn. Ice is harvested, stored, rationed, and missed. The city measures itself not by years alone but by events: the winter the eastern cistern cracked, the spring the herd lane was buried, the year the fog came late, the season when the ravens nested early, the generation that raised the long windbreak. Time lives in the bodies of the old, in the notches of water levels, in the layers of floors, in the repaired edges of tools.

Even sound lives there. The city’s soundscape is sparse but full of meaning. Wind over stone lips is not just weather; it tells which passages are open. The creak of a hide cover tells how cold has tightened it. A distant rockfall tells where danger has moved. A drip into clay tells whether fog has been generous. Hooves on gravel tell whether the herd is calm. A bird calling from a roof edge may mean insects are rising, or storm pressure is changing, or simply that the bird has claimed its place again. Voices, when they occur, are brief. They do not echo grandly. They are absorbed by wool, stone, fog, and distance. The city has learned to speak at the volume of its weather.

And what lives there is also the ordinary courage of continuing.

No one there is heroic in the way stories from warmer places imagine heroism. There are no engines, no conquests, no great machines defying the sky. The courage is in rising before light to check a frozen trough. In carrying a message across a high causeway when the wind has edges. In repairing a wall for the fourth time because the fifth repair will be needed by someone not yet born. In sharing fuel when stores are low. In knowing when to abandon a room and when to defend it. In listening after cold nights. In not building beneath every handsome overhang. In marking a crack and believing the mark will matter to someone later.

At dusk, the answer to who lives there becomes most visible. The sun falls behind the sharp plateau rim and the light turns blue. Doors close. Hide curtains are drawn. Animals press into pens. Lamps are fed sparingly. Cisterns tick. Fog gathers in lower lanes and moves through them like a slow pale animal. On roofs, watchers take one last look at the sky and the cliff faces. In sleeping spaces, children are pulled close to warmth. Old people listen to the house settle. Somewhere far off, a stone cracks and echoes, and everyone who hears it knows without being told: the plateau is still young, still shedding, still making itself.

What lives there, in the end, is a plurality of presences holding together against cold. Humans, herds, birds, small diggers, insects, lichens, mosses, water in hidden veins, stone in slow motion, customs repeated until they become instinct. The city is not occupied by a single species or a single image. It is inhabited by patience. It is inhabited by attention. It is inhabited by many small lives arranged around the fact that warmth is borrowed, water is counted, and the world is still new enough to fall.
