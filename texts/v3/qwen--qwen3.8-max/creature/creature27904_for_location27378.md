Predators such as bell-jackals hunt them in loose packs, using resonant lures—strange, almost musical phrases that imitate the recognition interval of a calf, the safe hollow-chord of a grazing circle, or the low distress note of a strider whose membrane has torn. When the lure finds its mark, a husk-cattle will lift its armored head and answer with a querying hum. That is often enough. The jackals do not bite first. They strike with sound: a coordinated burst of narrow-band pressure aimed at the joints and breathing valves of their prey, stunning it, folding its limbs beneath it, opening the armored plates along their oldest seams. Only then do they feed, dry-mouthed and precise, absorbing compressed nutrient pellets from the exposed tissue rather than tearing wet meat.

But to ask what lives on the High Phloem Terrace is to ask what lives inside an instrument that is also a body, a polity, and a slow administrative god.

The first inhabitant is the Terrace itself.

Not as a passive surface, not as a platform for lesser lives, but as the oldest and most distributed resident of its own expanse. The bark-plains, the hollow stems, the buried root-lattices, the seed-vaults, the capillary light-lines, the dormant spore-crusts: all of these are organs of a single vast condition of being. It does not think in swift, human sentences. It thinks in pressure gradients, acoustic memory, growth correction, and long electrical patience. When the towns sing, they are not merely communicating with one another. They are speaking inside a larger listening. When the wind moves through the pillar-husks, the Terrace does not merely resonate. It remembers.

It remembers everything that has ever been tuned into it.

That is why the settlements do not feel like colonies imposed upon a landscape. They feel like thoughts the landscape has learned to have.

The peoples of the Terrace—if “peoples” is still the right word—are many in form, but all share the same fundamental adaptation: they have passed beyond the old wet economies of life. They are not waterless in the way of stone or dust; they are waterless in the way of charged crystal, living wax, dielectric gel, and gas-lattice tissue. Their bodies do not sweat, do not salivate, do not weep in any familiar sense. Their interiors are ordered around solid-state ionics, fibrous musculature, and slow electric turgor. Where other creatures might have blood, they have mobile electrolyte lattices moving through capillary channels lined with insulating wax. Where other creatures might have lungs, many of them have resonant chambers, bellows-valves, and gas-filled bladders used both for buoyancy and sound production. Their skin is usually matte, layered, and faintly warm to the touch, more like cured leaf than mammal hide. Some have dermal plates. Some have fine electrostatic filaments instead of hair. Some have smooth, pale surfaces that flicker with subdermal light when they are thinking strongly.

They are not all human. Some began human, long ago, and were changed by the Terrace. Some were never human at all, but became persons through residence, resonance, and law. The oldest families do not trace descent by blood. They trace descent by interval: by the chord that first accepted their ancestors into the living substrate.

They call themselves many things. The Dry Chord. The Terrace-born. The Listeners. The People of the Standing Wave. Each town has its own local name, often untranslatable into spoken language because it is not a word but a pattern: a hand-shape, a footfall rhythm, a pressure-code, and a faint color pulse combined into a single identity. Outsiders who cannot produce the full pattern usually give such places simpler names: Stemwatch, Low Bell, Amber Vault, the Ninth Hollow, the City of Open Tines. But those are approximations. The true names are lived, not said.

Their bodies are shaped by sound as much as by sight.

Most have large, quiet eyes adapted to the dim particulate light, with broad pupils and reflective layers behind them. Many see polarization in the spore-haze and can read the faint electric weather moving across the sky. Their hearing, however, is not concentrated in simple ears. It is distributed through the skeleton. They feel bass through the soles of their feet, through the long bones of the legs, through the jaw, the sternum, and the hollow chambers of the chest. Some species or lineages have external tympanic membranes, but these are usually protected by latticed covers because open hearing can be dangerous during storms or resonance events. The most refined listeners can identify a town from the way its warning chords refract through the ground kilometers away. They can tell a predator by the pressure signature of its footsteps. They can feel a lie as a micro-dissonance in someone’s posture.

Because the Terrace is loud, speech as older worlds would recognize it is rare and often impractical.

Instead, the inhabitants use layered languages.

The everyday public language is hand-sign: precise, angular, rapid, visible even in dust haze. It is not merely visual; it carries rhythm. The hands move in beats, and the beats matter as much as the shapes. A slight delay can change a sentence from statement to request to legal claim.

The intimate language is touch-sign: patterns tapped against skin, rail, bone-mask, or the living wall. Lovers, kin, and long-standing work partners develop private tactile dialects. A mother can calm a child by tapping a familiar interval against the child’s sternum. A technician can ask a wall to open by touching a sequence into its surface. In some towns, public railings are dense with civic information, their surfaces humming faintly under the fingers of passersby.

The formal language is substrate-code: low-frequency vibrations sent through the ground, carried by root-lattice and callus roads, received by the feet and translated by trained bodies or architectural membranes. This is the language of law, weather warning, growth permits, boundary disputes, and inter-town diplomacy. It is slow, expensive, and difficult to falsify. A false message sent into the substrate can be detected because the Terrace itself will not sustain it cleanly. Dissonant codes decay strangely. They leave traces.

The deepest language is silence.

Not the absence of sound, but engineered quiet: pockets of expensive, maintained stillness used for meditation, judgment, surgery, mourning, and secret council. Silence is never casual. To create silence on the Terrace is to interrupt the living body of the place. It requires energy, permission, and repair afterward. A silent room is more sacred than a temple because it is more dangerous.

The inhabitants dress for resonance as much as for warmth.

Their garments are made from woven fiber, shed husk-plates, spore-felt, and thin sheets of living membrane that can be charged to change stiffness. Clothing often has acoustic function. Collars protect the throat during high-wind phases. Chest-plates help focus internal sound. Boots are layered with sensing soles that allow the wearer to read the ground without being overwhelmed by it. Many wear bone-conduction masks: smooth, carved faces made from seed-shell, mineralized bark, or polished callus. These masks do not hide identity so much as tune it. They carry vibration from the wearer’s skull to the air, or from the air into the wearer’s bones. Some masks are ceremonial, painted with dormant bioluminescent pigments that wake in dark phases. Some are utilitarian, scarred and repaired. Some are legal objects: a mask may carry the authority of a town, a guild, or an ancient office.

Children learn to wear masks before they learn to sign fluently. A first mask is a major event. It means the child has been judged stable enough to participate in the acoustic commons.

The towns are their greatest living art.

A settlement on the High Phloem Terrace is not built so much as negotiated. The inhabitants do not conquer the substrate. They persuade it. They select pathways, encourage callus growth, guide leaf-tile outward from stems, train root-stairs into spirals, and coax chambers open by applying the correct vibrations over seasons or generations. A house is not a separate object. It is a maintained indentation, a local agreement between person, town, and Terrace. Walls can become doors when addressed correctly. Floors can rise or lower according to need. Ceilings can open like pupils in response to light, sound, or chemical signal. Rooms can be grown larger when a family expands, or sealed into memory when a lineage ends.

The interiors are quiet, dry, and strangely tender.

Sleeping hollows are lined with insulating spore-felt that holds warmth without dampness. Tables and shelves are grown as smooth callus shelves, often curved to follow the anatomy of the body that uses them. Light comes from living veins: amber lines along walls, blue-green capillaries near work surfaces, slow red pulses where warning or mourning is required. There is no fire. Fire is feared more than flood, because uncontrolled oxidation would wound the Terrace and create acoustic scars. Where heat is needed, it is produced by focused light, by resonant friction, or by phase-shifting waxes embedded in walls.

Food is dry, concentrated, and often musical in preparation.

There are no orchards, no fields of grain, no wet fruits. Instead, there are cultivated crusts, charged spore-loaves, mineralized leaf wafers, compressed nutrient pellets, resin-sugars, and fibrous stalks that release electrolyte salts when chewed. Some foods are grown in shallow amphitheaters where sound can shape their texture. A loaf may be sung into density. A seed-cake may be tuned to release energy slowly during long maintenance shifts. Certain delicacies are not eaten so much as absorbed: small crystalline lozenges placed against the tongue or the inner wrist, where dielectric membranes take them in. Cooking is called tuning. A kitchen is a low-lit chamber full of resonant bowls, light-knots, drying racks, and seed-kernels used as gravitic stabilizers.

Markets are quiet to the ear but intense to the body.

They are held in amphitheaters or broad depressions where sound can be shaped without becoming destructive. Vendors do not shout. They display. They tap. They sign. They offer samples on vibrating trays. Goods include woven fiber, repaired masks, seed-rights, stored light, charge-knots, spore-pigments, gravitic anchors, memory-bark, legal harmonics, and contracted growth services. Wealth is not measured by accumulation alone but by stability: by how well one’s holdings remain in tune with the town. A rich person is someone whose presence reduces dissonance. A poor person is not necessarily one who lacks goods, but one whose frequencies have become unsupported, isolated, or unattended. Poverty on the Terrace can be acoustic as much as material.

The towns are governed by councils of specialists, but no one rules alone.

There are Tuners, who maintain the public chords. Root-readers, who interpret the predictive lattices beneath the ground. Valvewrights, who care for sphincter-locks and storm-seals. Husk-singers, who keep the great stems stable during wind. Seed-wardens, who protect vaults and genetic memory. Silence-keepers, who maintain the costly quiet chambers. Weavers, Loom-tenders, Callus-shapers, Light-knotters, Spore-scribes, and Bridge-trainers. Some offices are elected by hand-sign. Some are inherited by interval. Some are assigned by the substrate itself, when a person’s body proves uniquely able to carry a certain civic frequency.

Law is literal resonance.

A legal boundary is not merely marked; it is maintained as a standing pattern in the local architecture. A contract is stored in growth: in the way a bridge is tensioned, in the pattern of ribs along a road, in the harmonic response of a public floor. To break a law is not only to commit an act. It is to introduce dissonance. Punishment therefore focuses on retuning, containment, or exclusion. Minor offenses are repaired through public correction: the offender must help maintain the damaged chord, sometimes for years. Serious offenses may result in detuning: a person’s mask is altered, their touch-codes revoked, their access to substrate channels limited. The severest punishment is exile into the Silent Basins, where the ground does not answer, where footfalls die, where the living Terrace feels far away and the mind begins to lose its rhythmic mooring.

There is also a punishment older than exile.

In rare cases, a person may be placed into callus.

This is not death, not exactly. The condemned is enclosed within a living chamber of slow-growing tissue, where their vibrations are recorded, dampened, and stored. Some become warnings. Some become archives. Some become foundations for new growth. The towns do not speak of this often. When they do, they sign it with one hand closed over the other, then still.

Water is the deepest crime.

Or the deepest miracle. The distinction is not always stable.

The Terrace has passed beyond water, but it remembers water the way an old body remembers fever. A sealed vessel containing water is not merely contraband. It is an ontological disturbance. Root-fields lean toward it with something between hunger and dread. Towns sing warning harmonics without waiting for council decision. Maintenance channels flush with cold blue light. Children are moved inward. Elders touch the ground and wait. The ancient administrative systems, usually slow and patient, become suddenly precise.

If a water-bearer enters a settlement, the town does not attack. It isolates. Doors close. Floors tilt subtly away. Air-locks contract. The air grows charged. The person is surrounded not by soldiers but by custodial tissue: walls that thicken, floors that rise, paths that guide without force but without choice. They are brought before the oldest resonant chamber, where the Dry Custody decides what must be done.

The Dry Custody is not a single creature. It is the accumulated maintenance reflex of the Terrace made articulate. It lives in the root-lattices, the seed-vaults, the administrative chords, and the trained bodies of those who serve it. It may speak through a council of masked wardens. It may speak through a town’s architecture. It may speak through a sudden, overwhelming silence. It may send agents: human or post-human functionaries wearing blank masks, or stranger beings grown for judgment alone—tall, hollow-boned figures whose chests are resonance chambers and whose hands can open locks by touch.
