What lives there is not one kind, and not even one kind of city-dweller in any simple sense. The true population of the place is a saturation: a stacked, breathing, dripping multitude that fills every ledge, gutter, shaft, cable, wall-face, warm hollow, and fogged interval between towers. To ask who lives there is to receive too many answers at once. Life there is not arranged as human settlement surrounded by nature. It is closer to an urban reef, a warm mineral wetland in which architecture, machinery, water, fungus, bacterium, and animal have grown into one another over such long use that no one layer can be called merely background.

The first inhabitants, if one counts by necessity rather than by size, are the living films. They are not scenery. They are the foundation population, the slow citizens underfoot and on every weeping wall. Black bacterial felts spread over iron-rich stone, thin and glossy when wet, dull when drying. They feed on mineral seepage and give off a faint metallic sweetness after warm rain. In places they form broad living pavements, rippled where water has flowed over them for years, cracked into tiny plates where the stone breathes heat at night. Pale fungal threads move through fissures and behind loose mineral crusts, not as mushrooms or soft white molds but as dense, felted networks, grayish or bone-colored, sometimes beaded with condensation. They break down old chitin, shed skin, animal oils, and the dust that arrives on clouds. Chalky microbial mats bead water into lenses along shallow basins, their surfaces rough and pale, almost stony until one sees them move almost imperceptibly over days, adjusting to moisture. In mineral pools, violet-black crusts concentrate salts and give the water a dull iridescence. These films are grazed, scraped, harvested, irrigated, and protected. They are food, skin, filter, and living mortar. Without them, the higher life of the city would fail within a season.

Upon these films live the scrapers. They are everywhere in the small wet places: flattened crabs and crab-like creatures with coppery or ash-gray shells, their lower faces fitted with hard combing mouthparts that rasp bacterial layers from stone without tearing the living substrate too deeply. Some are no larger than a thumb. Others are broad and heavy, moving along reservoir walls with a slow, deliberate patience. Their shells are often encrusted with mineral bloom, so that a resting grazer can look like a piece of the wall until one of its eyes shifts, or a leg lifts, or water beads off its carapace in a small bright sheet. They live in dense tiers along ledges, under stairs, around pipe collars, and inside shallow channels where nutrient water is directed over them. Their constant scraping keeps the stone surfaces productive. If they are removed, the films thicken, clog gutters, choke vents, and rot into sour black sludge. If they become too numerous, the films are stripped faster than they can regrow. The city’s keepers therefore manage them as one manages weather: not by eliminating them, but by moving warmth, water, and predators in careful patterns.

There are smaller scrapers too: bead-worms, mites, bristled larvae, and shell-backed crawlers that live inside the thin water films themselves. Some are so flat that they move through capillary moisture on vertical stone. Some form living speckles on damp walls, each one grazing a patch no larger than a coin. When a warm shower passes over them, they become active all at once, and the walls seem to shimmer with minute motion. They are prey for many things, but their abundance is so great that they seem less like individual animals than like a condition of the surface. They hatch in mineral crusts, feed for a few days or weeks, molt, reproduce, and are consumed by beetles, eel-like pipe dwellers, or fungal networks. Their bodies return quickly to calcium, phosphate, and chitin.

The walls and ledges belong to roosting bodies in tiers. Some creatures cling in rows so regular that they look like tiles laid by a careful mason. These are often broad-bellied, low, and warm-blooded or warm-conditioned in some slow way, with suctioning mouths or padded limbs that let them rest on wet vertical stone without slipping. They face outward, eyes half shut, skins slick, letting condensation run over them. They are not beautiful in a delicate sense; they look used by the place. Their hides are thick, folded, and darkened by mineral water. Their bellies are pale and calloused from stone. They gather where heat rises, especially above vent shafts and warm pipes, and their roosts become blackened over time with oils and body warmth. When disturbed, they do not scatter immediately. They make low clicking or humming sounds, shift as a group, and then, if pressure continues, they peel away from the wall in a wet rush of limbs and membranes, falling a short distance before catching cables, cornices, or thermals.

Among these ledge-dwellers are creatures that seem almost architectural themselves. Some have shells or plates that interlock when they sleep, reducing water loss and preserving warmth. Others excrete a thin mineral coating from their skin, so that older individuals look half-built into the wall. A few colonies of these animals are encouraged to settle around iron armatures and stone frames. Over years, their bodies and secretions thicken into living buttresses. They are not intelligent in any human sense, but their behavior is guided by taste, heat, moisture, and the chemical traces left by older colonies. The city’s builders do not simply construct; they invite, seed, feed, and wait.

The pipes, gutters, drains, aqueducts, and submerged galleries have their own inhabitants. Long pale animals like eels live in downspouts and service channels, their bodies flexible enough to bend around elbows and grates. Some are filterers, lying with frilled mouths open in moving water, straining bacteria, spores, and microscopic nutrient bodies. Some are hunters, slipping through warm dark channels after larvae and small crustaceans. Their skins are often smooth, nearly blind, sensitive to vibration and mineral taste. They know the city by flow. A change in condensation, a blocked weir, a closed sluice, a new leak: these are events to them as clearly as a change in season would be to creatures in another world. In larger drains, thicker eel-like animals wait near turbine outflows, where warmth and food drift continuously. Their bodies polish the insides of pipes by constant movement, preventing crusts from closing the passages too quickly.

In reservoirs and broad canals live slow filter-feeders of greater bulk. Some glide just under the surface with wide mouths open, straining cloudy water through comb-like plates. Others lie on the bottom of warm basins, their bodies broad and dark, moving so slowly that mineral films sometimes form on their backs. They are not majestic in the manner of open-water animals; they are municipal, patient, adapted to stillness and murk. Their breathing is audible in quiet places: a soft sucking, a low release, a stirring of warm water. They keep reservoirs from becoming too dense with microbial bloom. Their waste settles into channels where beetles and larvae break it down further. Some are guided by low electric fences or by subtle changes in water chemistry, moving from one basin to another according to the city’s needs. They do not know they are managed. They only follow warmth, food, and flow.

The air above the terraces is thick with winged life. Mist moths drift through vapor on broad wings that absorb moisture rather than repel it. Their bodies are often dusted with spores and mineral powder, and they carry fungal life from district to district as bees might carry pollen in another ecology. They do not flutter brightly. They move with a slow, damp heaviness, appearing suddenly in beams of light and then dissolving into fog. Some are small as fingernails. Others have wingspans as wide as a hand or larger, and when they roost together under arches their folded wings resemble layers of wet gray cloth.

Flue swifts ride the warm columns rising from vents, chimneys, and turbine galleries. They are narrow, quick, and dark, with stiff wings and sharp calls that echo off stone. They nest in heat shafts and around insulated pipes, where their young are warmed by the city’s exhalations. They eat airborne larvae, spore-clumps, and small insects lifted by thermals. Their flight paths are tied to the rhythm of the machines: when a vent opens, they rise; when a turbine shifts pitch, they turn; when evening cooling changes the updrafts over the western terraces, they descend in long spirals toward roosting towers.

Beetles hum between cable lines and copper pipes. Many are glossy, dark, and warm to the touch, their shells polished by mist. Some feed on fungal growths inside service ducts. Others clean dead matter from joints and crevices. A few are drawn to the faint crackle of electric equipment and gather near insulated knobs and mesh guards, not because they understand the current but because warmth and vibration collect there. When they take flight together, their sound can be mistaken for a failing generator: a low, metallic buzzing that rises and falls with the movement of the swarm.

There are gliders too. They drop from high towers on membranes stretched between limbs or along ribbed frames of skin, catching thermals that rise from heated stone. They do not soar in the effortless way of birds over open country. They move in short, deliberate passages between vertical structures, using the city’s own heat as an elevator. Some are small and insect-like. Others are larger, with broad damp membranes and heavy bodies, and when they land on a warm wall they remain there for hours, breathing slowly, letting mist bead on their skin.

Predators live in the city, but they are not wild in the sense of being outside its order. They are part of its regulation. On high catwalks and around breeding stacks, long-limbed hunters wait in stillness, their bodies low, their feet padded or hooked for wet metal. Some resemble herons or large insects more than mammals; all are adapted to patience. They strike at molted grazers, young moths, injured roosters, and animals weakened by breeding exhaustion. Their presence keeps herds moving and prevents any one population from consuming the films too completely. In the channels, predatory eel-forms and armored water-crawlers hunt near sluice gates, where prey is carried by flow. On walls, camouflaged stalkers move among roosting tiers, taking individuals without causing full panic. The city has hunting corridors, feeding basins, and molting galleries arranged so that predation happens where it can be absorbed without collapse. Death here is not an interruption of order. It is one of the ways order continues.

Some of the most important inhabitants are builders. They are small colonial animals, often worm-like or larval in their active phases, that secrete calcium carbonate, silica, or mineral-cemented crusts around provided armatures. They do not build freely like architects. They are induced, fed, and guided. Keepers place frames of iron, ceramic mesh, or old shell-lime in positions where new thickness is needed: around a leaking aqueduct joint, along a weakened terrace edge, inside a condenser tower whose walls have grown thin. Then nutrient-rich water is directed over the colony. The creatures settle, feed, and slowly deposit mineral matter. Over months and years, a wall thickens. A crack seals. A new ledge forms. When the colony matures, it may die back or move to warmer crevices, leaving behind a hardened structure that becomes home to films, grazers, and roosting animals. In this way, much of the city is not built once but continuously grown, repaired, and inhabited again.

There are also animals whose lives are bound directly to machines. Their eggs must be warmed by pipes. Their larvae develop only in the steady outflow of turbines or in the slow vibration of engines. Some lay their eggs in the insulation behind arc lamps, where heat is constant and predators have difficulty reaching. Others require the rhythmic opening of sluice gates to trigger hatching. If a machine is stopped for too long, a generation may fail. If it runs too hot, eggs may cook. The keepers of such machines therefore know that their work is not only mechanical. A valve turned at the wrong time is not merely an engineering error; it may thin a roost, starve a grazing tier, or flood a breeding gallery. In this city, industry is ecology.

The large animals of the upper terraces are among the most visible and least hurried inhabitants. They stand in drizzle on broad stone platforms, their hides slick and dark, their bodies heavy and warm. Some are quadrupedal, broad-backed, and slow, with thick skins folded around their shoulders and flanks. Others stand more upright, waiting in mist with a stillness that makes them seem carved from the same stone. They are not aggressive. They do not need to be. Their size, their thick hides, and their position in the city’s heat economy protect them. They may stand for hours over warm vents, regulating their temperature, letting water run off their backs in thin sheets. Some graze fungal felts from protected ledges. Others wait for mist-borne prey or nutrient bodies carried upward by warm air. When they move, the movement is slow and deliberate, and smaller animals scatter in front of them like water before a barge. Their dung is valuable. It falls into collection gutters and is carried to digestion basins where beetles, larvae, and microbial mats break it into fertilizer for cultivated films.

In lower districts, herds of medium-bodied grazers move through streets in dense, patient bodies. They are not like cattle in open fields. They move through architectural channels, along worn ramps, under aqueducts, past humming cabinets and dripping walls. Their claws make a dry rattle on stone; their breathing is wet and close. They are guided by low fences, scent lines, heat gradients, and the pressure of their own numbers. They graze bacterial crusts from designated surfaces, then are encouraged onward before they strip a district bare. Their passage leaves stone cleaner, slightly warm, and faintly shining. Children or tenders may walk beside them, not as masters, but as minor currents within the herd’s movement.

The people who live there, if people there are, are not the dominant presence. They are more like maintainers, listeners, and negotiators. They move through the city in oilskin and heavy boots, their clothes darkened by moisture and patched with waxed fabric, leather, and metal foil. They wear gloves resistant to heat and sharp crusts. Some carry ceramic tools, valve keys, insulated cutters, spore pouches, or slender lamps that burn fungal oil. Their faces are often damp, their eyes accustomed to glare from wet stone and sudden breaks of sunlight through cloud. They do not walk as if the city belongs to them. They walk as if they are passing through many territories that tolerate them only so long as they remain useful.

They are engineers, but of a patient kind. Their work is to keep water moving, heat distributed, electricity insulated, and animal populations balanced. They listen to pipes. They place a hand on metal to judge temperature. They watch the behavior of moths to predict condensation. They count roosts, inspect incubators, clear blocked gutters, and open valves when the western clouds begin to gather. Their knowledge is not only technical; it is ecological and almost liturgical. They know which terrace must remain damp for a certain fungal mat, which gutter must run warm for a particular grazer, which cable must not be disturbed during molting season. A mistake can cascade. If one flow is interrupted, a district may starve. If condensation is diverted carelessly, a bloom may choke a ventilation shaft. If predators are frightened away, grazers may increase too quickly and strip the walls. The people therefore act with restraint. They repair, adjust, encourage, and redirect. They rarely eliminate.

Their homes are small and practical, built into dry pockets above the wettest levels. They have heated floors, ceramic walls, and windows that look out over terraces, cables, and steam. Their rooms smell of warm dust, oil, ammonia, and fungal bread. They eat what the city produces: fungal cakes, rendered animal fats, eggs gathered under license, shellfish-like grazers, dried microbial sheets, mineral salts, and cultivated protein bodies from reservoir farms. Nothing green is served. Nothing leafy. Their food is dense, warm, salty, and often steamed or baked on hot stone. Water is never wasted, but it is also never worshipped as if it were simple. Water is wealth, danger, medicine, and law.

There are human children in this city, though they are few relative to the animal multitudes. They learn early not to fear noise, wetness, or crowding. They learn to read the sound of a pump, the smell of a coming rain, the difference between a healthy film and one turning sour. They grow up knowing that a wall may be alive, that a machine may be a nursery, and that a roost is not a nuisance but a public organ. Some become valve-tenders. Others become roost-counters, mason-guides, predator-keepers, or film-cultivators. Their professions are named after flows, temperatures, surfaces, and animal stages. They do not speak of conquering the city. They speak of keeping it in balance.

Yet the city could perhaps continue without them. That is part of its unsettling calm. If the last engineer vanished, the machines might slow, some districts might dry, some roosts might fail, and new arrangements would begin. Films would spread. Some channels would clog. Others would be cleared by burrowers and filterers. Heat would still rise. Clouds would still form below the lower terraces. Animals would still move toward warmth and moisture. The city would not become a ruin so much as a different metabolism. The people know this, perhaps not consciously, but in their habits. They do not stand above the system. They are inside it, one guild among many, useful because they can intervene across scales that individual animals cannot reach.

The sound of the inhabitants is immense and layered. It is not the chaotic noise of panic. It is the noise of saturation. Water drips, runs, beads, and strikes surfaces in continuous rhythm. Gutters gurgle. Steam valves sigh. Turbines moan. Electric insulators tick in mist. Over and under these mechanical sounds are the animals: the scrape of mouthparts on stone, the click of shells, the soft thump of bodies shifting in roosts, the rattle of claws, the wet sliding of eels in drains, the cloth-like flutter of wings, the low calls from reservoirs, the high answering calls from towers. When a roost takes flight, the sound can become a wall of air and movement. When a herd passes through a lower street, the noise is dense and patient, like rain moving over gravel. The inhabitants do not live in silence. They live inside a constant acoustic weather.

Their lives are shaped by wetness. No creature here is truly dry for long. Even those that rest near hot pipes carry moisture in their skins, shells, or feathers. Water is not only something they drink. It is the medium through which food travels, heat moves, eggs hatch, spores spread, and waste is carried away. Many animals have bodies flattened for thin water films. Others have frills, combs, or porous skins that use moisture for feeding or breathing. Some sleep with their bodies aligned to drip lines so that condensation runs over them. Some breed only when surfaces are slick. Their cities of stone and metal are therefore not dry shelters from weather; they are instruments for conducting weather through life.

Heat is equally important. The warmth rising from deep seams of heated ground makes the high plateau livable despite its altitude and cloud. It draws animals to vents, pipes, and sun-warmed stone. It creates thermals for winged life. It allows microbial films to remain active through damp spells that would otherwise slow them. The city’s machinery adds to this warmth in measured ways. Engines, turbines, incubators, and heated floors all become part of the thermal landscape. Animals know these gradients intimately. They move from a cool ledge to a warm cabinet, from a shaded stair to a sunlit copper pipe, from a misty terrace to a steam gallery, according to need. To live there is to know heat by touch, not by abstraction.

At evening, the inhabitants do not vanish. They change arrangement. Diurnal grazers retreat into crevices or under sheltering crusts. Night roosts thicken. Moths become more numerous in the mist. Arc lamps glow pale against wet stone, drawing insects and the predators that feed on them. Some machines slow, altering the pitch of the city’s hum. Others continue unchanged. The warm stone releases stored heat slowly, and animals gather where that heat is strongest. People in oilskin make rounds with lanterns, checking valves, listening for blocked flows, marking nests that must not be disturbed. Their lights move briefly along catwalks and disappear into steam.

In the night reservoirs, filter-feeders continue their slow work. In the walls, fungal threads grow by fractions. In breeding galleries, eggs warm against pipes. In high roosts, thousands of bodies press together, shifting as one when a gust passes. In the streets below, a herd settles into a resting yard, its mass breathing. On the outer terraces, rain may fall in a gray curtain while nearby towers remain in clear light. The city does not become quiet. It becomes differently layered.

Whoever lives there lives as part of this negotiation. The people are not alone. The animals are not pests. The films are not dirt. The machines are not separate from the bodies around them. Every inhabitant, human or otherwise, is a participant in a wet industrial ecology without green things: a high mineral city of warmth, fungus, bacteria, water, shell, wing, claw, pipe, stone, and slow persistent repair.

If one had to name the dominant life of the place, it would not be a single species. It would be the collective itself: the dense, noisy, dripping, warm, mineral-crusted commonwealth of bodies that fills every available space and keeps the city from becoming either ruin or desert. The true resident is the whole saturated condition. To live there is to be continuously touched by water, heat, and other bodies. To live there is to be counted not as an individual first, but as a function in a vast, patient, wet urban metabolism.
