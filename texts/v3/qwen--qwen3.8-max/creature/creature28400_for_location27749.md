What lives there lives first as a skin.

Before any shell, limb, hunting mouth, or listening head, the basin is inhabited by films. They are the first fact of life in a country without green. They cling to the wet stone in thin continents, in threads, in crusts, in oily sheens. They are not plants. They do not stand, do not leaf, do not flower, do not cast shade. They spread. They bloom when water comes, retreat into dormancy when the surface dries to a matte chill, and return again with the next mist. In some places they are rust-colored and soft as wet fur. In others they blacken hollows like varnish, so dense and old-looking that a careless eye might take them for stone. In shaded cracks they grow as pale filaments, fine as breath laid over rock. Where mineral-rich water meets air, they make rainbow slicks that smell of cold iron and salt.

These living films are the base of everything. They are food, surface, signal, and sanctuary. They are grazed, scraped, broken, swallowed, smeared across hide, dried into fuel, and pressed against wounds. They are the nearest thing the basin has to grass, to crop, to orchard, to pasture, but they are none of those things. They are microbial and fungal, lithic and aquatic, patient in the way only very small life can be when it has learned to wait on weather.

The commonest animals are those that eat this skin.

They are spread over the wet pavements in slow, scattered multitudes. Many are low and limpet-like, shelled in smooth domes worn by centuries of rain and movement. Their shells are not bright or ornamental. They are the color of wet rock: blue-gray, smoke-gray, rusted at the edges, sometimes glazed with mineral water until they shine like old metal. They cling to stone with broad, wet feet and move with a patience that makes them seem less like creatures than like thoughts the landscape is having very slowly. Their mouths are hard, rasping, crystalline, built to scrape films from rock without breaking the rock beneath. When they feed, they make a fine dry scraping sound, thousands upon thousands of tiny files at work. This sound is one of the basin’s constant voices. It is not loud in itself, but there is always enough of it to remind a listener that the stone is being lived on.

These grazers do not move as a herd moves in a place with grass. They move as moisture moves. After rain, or after a long condensation when the ground sweats into shallow sheets, they spread outward across the pavement in slow waves. They follow the bloom of microbial films, especially the rust-fur that quickens on newly wet basalt. When the surface dries too much, they draw back into pans, seepage lines, and shaded hollows.
