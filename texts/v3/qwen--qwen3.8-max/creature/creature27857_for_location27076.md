What lives there has accepted the finality of the place. Nothing among its inhabitants is a builder, a maker of tools, a carrier of worked objects. There are no hands shaped for gripping, no limbs that have become hammers, no mouths that carry seeds in woven baskets or dig channels with borrowed stone. The living there have not overcome the landscape by invention. They have entered it so completely that they perform its processes with their bodies.

They are not occupants of the wetland so much as moving organs within it.

The first and most numerous inhabitants are barely distinguishable from the ground itself. They live inside the compressed vegetal substrate, in the cold, springy layers where old plant tissue has become floor, wall, pipe, and larder all at once. These are the root-borers, bladder-feeders, soft worms, and translucent larvae that move through the living mat like slow impulses. They have no bones. Their bodies are clear or milky, segmented or folded, some flat as wet membranes, others plump with stored fluids. They feed on the oldest, softest matter: the saturated hearts of buried plants, the sugary bleed of root-pumps, the microbial films that grow along capillary channels.

Their work is constant. They open pores that have begun to close. They thin dense patches of felt-like tissue so water can pass. They consume what has ceased to conduct, and in doing so keep the plain’s living skin permeable. Where they move in great numbers, the substrate seems to think in slow currents. A footprint may fill, heal, and then reappear because these deep dwellers have reproduced the pressure pattern, drawing fluid back into the old shape without any intention of memory. They are the plain’s unconscious maintenance.

Some of them are almost vegetal. They branch. They pause for long periods with their tissues pressed into the substrate, exchanging heat and moisture. When they move, it is as a thickening or brightening within the ground, a pale worming glow beneath dark wet felt. Other creatures prey on them without violence in any sharp sense. There are blunt, sucking mouths below the surface, enveloping predators that simply close around smaller life and digest it over hours, drawing it into warm, acidic folds. Nothing here tears cleanly. Death is softened, wetted, and taken downward.

On the surface live the grazers: broad, low bodies adapted to moving across the living floor without breaking it. They are flat and patient, more like rays or lichens than land animals, though they move with slow purpose. Their undersides are fringed with cilia and soft rasping pads. They feed on microbial film, electrostatic dust, spore fall, and the oily secretions that bead on plant skins. Some are the color of wet charcoal; others are green-black, blue-grey, or the dull amber of old resin. Their backs are often ribbed or pocked, collecting moisture, channeling condensation toward their mouths.

These grazers do not form herds in the familiar sense. They occur in broad, slow accumulations. For miles there may be only one, motionless, its edge rising and falling with faint breath. Then, without warning, the surface darkens with thousands of them, moving together in a low tide of bodies. They are responding to some change in charge, vapor, or pressure. When they feed, the sound is not a chewing but a soft, continuous hiss, like wet cloth drawn across glass. When pressure drops, they lift their margins and produce thin whistles. The plain then seems to exhale through them.

They are prey for larger surface-dwellers, but predation here is not a matter of pursuit and tearing. The hunters are ambushes of texture and adhesion. Some flatten themselves into the substrate until they resemble a harmless dark patch. When a grazer passes over them, they rise in a single enveloping fold, sealing prey inside a living pocket. Others secrete sticky filaments that gum the cilia of their victims, slowing them until the hunter can creep close and cover them. There is little blood. There is much suffocation, dissolution, and absorption. Even hunger has been rounded soft.

The most visible animals on the open plain are the waders: tall, sparse creatures that move through shallow sheets and capillary furrows with extreme deliberation. They are not birds, though they share some of the posture of birds, and they are not insects, though their joints sometimes fold with insect patience. Their legs are hollow, ribbed, and semi-rigid, more like living pipes than limbs. Through these legs they sense pressure, temperature, and the faint electrical gradients of the ground. Each step is tested before weight is accepted. When they stand still, they seem to be listening through the whole plain.

Their bodies are narrow, often pale, with internal fluids visible as dim shadows beneath translucent tissue. They carry moisture in bladders along their flanks, and in cold weather these bladders glow faintly with metabolic heat. Their heads—if head is the right word—are clusters of sensory membranes, venting slits, and soft intake crowns. They do not have sharp beaks. Their mouths are rounded, muscular, and capable of drawing in vapor, spore clouds, tiny swarming bodies, or nutrient gel from the surface. Some feed by standing in one place for hours, filtering fog through folded throat tissues. Others follow pressure fronts, stepping from basin to basin as the land shifts its hydraulic moods.

Their calls are among the loudest sounds on the plain. These are not songs so much as commands, warnings, and tuning notes. A wader may open its throat and emit a long, resonant tone that travels through the substrate. Far away, a vent slit may answer. A seed tower may shiver. A mat may tighten or relax underfoot. The creatures seem to know this. Some call not to one another but to the landscape itself, using sound to open pathways, draw vapor toward them, or trigger the release of edible spores. In this way, they are both animals and operators. Their ecology includes acoustic engineering without tools.

The air above the plain is not empty. It is populated by drifting, swarming, filamentous lives that move in ribbons, sheets, and clouds. The most common are small winged bodies, countless and dim, feeding on spores, exhaled oils, and electrostatic films. Alone, each is little more than a mote with a moist membrane and a few vibrating filaments. Together, they become weather-like forms. They rise in dark bands, bend as one body, and pour sideways across the plain when charge gradients shift. From a distance, they look like smoke, but smoke with hesitation, with decision, with sudden thickening.

Some of these aerial populations are colonial. They link themselves with fine living threads or by shared charge, forming temporary nets, veils, and drifting lattices. These colonies collect moisture from fog and feed on the particles they filter. At times they resemble slow-moving jellyfish in the air; at other times they are more like loose cloth blown into false shapes. When static builds before a storm, their bodies flash faintly along their margins. They do not produce lightning, but they guide it, offering conductive paths for the silent blue discharges that move through the fog. In storm-light, they appear as hanging tapestries of pale fire, then vanish into ordinary dimness.

Higher still, where the air is thinner and colder, there are rarer aerial forms. These are larger, slower, and more membranous. They do not flap so much as adjust their surfaces, catching updrafts from venting plants and riding warm plumes until the cold forces them down again. Their bodies are adapted to condensation; they gather water from fog and store it internally, then release it as a fine trail that may freeze into temporary filaments. They are solitary for the most part, drifting miles apart, but when a seed tower begins to discharge, they descend in long spirals, drawn by the chemical richness of the release.

The hollow organ-towers that rise sparsely from the plain have their own dedicated inhabitants. These growths are not homes in the sense of nests or burrows, but many creatures live within their vascular chambers, around their rotating internal vanes, and along their wet inner walls. Some are small and constant, part of the tower’s function. There are valve-worms, eel-like and flexible, whose bodies are shaped to ease the opening and closing of living passages. They slide into narrow ducts, swell or thin themselves, and thereby alter flow. They do not understand the tower as a whole, yet their activity is essential to its rhythm.

There are heat-beetles, though beetle is only an approximation. Their shells are not hard mineral armor but dense, wet cellulose layered with heat-resistant secretions. They rest near vent slits, motionless for hours, waiting for warm vapor to surge upward. When the tower exhales, they lift their bodies and release eggs into the rising current. The eggs are carried upward, some into cold air where they crystallize and fall later as spore-like grains, others into higher fog where they hatch into drifting larvae. These beetles do not build nurseries. They use the tower’s breath.

Inside the larger towers, where internal vanes turn under pressure, there are creatures whose whole existence is tuned to the slow drone of moving parts. They are not machinists. They are symbionts, cleaners, feeders, and accidental regulators. Some scrape excess film from vascular surfaces. Some consume condensed oils that would otherwise clog channels. Others rest against the vanes and let the movement carry them in circles, absorbing vibrations through their bodies. When they move in unison, their mass can alter the balance of a rotating structure, changing the tone of the tower’s hum. This is not deliberate labor, but it is labor nonetheless.

The seed-production towers draw the greatest concentrations of animal life. These broad, layered growths are sparse across the plain, but when they become active, fauna gather around them from great distances. The towers function as refineries and packaging organs. Fluids enter from the substrate, are separated and enriched, then formed into spores, capsules, nutrient droplets, or fibrous seed-bundles. The process is slow, internal, and invisible until release. Then the chambers open, pressure equalizes with a deep boom or hiss, and the air fills with drifting biological product.

At that moment, the plain seems to forget its emptiness. Swarms arrive in dark, excited ribbons. Grazers move toward the towers in broad, crawling fronts. Waders stand at the edges with throats open, filtering the richest clouds. Tower-specialists emerge from hidden slits and begin rapid, repetitive movements. There are creatures that feed only on the outer casing of expelled capsules, others that drink the fluid inside without breaking the membrane, others that carry spores away on their bodies and thus serve the tower’s reproduction. Some animals come to eat. Some come to breed. Some come because the chemical signal is indistinguishable from the trigger of their own maturation.

In these release events, life becomes dense, noisy, and almost mechanical in its regularity. The fauna pulse around the tower in cycles: approach, feed, scatter, return. The tower may release several clouds in succession, each with a different composition. One may be fine, dry, and electrically charged; another may be heavy, oily, and sweet. Different creatures respond to each. Some fall into torpor after feeding. Others become briefly aggressive, competing for the richest fall by crowding and smothering rather than by attack. When the tower closes again, the multitude disperses slowly, leaving behind bodies, shed membranes, waste, and the beginning of new growth on the enriched surface.

Beneath all this visible abundance lies a quieter population of those who live with the old woody nubs, the last durable hearts of long-buried plants. These rounded nubs are not stone, but they are the closest thing to permanence on the plain. Around them gather slow organisms: fungal-plant hybrids, root-clinging worms, and small, blind creatures that feed on the moisture weeping from their pores. The nubs are often warmer than the surrounding surface, or more chemically active, and they serve as minor centers of exchange. Animals rest against them not for shelter, since shelter implies enclosure, but for thermal and hydraulic advantage. They are places where the plain’s deep memory leaks upward.

There are also beings so sparse, so large, and so slow that they are easily mistaken for features of the land. These are the walking wetland organs, if one must give them a name. They are not single animals but dense mutualisms: masses of hollow stems, living felt, bladders, cilia, larvae, and vascular tissue bound into a mobile form. They may stand for seasons in one place, indistinguishable from a cluster of organ-growths. Then, when pressure gradients demand redistribution, they detach from the substrate and move. Their movement is not fast. It is a regional migration measured in days or weeks, a slow shifting of living machinery from one part of the plain to another.

Their bodies are rounded, blunt, and saturated. They have no faces. Their upper surfaces may open into intake crowns, vent slits, or glowing filtration chambers. Their undersides press and release the substrate, leaving temporary prints that fill with water and seal over. They feed by drawing in fog, surface fluid, spores, and small organisms, but they also photosynthesize dimly, absorb heat from vents, and exchange charge with the plain. They are not masters of the landscape. They are its largest mobile adjustments.

It is among these slow beings and the sound-sensitive waders that something like personhood might exist, though not in human shape or human sense. If there are “whoever” in this place, they are not individuals with faces and possessions. They are presences that become coherent under certain conditions: pressure, sound, saturation, and need. They may form as temporary congregations of smaller lives, or as stable knots within the larger organ-beings. They do not call themselves by names that can be spoken with a single throat. Their identity is more like a sustained interval, a pattern of resonance recognized across the plain.

Such beings do not build. They tune.

Their bodies, when they can be said to have bodies, are composed of living pipes, membranes, bladders, and conductive filaments. They move by redistributing water and gas within themselves, by extending adhesive root-feet, by letting the substrate carry them. They have no hands. Where manipulation is required, they grow temporary organs: a broad pad for pressing, a sticky frond for holding, a hollow tube for directing vapor. These organs last only as long as the task. Afterward they are reabsorbed. Nothing is stored. Nothing is kept. To keep would be to introduce a fixed object into a place where all function must remain fluid.

Their perception is distributed. They feel pressure changes over wide areas. They taste gradients in water and vapor. They hear sound not only through membranes but through the ground itself. Because sound has force in this landscape, their attention is not passive. When they listen, they alter what they listen to.
