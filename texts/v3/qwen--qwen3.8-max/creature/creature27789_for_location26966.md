What lives there is not first a single shape but an attention.

Something in the basin is always listening. Not with ears alone, and not with eyes, because eyes are of limited use in that wet blackness. It listens through water, through plant fiber, through the faint tremor of a root-thread being touched at the far end of a wall. The place feels before it sees. The creatures inside it are part of that feeling.

If one must begin with the makers of the canals, the tiers, the little sluice gates and root-bound terraces, then begin with them: the basin-dwellers, the root-tenders, the ones who live inside the living architecture as though they were organs within a dark, saturated body. Outsiders, if there are any outsiders who know of them at all, might call them the Root-Sewn. That is not quite their own name for themselves, because their truest names are not spoken aloud in the way surface voices understand speech. They are pressed, tapped, traced, rhythmically signed against skin or stem. A name here is more like a pattern of contact than a sound.

They are small. An adult standing fully upright would reach only to the waist of a tall human, but they rarely stand in such an exposed way. Their natural posture is lower: knees bent, shoulders drawn inward, head lifted just enough to read the dark by touch and by the faintest glow. Their bodies are broad without being heavy, compacted for narrow passages and low ceilings. They move with the deliberate economy of creatures who know that every unnecessary motion costs warmth.

Their skin is smooth, cool, and faintly resilient, more like the hide of a marsh-eel or a young amphibian than the skin of a dry mammal. It is dark, green-black or peat-brown, sometimes mottled with paler patches where mineral water has marked them over years. Along the throat, wrists, elbows, and the soft webbing between fingers and toes, there live colonies of harmless luminous bacteria that glow blue-green when the dweller is active, alarmed, or communicating in darkness. The light is not bright. It is the color of drowned stars. It appears as a faint seaming on the body: a line at the jaw, a pulse in the palm, a ghostly tracing along the forearm when a hand moves through black water.

They have fine down over their bodies, so short and oiled that it does not soak easily. It is not fur as a land animal would have it, but a cold-wet insulation, dense enough to hold a thin layer of warmth against the skin. In older individuals, especially those who spend long hours in the deepest reservoirs, this down becomes colonized by tiny growths: algal bloom, moss spores, pale fungal threads. An elder may wear living green at the shoulders and crown without having chosen it, because in that place a body is never entirely separate from the room it lives in.

Their faces are blunt and patient. The mouth is wide, lipless, and precise. The nostrils sit low and can close tight against water. Whisker-like sensory hairs grow at the corners of the mouth, along the cheeks, and in short rows on the forearms; these are not decorative but constantly active, reading texture and pressure. Their eyes are large, dark, and shallowly reflective, adapted to making use of the faintest biological light. They blink slowly. When submerged, a second membrane closes across the eye, leaving only a silvery sheen. They do not have the stare of surface predators. Their gaze, when one encounters it, is more like the gaze of something that has learned to see only what moves in stillness.

Their hands are their most human-seeming feature, and even they are strange. Four long fingers curve from broad palms, each finger padded along the underside with sensitive flesh. The thumb is set low and can oppose either side of the hand, useful for pinching roots, tying cords, or steadying a shell blade. Between the fingers stretches a strong web, not enough to make the hand a paddle but enough to turn it into a tool for testing currents. The fingertips are not clawed, but tough, and in many individuals the nails have been worn to smooth, usable edges from working plant fiber and bone. Their feet are wider, gripping, with toes that can spread against wet moss and hold a stem while both hands work.

They are amphibious in the deepest sense: not simply able to swim, but divided in need. Adults breathe air, but they are never comfortable away from wet surfaces. Their young begin in water. Some of the jelly-clusters clinging beneath the causeways are not only animal nurseries but theirs. Inside those faintly luminous masses, curled translucent forms wait through long cold development. When the young hatch, if “hatch” is the right word for that slow emergence, they are small, eyeless, gilled, and almost entirely silent. They drift in protected channels, feed on microscopic growths, and learn the city by passing through it repeatedly until its currents become part of their bodies.

The dwellers see less than they feel. They navigate by hand-rails of polished root, by changes in water temperature, by the smell of oxygened flow, by the tiny pressure differences that tell them a channel has narrowed or a gate has been left open. Along their calves and forearms run lines of pores sensitive to movement in water, not unlike the lateral lines of fish. In darkness, this sense is more reliable than sight. A dweller can know that something has passed through a canal before it sees the creature itself. It can detect whether the movement was large or small, fast or slow, hungry or merely drifting.

They speak little with sound. When they do, it is usually underwater: soft clicks, low throbbing pulses, or brief percussive knocks made by striking palm against stem or knuckle against root. In air, their voices are rare and thin, more like wet hiss or the creak of reeds than speech. Most communication is signed in close quarters: finger pressure, palm patterns, taps on the shoulder or wrist, the angle of the head, the opening or closing of the luminous patches on their throats and hands. A flash may mean attention. A slow dimming may mean safety. A quick flare along the forearms may mean danger, or intrusion, or a change in water.

They are not gentle in the surface sense of the word. They are careful. There is a difference. Their world is too constrained for casual violence, but it is not merciful. They kill when necessary, harvest when necessary, bind and prune and cull when necessary. What distinguishes them is patience. They do not force growth if they can encourage it. They do not carve a beam if they can train a stem. They do not make a wall dead if they can keep it alive. Their technology is an extension of tending: knotting, pressing, weaving, lashing, pegging, grafting, feeding, waiting.

They dress sparingly. Clothing in the basin is less about modesty than about protection, storage, and warmth. They wear close wraps of woven moss and reed felt, sometimes daubed with clay or sealed at the shoulders with dark resin. These garments are flexible and quiet. They do not rustle. Pouches hang at hip or chest, made from bladderweed or the cured membranes of aquatic creatures, tied with braided grass. Inside them one might find a shell scraper, a bone needle, a coil of fine root-cord, a thorn awl, a folded leaf of dried larvae, or a small polished river-stone used for smoothing resin. Metal is absent. Fire is nearly unthinkable. Their tools are bone, shell, thorn, hardened wood, plant fiber, and time.

The shell blade set into a root notch is one of their common instruments: part knife, part pry, part scraper. It is not precious, but it is valued. A good shell edge can cut reed, open egg cases, clean fish, shave bark, and split stems without bruising them too badly. Bone hooks hang in rows not only as fishing gear but also as fasteners, levers, and ceremonial markers. Some hooks are old, passed from one tender to another, their surfaces worn smooth by handling. Others are new, pale, still sharp. In the basin, even a hook can become a kind of record.

Their society is small. Not because they are incapable of more, but because more would be dangerous. The basin can hold only so much life before its balances tilt. A blockage here, an overfed predator there, too many bodies drawing heat from a room already cold, and the whole place could sour. So they live in restraint. Perhaps a few dozen at most occupy the chamber at any one time, and even then they do not cluster as surface cities cluster. They spread themselves across platforms, shelves, and hollows, each individual or pair maintaining a particular stretch of canal, gate, nursery, or filter. Their settlement feels sparse because their ethics are hydrological. Empty space is not failure. Empty space is room for water to move.

They divide labor by aptitude and need rather than by spectacle. Some are root-speakers, the ones most patient with living structures. They know how to bind a wound in a wall so that growth closes it instead of rot opening it. They understand which shoots should be left upward, which should be trained sideways, which must be cut before they choke a passage. Some are water-listeners. They spend long periods crouched beside sluices with fingers in the flow, reading whether the level is rising too fast or draining too far. Others tend eggs, young, drying racks, moss beds, or the pale slime cultures that provide both food and light. There are also those whose work is less obvious: they watch predators. They note which creatures are becoming too bold, which canals have grown stagnant, which root ceilings are accumulating too much condensation in one place.

There is no throne in this city, no chief’s platform, no monument. Authority, if it can be called that, belongs to those who best understand consequence. A dweller who can predict how a blocked root will alter the waterline three terraces away is listened to. A dweller who knows when not to intervene is trusted more. Decisions are not made by loud argument. They are made by inspection, by touch, by repeated observation. A council, if one happens at all, is a group of bodies kneeling in the dark with hands laid against living stems, waiting until the basin itself seems to indicate what must be done.

Their homes are low and close. The shelters are woven chambers, sometimes sealed with clay and resin, sometimes left partly open so moisture can circulate. Inside, there is little furniture. A sleeping hollow may be lined with compressed liverwort and covered by a mat of woven reeds. Warmth is shared: bodies curl together, not sentimentally but practically. A sleeping platform may hold several dwellers in a slow-breathing knot, limbs folded, skin glowing faintly where pressed against one another. They do not build hearths. Instead, they rely on insulation, body heat, and the slow biological warmth of decomposing plant matter carefully tended in certain root pits. Even that heat is modest. It is not enough to dry the air. It is only enough to keep cold from settling into bones.

They eat what the basin gives, and they shape the basin so that it continues to give. Their diet is mixed, opportunistic, and seasonal in ways tied less to sun than to water chemistry and breeding cycles. They eat larvae, but not all larvae; some are left to mature into insects that clean surfaces or produce silk. They eat small fish, blind mites, snail flesh, tender reed shoots, bladderweed buds, black root pith when prepared correctly, and cultivated slime scraped from stone-like masses of compressed vegetation. Some foods are eaten fresh. Some are dried on racks. Some are preserved in sealed pouches with oil or brine. Nothing is wasted. Even the membranes of eaten creatures may be stretched, cured, and used. Even the shells of meals become tools, beads, gate-pegs, or grit for drainage.

They are farmers, but their farms are not fields. They cultivate conditions. A pool is enriched so that certain larvae thrive. A root lattice is maintained so that egg masses have a safe underside. A flow is slowed so that edible slime spreads across a wall. A predator is fed at intervals so it patrols one canal and not another. To call this primitive is to misunderstand it. It is a technology of relationships rather than objects. They do not impose a shape upon the world and expect it to remain. They negotiate with the world continuously, knowing that every agreement must be renewed.

Death among them is practical and intimate. A body that simply remains becomes part of the substrate. That is not considered horror. It is return. Yet they do not treat the dead carelessly. Some bodies are smoked or oiled only when preservation is necessary for memory, for a tool, for a ritual object. Most are folded into root-baskets and lowered into designated hollows where water and plant tissue can take them apart slowly. The nutrients feed the walls, the walls feed the animals, the animals feed the young. To be consumed by the basin is not annihilation; it is continued citizenship. In rare cases, a bone may be retained: a hook carved from an ancestor’s rib, a peg made from a finger joint, a polished tooth worn as a marker of lineage. These relics are not displayed with pride. They are used. Use is the highest respect.

Their emotional life is quiet but deep. They feel satisfaction in the right movement of water. They feel distress at stagnation. They feel alarm at the smell of drying. They feel something like reverence for erosion, because erosion opens new material and new routes, though it also threatens collapse. They do not hate the cold; they live within it as one lives within a fact. They are capable of tenderness, especially toward the young, but their tenderness is shown by maintenance: clearing a channel so a larva can pass, adjusting a nursery shelf, moving an egg cluster away from a draft. Affection between adults is often expressed through grooming, through the sharing of warmth, through the giving of a useful object without ceremony.

They are wary of fire, of drought, of loud noise, of large clumsy bodies. They would find surface cities unbearable: too bright, too dry, too fast, too separated from consequence. To them, a structure that does not live is already dying. A road that does not drink is a scar. A light that burns rather than grows is a wound. Their sense of beauty is tied to function, but not coldly. They admire the curve of a root that has been trained over years into a handhold. They value the faint blue glow of healthy slime. They delight, in their restrained way, in the moment when a newly tied gate begins to grow into its own support.

Yet they are not alone, and they know it.

The basin is shared. The dwellers are the most evident makers, but they are not the only inhabitants, and they would not claim to be. The other lives there are as much citizens as they are, though with different duties and different hungers.

In the deepest reservoir lies the moss-backed one. It is broad, low, and ancient-looking, its carapace or hide so thick with moss, liverwort, and trailing rootlets that it appears to be a living island. It may be amphibian, or reptilian, or something with no surface analogue. It barely moves. When it breathes, the water rises and falls almost imperceptibly. The dwellers clean its back, remove parasites from the folds near its mouth, and sometimes feed it by placing small offerings within reach. In return, its body filters water, stabilizes temperature, and perhaps protects the reservoir from larger disturbances. Some of the young learn to rest against its sides, trusting its slowness. Whether it is intelligent in their manner is hard to say. It may be intelligent in another manner entirely. It is old enough that the distinction seems less important.

There is also the pale predator that moves through the canals. Long, eyeless, and flexible, it swims without breaking the surface, taking larvae, small fish, and anything weak enough to be caught. The dwellers do not love it, but they respect it. They manage its routes with gates and barriers, encouraging it into channels where it is useful and away from nurseries where it is dangerous. Sometimes they feed it deliberately, dropping wounded prey into a chosen flow. This is not worship. It is municipal policy. A predator with no reason to hunt elsewhere is easier to predict. Predictable danger is safer than wandering danger.

The wingless marsh-moths are gentler presences. Furred, slow, and cold-adapted, they rest in the dark angles where condensation gathers. Their bodies are soft and thick, their legs broad and careful. They do not fly; they walk, climb, and sometimes drift short distances on humid air currents within the basin. They feed on microbial films, spore masses, and sweet exudates from certain roots. In doing so, they carry spores and pollen-like material from one plant structure to another, helping the living walls regenerate. Their larvae produce a tough, water-resistant silk that the dwellers harvest or collect after molting. Some gates are reinforced with this silk, either by direct application or by allowing larvae to spin in prepared frames. The moths themselves are tolerated, even welcomed.
