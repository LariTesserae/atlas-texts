What lives there is not easy to count.

At first, it seems as if the settlement itself is the inhabitant: the breathing canopy, the pumping water, the slow green architecture, the engines keeping time like hot, patient hearts. The place has moods. It has rhythms. It has days when the channels run high and days when the root floors are dry enough to creak. It speaks in valve-hisses, leaf-rattle, falling water, and the deep wooden thud of machinery working against load. If you stand still long enough, you begin to feel that you are not standing in a town at all, but inside some vast, low-living body whose veins are culverts and whose bones are trained roots.

But there are also those who tend it.

They are few. There are not enough of them to make crowds, and they do not move through the lanes as if they own them. They move through the settlement the way water moves through its channels: by known routes, by habit, by necessity, with very little wasted effort. You might see one crossing a root bridge with a long hook in one hand and a folded bark cloth under the other arm. You might see another crouched beside a pump housing, ear close to the iron, fingers resting on a bolt as if taking a pulse. You might see a figure high in the canopy screens, silhouetted against green-gold light, cutting a shoot no thicker than a finger while the whole settlement hums below.

They are human in shape, but not quite human in rhythm.

The settlement has trained them as it has trained its trees. Their bodies have learned the place. They walk with a low, sure balance because the paths are sometimes slick, sometimes spongy, sometimes tilted by growth that has lifted a plank or shifted a stair. Their feet are broad-soled and hard, comfortable on wet bark and rounded roots. Their hands are never empty for long; even when resting, they worry a strap, test a rail, rub a patch of moss from a gauge face, or check the warmth of a pipe. Their fingers are stained with tannin, oil, chlorophyll, and rust. The creases of their palms hold dark lines that do not wash out.

They do not speak much. Not because they are unfriendly, but because speech is inefficient in that noise. The engines thump, the water gurgles, the belts slap, the leaves hiss overhead. Voices would be swallowed. So they have developed other ways. They tap on rails. They use short hand signs. They read posture. A pump-tender can tell from the way another person stands whether a valve has been opened or a channel has backed up. They know each other by gait, by lantern position, by the particular sound of boots on certain boards. Some of them communicate with the machines almost as naturally as with one another: a hand pressed to a warm boiler side, an ear turned toward a gear housing, a foot feeling for the tremor beneath a floor.

Their faces are calm in the way of people who have stopped minding constant noise. Their eyes are accustomed to green shade and sudden wet glints. They blink slowly in the patches of direct sun. Many wear their hair bound tight or cropped short, because loose hair catches on bark, vines, and small moving parts. The older ones have skin polished by humidity and heat, and their expressions are set into patient lines. They look neither hurried nor idle. They look continued.

Their clothing is practical and almost entirely grown or adapted from the settlement. They wear layered barkcloth softened by steam and wear, oiled leaf-leather, woven stem fibers, and aprons stained black with machine grease. Buckles are copper or brass. Fasteners are often living loops trained around buttons of polished wood. In wetter lanes they wear wide hats with overlapping leaf shingles, and their shoulders shine with damp. Their clothes smell of warm wood, oil, green water, and the faint mineral sweetness of steam. Some garments have been repaired so many times that they seem more graft than fabric: new panels grown over old tears, vines stitched through worn cuffs, iron rings replacing broken clasps. Nothing is discarded if it can still serve.

They are not one people in the simple sense, but a set of functions that have become lives.

There are the channel-keepers, who walk the lowest lanes and know every seep, blockage, and slow drain in the settlement. They carry poles with curved ends, short blades, and little baskets for pulling debris from waterways. Their boots are always wet. Their calves are marked with dark waterlines. They are the first to notice when a pump note changes, because they feel it in the canals before they hear it. They know which pools are safe, which channels have soft bottoms, and which reed walls will overflow if rain comes too fast.

There are the boiler-tenders, who live near heat. They work in enclosed rooms of iron, brick, and charred-smelling bark, feeding furnaces with dead prunings, spent fiber, compressed leaf waste, and dried stem-cake. Their skin is warm to the touch. Their eyebrows are often singed at the tips. They watch gauges, clean flues, bank fires, and keep the dangerous heat separated from the living walls. They speak even less than the others, because their work requires long attention. Some of them sleep in alcoves beside the boiler housings, wrapped in heat-proof mats, lulled by the very sound that would keep strangers awake.

There are the canopy-pruners, who climb above the dense lower wards and work in the high green screens where light is strongest. They wear belts and hooks, and their arms are scratched by branches. They decide where sunlight may enter, which limbs must be eased back, which leaf panels may remain closed. Their work is slow and consequential. One careless cut can weaken a roof. One missed season can let a bough grow too heavy. They know the trees by name, or by something like name: by habit, by strength, by the way each species responds to binding. When they descend, they bring down bundles of cuttings that will be used for fuel, weaving, grafting, or mulch.

There are the grafters and shapers, who are part gardener, part carpenter, part engineer. They are the ones who guide new rooms into being. They set frames, choose shoots, bind stems, cut notches, and wait. They understand that in this place a wall is not built so much as persuaded. They know which plants will grow together and which will reject one another. They carry small knives, wax, binding cord, and packets of nutrient paste. Their hands are gentle but unsentimental. They can spend years raising a single platform, then prune it back if the load changes. They do not expect immediate results. They plan in growth-rings.

There are the wire-keepers, who guard the dry paths of electricity. They are careful, precise, and a little feared, because their mistakes are not always visible. They route cables upward, replace cracked ceramic insulators, seal joints, and keep dripping points away from live fittings. Their tools have polished handles. Their fingers are sensitive to warmth and vibration in ways that suggest they can feel current as a kind of weather. They do not like moss near junction boxes. They trim it away with the seriousness of surgeons.

There are also those who simply listen.

These are often the oldest, or the youngest who have not yet learned to distrust their own stillness. They sit near pump stations, root junctions, or boiler galleries and do very little that appears useful. But they are attending to the settlement’s sound. They know when a thump has changed by half a beat. They know when water is moving wrong because the pitch of its rush has become thin. They know when a root floor is too dry, or when a wall is growing against a bracket and beginning to strain. Sometimes they send a message by tapping a rail. Sometimes they simply go and fix the trouble themselves. Their knowledge is not mystical; it is accumulated attention.

Their homes are small, warm, and rounded.

They live in the spaces the settlement provides: inside buttress roots hollowed and smoothed by generations of use, in lofts woven between trunk columns, in rooms grown around old engine frames, in bark-shelled alcoves overlooking pump plazas. The interiors are rarely rectangular. Walls curve. Ceilings lean. Doorways are oval or arched, framed by living stems that have been trained to hold their shape. Floors are polished root mats or layered leaf-litter pressed hard enough to feel almost wooden. Every surface has been touched until it is comfortable.

Their rooms are heated by steam coils, warm water pipes, or insulated walls packed with dry fiber. They are humid, but not cold. The air inside smells of leaves, oil, faint smoke from enclosed stoves, and the soft sweetness of steamed bark. Beds are shallow cradles grown into floors or platforms, filled with dried moss, shredded cane, or soft fiber mats. Blankets are woven or felted plant cloth. At night, lamps glow through green or amber glass, turning the rooms into dim lanterns. Water is always near: a drip into a basin, a channel under the floor, a condensation cup by a window slit. No house feels truly dry. The idea of dryness here is not absence of water but control of it.

They keep few possessions, but those they keep are cared for. A polished valve wheel may hang beside a sleeping place. A gauge face with a cracked rim may be preserved as a relic. A tool worn smooth by a parent’s hand is more valuable than a new one. They repair everything. If a cup cracks, it is bound. If a lamp fails, its parts are reused. If a boot wears through, it is resoled with treated bark or woven fiber. They do not hoard, but they remember. Objects in their homes often have histories longer than the people using them.

Their food is plant-born, careful, and unextravagant.

They eat what the settlement can produce without harming itself: tender shoot tips, seed cakes, steamed pith, aquatic stems, root tubers grown in side beds, soft inner bark prepared in long processes to remove bitterness, leaf-curd pressed from certain broad-leaved species, and warm-water greens cultivated along slow channels. Some areas of the ward are given over to quiet vats and shallow trays where edible growths are encouraged under controlled light. They do not hunt. There is almost no animal life to hunt, and they would not risk the balance for it. Occasionally, if there is surplus, they may trade with places beyond the canals for salt, mineral powders, glass, iron parts, or ceramic fittings. But their daily life is fed by the green and wet world they maintain.

They eat in small portions and at irregular times, because machinery does not permit everyone to pause at once. Meals are often carried in wrapped leaf packets or bark bowls. They drink hot infusions made from bark, seeds, or dried leaves. Their hands are always warm when they hold a cup. They do not feast loudly. They share food quietly, often near a pump or a work platform, passing things with a nod rather than a ceremony. Still, there are moments of pleasure: a rare fruit from a canopy vine, a sweetened starch cake after a difficult repair, the first clean water drawn after a channel has been cleared.

Their social life is shaped by noise, damp, and interdependence.

They do not need large gatherings to feel connected. The settlement is small enough that most people know the sounds of everyone else’s work. A missing pump-note is noticed. A changed footstep is remarked upon. If someone fails to appear at a valve check, another will go looking without drama. They trust routines. They trust maintenance. They trust that if a person is alive and able, they will be found near their task.

There are no great markets, no open plazas full of shouting vendors. The small plazas that exist are centered on pumps, valves, or engine mounts. People meet there in passing. They exchange information in short phrases or signs. A hand is placed on a rail and felt for vibration. A chalk mark is made on bark and later absorbed or overgrown. A tool is left at a junction with a meaning understood by the next worker. Their social order is not built on spectacle but on reliability.

They have rules, though they may not call them laws.

Water must move.
Roots must be understood before they are cut.
Machines must be allowed room to change.
Heat must be contained.
Electricity must be kept dry.
Growth must be guided, not denied.
Noise must be endured where it cannot be changed.
Old things must be given a second use when possible.
Nothing should be made so rigid that it cracks when the settlement shifts.

These rules are not merely practical. They have become ethics. A blocked channel is not just an inconvenience; it is negligence. A bolt tightened without slack is not just poor workmanship; it is arrogance. To kill a structural root without cause is a kind of violence. To waste dead prunings is ingratitude. To ignore a strange sound is to invite disaster. The people of the settlement are patient, but they are not tolerant of carelessness. Carelessness in such a place can rot a floor, flood a workshop, short a cable, or set fire to a wall.

They have affection for their machines.

Not worship, exactly, but something close to respect. The engines are old, and some have outlived the memories of the people who first tended them. Their names, if they have names, are not spoken often. They are known by behavior. This pump is stubborn in cold weather. That boiler likes a slower feed. The dynamo near the east root wall sings if the load is balanced. The oldest gearbox should not be asked to reverse too quickly. Such knowledge is passed hand to hand. A new tender learns by watching, by listening, and by being corrected before a mistake becomes expensive.

When a machine finally fails beyond repair, it is not always removed. Sometimes it is decommissioned and allowed to become part of the place. A dead boiler becomes a support for climbing stems. A broken flywheel becomes a junction plate. A cracked pump housing becomes a basin. The settlement does not erase its dead tools. It composts them into function. Iron becomes trellis. Copper becomes ornament. Glass becomes a window set in a bark wall. The history of labor remains visible, folded into the living structure.

Their relationship with the plants is equally intimate.

They do not see the buildings as separate from the vegetation. A wall that grows is not defective; it is succeeding. A roof that thickens with leaf layers is not overgrown; it is healthy. A stair lifted by a root is not broken; it is asking to be adjusted. The shapers know which plants are load-bearing, which are ornamental, which are useful for drainage, and which must be controlled. They prune without resentment. They graft without sentimentality. They allow beauty, but beauty must not interfere with function. A vine may be lovely, but if it reaches toward a belt drive, it is cut. A flower may open in a crack, but if it attracts too much insect life near an insulator, it is removed.

Still, they allow small wildness.

Moss fills seams where it does no harm. Tiny ferns root in shaded brackets. Epiphytes hang from upper beams and collect rain in little cups. Sometimes a pale moth rests near a lamp, motionless except for the faintest tremor of wings. A snail crosses a wet step and is left alone. A beetle disappears under a bark panel. These creatures are not celebrated, but they are accepted as minor neighbors. The people of the settlement are not hostile to animal life; they simply live in a place where it cannot become dominant. The noise, the vibration, the humidity, the enclosed green, and the machinery have made another kind of ecology possible.

Children are rare enough to be noticed.

When there are children, they move through the ward with a quiet competence that can unsettle outsiders. They learn early not to be afraid of noise. They learn to walk on wet roots before they learn to speak in full sentences. They learn the meaning of different sounds: the healthy hum of a dry cable, the sickly grind of a starved bearing, the hollow rush of water where it should not be. They are given small tasks as soon as they can be trusted: carrying wrapped tools, fetching dry fiber, watching a gauge, holding a lamp. They are not sheltered from work, but they are not exposed recklessly. The settlement is dangerous in its own ways—steam, heat, moving belts, deep channels, electrical fittings—and children are taught to respect those dangers before they are allowed near them.

Some children seem to come from outside, brought in by necessity or drawn by the settlement’s strange steadiness. Others are said to have been grown in the older nurseries, though outsiders rarely see those places and the people who live there do not explain them easily. It may be that both are true. It may be that in such a place the line between birth and cultivation has become less important than continuity. What matters is whether a new life can learn the rhythms. Can it listen? Can it move without breaking? Can it understand that the place is always changing, but slowly? Can it accept that it will be shaped by the settlement even as it helps maintain it?

Their old age is not a decline into uselessness.

Elders often become more valuable as their bodies slow. They may no longer climb canopy screens or haul heavy parts, but they know where the old pipes are buried. They remember which roots were grafted after a flood. They can identify a failing sound because they heard it once decades ago. They teach. They sit with hands on rails and translate vibration into warning. They mark patterns that younger people miss because younger people have not yet lived long enough to see repetition. Some elders become almost still, spending hours in warm corners where engine heat seeps through root walls. They are not idle. They are attending.

Death, when it comes, is folded back into the place.

There is no great animal funeral, no open pyre, no burial in soil that might disturb foundations. The settlement cannot afford careless decomposition any more than it can afford careless fire. The dead are given to the root walls in controlled chambers, or to deep composting beds where their bodies can enrich growth without rotting living structure. Tools they loved may be set beside them, or passed to successors. If a person was especially tied to a machine, a cutting from that machine’s mounting frame may be trained into a new memorial lattice. Names are not always carved; bark heals over marks too quickly.
