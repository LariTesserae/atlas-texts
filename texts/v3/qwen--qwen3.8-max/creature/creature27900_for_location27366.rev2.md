What lives there is not one kind of thing, and cannot be understood as a single creature. It is a population so dense and so interwoven that the word “inhabitants” feels too separate, too polite. These are beings that live by wet stone, by vibration, by chemical traces, by the minute warmth of other bodies. They do not dwell in the darkness so much as they are the darkness made active.

The first of them seem less like animals than extensions of the rock itself. They are low, flattened, slow-pressured things that cling to the basin floors and the worn sides of channels. Their bodies are broad and disk-shaped, or plate-shaped, or shaped like wet coins fused to stone. They have no eyes, no visible face in the ordinary sense. Their undersides are mouths: rough, rasping, endlessly patient scraping surfaces that crop bacterial film, mineral slime, and the thin living skin that forms on damp rock. When they move, they move in inches, in fractions of inches, leaving faint polished trails where the crust has been removed and fresh moisture beads up. These are the mat-grazers, the primary croppers of the living stone. They are not beautiful. They are functional in a way that feels ancient. Their shells or plates are dark and pitted, often crusted with the very mineral deposits they feed upon, so that a still cluster of them is indistinguishable from a rough patch of floor. Only when the surface shifts, when dozens of them slowly change position in response to a new seep or a change in humidity, does the stone reveal itself as covered.

They are not solitary, but they are not social in any deliberate way. They gather because conditions gather them. Where moisture is steady, where microbial crust is thick, they accumulate in layers, edge against edge, body against body, their rasping becoming part of the general noise: a dry, gritty, endless sandpaper sound beneath the louder chorus of the place.

Where the mats are thicker and the stone more humid, there are others that move with more speed. These are longer, more segmented, more restless. They have jointed limbs and flexible bodies, sometimes worm-like, sometimes arthropod-like, sometimes disturbingly between the two. They feed on fungal threads, decaying films, and the waste left by larger animals. Their bodies are pale, often translucent enough that the darker contents of their guts can be felt moving inside them. Some are glossy black, as if lacquered by the same mineral darkness that covers the walls. Their heads, if they can be called heads, bear fine filaments, comb-like feelers, and chemical pits that taste the air and the stone at the same time. They run with their bodies close to the ground, reading gradients of dampness and decay. They are the cleaners, the followers, the underfeeders. Where anything dies, they are already near.

The pools have their own life, and here the creatures seem softer, stranger, more exposed. In the still black water lie swimmers that are not quite insects, not quite crustaceans, not quite anything familiar. Some are little more than flattened sacs with feathery limbs, filtering mineral-rich water through delicate fringes that tremble constantly. They hang near the surface or cling just below the crusted rims, drawing sustenance from suspended particles and microbial matter. Their bodies are often translucent gray or milky white, and when they are disturbed they flash with a faint bioluminescent irritation, a cold sparkle that dies almost immediately. Others are more active: blind, slick, segmented swimmers that row through the water with paddles of stiffened hair or chitin. They hunt smaller pool life, and their movements are quick, nervous, angular. They bump against the rims, turn, dive, rise, and turn again.

The breeding pools are even more crowded. In them, larvae exist in such sheets that the water appears thick, almost curdled. These larvae are soft-bodied, pale, and voracious. Some have sucking mouths, others rasping hooks, others filter baskets formed from modified limbs. They grow quickly, hatch quickly, die quickly. When rain comes from unseen heights and the pools swell, the entire basin seems to answer with reproduction. Eggs laid in dried hollows absorb moisture and swell. Gelatinous masses unfold in the water. Newly hatched forms move in clouds so dense that predators wade into them and simply open their mouths. The sound in those places becomes wet and frantic: a soup of tiny struggles, snapping mandibles, soft ruptures, and the low continuous stir of living matter churning through living matter.

Around the pools gather the ambushers. These do not chase. They wait. Their bodies are shaped to fit the worn hollows and shallow depressions at the water’s edge. They are often broad and low, with limbs folded beneath them, their surfaces dusted with mineral powder or darkened by slime so that they seem to be part of the basin floor. Some have mouths that open sideways, like wet valves. Some have forelimbs or grasping structures that close with sudden speed when vibration touches them. They do not see prey. They feel it. A struggling larva, a stepping grazer, a falling flier—each sends tremor through stone or water, and the ambusher responds before the prey has time to escape. Their stillness is not patience in any moral sense. It is economy. In a place where energy comes slowly and is spent quickly, waiting is a way of being.

The crevices and cracks belong to hunters of another kind. These are long, flattened, elastic-bodied things made for narrow space. They slide into seams too tight for most predators and follow the minute vibrations of prey moving ahead of them. Their bodies are often pale and polished, reducing friction against stone. Their limbs, when they have limbs, are short and strong, capable of bracing against walls. Some lack limbs entirely and move by muscular waves, reading the rock with sensory pits along their sides. They are not fast in open space, but in the compressed dark they are terrible. They can follow a tremor through branching cracks for long periods, turning by subtle differences in signal strength. When they catch something, they do not make much noise. There is a brief struggle, a soft crushing, then the slow process of consumption. Sometimes their bodies become stuck in the very fissures they hunt, and then they are eaten in turn by scavengers that pry them out over days.

The air belongs to fliers, and here the life of the place becomes most weather-like. There are swarms of small winged forms so numerous that they alter the density of the atmosphere. They rise in columns, curtains, and low rolling clouds, especially when moisture gathers. Many of them do not feed as adults. They emerge from larval stages with only one purpose: to swarm, mate, and die. Their wings are thin, often clouded or frayed, their bodies fragile, their mouths absent or reduced. They live for hours, sometimes only long enough to join the swarm and complete the act of reproduction. Their wingbeats make a constant droning hush, a pressure more than a sound. When they turn together, the air seems to shift as if moved by a single mind. They are not intelligent, but their coordination is close enough to suggest one. A predator’s vibration below, a change in temperature, a pulse of chemical signal—these can make the whole swarm bend at once.

When the swarms die, they fall. They fall onto ledges, into pools, across the backs of grazers, into the waiting mouths of things that never look up but only feel the impact. In some basins the dead fliers accumulate in soft drifts, but the drifts do not remain still. They boil with consumption. Small scavengers crawl over them, pull them apart, lay eggs inside them, and are themselves attacked by other scavengers. Fungal threads spread through the remains. Bacterial films bloom. Within a short time, the bodies are no longer bodies but a substrate, a nutrient weather, a temporary richness. Nothing lies unused for long.

Among the fliers there are larger forms, slower and heavier, with broad membranous wings and blunt bodies. They are not numerous, but their wingbeats add depth to the noise. Some are predators of other fliers, catching them in clumsy aerial collisions. Some are carriers of mites or parasitic larvae, their bodies hosting smaller lives while they move through the dark. A few seem almost mechanical in their flight paths, repeating circuits between humid hollows and high unseen ceilings. They do not navigate by sight. They navigate by pressure, by echo, by the texture of air moving over stone. When one strikes a wall or falls injured, others do not notice unless its fall creates a signal worth noticing.

There are also climbers on the walls and low benches: slow, tenacious creatures that move across vertical stone with flattened pads, hooks, or suckers. Some graze on microbial films that grow where condensation is steady. Some wait for smaller animals to fall or be blown against the wall. Their bodies are often darker than the floor-dwellers, their plates more tightly sealed against drying. When the air grows drier, they retreat into pits and seams. When moisture returns, they spread again, leaving faint tracks in the pale mineral dust. From a distance, if there were distance to see from, they might appear as a living stain moving across the rock.

Some of the most unsettling inhabitants are those that seem to be surfaces. They are colonial or gregarious, gathered so closely that their individual shapes disappear. A patch of floor may appear to breathe because it is covered with small bodies expanding and contracting in slow rhythm. A wet seam may glimmer when touched because the organisms packed inside it react chemically to disturbance. A crust that cracksles under pressure may suddenly reveal itself as a layer of living animals hidden beneath a mineral shell. These beings are not exactly individuals. They are accumulations, clusters, pressure-formed communities. They do not build, but they aggregate. They do not speak, but they signal by touch, scent, and vibration. When one part of the cluster is disturbed, the disturbance travels through it, and the whole surface may ripple, tighten, or disperse.

The larger forms of the place are rare, but their presence organizes everything around them. Some are almost immobile, enormous by the standards of that world, though “enormous” here might still mean only the size of a fallen slab or a rounded boulder. They may be filterers, or fungus-feeders, or consumers of mineral-rich seepage. Their bodies are often encrusted with deposits, colonized by smaller organisms, traversed by scavengers. They stand or lie in damp depressions for hours, for days, perhaps for seasons. When they move, it is with a slowness that makes stone seem impatient. Their limbs, if they have limbs, are thick and bracing. Their heads, if they have heads, are blunt and eyeless. Sometimes they produce sound: a low, resonant booming made by vibrating plates within their bodies or by striking stone with a specialized structure. The sound travels through the basin floors and can be felt far away. It may serve to warn rivals, to attract mates, or to establish territories. It is impossible to know with certainty. It simply happens, and the smaller lives around it respond.

Other large forms are hunters. They do not chase in open pursuit, for that would waste too much energy. Instead they patrol slowly, reading tremors across broad areas. Their bodies are low and heavy, their sensory organs spread wide. They may remain still for long intervals, then move with sudden precision toward a struggling creature caught in a pool or pinned by smaller predators. They are not rulers of this place. Nothing rules here. They are only heavier expressions of the same relentless logic: sense, consume, persist.

There are beings that live in the spaces between lives rather than in the open. Parasites, hitchhikers, commensals, and half-living dependents are woven through every larger animal. Some attach themselves to the joints of grazers and feed on secretions. Some live inside the bodies of larvae, consuming them slowly from within. Some ride on the backs of predators, scavenging fragments from kills. Some are so small and numerous that they seem like a condition rather than a species. They do not appear individually to any casual presence, but their effects are everywhere: a weakened swimmer, a torn wing, a grazer moving sluggishly, a predator abandoning a kill because something inside it has begun to feed first.

The inhabitants know one another, if “know” is the word, through nearness and consequence. They do not recognize faces. They recognize chemical signatures, vibration patterns, moisture trails, heat, pressure, and the specific texture of another body moving against stone. Territories, where they exist, are defended by sound, posture, scent, and violence. A grazing territory may be a patch of microbial crust kept wet by a seep. A hunting territory may be a network of crevices or a stretch of pool rim where prey vibrations arrive clearly. A breeding territory may be only a temporary hollow with the right depth, dampness, and chemical residue to trigger egg-laying. These territories are not marked by objects. They are maintained by repeated presence and aggression. When one creature displaces another, there is no boundary line left behind, only the altered pattern of movement.

Their sounds are not language, but they are not meaningless either. Clicks, rasps, booms, wing-drones, and body-drumming carry information. A sudden burst of vibration may signal danger. A low repeated pulse may indicate readiness to mate. The collective noise of feeding may draw more feeders. The silence of a prey animal may attract predators more effectively than movement, because in a place so full of sound, silence becomes a gap worth investigating. Some creatures produce deceptive tremors, imitating the struggle of trapped prey to lure other predators into reach. Others answer the calls of rivals with louder, slower pulses, using stone as an amplifier. In the densest regions, these signals overlap until they become a chorus without center, a living pressure that fills the basin.

There is no gentleness in this life, but there is also no malice. It is not cruel by intention. It is cruel by necessity. Everything is hungry. Everything is edible. Everything is in the process of becoming something else. A falling flier is food. A shed skin is food. A cracked egg is food. A dead predator is food. Even waste is quickly colonized and transformed. The inhabitants do not store much, because storage invites theft and because the place itself recycles with such efficiency that long accumulation is rare. What little surplus appears is seized immediately. The result is an economy of instantaneous consumption, where energy passes from body to body with little pause.

Yet this is not chaos. It has rhythm. It has pulses. When condensation builds and cold rain falls, the inhabitants shift. Dry terraces become populated. Channels that seemed empty fill with movement. Egg masses swell. Swarms rise. Grazers move toward newly wetted crusts. Predators follow. The whole basin reorganizes itself around moisture. When the water retreats, the movement reverses. Life contracts toward pools, seeps, and humid hollows. The population becomes denser in smaller areas, and violence increases. The sound changes too: during wet periods it is broader, more scattered, full of rising wings and fresh feeding; during dry periods it becomes concentrated, sharper, more desperate around the remaining water.

Their migrations, where they occur, are not guided by intention but by accumulated response. They follow chemical trails, humidity gradients, inherited rhythms, and the pressure of other bodies moving in the same direction. Over immense time, these repeated movements have worn shallow grooves into the rock. These grooves are not roads. They were not made as roads. They are erosions caused by countless bodies passing the same way. Along them, life is even more compressed. The stone beneath is polished smooth. The air above is thick with wingbeat and drifting sensory filaments. The sides of the grooves are lined with ambushers and scavengers taking advantage of the flow. To be near such a path is to be inside a current of animals, not a line of travelers.

What lives there also lives in layers. The same stretch of stone may be occupied at different levels. Beneath a dry crust, there may be a moist layer of worms or soft-bodied feeders. Above them, grazers scrape the surface. Above those, fliers skim the air. On the underside of overhangs, clustered creatures wait for falling particles. In crevices below, predators hunt whatever falls through the upper layers. A single basin floor is not one habitat but many stacked habitats, each inhabited by different forms, each exchanging matter and energy with the others. The darkness hides these layers, but vibration reveals them. A footfall, if anything with a foot could be imagined standing there, would send signals through multiple levels of life at once.

Some inhabitants are almost beautiful in the way deep-sea things are beautiful, not for color but for precision. Translucent limbs arranged for filtering. Pale antennae branching like frost. Plates fitted so tightly they seem carved. Mouths shaped exactly to their function. Bodies reduced to what is necessary. Others are ugly by any inherited standard: bloated scavengers, bloated larvae, wet mouths, crushed remains, bodies twisted into seams too small for them. Beauty and ugliness do not matter here. Fitness matters. Persistence matters. The ability to remain wet enough, fed enough, hidden enough, and fertile enough.

There are no voices that ask questions. There are no hands that shape tools. There is no memory kept in objects. The place does not produce culture, because nothing in it needs to remember beyond body, instinct, and immediate signal. If there is learning, it is the learning of pressure: where moisture gathers, where vibrations mean food, where stillness means safety. If there is inheritance, it is chemical and behavioral, passed through reproduction and environmental continuity. The inhabitants do not name the place. They do not name one another. They are not a people. They are an ecology so complete that it behaves almost like a single body with many appetites.

And yet, if one had to say who lives there, the answer would not be only “animals.” The place is also inhabited by fungi in the broad sense, by filamentous beings that spread through waste and dead bodies, threading decay into the food web. It is inhabited by microbial crusts and bacterial films, by the living mineral skins that form the base of everything. These are not green, do not reach upward, do not yearn toward absent light. They spread where chemistry allows. They are eaten, overgrown, buried, and renewed. They are as much part of the living population as the fliers and predators, though they are quieter. Without them, the higher animals would starve. With them, the stone itself becomes a slow food.

To describe what lives there is finally to describe a condition: crowdedness without society, abundance without comfort, motion without destination. The inhabitants are always in contact with one another, directly or indirectly. Even the apparently solitary hunter is embedded in a web of signals, prey, competitors, parasites, and scavengers. Even the clustered surface-life is made of countless separate bodies responding to local pressure. There is no true isolation here, but also no companionship in the tender sense. Bodies are near other bodies because resources draw them, because reproduction demands it, because hunger moves them, because the dead and the living occupy the same limited stone.

If something from outside were to enter, it would not be recognized as a stranger in any conscious way. It would be recognized as temperature, texture, chemical disturbance, vibration. Small creatures would crawl over it to test it. Larger ones would avoid it if it moved too violently, or approach if it seemed weakened. If it remained still long enough, it would become a surface. If it died, it would become food. If it breathed too heavily, its breath would alter the air and draw sensory filaments toward it. The inhabitants would not wonder at it. They would incorporate it.

That is the final truth of what lives there: everything is incorporable. Stone is incorporated into crust. Crust is incorporated into grazer. Grazer into predator. Predator into scavenger. Scavenger into fungus and bacteria. Bacteria back into crust. Sound is incorporated into movement. Moisture is incorporated into reproduction. Death is incorporated into continuation. The beings of that low country are not merely living in the dark. They are the dark’s metabolism, old beyond reckoning, crowded beyond comfort, and complete enough to need nothing from the world above.
