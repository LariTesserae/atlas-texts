The basin is inhabited by something that has not yet decided whether to be a body, a population, or a weather.

At first, you would swear the life there is only the small, obvious life: the midge-columns turning above warm pools, the flat moth-like drinkers pressed to weeping stone, the long-legged marsh things picking their way across slick black shelves, the translucent shapes gliding beneath shallow water with their slow internal lights. All of these are real. All of them matter. But if you remain long enough for one full pulse of heat and rain to pass through the hollow, you begin to suspect that they are not merely separate creatures going about separate needs. They behave like notes in a listening.

What lives there is a tending.

If it must be given a shape, it gathers itself most often near the stilted platform and the shallow pans of dark water where the ground has agreed, for now, to be ground. There, out of mist and drip and the low amber glow, a larger presence arranges itself from the smaller ones. It is not a body so much as a temporary consensus. A dozen long-legged marsh things may become the scaffolding of its legs. The flat winged creatures settle over its back like wet leaves making a cloak. The transparent pool-shapes drift within its chest or throat or whatever central cavity it has, blinking in sequence, giving it the look of a lantern full of slow thoughts. Pale grains rise around it in shimmering veils, undecided between sand, stone, and skin. Black moss thickens where it stands, as if its standing is enough to convince the ground.

It is tall when it chooses height, but not always. It can fold itself low, segmented and patient, moving along the margins like the flattened hunter with too many articulated limbs. It can become nearly flat, spreading across a weeping wall as a film of moisture and intention. It can scatter into a hundred midge-columns when startled, each column vibrating with a fragment of the same attention. Sometimes it seems singular: one warden, one keeper, one unfinished priest of the basin. Sometimes it seems plural beyond counting, a congregation of small lives that only briefly agree on the outline of a self.

Both impressions are correct.

Its preferred form, when the basin is calm, is that of a wading figure made of wet ceramic, horn, moss, and water. The legs are long and widely spaced, ending in broad pads that distribute weight over uncertain surfaces. The joints move with the careful excess of something still learning where its limbs end. Its torso, if it can be called that, is a lattice: pale mineral grains suspended in a faint shimmer, sealed here and there by patches of slick black stone, broken by gaps through which mist passes. Within those gaps, small creatures move as organs might move—bright larvae pulsing, moth-wing surfaces opening and closing, threads of microbial film sliding like slow green-black blood.

It has no face in the human sense. Where a face might be, there is a shallow concavity, a lens of still water held in a ring of dark metal or shell-like substance. That lens does not reflect the basin as it is. It reflects the basin a moment behind, or a moment ahead, or as it might be if one small adjustment were made. When the being turns its head—if it is a head—the reflection lags, and you see droplets falling in the glassy surface before they fall in the air around you.

Its hands, when it makes hands, are bundles of reed-like stems, stiff as wire, translucent as horn, trembling even in stillness. From the tips of those stems it produces sound: not music exactly, but precise pressure. A tap. A hollow note. A thin ringing tone that makes condensation bead and run in new directions. Water follows sound in the basin, and this being knows it. It does not command the water so much as propose a path. A hum low enough to be felt in the teeth can make a puddle lean toward a crack. A sharper chirp, glass-bright and sudden, can make droplets hang suspended until the air itself seems to thicken around them.

It lives by calibration.

It moves through the basin in slow rounds. It visits the warm seams where heat rises through the unfinished ground. It pauses beside pools whose bottoms glow with faint geometries, and it lowers its lens-face until the surface stills. Then it listens. If the geometry is stable, it moves on. If it is not, the being unfolds one of its limbs over the water and releases a swarm of particulate light. The light sinks into the pool like sediment falling upward. Beneath the surface, lines brighten, correct themselves, and fade. The pool may deepen after that. It may become shallower. It may close entirely and become a patch of matte chalky stone. The being does not seem disappointed by any of these outcomes. It is not trying to preserve one version of the basin. It is trying to find which versions will hold.

On firmer terraces, it tends the low black moss. It does not plant the moss in any ordinary way. It stands over bare places and makes a sequence of small sounds with its reed-fingers, clicks and resonances that seem to irritate the air into becoming fertile. Where the sound settles, microbial films spread, dark and oily at first, then furred with moss. These are the places the basin accepts as ground. The being marks them by lingering there, pressing its wide feet into the warmth, letting its body weight become part of the pattern. Sometimes it lies down, if lying is the word, spreading across the moss like a wet cloak, and for a while the moss grows over it. When it rises, the ground is more real than it was before.

It eats, but not in a way that is easy to watch or interpret. Sometimes it feeds on the midges, unfolding suddenly to catch them in a net of limbs and condensation. Sometimes it seems to feed on warmth alone, standing over a vent with its chest-lattice open, drawing in copper-smelling vapor. Sometimes it presses its lens-face to the inscribed plates leaning against the rock, and the microscopic patterns on their surfaces brighten while its own internal lights dim, as if it is taking in information instead of nourishment. Sometimes it drinks through the flat moth-creatures that rest upon it, their undersides absorbing condensation, their wings trembling as the water passes into the larger whole. Whether these are separate acts of feeding or different aspects of one metabolism is difficult to say. The basin does not offer clean distinctions.

The segmented hunter seen along the margins is both part of it and not part of it. When the hunter moves, tapping its articulated limbs against stone in rapid irregular code, the being’s larger form may be nowhere to be seen. Yet the code travels through the water, and the basin answers. A pool ripples. A swarm rises. The hunter unfolds and strikes. Fliers burst upward, chirping like struck glass, and the sound triggers other swarms. In the chaos, the being may assemble itself briefly out of the scattered wings and droplets, taking advantage of the disturbance to move quickly across ground that is otherwise too uncertain. When the basin resettles into its loud, dripping quiet, the hunter remains still, tasting the air with feathery feelers. It is not clear whether the hunter serves the being, is the being, or is a habit the being has not yet learned to abandon.

The built fragments are its work, or its sheddings, or its unfinished thoughts made solid.

The stilted platform near the firmer terrace is one of its favored resting places. The ceramic shards and polished stones in its floor are not merely arranged; they are continually renegotiated. Some pieces are old, worn smooth by some memory of touch. Others are new, still warm, extruded from the mud below during the last heat-pulse. The being stands there when the air is thick and brown-gold before rain, and the platform hums beneath it. The low wall nearby curves without enclosure because the being has not decided whether enclosure is useful. It may be a boundary. It may be a spine. It may be a sentence in a language of mineral and moisture that has not yet reached its verb.

The single doorway opening into mist is not a ruin. It is a threshold with no room attached because the being does not need rooms. It needs transitions. Beyond the doorway, the shallow basin full of moving water and tiny bright larvae is not empty. It is a nursery, or a memory pool, or a place where the being keeps its earliest stages. The larvae there blink in slow sequences, and when the larger presence approaches, they all pulse together, as if recognizing a chord they have not yet learned to sing.

The broken causeway is one of its most telling works. Its segments of rough basalt and smooth white composite are not meant to remain fixed. The missing sections, filled only by hard light or compacted vapor that solidifies under weight, are not failures. They are invitations. The being crosses by making the crossing necessary. It steps, and the basin provides a floor because the pattern of its step asks for one. If another creature follows too quickly, without the right rhythm, the floor may not appear. This is not cruelty. It is procedure. Solid ground follows pattern. The being has a pattern. The basin knows it.

It sleeps, if sleep is the word, in the breathing shelter. The walls of layered moss and ceramic are stable enough, but the roof is a membrane stretched by unseen forces, rising and falling with pressure changes in the air. Beneath it, the being reduces itself. The long limbs fold. The moth-wing surfaces settle. The larvae-lights inside its lattice darken to slow amber points. The membrane breathes over it, and the water in nearby pools breathes too, rising and falling in miniature sympathy. During these periods, the basin becomes quieter, though never silent.
