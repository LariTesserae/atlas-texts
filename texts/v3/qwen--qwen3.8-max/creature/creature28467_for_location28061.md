What lives there does not live as a single occupant lives in a house. It lives as a crew lives in an engine room: spaced out, alert to pressure, each small life fitted into a niche that is mostly cold stone and moving water.

No one owns the hollow. Nothing makes a permanent bed there, no den is dug, no nest built into the wet walls. The residents are workers, passers-through, hunters, filterers, listeners, and hidden breathers. They are distributed like parts of a mechanism that has only just begun to run.

The first lives are the ones that seem least like animals at all.

Moss lives there in thick black-green cushions, gripping every surface stable enough to hold it. It is not decorative moss, not soft lawn moss, but a cold, saturated fur that drinks through its whole body and keeps the stone from drying. When the hollow is quiet, the moss seems to be sleeping. When water runs, it darkens and tightens and becomes almost metallic. Liverworts lie against the wet rock like pressed leaves, flat and glossy, so dark they look bruised. They live in the thinnest films of water, in places where spray falls and evaporates and falls again. Sedges lean down from the torn rim with their roots exposed, tasting the air of the hollow as if undecided whether to enter it. Ferns are few and stiff, their fronds short from cold, uncurling slowly as if each inch costs them something.

A dwarf willow root hangs from the broken ceiling, bared by the collapse. It is not the willow itself that lives in the hollow, only one wandering limb of it, but that root becomes a presence: a dripping cord, a slow metronome, a living pipe feeding drops into the pool. It has taken on the room’s habits. It no longer belongs entirely to the marsh above.

In the dim water, algae lives not as bright weed but as olive and black-green films, thin skins laid over stone. Where the weak light reaches, it works quietly, exhaling small beads of oxygen that cling and tremble before rising. Those bubbles are among the few bright moving things in the pool. They look like sparks struck from the water.

Fungi live in the corners where peat fibers and shredded leaves collect. They are not large, not showy. They appear as pale threads, small cold discs, faintly shining surfaces that give off the barest suggestion of light when the hollow is very dark. Their glow is not enough to reveal anything; it only makes the darkness seem aware of itself. They are among the place’s decomposers, but because of the cold they do their work slowly, preserving almost as much as they consume.

Then come the smaller animals, the insect lives that give the hollow its busiest sense of manufacture.

Caddisfly larvae live in the shallows like little wandering builders. Each one carries a case made from the hollow itself: grit, tiny flakes of rock, fragments of leaf, bits of root, all bound with silk. They move over the bottom with absurd seriousness, dragging their portable architecture through the cold. They are always repairing, enlarging, anchoring themselves against the pull of water. In a place with no artifacts, they are the closest thing to makers. Their cases are not tools left behind; they are worn houses, living armors, part of the body’s extension.

Stonefly nymphs cling beneath stones where the current is strongest. They are flattened, patient, clawed, their bodies shaped like they have been pressed by the water itself. Their gills flutter in thin rhythms. They live under stress, in the places where the pool’s throat tightens and the flow becomes urgent. If one is dislodged, it tumbles briefly, then catches again, or is carried into the dark exit where the water disappears through cracks.

Black flies and midges hover in the damp air, their wings making that fine electric whine that seems less heard than felt along the skin. They are small enough to seem like weather rather than creatures, a living part of the mist. They gather where the air is still, then scatter when a drip falls or a bat’s wing cuts the dark. Their larvae live in the wet films on stone, in the places where water moves too fast for larger things to rest.

Other small lives are glimpsed rather than understood: pale springtails in the moss, tiny worms in the black sediment, water mites moving like red-brown beads, perhaps a hunting spider under an overhang where spray cannot reach it. These are not the hollow’s protagonists. They are its fine machinery, the lives that make the larger lives possible.

Among all this, the water shrew is the most intensely alive.

It is a tiny thing, but it brings heat into the cold room. It runs the margin with nervous speed, whiskers working, body low, tail flicking. Its fur is dense and dark, carrying silver bubbles when it dives. It hunts larvae, worms, anything small enough to be seized and crushed. It is always hungry. Its heart beats like a small engine pushed too high. In the hollow’s blue-green shadow, it seems almost too warm to belong there, too quick, too urgent, too mammalian.

The shrew does not live in the pool so much as it raids it. It appears, hunts, kills, disappears into a root tangle or a dry crack, then returns. It is one of the few lives there that makes the place feel suddenly dangerous in a familiar way. When it splashes, the whole room seems to notice. The sound is too large for the body. The hollow magnifies it into an event.

The dipper, if it comes, is different. It does not bring warmth; it brings precision.

It stands on a wet stone as if it were part of the current’s own instrumentation, bobbing once, twice, reading the flow. Its feathers are gray and tight, its body compact, its eye dark and bright. It does not hesitate long. It drops into the pool and becomes for a moment impossible: a bird under cold water, walking or swimming along the bottom, hunting in the same world as the larvae. Then it rises, shakes itself, and returns to its bobbing.

The dipper seems to belong to the hollow’s industrial character more than any other bird. It does not sing the place into softness. It measures it. It tests it. It treats the pool as a working surface. If the water is too high or too cold or too turbid, the dipper leaves. If it stays, it makes the hollow feel tolerable, briefly calibrated, as if some living thing has inspected the engine and found it still functioning.

Salamanders live in the darkest crevices, where the water is coldest and the light fails. They are not numerous. One may be found under a slab, slick and still, its skin black or smoke-brown, perhaps with a dull gold or rust-colored marking. It moves with great slowness, not from weakness but from a different tempo. It does not hurry because the hollow does not reward hurry. It waits, tastes, shifts, waits again. When it moves, it seems to move the whole room’s silence with it.

A salamander in that place feels less like prey or predator than like a valve. It is a soft, living body fitted into hard, sharp stone. It breathes through wet surfaces. It endures the cold by becoming patient. If disturbed, it retreats deeper into the crack, and the hollow seems to close around it.

Frogs live there mostly as voices.

They are hidden in ledges, in seeps, in pockets where cold water threads through stone. You hear them before you see them: a rattling croak, a short wet note, then another. The chamber takes their calls and thickens them, makes them come from several places at once. In the dimness, a single frog can sound like three. A pair can make the whole pool seem inhabited by something larger.

When the frogs call, the hollow becomes a resonating body. The sound is not pleasant in the ordinary sense. It is not pastoral. It is mechanical and amphibian at once, like the room clearing its throat. When rain rises and the intake fissure gushes, the frogs stop. Their silence is part of the flood’s authority.

Bats do not live in the hollow, but they pass through it at dusk like inspectors making a brief round. They brush the upper darkness, wings snapping softly, mouths opening to take midges and flies from the air. Their presence is almost too fast to register. One moment the space above the pool is empty; the next, a shadow cuts through it and an insect’s whine stops. They do not stay. They do not nest there. The hollow is too wet, too cold, too loud for them to remain. But they use it, and their brief visits connect the sunken room to the larger night outside.

Below all these visible lives are the unseen ones, the ones that make the hollow feel electrified and productive even when nothing moves.

Bacteria live in the films on stone. Some lay down iron stains, turning seepage into rust bands, making the rock look banded with old heat even though the place is cold. Others work at the slow chemistry of decay, breaking leaves, peat fibers, insect husks, and dead moss into dark sediment. Diatoms, if light permits, make glassy films in the shallows. Rotifers and microscopic worms live in the water trapped among moss. Tardigrades may persist in the wet cushions, shrinking and reviving with the changes in moisture. They are almost imaginary in their smallness, but they belong exactly here: lives adapted to interruption, to cold, to wet, to pause.

These are the lives that give the hollow its faint sense of charge. Not literal lightning, though the air can smell sharp after a surge, but the feeling of many small gradients meeting: acid peat water touching mineral rock, cold seepage crossing warmer damp, living films altering the surface of stone. The place prickles with process. It is not haunted. It is busy.

If one asks who lives there in the singular, the answer begins to shift.

No person lives there. A human body would be an obstruction in such a room. It would bring too much heat, too much clumsiness, too much need for stillness. The cold would push it out. The water would make it slip. The sound would become unbearable within minutes. The sharp rock would cut. The wet would enter clothing and stay there. Anything built would fail. Anything carried would rust, rot, or be scoured away. The hollow is not made for a human tenant. It is not even made for comfort.

Yet if there is a “who,” it is not altogether absent. It is composite. It is the hollow itself considered as a living body.

The pool is its heart and mouth. The narrow throat where water enters and exits is its valve. The fissures are its hidden passages. The broken columns and slabs are its bones, newly exposed and still sharp. The moss is its skin. The hanging roots are tendons pulled loose from the marsh above. The frost on the sharpest edges is its enamel. The weak light through the crooked opening is not an eye, exactly, but a cold aperture, a place where the room becomes aware of outside.

Its breath is water. It inhales rain. It exhales through unseen cracks. When the weather changes, the whole body changes state. Light rain makes its walls whisper. Heavy rain turns it into a throat, a sluice, a roaring chamber. In drought, it slows, condenses, holds moisture in the moss like a miser holding breath. In frost, it hardens at the edges. In thaw, it sweats and drips from every surface.

Its voice is the drone of water over stone. That sound is not accidental. It is the noise of the hollow living. The low boom, the grinding hush, the metallic hum, the whistle over fluted rock—these are not decoration. They are signs of pressure, flow, obstruction, release. The room speaks because it works. If it went silent, something would be wrong: the water would have failed, or the hollow would have filled, or the cold would have sealed it shut.

Its thoughts, if it has them, are not thoughts in a human sense. They are changes in pressure. They are rises and falls. They are the sudden cessation of frogs when the current surges. They are the shrew retreating to a dry root. They are the dipper leaving when the pool becomes too turbulent. They are sediment settling after a flood. They are moss swelling when the walls weep again.

Its appetite is not hunger but throughput. It takes in leaves, peat fibers, insects, rain, cold, mineral dust, and weak light. It passes them through itself. It sorts them. It breaks some down. It deposits others. It feeds moss, algae, larvae, shrews, fungi, frogs, and the unseen films on stone. It does not hoard. It cannot. Everything in it is moving or being changed. Even the stone is being cut, worn, stained, and sounded.

In this sense, the hollow is not merely a place where things live. It is itself a living kind of engine.

But it is not one being only. It is also the sum of its separated tenants.

The water shrew is its hot, nervous spark.
The dipper is its measuring instrument.
The salamander is its patience.
The frogs are its echo.
The caddis larvae are its builders.
The stonefly nymphs are its current-grippers.
The mosses are its memory of moisture.
The fungi are its cold lights.
The algae are its breathing.
The bacteria are its slow metallurgists.
The midges are its airborne static.
The bats are its fleeting shadows.

Each life is isolated by cold, water, stone, or shadow. None fills the room. The volume is too large for any single body. The hollow remains visually sparse: a perch of rock, a cushion of moss, a hanging root, a flick of wings, a dark shape under a slab. But that sparseness is what makes every life so noticeable. A single splash becomes an event. A single call becomes a chorus. A single moving leaf becomes a sign of something alive.

There is, too, a suggestion of something else, something not quite nameable, that emerges only if one stays long enough.

It is not an animal exactly. It is not a spirit in the sense of something separate from the place. It is more like the shape the hollow makes when all its processes are felt at once. If the mind insists on giving it a figure, it might be imagined as a low, crouching presence beside the pool: not flesh, but wet root and mineral glitter and condensed air. Its eyes would be reflections without a face. Its hands would be drips falling in regular intervals. Its breath would be the cold that makes itself visible. Its voice would be the drone under all other sounds.

But this figure would be false if made too solid. It would vanish if looked at directly. It exists only in the corner of attention, in the moment when the water’s rhythm seems intentional, when the frogs answer one another like signals, when the shrew freezes and the dipper stops bobbing and the whole chamber appears to be waiting.

That is the nearest thing to a “who” the hollow has: not a ruler, not a monster, not a keeper, but the room’s own gathered awareness, born of water, cold, and living chemistry.

When rain comes, all lives adjust.

The pool rises. The intake gushes. The sound deepens. Insects climb to higher ledges. The shrew withdraws. The frogs fall silent. The dipper leaves. The caddis larvae tighten themselves into their cases. The stonefly nymphs grip harder. The mosses flatten under the surge. The fungi glow faintly in the darker air. The walls weep in threads. The whole hollow becomes briefly more machine than place, more process than landscape.

When the water drops again, life resumes in the same spaced pattern. Hunting returns. Calling returns. Filtering, grazing, depositing, breathing—all begin again. No one celebrates. No one mourns. The hollow simply continues its work.
