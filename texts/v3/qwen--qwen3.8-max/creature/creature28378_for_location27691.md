What lives there is plural before it is singular.

The basin does not offer one emblematic creature, one lord of the marsh, one face to stand for all of it. It produces pressure of life: layered, stacked, crowded, wet, cold, alert. To ask who lives there is to receive not a name but a census, and then to realize the census is impossible, because the boundary between living thing and living system has worn thin in the mud.

The first inhabitants are too small to see.

They move through the silt as motes, as black grains with intention, as engineered microsystems old enough to seem natural and active enough to remain necessary. They are not merely present; they are working. They bind sediment before it becomes slurry. They break down toxins before the water sours. They keep oxygen moving through root zones that would otherwise suffocate in the cold, fibrous dark. Where they gather in sufficient numbers, the mud seems to breathe. Where they pass, the basin becomes a little more stable, a little more capable of supporting reed, fish, bird, and footstep. They are not loved in any human sense, but they are respected by everything that depends on the ground not dissolving beneath it.

They are also part of the marsh’s memory. Some behave according to instructions laid down long before the current channels existed. Others adjust in real time, responding to pH, temperature, flow, salinity, oxygen, the chemical signature of decay, the pressure of a coming surge. They are the smallest residents and, in a way, the most pervasive. If the basin has white blood cells, they are here. If it has gardeners, they are here. If it has a slow, dispersed intelligence, it begins with them.

Then come the filaments.

They run through the mud like black veins, sometimes root, sometimes network, sometimes something in between. They are not passive infrastructure. They live in the sense that they grow, repair, transmit, and react. When a bank begins to fail, they thicken. When a channel becomes too choked, they alter the chemistry around them until other organisms move in to clear it. When something cuts them, they seal quickly, release a mineral tang into the water, and send a warning through nearby systems. Birds avoid stepping on exposed clusters unless they have learned otherwise. Fish brush past them without alarm. The people who move through the marsh treat them with the same caution one might give a sleeping animal or a live wire.

The plants are inhabitants too, and not merely scenery.

The reeds are the most visible citizens. They grow in dense stands, stem against stem, dark green to nearly black, stiff with silica and cold endurance. They are not gentle. Their edges can cut skin. Their roots bind the young ground together. Their dead stems collapse into peat and become the next layer of land. They are colonizers and stabilizers, loud when the wind moves through them, indispensable when the water rises. They do not stand alone; they stand as regiments. Each bed is a territory, a windbreak, a nesting place, a filter, a warning system. When the reeds flatten in a sudden line, something is moving through the water beneath them. When they hiss and rasp without wind, the basin is adjusting.

Mosses live like soaked flesh over rock and root. They hold water, soften sharp edges, and create microhabitats for insects, larvae, and tiny crustaceans. They are not soft in any weak sense. They are patient. They survive frost, submersion, low light, and long darkness. Where they spread over old structural fragments, they make the built world look grown rather than placed. In some places they are so thick that a boot goes in to the ankle and comes out blackened, smelling of cold iron and crushed green.

Low creeping plants with oily leaves spread through the shallows, shedding water from their surfaces, trapping heat in small dark pockets. Bladderwort floats in sheltered corners, its traps working with minute, cold efficiency. Duckweed clusters in still backwaters, bright only by comparison with the black water beneath it. Submerged vegetation moves in slow ribbons through the channels, offering cover to fish and larvae. Dwarf willows and tangled shrubs grip firmer banks, their branches woven into low thickets that resist wind and spray. Birds nest in them. Small animals hide in them. Ice forms on their leaves and glitters briefly before the next surge breaks it loose.

The invertebrate life is dense enough to feel like weather.

Over warm seeps and hidden vents, insects hatch in clouds. They are not delicate summer insects. They are cold-adapted, quick, numerous, often dark-winged, often metallic at the edges. Midges rise in shifting veils. Caddis larvae build cases from basalt chips, fragments of black composite, and tiny flakes of sensor material shed from older systems. Stoneflies cling to weeping rock faces. The larvae of flies and beetles occupy stem pith, root tangles, and the spaces beneath thin ice. Where light touches them, they flash blue-green or vanish into the water.

Crustaceans work through the roots in armored swarms. Some are small as seeds. Some are long, flat, and quick, with pale eyes that catch lamplight. They clean detritus, feed on algae, and become food in turn. Leeches and worm-like forms move through the mud, some natural, some so integrated with the maintenance systems that it is difficult to say where biology ends and engineered function begins. There are eel-like juveniles of several species that slip through flooded gravel, testing crevices, feeding on larvae, hiding from birds and larger fish. Their bodies are sinuous and dark, often marked with faint bioluminescent lines that pulse when they are alarmed.

Fish live in the channels in dense, shifting bodies.

They are not ornamental. They are stocky, quick, and practical, built for cold water and low light. Some are small and silver-dark, moving in schools that turn as one when pressure changes. Some are longer and bottom-heavy, with broad heads and sensitive barbels that taste the water. Their scales rarely shine brightly; instead they give off brief cold flashes, blue or green, as if light itself were scarce and had to be used carefully. They feed on larvae, algae, crustaceans, and the rich organic drift that the basin constantly produces. They spawn among reed roots and submerged shrubs, their eggs adhering to stems and stones. When a surge moves through the marsh, they ride it, using the sudden flow to reach new pools before the water settles again.

Some fish are sensitive to the hidden gradients in the water. They do not understand the engineered fields, but their bodies have learned them. They avoid zones where the current will reverse. They gather where oxygen is being increased. They scatter seconds before a weir releases, not from fear but from a long inheritance of responsiveness. To watch them is to watch the basin’s invisible rules made visible through movement.

Birds are the loudest and most obvious residents.

They do not merely visit; they occupy. Herons stand motionless in black water, their legs like dark stakes, their eyes fixed on the small movements below. Rails and gallinules slip through reed beds with quick, secret movements, their bodies compressed for passing between stems, their calls sharp and complaining. Waterfowl rest in uneasy lines on open sheets, heads lifting together whenever something unseen disturbs them. Ducks and geese use the marsh as stopover and nursery, but some species remain through the cold, huddled in wind-hardened thickets or sheltered channels. Smaller birds move in quick bursts between cover, feeding on insects and seeds, vanishing almost as soon as they are noticed.

The birds nest wherever the basin allows. They use reed islands, mossy hummocks, dwarf willow tangles, abandoned machinery, half-sunken pylons, and the upper shelves of fractured basalt. They roost on old sensor housings without knowing what they are. They defecate on regulator stones and add phosphorus to the system. They carry seeds and eggs from pool to pool. Their alarm cries are part of the marsh’s warning network. When many birds rise at once, the water is about to change. When they fall suddenly silent, something has happened that even the noise cannot explain.

There are other warm-blooded lives in the margins, though fewer than one might expect.

Small mammals with oily fur move along the firmer banks: water shrews, root voles, and things shaped similarly, all adapted to cold wetness and short bursts of activity. They build nests in woven grass and sheltered cavities, sometimes in the hollow cores of broken platforms. They eat insects, eggs, seeds, and small fish. They are prey as often as predators, and their lives are fast. Occasionally a larger, otter-like form slides through the deeper channels, low-bodied and dense-furred, hunting where the fish gather. It does not stay long in one place. The basin is not generous to large bodies that require dry rest and stable ground. But it is not empty of them either.

Then there are the ones who use the shelters.

They are not numerous. The place cannot support dense settlement, and they do not try to force it. They live lightly, in scattered groups or alone, moving between stilted shelters, short walkways, maintenance paths, and old perches that have survived enough surges to be trusted. They are not visitors in the usual sense. They belong here, but they belong the way reeds and herons belong: by fitting into the system, not by commanding it.

If they were ever human, that is only a starting point.

They have been shaped by the basin, or shaped themselves to match it, or both. Their bodies tend toward compact strength, with broad feet for uncertain ground, hands accustomed to wet rope, cold stone, and delicate repair. Their skin often carries a faint blue-green cast from symbiotic algae or from long exposure to the engineered luminescence in the water. Their eyes are adapted to darkness, reflective at the edges, quick to catch movement. They do not move with the confidence of people who trust solid land; they move with balance, testing weight, reading sound, keeping their center low. They are always listening beneath the roar.

They wear layered clothing made from what the marsh provides and what the old systems leave behind: woven sedge, cured fish skin, felted moss, salvaged composite too smooth to be stone and too warm to be metal. Their garments are dark, practical, often patched, often wrapped with cordage or thin straps for tools. They carry lamps, poles, probes, small baskets, repair kits, and sealed pouches. Light is precious, so they do not waste it. A lamp on their belt is not decoration; it is access, warning, or repair.

Their shelters stand above the waterline on legs set deep into gravel, wrapped in creeping plants and sometimes strung with dim marker lights. They are not houses in the sense of permanent domestic spaces. They are places to rest, to dry, to mend, to wait out a surge. Inside, they are close and warm, smelling of reeds, fish oil, wet wool, mineral mud, and the clean electrical undertone of powered fragments. Floors are slatted or layered with mats. Tools hang from hooks. Food is stored high. There is little excess, because anything heavy sinks and anything careless is taken by water.

They do not build cities here. The landscape is still settling. Anything too heavy is undermined. Anything too rigid cracks. Anything too proud is flooded. So they build small, low, temporary, repairable. They use broken machinery as anchors. They use old platforms as roosts or drying racks. They use black composite fragments as cutting surfaces, roof patches, or signal plates. They do not excavate grand things. They know the ground is young and treacherous. They know that what appears to be a bank may be a crust over slurry. They know that a channel that is ankle-deep in the morning can be chest-deep by evening if a threshold releases upstream.

Their social life is organized around routes, thresholds, and warnings rather than fixed property.

A family or work group may control a stretch of safe walkway for a season. A lone tender may keep a lamp line burning through the dark months. Several groups may use the same shelter at different times, leaving small signs of passage: a fresh knot, a repaired slat, a cache of dry tinder, a marker light turned to a new angle. They do not need large gatherings. The marsh is too loud for long-distance speech and too unstable for crowds. Their communities are networks of known paths, trusted signals, and remembered water behavior.

They communicate in ways suited to the noise. Voice is used, but not as a primary channel. The wind in the reeds, the sluicing water, the bird cries, and the deep hum of hidden systems fill the air too completely. They use hand signs, lamp flashes, taps on poles, percussive strikes against hollow fragments, and low throat-clicks that carry through structure better than through air. Their languages are likely practical, dense with terms for water levels, surge signs, ice types, channel conditions, filament states, and bird behavior. They probably have few abstract words for ownership and many for responsibility.

Their knowledge is exact but not displayed as theory.

They know not to linger in the lowest channels when the deep hum begins. They know the difference between a normal rise and a release. They know that a sudden mass takeoff of birds means something is about to shift. They know which reed beds hide new holes. They know which gravel bars will hold weight and which will liquefy under pressure. They know which amber marker lights mean safe access and which mean repair in progress. They know the black filaments in the mud must not be cut. They know that silence, if it comes, is not peace but warning.

They fish, gather, trap, harvest reeds, collect moss, tend small algae beds, and salvage usable fragments. They eat what the marsh gives: fish roasted over sealed burners, insect larvae prepared carefully, tubers from firmer islands, dried waterfowl eggs, edible algae, mineral-rich broths. Their food smells of the place: brine, wet vegetation, iron, cold smoke. They do not waste much. The marsh is abundant, but its abundance is not tame. It can withdraw quickly. A pool that teems one day may drain through a newly opened seam. A reed bed may be flattened by ice. A safe path may vanish under a surge.

They are not primitive. That is the wrong word.

They live among technology beyond modern understanding, but they do not treat it as magic. To them, it is environment. A field gradient that moves water uphill is not miraculous; it is a current one learns to read. A sensor housing covered in moss is not an altar; it is a landmark. A maintenance mote cloud is not a spirit; it is a sign that the sediment is being stabilized. They may not know the full engineering history of these systems. They may not need to. Their expertise is operational, embodied, ecological. They know how to live inside the machine without breaking it.

They are also changed by that closeness.
