What lives there is not one thing, but a scattered, patient congregation.

The first inhabitants are almost too small to be called individuals. They are the living films and crusts: algae blooming in soft green veils over still water, microbial mats tightening the mud where pools shrink, thin colonies that turn wet sediment into something that holds together. They are the plain’s first skin. They make the bare ground less bare. They give the water its faint green scent, its living warmth, its taste of sunlight held in shallow places. Under them, the mud breathes in slow chemical whispers.

Above that living skin come the smaller animals, and then the larger ones, each spaced out as if the landscape had measured them and decided how many it could carry without becoming crowded. Insects skim the lenses of water. Beetles row through the shallows with deliberate, jerky grace. Midges and other soft-winged things rise in thin clouds when the air thickens before rain. Their larvae hang in the water, twitching, curling, waiting. Dragonfly-like forms rest on the tips of rushes, then lift away in flashes of iridescence. Wherever the sun warms a pool, insects gather, and wherever insects gather, the larger lives follow.

Snails move over wet stone and submerged stems with enormous patience. Their shells are old-looking even when they are young: pitted, stained, patched with mineral residue, sometimes furred with algae. They rasp at films of growth, leaving faint trails that shine briefly in ordinary light. In the deeper hollows, small fish hold where the water lasts longest. They are not swift, showy fish. They are dull-bodied, mud-colored, patient things, adapted to low oxygen, warm shallows, and long periods of stillness. They feed on larvae, detritus, and whatever falls onto the surface. When a pool begins to shrink, they retreat to the lowest center, their movements growing slower, until either rain returns or the mud closes over their absence.

Amphibians are everywhere in voice, if not always in sight. They lie in the margins with only their eyes above the water. Their skin is wet, mottled, and soft-edged, matching the clay and silt around them. Their throats pulse as they call. At dusk and after rain, their choruses swell until the plain seems to be breathing in sound. Some are small and numerous, their voices like glass beads struck underwater. Others are heavier, lower, more deliberate, calling from beneath overhanging mud or beside weeping stone. They are the plain’s oldest singers, and their songs are not merely noise. They are announcements of place, water, readiness, and presence.

Birds move through the openness like scattered thoughts. Wading birds stand alone in the shallows, necks curved, still as if carved from weathered bone. They step forward with great care, each footfall testing the mud. Their legs are long, their eyes bright, their patience almost mineral. Other birds pass overhead, calling across the flats in clear, far-apart voices. Some probe the mud. Some take insects from the surface. Some descend only for a moment and then leave again, as though the plain were too wide for them to settle. Their tracks remain for a while: small prints, beak marks, disturbed algae, a feather caught on a sedge stem. Then rain or seepage erases them.

But if one asks who lives there, not merely what lives there, the answer changes.

There are those who belong to the wetland so completely that they seem less like visitors upon it than expressions of it. They are low, broad, amphibious beings, shaped by the same long patience that shaped the plain itself. They have no shelters, no tools, no walls, no paths, no devices. They do not alter the land except by moving through it, and even that movement leaves only brief traces: a depression where a body rested, a ripple widening outward, a track filling slowly with water. They are not makers of things. They are listeners, waiters, breathers, singers, inhabitants.

They might be called the silt-born, though they would not use such a word lightly. To them, names are not labels laid upon the world but sounds drawn out of it. Their language is made of low calls, throat pulses, clicks made underwater, soft exhalations, and pressure changes felt through the mud. Much of what they say does not travel through air at all. It travels through water, through the film on the ground, through the thin skin of the plain. A single vibration can carry far in a flooded hollow. Their speech is therefore slow, resonant, and full of pauses. They do not speak to fill the quiet. They speak when the water is right to carry them.

Their bodies are suited to such a world. They are not tall. Height would be useless here, exposed and wind-touched. Instead they are low-slung and rounded, their backs broad and gently arched like the long flat rises of the land. Their skin is smooth, moist, and porous, able to breathe in water as well as air. It is mottled in the colors of the basin: clay brown, gray-green, pale marl, rust stains, the dusky blue-green of algae in shade. Mineral flecks settle into their skin as they age. Old individuals look almost stony, as if the plain were slowly claiming them back.

Their faces are wide and calm. Their mouths are broad, soft-lipped, made for sifting, browsing, and gentle suction rather than tearing. Their nostrils can close against water and mud. Their eyes sit high on the head, allowing them to lie almost submerged while still seeing the air. The eyes themselves are dark, amber, or gray-gold, with broad, horizontal pupils that give them a wide view of the open plain. A translucent inner lid protects them underwater. When they blink, the movement is slow and unhurried, as if even sight must not disturb the stillness.

Their limbs are short but strong, ending in spreading digits partly joined by soft webbing. The digits are blunt rather than clawed, sensitive rather than grasping. They are used for resting, steering, pressing into mud, and feeling vibrations. They are not hands in the sense of making. They are hands in the sense of knowing. When one of these beings places a palm flat against wet ground, it can tell much: whether the water table is rising, whether the mud is about to give, whether another creature has passed recently, whether the seepage beneath is sweet or sour with minerals. They taste the world through skin as much as mouth.

They live scattered across the basin, never in dense groups. The plain itself discourages crowding. Water decides where life may gather, and because water here is spread wide and thin, so are they. One may occupy a shallow pan for a season. Another may rest beside a weeping rock face where moisture never fully leaves. Others may drift across the firmer rises during drier intervals, moving from one damp hollow to the next. They do not mark territory with objects or barriers. They mark it with sound. A pool is occupied when a voice rises from it and is recognized. If two approach the same hollow in a dry time, they do not fight at once. They lower themselves into the water, taste it, feel one another’s pressure, and usually one moves away. Conflict among them is mostly a matter of calls: deeper tones, longer silences, deliberate turning away. Violence is rare because there is little to defend that can be kept.

They are warm to the touch when the sun has been on the shallows. In hot weather they become slower, more contemplative, more still. They submerge until only their eyes and nostrils show, their bodies sinking into the warm mud as if returning to a familiar shape. When the air cools and mist collects in the hollows, they become more vocal. Rain brings them out in greater numbers. Before rain, they gather near the edges of pools and lift their faces slightly, tasting the dampness. They can sense it long before it arrives: the pressure in the air, the change in the smell of mud, the faint electrical sharpening above the water. Their calls before rain are different from their calls after it. The first are questioning, expectant. The second are fuller, settled, almost satisfied.

They do not build nests. They do not weave, stack, dig channels, or arrange shelters. When they breed, they use what the plain already offers. After heavy rain, when the pans fill and the water spreads into broad shallow sheets, they come together in loose gatherings. These gatherings are not dense. They are spaced rings or scattered points of sound, each individual far enough from the next to remain distinct, yet close enough for the chorus to become one body of vibration. They sing into the water. Their throats swell and darken. Their skin flushes faintly with moisture and heat. The eggs are released into algal films and fine sediment, not placed, not guarded by structures, but attended through presence. Adults remain nearby for a time, calling softly, stirring the water with small movements, keeping the immediate space alive with recognition.

The young begin as aquatic, translucent, soft-tailed things, hardly distinguishable from other small lives in the pools. They feed on microscopic growth and tiny organic particles. As they grow, limbs thicken, lungs strengthen, and their bodies flatten into the low, broad shape of their kind. They disperse when water allows. If the season is wet, they spread widely across the flooded plain. If it is dry, they remain in the deeper hollows, crowded but not trapped, waiting for rain or seepage. Many die. This is not tragedy to them. It is the plain’s arithmetic. The dead sink, soften, and become part of the sediment. Their bodies feed insects, microbes, plants, and eventually the mud itself. Nothing is wasted, because nothing here believes itself separate from the rest.

They eat what the wetland gives. Their food is not hunted in the dramatic sense. It is gathered, sifted, browsed, and occasionally seized when the opportunity presents itself. They consume algae, soft shoots, decaying plant matter, insect larvae, small snails, fallen insects, and the occasional small fish if it is weak or trapped. Their feeding is slow and unhurried. They do not store food. They do not hoard. Their bodies carry enough fat to endure lean intervals, and they know how to lower their needs when water grows scarce. In dry spells they can become almost torpid, half-buried in cool mud, breathing through skin, their heartbeat slow, their awareness dim but not gone. When moisture returns, they wake gradually, first by smell, then by sound, then by movement.

What is remarkable about them is not that they survive there, but that they survive there without turning the place into something else. They have learned, over immense time, to live as the plain lives. They do not fight water. They follow it. They do not resist heat. They sink into it. They do not seek permanence. They accept that the wetland is a shifting negotiation, and they shift with it. Their intelligence is not the intelligence of building, but of attunement. They know where the ground will bear them. They know which hollows hold seepage through dry seasons. They know which stones weep, which mud is sour, which pools will vanish first, which algae films signal good water, which bird calls warn of danger, which insect sounds mean heat, which silence means rain.

Their memory is long but not written. It is carried in song, in bodily habit, in inherited preference, in the way young ones learn to follow certain vibrations. They remember old water levels not as dates but as sensations: the taste of a particular mineral layer, the depth at which a certain call returns, the smell of mud when it has been dry too long. Some of their songs are very old, passed down through countless wet seasons and dry intervals. These songs describe things that no longer exist in the same form: a deeper pool here, a wider sheet of water there, a place where seepage once made the ground tremble faintly at dawn. The songs are not maps. They are more like echoes. To hear an elder sing is to hear the plain remembering itself.

They are not gentle in the way that weakness is gentle. They are gentle in the way that old water is gentle: because they have no need to be sharp. They can be formidable when pressed. If a predator threatens young or disturbs a resting hollow, several adults may begin a deep, resonant call that travels through the water and mud. The sound can disorient, pressing against the inner senses of other creatures. They may also rise together, spreading across the shallows in a slow, deliberate display, making themselves seem larger and more numerous. They do not carry weapons. Their defense is coordination, stillness, camouflage, and the knowledge of where the mud will swallow an intruder while bearing them.

Predators still take them. Birds snatch the young. Large fish in persistent pools may take the unwary. Sometimes drought traps them in shrinking hollows where life becomes too concentrated and death too easy. They know these dangers. They live with them. Their response is not to eliminate danger but to remain aware of it. They are always listening. Even when they seem asleep, some part of them is tuned to the water. A sudden silence can move them as quickly as a sound. In an open land with little cover, stillness is often safer than flight. They have perfected the art of becoming part of the ground. An unmoving silt-born among reeds or weeping stones can be almost impossible to see. Algae may gather on their backs. Mineral film may dull their skin. Small insects may land on them. They permit this. In a way, they become temporary landscapes.

Their relationships with other creatures are complex and quiet. Snails sometimes trail over their resting bodies. Birds may stand near them without alarm, recognizing them as part of the wetland furniture. Insects gather around the warmth they give off. Young ones learn to ignore harmless lives and notice dangerous ones. There is no mastery here. They are embedded in a web of mutual presence. When they move, insects scatter, fish shift, birds watch, and the water records the disturbance. When they are still, the world settles around them again.

They have a kind of society, but it is not built. It is sung. Their gatherings are temporary. Their communities are defined by sound and season rather than by fixed settlement. After rains, when water connects what was separate, they may travel farther and meet more often. Choruses grow larger. Calls cross the flats in long arcs. The plain becomes more vocal, more alive, more like a single breathing body. In dry intervals, the community contracts. Individuals retreat to persistent hollows. Communication becomes sparser. The land feels emptier, though it is not empty. Life is simply waiting lower, closer to water, closer to mud.

They do not own. They occupy. There is a difference. To them, a pool is not property. It is a condition. One may be present in it while it lasts. When it dries, one leaves or dies. There is no shame in leaving, and no claim remains behind. This attitude shapes everything about them. They do not mourn lost places in the way that builders might mourn ruins. They remember them in song, but they do not cling. The plain has taught them that all water is borrowed, and all mud is temporary.

Yet they are not joyless. They have pleasures. They enjoy the first warmth of sun on shallow water after rain. They enjoy the taste of fresh seepage. They enjoy the sound of droplets falling from low plants into still pools. They enjoy the feeling of cool mud under the belly on a hot day. They enjoy the resonance of a chorus when many voices align without effort. Their pleasure is sensory and immediate, but not shallow. It is the pleasure of being exactly suited to a place. When rain comes after a long dry interval, their whole manner changes. They lift. They breathe more deeply. Their skin brightens. They move through the new water with a kind of grave delight.

They also have what might be called reverence, though it has no temple, no object, no carved image, no altar. It is a reverence for seepage. For water that comes slowly. For the way the ground sweats moisture from ancient stone. For the patience of rain. They do not worship gods made in their own image. If they sense anything divine, it is in the slow movement of water through fine ground. Their deepest calls are reserved for moments when the plain is full but not flooded, when every hollow holds a lens of sky, when the air is damp and the insects rise in silvering swarms. Then their voices become lower, longer, more interwoven. One might almost think the land itself was singing through them.

They are old, as peoples go. Not necessarily in the length of individual lives, though some live long, slow lives, their skin thickened by mineral deposits, their movements deliberate as drifting sediment. They are old in habit. Their bodies remember ancient conditions. Their rhythms fit a landscape that has already passed through violence into settlement. They do not carry the urgency of creatures in new, unstable lands.
