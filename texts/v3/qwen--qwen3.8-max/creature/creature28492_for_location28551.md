**What Lives in the Anhydrous Ward**

What lives in the Anhydrous Ward does not announce itself in the manner of life accustomed to wetness, softness, or abundance. Nothing there crowds. Nothing rushes. Life has been reduced to gradient, patience, and pressure: a difference in charge across a crystal face, a faint warmth rising along a tower, a dust-veil leaning through a street, a fracture opening a new surface to colonization. The ward is inhabited, but its inhabitants are sparse, slow, and mostly silent. Some are creatures. Some are growths. Some are little more than recurring behaviors given temporary form.

The first thing to understand is that the city is not occupied the way a house is occupied by a tenant. It is occupied the way a reef is occupied by coral, or a bone by marrow. The living and the built are not fully separate.

### **The Saltwrights**

The most important inhabitants of the ward are not the things that move through its streets, but the things that are its streets, walls, terraces, and towers.

They might be called **Saltwrights**, though the name suggests craft and intention where there is only growth, secretion, and response. They are living mineral fronts: thin, active lattices within the pale evaporite masses, threaded with filaments finer than the dust that drifts through the basin. They are not bodies in any familiar sense. They have no faces, no limbs, no mouths, no eyes. They are more like organized surfaces than organisms, more like living crystal skins than animals or plants.

They are the reason the ward looks built.

The lower city—white, gray, satin-smooth, fibrous, plate-like—is their accumulated presence. They deposit mineral where ion gradients permit. They thicken where stress repeats. They stop where dust scours too fiercely. They arch where rising thermal currents encourage upward accretion. They leave passages open where airflow prevents settling. They seal chambers where stillness allows fine particles to bind. They do not plan streets, yet streets appear where growth is inhibited. They do not design towers, yet towers rise where charged drafts concentrate mineral deposition. They do not make tools, yet the result resembles architecture.

They are sensitive without being nervous. They know the ward as a map of strains: the weight of a grazer crossing a terrace, the slight electrical lean of dust moving along a wall, the subaudible tension in a slender fin before it cracks. When something disturbs them—a falling flake, a predator’s impact, the brush of a glider’s membrane—they may produce that faint blue-green luminescence seen in stressed crystal lattices. It is not a lamp. It is a response, perhaps a signal, perhaps a discharge, perhaps something closer to pain or memory.

They do not flee collapse. Collapse is part of their existence. When a shelf breaks or an arch fails, the Saltwrights are wounded in the only way they can be: fractured. But fracture also disperses them. Fresh surfaces are exposed. Dormant filaments wake. Broken plates become new substrates. A sealed chamber split open may reveal a blue-green glow where a fragment of living lattice has been carried into darkness. In this way, the city rearranges itself without intention. The Saltwrights do not repair the ward; they continue it.

If the ward has a people, they are this: a slow, distributed, mineral intelligence that experiences time in decades and speaks in strain.

### **The Dry Garden**

What passes for vegetation in the Anhydrous Ward is not green, not soft, and not rooted in soil. It is closer to crystal, wire, and paper. It lives by light, heat, electrostatic potential, and the slow exchange of ions across dry surfaces. It is austere, brittle, and beautiful.

The **black filaments** are among the most widespread. They root themselves in cracks where mineral stress produces chemical gradients. They are thin as wire, dark as old glass, and almost motionless. When static charge builds in the air, they orient themselves toward the strongest gradients. At such times they seem less like roots than antennae. Small discharges sometimes flicker along their length, pale and brief. They are not plants in any ordinary sense. They behave more like a nervous system spread across the ward’s brittle surfaces.

The **salt fronds** are stranger. They rise in stiff, pale fans from protected ledges, their surfaces covered in microscopic facets that turn toward the weak sun. They look carved rather than grown. When light shifts, their facets can produce a faint glitter, not bright enough to be called sparkle, more like a cold mineral trembling. If one breaks, it makes a tiny tick and leaves a sparkling fracture. There is no sap, no moisture, no wound. Only interruption.

The **rust crusts** cling to shaded undersides and the interiors of alcoves. They are dry, papery, reddish-brown, sometimes sulfurous yellow at the margins. They metabolize iron compounds and sulfur-bearing minerals, extracting energy from gradients too subtle for larger life to notice. They grow in layers so thin that a century might be recorded in a thickness no greater than a fingernail’s edge. When disturbed, they may release fine spore-like particles into the air, but these are not seeds in a wet-world sense. They are dormant chemical packages, waiting for the right charge, the right temperature, the right surface.

Together these growths form the ward’s dry garden. They do not rustle. They do not sway except when moved by air. They do not invite touch. Yet they are the foundation upon which nearly everything else depends.

### **The Parchment Grazers**

The most common moving creatures in the ward are the **parchment grazers**.

They are low, flat-bodied, and slow. They are not quite insects, not quite reptiles, not quite leaves. They resemble animated sheets of dull parchment or smoked glass, some no larger than a thumbprint, others broad enough to cover a hand. Their bodies are thin to the point of seeming impossible. Viewed edge-on, many disappear almost entirely, becoming mere lines in the dim light.

They move across walls, floors, terraces, and vaulted ceilings, feeding on microbial crusts, charged dust, and the outermost living layers of the Saltwrights. They do not bite or chew in any familiar way. Their undersides are lined with microscopic abrasive plates that rasp mineral films loose. They feed by contact, by slow scraping, by electrostatic attraction of fine particles to their ventral surfaces.

They are nearly silent. The only sound they make is a faint rasping when they cross crystal, so delicate that it can be mistaken for imagination. They leave behind tiny trails: cleared lines in dust, faintly polished patches on salt, small deposits of excreted mineral grains. These grains become part of the ward’s drifting powder. In this way, even the grazers contribute to the city’s slow circulation.

They are prey, but their lives are not dominated by fear. Fear, in such a place, would be too costly. They move slowly because slowness is survivable. They hide by becoming flat, translucent, and still. When a shadow passes over them, they often freeze. If a predator strikes, the encounter is brief. There is no scream, no prolonged struggle. Only a crack, a stillness, and then the long process of incorporation.

Their husks remain. A dead grazer does not rot as wet-world flesh does. It dries, stiffens, and eventually becomes part of the surface on which it died. Mineral creep covers it. Dust settles into its seams. The Saltwrights grow around it. In time, the grazer becomes a faint relief in a wall: a shape like a pressed leaf, a ghost of parchment sealed in salt.
