What lives there is not one resident but a crowded, unfinished plurality. The first thing to understand is that the pocket itself is the oldest inhabitant, though it has only just begun to be. It lives in the way a womb lives, or a kiln, or a mouth full of breath: as a containing body that is also a process. Its floor is a warmed membrane. Its channels are veins. Its reed pipes are windpipes. Its basin is a slow heart. The walls sweat because the walls are alive enough to sweat. The ceiling drips because the ceiling is thinking in water. The scaffolds are not merely leaned there; they are ribs still deciding what chest they belong to. The handprints flex because they are not marks of someone who has gone away. They are the place’s first hands, still attached to the body of the room.

So if you ask who lives there, the answer begins with the chamber itself. It is a newborn landscape with no childhood behind it, no inherited stone, no ancestral soil. It is all arrival. It lives as an act of constant self-assembly. It does not rest. It does not settle. It is always in the middle of becoming something, and everything inside it shares that condition.

Inside that larger life are smaller lives, packed so closely that they cannot be separated cleanly from one another. There is no room for distance there, and therefore no room for loneliness in the ordinary sense. To live in that pocket is to be continually touching, brushing, overlapping, answering. A leaf touches a clay lip. A drip touches a moss cushion. A root touches a buried strip of cloth. A shadow touches another shadow and does not quite know where to end. The inhabitants are not isolated beings; they are knots in a wet, warm, noisy fabric.

The most obvious presences, if you have the patience to see them moving between the reeds and the steam, are the makers.

They are not human, though they are full of hands. They are small, some no larger than a thumb, some as long as a forearm when the room requires a longer reach. They are built from the same materials as the place itself: root-mat, old cloth, charcoal dust, crushed shell, kiln ash, softened clay, copper pins, strips of woven fiber. They have no true bones. Their joints are lashings. Their spines are bundles of cane or rolled bark. Their skin, if it can be called skin, is a patchwork of membranes: thin clay, leaf-fiber, damp paper-like bark, patches of moss pressed smooth by repeated touching. Where a face might be, there is often only an impression: a thumb-hollow, a seed-case, a smooth clay cup, a knot that resembles an eye only because you want it to. Some have no face at all. Some have too many hands. Some have hands instead of heads, and this does not seem to inconvenience them.

They are the hand-born. They come from the handprints in the walls.

When water runs over one of those five-fingered impressions and the fingers flex, a maker may peel away from the surface as if the wall has lent a hand to the air. It stands for a moment on the narrow ledge, dripping, newly compacted, its chest full of charcoal dust and its shoulders still carrying the shape of the wall. Then it goes to work. It does not seem to choose tasks so much as become equal to them. If a channel is weeping where it should not weep, a maker is already there with a pellet of wet clay and a strip of cloth. If a reed has gone flat in the wind-chord, another maker climbs the scaffolding with a bone needle and a length of fiber cord. If a patch of moss has dried at the edge, small hands press it back into life and hold it there until the humidity remembers it.

They move with an exact, insect-like patience. They are never hurried, but they are never still. They climb copper straps, slide along wet tile, disappear into finger-wide drains, reappear behind curtains of reed matting. They work in groups when the room requires many hands, and alone when only one touch will do. Sometimes three of them will hold a single leaf steady while a fourth stitches a tear in its surface with a fish-spine needle. Sometimes one will sit motionless beside a dripping pipe for what feels like an hour, listening, until it decides that the drip is wrong by the width of a hair and must be redirected. They do not complain. They do not celebrate. They repair.

They are not individuals in the way surface creatures are. They are more like verbs that have temporarily gathered bodies. One is mending. Another is tuning. Another is moistening. Another is holding. Another is listening. If a task ends, the maker may return to the wall and become a handprint again, fingers spread faintly in the clay. If it is damaged, others repair it without ceremony, stitching its arm back on, replacing a cracked shoulder with a new twist of fiber, smoothing the clay of its chest. If it is destroyed, it does not die so much as disperse. Its materials are gathered back into the room. Its copper pins are reused. Its charcoal becomes part of a new mixture. Its hands, perhaps, remain as impressions somewhere else.

They are shy, but not because they fear being seen. They are shy because attention changes them. If you stare too directly at one, it may become still and resemble a tool left on a ledge. If you reach for it, it may flatten into a seam or fall apart into cord and wet dust. But if you remain quiet, if your breathing slows and your hands stay open, one may approach. It will not touch you at first. It will stand near your sleeve or your knee and study the warmth coming from you. Then, very carefully, it may press one finger to the nearest wall. A bead of clay will form there. The room will have noticed you through the maker’s touch.

These beings are the pocket’s immune system, its priesthood, its labor force, and its children all at once. They do not worship the place. They maintain it. They do not rule it. They inhabit it the way blood inhabits a body: by moving where needed and leaving no monument behind.

Yet among all their works, they are especially attentive to the basin.

In the basin lives the creature that comes closest to being a single inhabitant of the pocket: the pale salamander.

It is not large. It could rest in two cupped hands with room to spare. Its body is translucent, milky, faintly bluish in the steam-light, and so thinly made that the dark pulse inside it is visible as a slow moving shadow. It has a tail that moves like a ribbon through water. Its feet are small and thoughtful, each toe pressing with ceremonial care against clay or leaf or shard. It has no eyes. Or rather, it has not yet decided whether eyes are necessary. Where eyes might be, there are only shallow depressions, smooth and patient, as if the face is waiting for an opinion to arrive.

The salamander is the pocket’s slowest and most concentrated life. It does not seem to eat in any ordinary way. Sometimes it opens its mouth and drinks heat. Sometimes it lies over the blue mosaic at the bottom of the basin and appears to listen to the water. Sometimes it rises to the surface, places one foot on the rim, and remains there for a long time while the makers pause around it. When it does this, the room becomes more defined. The channels deepen slightly. The moss brightens. The clay lips of the troughs sharpen. It is as if the salamander’s act of touching the edge helps the pocket decide to continue having edges.

It is not a god, exactly. It is too unfinished for that. It is more like a thought the room is having about itself, a thought that has developed a pulse and a body because mere thought was not enough. The makers tend to it without seeming to command it. They clear debris from the basin. They adjust the temperature by opening or closing tiny clay valves. They brush spores from the water’s surface with woven pads. They do not feed it worms, but sometimes the white worms gather near the salamander’s resting place, as if drawn by the same slow gravity.

The salamander casts several shadows. This is one of the unnerving things about it. When it lies still, one shadow may belong to its present body: a soft amphibian curve. Another shadow may belong to a future posture: stretched longer, more angular, with a lifted head. Another may belong to a posture it will never have, perhaps a version of itself that has eyes, or legs better suited to climbing, or a mouth made for sound. The shadows do not frighten the other inhabitants. They are simply part of the salamander’s unfinishedness. It lives in more than one moment at once, and the room permits this because the room itself is not fully located.

Sometimes the salamander sinks slowly out of sight beneath the water, and the basin continues to whisper as though it is still there. Sometimes it is not in the basin at all, but resting under a broad leaf near the brazier grille, its skin glowing faintly from the heat. Sometimes it appears in a place it could not have walked to, as if it has moved by deciding to be elsewhere. It is not capricious. It is simply not bound by the same habits of distance that govern older, more settled creatures.

Around the salamander, and around everything else, are the appointed small lives.

They are rare, as the place requires, but they are not accidental. Each seems placed with the same care as a blue shard in the basin floor.

The white worms live in the channels. They are thin as thread and pale as steam. They draw themselves through the shallow water in long, deliberate curves, leaving no trail except a faint stirring. They are not numerous enough to be called a swarm. There are only a few, perhaps seven, perhaps nine; their number seems to change depending on whether the room is thinking about arithmetic. They feed on mineral film, on spores, on the invisible skin that forms over new water. They are also stitches. When a channel begins to crack, they gather there and move through the seam as if sewing it with their bodies. The makers sometimes watch them work, then add clay where the worms have indicated weakness.

The springtails are smaller and quicker. They are the pocket’s punctuation. They jump from leaf to leaf, from moss cushion to copper pin, from the rim of a seedpod cup to the wet shoulder of a reed pipe. They are not playful, but their movement gives the impression of suddenness in a place otherwise thick with slow heat. They feed on fungal spores and the sweet residue that condenses inside bark membranes. When steam rises too quickly, they scatter. When the air cools by even a fraction, they vanish into crevices. They are the room’s smallest signs of alarm and adjustment. If the springtails leap all at once toward the ceiling, the makers know that a drip is about to fall from an unexpected angle. If they gather near the brazier grille, the heat below is about to deepen.

The eyeless centipede is the most severe of the appointed creatures. It lives behind the reed matting in a narrow space where the clay wall sweats most heavily. Its body is pale, segmented, and mechanically precise. It does not wander. It waits. When it emerges, it does so with the patience of a measuring instrument being drawn from a case. Its many legs move in a ripple that seems less like walking than like calculation. It hunts springtails, but not often. It does not hunt from hunger in the ordinary sense. It hunts when the room requires subtraction. If there is too much jumping, too much agitation, too many small sparks of motion disturbing the balance of leaves and drips, the centipede appears. It catches one springtail with a movement too exact to be called sudden, consumes it in silence, and returns behind the matting. The room’s rhythm settles. The water resumes its proper ticking.

These creatures do not form a full ecosystem. They form a grammar. They are just enough life to make the pocket’s sentences intelligible. They do not multiply beyond their need. They do not leave bones. They do not leave nests. They are appointments, as if the room has borrowed them from a future where they will eventually become necessary.

The green inhabitants are more numerous and more unruly, though they too are housed, trained, fed, and contained. The plants are not decoration. They are citizens.

The moss is the slow congregation. It grows over every horizontal seam in cushions of pale and deep green, thickening wherever water gathers. It is not one plant but many tiny lives pressed into communal softness. It drinks from the air as much as from the channels. The makers tend it, but it also tends the room: catching dust, softening harsh drips, holding moisture against clay so that new ridges can harden without cracking. When the moss swells, the whole pocket seems to breathe more easily.

The ferns are impatient. They unfurl in tight fists and then open too quickly, as if pushed by an unseen hand. They live in woven baskets and along the upper shelves where condensation collects. Their roots are bound in cloth strips, and they seem to accept this confinement in exchange for warmth. Their leaves rasp against one another when the air moves, making a dry, whispering sound beneath the louder water-noises. They are the pocket’s restless thoughts.
