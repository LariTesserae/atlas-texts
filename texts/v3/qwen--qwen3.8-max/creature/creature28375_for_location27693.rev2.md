What lives there is first known by its carefulness.

A visitor’s eye may take a long moment to find them, because they do not stand apart from the hollow. They seem to detach from it only when movement demands it: a hand closing around a valve wheel, a shoulder sliding between two hanging gardens, a face turning briefly in the amber light before disappearing again behind a curtain of woven fiber. The settlement is so densely filled with pipe, leaf, cable, stone, and shadow that the living inhabitants can appear at first to be only another set of functioning parts. Then one of them breathes. One of them lifts a cup. One of them touches a wall with the absent-minded familiarity of someone checking the temperature of a sleeping body.

They are human in origin, but the hollow has trained them into a shape suited to itself.

They tend to be lean and compact, their bodies built less for long strides than for turning sideways through gaps, climbing short vertical routes, and remaining still in hot, close air. Their movements are economical. Nothing in them is wasted—not motion, not breath, not moisture. They walk with a soft, rolling step, feet accustomed to uneven grating, loose mineral grit, slick moss, and the sudden give of a root-bound plank. Their hands are their most expressive feature: broad-palmed, callused in narrow patterns, nicked at the knuckles, extraordinarily sensitive. They can tell by touch whether a pipe is running too warm, whether a cable sheath has begun to split, whether a leaf is swollen with gathered condensation or merely damp with ambient air.

Their skin carries the mark of the place. It is not pallid in the way one might expect from underground life, but stained and altered: faintly mineral where dust has worked into it, faintly greenish in the creases where plant sap and algae have lingered, sometimes with an oily sheen from engine maintenance or the waxy lanolin-like dressings used to protect against heat-rash and dry stone. They sweat sparingly. Their bodies have learned, over years or generations, to hold water close. In the warm dark, their faces often glisten just enough to catch lamplight along cheekbones, brows, and the bridge of the nose.

Their eyes are large in dimness and cautious in brightness. Pupils open wide under the low lamps and the blue-green glow of fungal cultures. They are not night-eyed in the absolute sense, but they have become fluent in half-light. They read the settlement by fragments and textures: the shine of a wet thread, the silhouette of a railing, the way a leaf bends under the weight of a bead. They blink slowly when exposed to sudden glare, and many older residents keep their eyes half-shuttered even in the brightest maintenance hours, as if full sight were an unnecessary extravagance.

Their voices are quiet. This is one of the first things an outsider notices. They speak in low, softened tones, not because they are timid, but because the hollow teaches that sound has consequences. Sharp noise feels dangerous there. It seems to search for cracks. So the people have developed a speech full of pauses, short phrases, and breath-carried consonants. They do not shout. Even anger is often expressed by a lowering of the voice rather than a raising of it. In the louder lower tiers, where engines breathe heavily and boilers tick with heat, they rely heavily on gesture: a lifted hand, a tapped rail, a finger drawn across the throat of a pipe, a palm pressed flat against the air to mean wait. They have signals for stop, for drip, for pressure, for root-growth, for unstable stone, for borrowed passage, for silence.

They dress for friction, moisture, and repair. Their clothing is layered but not bulky: close wraps of woven plant fiber, fungal leather, waxed cloth, and fine mesh that allows heat to pass while keeping skin protected from sharp stone and hot metal. Garments are patched so often that the patches become the garment’s true history. Shoulders and hoods are often treated with dull sealants to shed condensation, while inner layers are soft and absorbent, designed to be wrung out or dried against warm ducts. Belts and harnesses matter more than ornament. A resident is likely to carry a small ceramic cup, a folding blade, a spool of waxed thread, a stub of sealing compound, a finger-width of spare wick, and perhaps a tiny polished stone or knot of cord that serves as personal token, memory, or luck.

They do not decorate themselves with anything that could snag, fall, or waste space. Beauty, when it appears, is functional and subtle: a row of polished ceramic beads sewn into a collar, a pattern of stitched repairs made deliberately visible, a dye of algae-green or rust-brown along a sleeve, a braid bound with copper wire salvaged from a dead coil. Some paint their cheeks or hands with faint lines of mineral dust or bioluminescent paste during rest-shift festivals, but even this tends to look like a continuation of the settlement’s surfaces—as if they are marking themselves as part of the machinery, not apart from it.

They live in vertical households rather than horizontal ones. A family, work-crew, or shared kin-group may occupy a volume that includes a sleeping alcove, a hanging rack, a section of wall, a partial platform, one or more drip-lines, a duct-facing opening, and the right to anchor tools or vines at certain points. Privacy is not achieved by distance but by texture. A drawn mat means do not enter. A shifted leaf-screen means someone is resting. A folded panel means a conversation is happening that does not wish to be overheard. Children learn early not to look too directly into another’s alcove, not because secrecy is shameful, but because attention itself is a kind of pressure in so confined a place.

Their social order is shaped by maintenance.

The people who live there are organized around the care of systems. There are those who tend the engines, those who tend the water threads, those who tend the roots, those who tend the air, those who tend the lamps, those who tend the fungi, those who tend the stone. No role is wholly separate from the others, because nothing in the hollow can be maintained alone. A root-pruner must know something about cables. An engine-tender must understand condensation. A lamp-keeper must know where heat gathers and where moisture fails. A child grows up learning not a single trade, but a web of dependencies.

The lower tiers are inhabited by people who carry the atmosphere of engines on them: warmth, oil, mineral dust, and the faint sourness of heated metal. They are often the strongest in the arms and shoulders, because they haul, brace, and turn heavy things in close quarters. Their voices are even quieter than the others’, because they have lived longest beside slow, absorbing machinery. They know how to listen to a flywheel and tell when its rhythm has changed by a fraction. They keep their hair bound tight or cut very short. Their skin often bears small burn-marks and calluses from hot tools. They are proud of patience. To them, a machine is not a brute; it is a slow animal that must be kept calm.

The middle tiers are fuller of daily life: kitchens, narrow markets, weaving racks, repair benches, sleeping alcoves, and the small communal platforms where people gather to eat, argue softly, mend clothing, or simply sit in shared warmth. These residents move more often between levels and tend to be the most socially visible. They trade favors, repair histories, and water-rights with a fluency that can feel formal to outsiders but is, to them, simply the grammar of survival. Their hands are busy. They shell dried fungal caps, scrape algae from trays, splice cord, sort seeds, fold pressed fiberboard, or measure tiny portions into cups. Even when they seem idle, they are usually still doing something small: twisting thread between fingers, rubbing rust from a bolt, smoothing a leaf, checking the dampness of the air.

The upper tiers belong to air-tenders, garden-keepers, lamp-workers, and those who watch the delicate balance of heat and moisture near the ceiling. These inhabitants are often leaner, quicker in the fingers, and more sensitive to changes in smell or draft. They work among hanging gardens, ventilation screens, condensation sails, and the slow fans that move the hot air in weak tides. They spend much of their time with their faces lifted. Their clothing is often stained green at the cuffs. They know which leaves are ready to be cut, which roots have grown too bold, which shaft is likely to exhale sulfur that day, which lamps must be dimmed to save power for the filters. They are the ones who notice first when the settlement “breathes” differently.

Water is not merely a resource to them. It is law, etiquette, memory, and moral weight.

They do not worship water in a simple way, and they would not call themselves desperate for it; desperation implies lack of control. Instead, they live inside a dense culture of measured moisture. Every drop has a path, and every path has a keeper. A bead forming on a pipe may belong to one household. A thread of condensation running along a copper channel may belong to another. A leaf positioned to catch drip may be tended by a child learning responsibility. A leak is not a private nuisance; it is a public matter. To waste water is not merely careless. It is a kind of violence against the settlement’s body.

They measure wealth not in abundance but in reliability. A rich person is not one who drinks more; it is one who can offer a cup without hesitation because their lines are well kept, their filters clean, their rights respected, their storage sound. A poor person is not necessarily one with less water, but one whose access is uncertain, whose wicks are frayed, whose gutter-rights are contested, whose position in the vertical town leaves them dependent on the goodwill of others. Gifts of water are serious. To offer someone a drink is to say, in effect, that you belong enough to be sustained. To refuse water to a person in true need would be almost unthinkable; to waste it in front of others would be shameful.

They have many words for moisture where an outside language might have only one. They distinguish the first faint film on cool metal from the heavier bead that is ready to fall. They name the sweat of walls after a heat-cycle, the mist that gathers near vents, the slow crawl of water along charged wire, the mineral damp that seeps through new cracks, the artificial rain that falls when upper cooling systems cycle. They speak of water as moving, listening, choosing, returning. This is not mere poetry. In a place where droplets can appear to creep along threads and surfaces, where walls dry and then bead again without obvious cause, it feels accurate to describe moisture as having habits.

Their food reflects the same closed logic. They eat what the hollow can produce or process: fungal cakes dense as bread, algae pastes seasoned with mineral salts, tender shoots from heat-loving ferns, the thick leaves of tank-plants, cultivated roots that grow in warm dark soil made from composted fiber, and occasionally insects or tiny filter-creatures when their populations are managed carefully. Nothing is served dry if it can be served moist. Meals are warm, soft, and often faintly earthy or metallic, depending on the mineral content of the water used. Cooking is done with vent-heat, pressure vessels, slow kilns, and sealed pots. Open flame is rare and feared. The taste of food is deeply tied to place: a faint sulfur tang, a green bitterness from leaf extracts, a comforting warmth from slow-heated grain-fungus, the clean sharpness of distilled water when it is given as a luxury.

Children learn to eat in a way that teaches them not to spill. They are given small bowls, small cups, and foods that hold together. They are praised for cleanliness, for finishing, for returning vessels dry. A child’s first useful task is often to watch a drip-line and call softly when the collection vessel nears full. This teaches patience, attention, and the dignity of small contributions. They are not burdened with terror, but they are taught consequence early. They know that a blocked gutter can rot a wall. They know that a root left unchecked can crack a fitting. They know that a loud cry near an unstable seam can make adults freeze not from anger, but from fear.

Their education is apprenticeship by proximity. They learn by carrying, watching, holding, fetching, listening, and eventually doing. A girl may spend a season shadowing a root-tender, then another with a filter-keeper, then another helping a weaver reinforce cable-sheaths with living vine. A boy may learn engine-rhythm by resting his hand on a warm housing until he can feel a stutter before it becomes audible. No one is taught to master the whole settlement; that would be arrogance. Instead, each person is taught enough of the whole to know when something outside their duty has gone wrong.

They are not without tenderness.

In such close quarters, affection becomes practical. A hand on the back steadying someone on a narrow stair. A cup offered before thirst is spoken. A blanket shifted over a sleeping neighbor when the heat-cycle drops. A parent pressing a child’s face briefly into the curve of a shoulder to quiet a nightmare. Elders are cared for not merely out of reverence but because their knowledge is stored in their bodies: they remember where an old crack ran before it was sealed, how a certain pump sounded when it was failing, which root system can be trusted and which must be cut. Even when their eyes fail, they are useful. Many older residents work by touch, sorting fibers, feeling moisture levels, listening to walls, telling stories in low voices while their hands continue some familiar task.

Their bodies age in the image of the place. Skin becomes lined and warm. Knuckles thicken. Breath shortens in the dense air. The eyes may cloud, but the hands remain precise. Some elders become almost indistinguishable from the settlement’s textures: wrapped in shawls of dark fiber, seated beside a duct, fingers moving slowly over cord or moss, face half-lost in shadow. They are not pushed aside. They are placed where their slowness becomes an asset. The settlement does not value speed. It values continuity.

They have games, but even their games are quiet. Children play at catching imagined drops, at tying knots in the dark, at finding their way along a route by touch alone, at balancing along low rails with arms outspread. They mimic adults with serious little faces, pretending to sample air or measure humidity. They make toys from scrap: tiny waterwheels that turn over a single drip, little woven beetles, puzzles of interlocking tiles, shadow-shapes cut from stiff leaf and held near lamps. Adults play with words, with riddles, with soft humming patterns that imitate engine rhythms. Laughter exists, but it is often breathy, close, shared between two people leaning together over a task.

Their art is small-scale and intimate. They carve shallow patterns into ceramic panels. They weave mats whose designs indicate household lineage or maintenance responsibility. They arrange fragments of rusted metal and polished stone into mosaics on hidden walls. They train bioluminescent algae into faint glowing lines that are both path-markers and decoration. They make low songs meant to be felt more than heard, songs whose rhythms match the turning of flywheels or the drip-cycle of a particular cistern. Some of their most valued objects are maps: not maps of land, but maps of systems. These show water paths, heat vents, cable bundles, root anchors, weak stone, and repair histories. Such maps are continually updated, often with new threads or notches, so that a family’s wall-hanging may become a record of decades.

Their relationship with plants is neither purely agricultural nor purely spiritual. It is intimate, reciprocal, and wary. They know the greenery keeps them alive. Roots bind the young rock. Leaves trap humidity. Vines stabilize cables. Moss filters dust and softens noise. Algae feed them and mark safe routes. Fungi digest waste and become material. But they also know that growth has its own will. A plant in the hollow is not scenery; it is a coworker that can become a problem. They prune with reverence and severity. They thank what they cut. They speak of roots as if roots can overhear. In some districts, when a particularly old root-anchor must be removed because it threatens a valve or a walkway, the work is done slowly, with several people present, as if attending a necessary death.

They tolerate and manage the small fauna with the same careful ambivalence. Pale beetles are allowed because they clean decay. Moth-like insects near the lamps are watched as indicators of air quality and spore balance. Filter-worms are cultivated and harvested. The rumored blind fish or eel in the deeper cisterns is not hunted. It has become a kind of ward: a sign that the lowest water is still alive, still able to support something more fragile than moss or machine. Children are told not to drop stones into the deep reservoirs. Not only because it wastes water or risks damage, but because the dark water there is considered a kind of reserve of stillness. To disturb it without purpose is vulgar.

Their rules are numerous, but most are not arbitrary.

One does not block a drip-line.
One does not let a vessel overflow when a smaller one would do.
One does not hang heavy cloth where it will trap moisture against a load-bearing pin.
One does not place flammable fiber near spark-prone junctions.
One does not prune a root without knowing what it anchors.
One does not walk quickly through a crowded tier when the air is hot and tempers are short.
One does not touch another’s collection thread without permission.
One does not strike stone sharply unless prepared for consequences.
One does not hoard air. This last is less literal than the others, but it is felt strongly: no one is allowed to seal off ventilation for private comfort if it starves another alcove or work-space.

Property among them is not chiefly about floor. It is about volume, access, and flow. A person may own the right to hang a sleeping net from a certain beam, the right to collect from a particular condensation pipe, the right to use warmth rising from a duct during rest-shift, the right to anchor a shelf into a seam, the right to tend a named patch of moss. These rights are inherited, traded, gifted, or revoked. They are recorded in memory, in knotted cords, in marks on metal plates, and in the testimony of neighbors. Because everyone is close to everyone else, reputation matters enormously. A person known for careful maintenance can gain influence far beyond their station. A person known for neglect is watched, corrected, and eventually restricted.

Conflict exists, but it is constrained by the environment. Violence is dangerous in a place where a broken pipe, torn cable, or cracked root-line can harm everyone nearby. Fights are usually broken up quickly, not out of moral delicacy but because bystanders have a direct physical interest in stopping them. Disputes over water-rights, noise, heat access, and root encroachment are common. They are settled by stewards or councils drawn from the relevant systems: an engine-warden, a drip-keeper, an air-tender, a stone-listener. These gatherings are quiet affairs. People speak in turn. Long silences are allowed. The goal is not victory but stability. The settlement cannot afford a wound opened by pride.

They have a strong sense of common vulnerability, and this makes them both generous and severe. They will share food, lend tools, take in a sick neighbor, sit with a grieving household, and work long hours to repair a failing filter. But they are unforgiving of recklessness. A person who wastes water, ignores a leak, or endangers the air system may be assigned harsher labor, lose collection rights, or be moved to a less desirable tier. In extreme cases, they may be sent to the outer dry passages to live with minimal support. This is considered a terrible fate, not because the outside passages are necessarily lethal, but because they are empty of the town’s care. To be separated from the web of drips, ducts, and watchful hands is to become less than fully human in their eyes.

Their time is cyclical rather than solar. They mark the beginning of a working phase by the warming of the lower rock, the brightening of certain lamps, or the first visible bead on collection threads. Rest phases are announced by dimming lights, slower engine rhythm, and the lowering of many voices to a near-whisper. There are longer cycles too: periods when the walls sweat heavily, periods when the air feels drier, periods when a particular vent opens and breathes sulfur into the upper reaches, periods when the artificial rain falls from overhead pipes and everyone pauses to watch the leaves shine. These events are not merely practical; they are emotional weather. The settlement becomes festive in small ways when condensation is abundant. People move more gently. Cups are filled. Children are allowed to stand under safe drips and laugh softly at the touch.

They have rituals, though they might not call them that. When the walls begin to bead after a cooling cycle, work pauses in some districts while vessels are set out and threads adjusted. This pause has the feel of prayer, even if no god is named. When a new pipe is fitted, the first drop it yields may be allowed to fall on the hands of the youngest worker present. When a child comes of age, they may be given a small maintenance responsibility of their own: a wick to tend, a lamp to clean, a section of vine to train. When someone dies, the settlement does what it must with reverence and practicality. The body is not wasted. Moisture is returned. Matter is composted into the beds that feed roots and fungi. The person’s tools are repaired and passed on. Their name may be spoken into an air shaft during a quiet hour, carried away on the warm current until it becomes part of the town’s breath. To an outsider this may seem harsh. To them, it is the final act of belonging.

They are not untouched by fear. They fear fire. They fear dry heat without moisture. They fear loud fractures in the young stone. They fear a failing pump, a poisoned air pocket, a root-system gone rogue, a lamp gallery shorting into sparks. They fear the hollow closing, though they rarely say so plainly. Because the place was recently opened, they live with an awareness that it may still change violently. They respect the rock’s newness. They know the settlement exists by negotiation, not dominance. This gives them a kind of humility that can be mistaken for fatalism. It is not. It is attentiveness.

To meet one of them is to be assessed by small criteria.

They will notice how you carry water. They will notice whether your footsteps scrape. They will notice whether you reach for a dripping pipe without thinking. They will notice if you speak too loudly, and their face may close as if you have raised a blade. If you are respectful, they can be generous in their restrained way. They may offer you a seat near a warm duct, a cup of filtered water, a bowl of soft fungal stew. They may show you how to hold your palm near a leaf to feel the humidity. They may point out a place where the rock glitters with dull crystals when a lamp catches it. They may even smile, though their smiles often begin in the eyes and only later reach the mouth.

They will not understand an outsider’s casual relationship to space, water, or noise. Open sky unsettles them. The idea of a horizon with no ceiling feels exposed, even obscene. They think of the surface as a place where moisture escapes and heat is lost, where sound has too much room and nothing returns. Some are curious about it, especially the young, but most prefer the compressed world they know. They find comfort in the nearness of things: the wall at the shoulder, the leaf brushing the cheek, the hum in the floor, the drip counted into a jar. They feel safest when the settlement is working around them, when every layer is occupied, when the air is thick enough to taste.

They are also, in their own way, beautiful.

Not decorative, not picturesque, not soft. Beautiful as a well-mended joint is beautiful. Beautiful as a vine trained around a hot pipe is beautiful. Beautiful as a row of cups filled by the same patient thread is beautiful. Their faces carry concentration. Their bodies carry repair. Their lives are dense with use. They have made a home inside a wound without pretending the wound is healed. They live within its sharp edges, and in doing so they have given the hollow a kind of breath it did not possess before.

What lives there, then, is not only a people. It is a composite being made of human attention, plant persistence, machine endurance, and stone’s raw new memory. The inhabitants are the conscious part of that being—the part that remembers to wipe the bead from the wire, tighten the root around the bracket, lower the voice, refill the cup, and listen for the next small change in the dark. They are the settlement’s living organ, its warmth made deliberate, its thirst made careful, its silence made human. They live there the way moss lives on a wall: closely, patiently, by holding on.
