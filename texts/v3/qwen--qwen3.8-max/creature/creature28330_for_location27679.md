What lives there is not, at first, a population of discrete bodies. It is a set of bindings.

The valley’s oldest and most numerous inhabitants are the things that make ground possible. They do not live on the land so much as they become the land’s first working surface. Mosses are the primary citizens. They spread in pale, cold plains where there is not yet soil, only mineral silt, crushed rock, and water. They are not beautiful in the ornamental sense. They are functional, absorbent, patient. Each filament and leaflet holds a little more moisture than the air would otherwise surrender. Together they make a skin. That skin catches dust, slows runoff, shelters microbes, and turns bare wetness into something that can be stepped on, though not always trusted.

These mosses live slowly and collectively. No single plant matters. What matters is the mat. In the hard light, the moss fields look almost architectural, like pale green concrete poured in uneven sheets. But when you come close, the surface is dense with tiny lives: beaded water, trapped sediment, minute tunnels of air, the faint smell of wet chlorophyll and cold metal. The mosses do not compete for height. They compete for continuity. Their ambition is not to rise but to persist. They are the valley’s first engineers, more important than any steel structure because they make the basic fact on which the other structures rest: a surface that can hold.

Among them live the sedges, rushes, and low cushion plants. These are the valley’s markers and knot points. They stand in scattered tufts, widely separated, each one a small anchor in the wet ground. Their roots are not dramatic, but they are decisive. They stitch loose sediment into shape. They turn a soft margin into an edge. Where sedges thicken, water slows. Where water slows, particles settle. Where particles settle, the ground becomes a little more credible.

These plants live lives of exposure. They are washed by shallow flows, chilled by night air, brightened by reflected glare, and pressed flat by fog. They do not form a meadow. They form signals. To someone crossing the basin, each tuft is a clue: here is stability, here is depth, here is a place where the root mat has thickened enough to bear weight. The plants themselves are not conscious of this, but they have become legible. In a place with few paths, life itself becomes the map.

Lower still, pressed against wet mineral surfaces, are liverworts, algae, and the first microbial films. These are the quietest residents, but not the least important. They skin the rock faces, line the margins of channels, darken the sides of concrete, and bloom in thin green-brown patches where water moves slowly. They live in films of moisture so thin that they seem almost accidental. Yet they are doing essential work. They bind surfaces. They alter chemistry. They feed the first small cycles of carbon and mineral exchange. Where metal weeps rust, some of them feed on that too. They make the industrial materials of the valley part of a biological process, whether the engineers intended it or not.

Underneath the visible plants, in the saturated peat hollows and anaerobic pockets beneath the root mats, live microbes that have no interest in the landscape’s appearance. They work in darkness and cold. Some break down dead fibers so slowly that the dead material accumulates instead of disappearing. Others transform iron, sulfur, and trace minerals, producing faint smells: a sour sweetness underfoot, a metallic sharpness near stained concrete, a clean ozone-like tang where electrified equipment hums. These microscopic lives are among the true founders of the valley. They are not picturesque. They are necessary. They make the first chemistry of ground.

Animal life is thin, but not absent.

The visible animals are few because the place is loud, cold, wet, and physically unstable. There is little shelter. There is no deep vegetation in which to hide. There are no warm dry banks, no dense thickets, no old trees. Most animals would have to pass through rather than remain. What does live there has either become very small or very transient.

Insects are limited in number, but they are present. Pale flies gather near wet concrete and the shaded sides of valve sheds. Black gnats hover in the rare sheltered folds where plant mats rise slightly above the water. Small beetles move through root litter and dead sedge stems, their bodies dark and quick, not numerous enough to form a swarm but persistent enough to matter. Springtails and mites live within the moss, invisible unless one kneels and looks closely. In the water films and shallow channels, there may be larvae, minute crustaceans, rotifers, nematodes, and other small beings that thrive in cold moving moisture. They do not make the landscape obvious. They live in its pores.

Birds are mostly visitors. A high-altitude bird may cross the bright air in a straight, fast line, using the open space but not trusting it. Perhaps a raptor passes overhead, riding the thin currents. Perhaps a small, hardy bird pauses at the edge of a root mat, feeding briefly where insects gather near a warm transformer box or sheltered culvert. But nesting is rare. The valley is too loud, too exposed, too wet. Birds use it the way one uses a bridge: quickly, and with an awareness of danger.

There are no large animals here. No herds, no dens, no tracks that suggest routine residence. If something larger passes, it does so at the margins, where the basin meets higher ground or descends toward deeper cover. But the valley itself belongs to smaller and stranger forms of life.

And then there are the machines.

They are not alive in the biological sense, but they are inhabitants nonetheless. They occupy the valley. They participate in its processes. They age. They weather. They become colonized. They fail and are repaired. They exert force on the water and therefore on the future shape of the ground. In a place where water is the dominant force, the machines are not mere objects. They are organs of the catchment.

The turbines live below the threshold of easy seeing. Their presence is felt through vibration: a deep cyclic pressure in the ground, a heavy rhythm under the roar. The pumps breathe in long mechanical pulses. The transformers hum. The valves wait, open, close, and wait again. The intake screens gather drifting fragments of plant matter and must be cleared. The sluice gates decide, by fractions, where water may go. The gauges register levels that no human eye could read by looking alone. These machines have lives of function. They have rhythms. They have vulnerabilities. They have dependencies. They are part of the valley’s ecology because the valley cannot now be separated from the systems that manage it.

Some of the machines are new enough to look deliberate. Others have been there long enough to become almost geological. Rust streaks down concrete piers. Calcium crusts form at the edges of weeping joints. Moss grows over steel flanges. Algae darkens the lower faces of culverts. Cables sag into curves that look less like failure than accommodation. Paint flakes, but the structure continues. The machines are not preserved in their original state. They are incorporated. The valley does not reject industry. It accepts it slowly, dampens it, stains it, softens its edges, and sets it to work inside the wider life of water and plants.

There are humans too, though not many.

They are not residents in the ordinary domestic sense. There are no hearths, no gardens, no soft evening lights. If people live in the valley, they live as attendants, watchers, repairers, or temporary custodians. They may come from settlements far below, or they may occupy a few sealed rooms in one of the low buildings whose roof reflects the sky. Their presence is careful and intermittent. They do not fill the place. They move through it like specialists in a hazardous laboratory.

The people who belong there, even briefly, develop a particular kind of body and attention. They wear insulated waterproof layers because the cold gets into sleeves and boots. Their hands are often wet, even when gloved. Their faces are exposed to brightness from above and below, so they squint habitually. They learn to walk slowly because speed is punished by the ground. They know that a footprint filling with water is not a small fact but a warning. They learn to read color: dark saturation means weakness, pale springy mats may hold, bright reflections may be shallow water over mineral ground, and too-perfect green may mean a root skin stretched over something softer.

They do not speak much. The roar makes speech impractical. They communicate by gesture, by written notes, by shouted fragments close to the ear. Some may develop a simplified sign language for work near turbines, weirs, and intake screens. Their voices, when used, are not conversational. They are for alerts, confirmations, and short commands. Silence between them is not quiet; it is merely the absence of human noise beneath the immense water sound.

Their tools are practical and unromantic: wrenches, gauges, grease, sensors, tags, rope, spare seals, sample jars, radios that struggle against interference from weather and terrain. They carry ear protection, but even that only changes the roar into a muffled pressure. They learn the valley by differences. A change in the pitch of water over a weir. A new tremor in a walkway. A valve that resists. A transformer whose hum has shifted. A root mat that compresses differently underfoot. These are the signs they attend to. Their work is not to conquer the valley but to keep its processes within tolerable limits.

If they live there for long, they become marked by it. They grow patient. They stop expecting dryness. They begin to measure time in seasons, water levels, and maintenance cycles rather than hours. They may develop a kind of hydrological reverence, not religious in any formal way, but grounded in the knowledge that the place is larger than their intentions. They do not tame it. They adjust.

There may also be those who come not to work but to observe. Surveyors, ecologists, engineers, recorders of slow change. They take cores from peat. They photograph moss expansion. They measure sediment deposition. They monitor whether a channel is shifting. They leave small markers that will later be swallowed by vegetation or buried in silt. Their presence is even more temporary than the workers’, but their attention is part of the valley’s life now. It is being watched into form.

If one asks for a single “who” in the valley, the truest answer is not a person.

It is a composite inhabitant.

The valley itself is beginning to live as a distributed body. Not conscious in the way an animal is conscious, but organized, responsive, and changing through feedback. Its tissues are plant mats, sediment, water, concrete, steel, cable, and cold air. Its circulation is the movement of water from snowfields, springs, rainfall, and condensation into channels, gates, turbines, root beds, and shallow flats. Its senses are instruments and living surfaces alike: gauges that measure level, screens that detect debris, roots that respond to saturation, mosses that absorb and retain, metal that registers temperature and strain. Its memory is stored in layers: peat accumulating, rust accumulating, mineral crusts forming, channels leaving traces where they once ran, concrete staining at old waterlines.

This composite being has no face. It has tendencies. It tends toward making ground. It tends toward slowing water. It tends toward binding loose matter into provisional platforms. It tends toward integrating whatever is placed inside it. A pipe becomes a perch for moss. A weir becomes a condition for sedge. A transformer’s warmth becomes a microclimate for insects. A walkway becomes a spine of firmness across the wet. Even human decisions become part of the valley’s shape: a gate opened here, a channel diverted there, a mat protected from scour, a culvert left to silt up slowly.

It is not gentle. It does not care for individual convenience. It can swallow a service path if the root mat shifts. It can undermine a concrete apron by changing the direction of a shallow flow. It can corrode steel, crack poorly placed joints, and make soft ground where ground seemed firm. But its violence is slow. Its failures are developmental. It is not destroying itself. It is still deciding what it will become.

The sounds it makes are part of its life. The roar is not empty noise. It is the valley speaking in pressure and flow. The mechanical thrum beneath the water sound is not separate from that speech; it is another voice inside it. The creak of stressed metal, the hiss through grates, the drip from leaves, the sudden pour from a culvert—all of it is continuous, immense, and impersonal. Whatever lives there must live inside that sound. The inhabitants are not merely surrounded by it. They are shaped by it. Insects choose sheltered folds because of it. Humans raise their voices or abandon speech because of it. Machines tune their operation to it. Even plants, in a sense, respond to it, because the movement of water that makes the sound also determines where they can root.

The brightness is also part of what lives there. Life in the valley exists under doubled light: sun from above, reflection from below. That light does not comfort. It exposes. It makes every bead of water visible. It makes rust vivid. It makes the pale moss glow. It makes steel hard to look at. The organisms that survive there are not shaded. They are lit from all directions, chilled while illuminated, clarified without being warmed. To live there is to be visible. There are few hidden places. Even the machines stand out like instruments. Even the few humans are small, bright, and temporary against the basin’s scale.

And yet the valley is not empty.

Its emptiness is a misconception produced by openness. There is little that looks like ordinary abundance, but there is enormous activity. The ground is being made. Sediment is being trapped. Water is being divided, measured, slowed, and used. Peat is beginning. Rust is becoming habitat. Moss is becoming floor. Concrete is becoming reef. The valley is thick with process. What lives there lives as contribution. Each inhabitant, whether plant, microbe, insect, machine, or person, adds something to the unfinished architecture.

There are future inhabitants too, though they do not yet fully exist. They are possibilities waiting in the valley’s trajectory. If peat thickens enough, more insects may come. If sedges become dense, small birds may nest at the margins. If water warms slightly in protected pools, amphibians may appear. If soil develops, shrubs may take hold where the ground is stable. If the industrial layer remains but softens, other pioneers may learn to use it. These future lives are not present in any full sense, but they are latent. The valley is being prepared for them by the slow work of moss, sediment, bacteria, and controlled water.

So the answer to “who lives there” is layered.

The mosses live there. The sedges live there. The cushion plants and liverworts and algae live there. The microbes in the peat and rust live there. The pale flies, black gnats, small beetles, springtails, mites, larvae, and hidden water beings live there. The passing birds live there in the briefest sense. The machines live there as functional beings, weathering and working. The human attendants live there in intervals, with their gauges, gestures, wet boots, and careful steps. And beyond all of them, a larger composite life is assembling itself out of water, root, stone, metal, sound, and time.

No one in the valley lives alone. No one lives in warmth. No one lives without being touched by water. Whatever resides there must accept cold saturation, immense noise, and slow reward. The inhabitants are not masters of the place. They are participants in its becoming.

The valley is being lived into existence.

Its residents are the ones who can endure brightness without warmth, sound without speech, ground that is not yet certain, and time that moves at the pace of sediment. They are the makers of the first usable surface. They are the keepers of the wet machine. They are the sparse, cold, luminous lives of a high basin still deciding what it will be.
