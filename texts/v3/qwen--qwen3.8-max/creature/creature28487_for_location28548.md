In the Cistern of Dry Sound, life has not been abolished; it has been fired.

Nothing there sprouts, bleeds, sweats, or rots. Nothing opens its pores to weather or softens in sleep. What lives has instead been hardened, tuned, and set into relation, the way a bell is set into a tower or a seal is pressed into wax. If you entered expecting breath, you would find none. If you entered expecting hunger, you would find only a more ancient arrangement of appetite. The Cistern is inhabited, but not by anything that asks for water.

The first thing one becomes aware of is not a creature. It is an attention.

The built chamber has been so completely made, so often repaired, so narrowly guarded, that it has acquired a kind of indwelling awareness. The walls listen. The floor plates remember pressure. The mica-covered slits do not merely admit light; they seem to strain faintly toward whatever passes before them. When the air inside changes by the smallest degree—when a lamp-lump shifts in its bronze dish, when a seam of bitumen gives off a dry tick, when a mote of sintered powder falls from a high cornice—the whole place takes notice in a way that is less like thought and more like resonance. The Cistern itself is the outermost body of whatever lives within it.

Yet there are bodies.

The most visible are the keepers, the small fired things that move through the gloom with patient, clicking industry. They are not tall. The largest might reach a person’s chest; most are lower, broad-shouldered and compact, as if designed to keep their balance in a world where no wind should ever move them. They are made of overlapping plates of fired clay, pale biscuit-colored and salt-glazed, joined at their edges with bronze pins, bitumen cord, and thin seams of dark glass. Their limbs move without muscle. Where a joint bends, there is a dry whisper of mineral against mineral, a sound like a fingernail drawn across unglazed ceramic. Their hands have too many fingers, all slender, all exact, each tipped with a polished point of bone-white porcelain or bronze. Some carry brushes made from frayed mineral fiber. Others carry tuning rods, scrapers, needles, little ladles of glowing powder, or flat pans of salt-dust.

They do not have faces in the ordinary sense. Their heads are rounded or boxlike reliquaries, slit with narrow bands of mica where eyes might be. Behind those smoky windows something glimmers when they pass near the lamps: not wet eyes, but a dry luminosity, a tremor of heat-light trapped in mineral lenses. Some of their faces are smooth. Some are scored with fine lines. The oldest among them have had their features repaired so many times that they resemble palimpsests: a new jaw-plate fitted over an older one, a replaced brow sealed with a darker line of bitumen, a crack filled with salt-glass until it shines like a healed wound.

These are the Sintered.

They are not born. They are assembled, fired, corrected, and named by use. A new keeper begins as a collection of parts laid out on a warm floor plate: clay segments, bronze pins, powdered salt, a hollow chest-vessel, a listening-core made from a single clear bead of mineral glass. The older ones work over it for a long time, adjusting each piece by fractions, tapping, heating, cooling, tapping again, until the whole body can hold a tone without cracking. Only when it can do that is it considered alive. Its first act is not to speak, but to listen. Its first labor is to repair the place where it was made.

They move in routes that seem ceremonial until one realizes they are practical. They go from seam to seam, lamp to lamp, wall-plate to wall-plate. They kneel at the edges of floor plates and press their many-fingered hands into the old joins, feeling for weakness. They scrape away powder that has become too coarse. They replace a cracked tile with another exactly like it, or, if none is available, with one so nearly like it that the difference becomes a small offense to be corrected over decades. They polish the bronze dishes until they do not shine, because shine would imply a wetness of surface they do not desire; instead they produce a matte, warmed bloom, a finish like sun-baked metal remembered rather than seen. They feed the shallow dishes with mineral lumps that glow copper and amber, using long tongs and hushed, rhythmic taps. They do not speak while doing this, but their bodies emit a constant faint sound: a dry percussion, a delicate ringing, the noise of exactness.

Their language is not speech. It is impact, friction, and pitch.

They converse by tapping one another’s shoulders, by drawing styluses across their own chests, by striking the floor in patterns too subtle for an outsider to follow. When several of them work together, the room fills with a complex, insectile music: tick, tick, shhh, ping, the soft scrape of clay, the dull knock of bronze, the high hairline whine of salt-glass under pressure. To them, this is not noise. It is law. It is memory. It is the way they keep the Cistern from changing.

They are not gentle in the way living things can be gentle. They are exact. If one of them damages another, the damaged one is not mourned so much as assessed. If the damage is small, it is repaired. If the damage is deep, the broken keeper is opened, its listening-core removed, and its usable plates sorted into the stores beneath the floor. The core is placed in a new body if the new body has earned it. If not, it is sealed into a wall niche, where it continues to listen without moving. The Sintered do not see this as death. They see it as a change in duty.

There are other lives smaller than the keepers, and stranger.

In the old seams of bitumen live the glassworms: pale, segmented things no thicker than a reed, with bodies of layered mineral film and jaws that can rasp salt-crust into powder. They eat fatigue. That is the only way to describe it. They consume the tiny failures in surfaces, the micro-cracks, the dulling of glaze, the places where time has begun to press its thumb into the work of hands. What they excrete is not waste but finer binding material, a dry paste that hardens into something very like the original seal. The keepers tolerate them, even tend them, though they will crush a glassworm without hesitation if it strays into a tuned surface.

In the corners where powder has sintered into soft ridges graze the ash-mites, so small that they seem less like creatures than like movement in the dust. They are the Cistern’s lowest order of animation: dry, pale, many-legged, patient. They do not eat organic matter, because there is none. They eat sameness. They crawl over the fixed powder and remove the particles that have become loose, carrying them into crevices where they are compressed into new surface. They are constantly being stepped on by the keepers. They are constantly being eaten by the moths.

The moths are the Cistern’s only flying things.

They are called kiln-moths, though that is a name an outsider would give them. Their wings are not soft. They are thin plates of hammered copper and mica, so fine that when they beat they make a sound like a coin trembling at the edge of a bowl. They do not feed on flame, because there is no flame; they feed on the radiance of the glowing mineral lumps, hovering before the bronze dishes with their long proboscises extended—delicate needles of hollow glass that draw in not heat itself but the finest vibration heat produces. Their bodies are warm to the touch, dry as paper, and their abdomens are filled with luminous dust. When they mate, they do not touch. They circle one another in precise rings above a lamp, sounding higher and higher until one of them drops, exhausted, into a waiting keeper’s pan. The keeper then opens its abdomen, removes the glowing dust, and uses it to feed a new lamp. The rest of the body is given to the glassworms.

But these are only the lesser lives.

The Cistern also has its fish.

This is the part that surprises those who imagine dryness as emptiness. A cistern, even a dry one, asks to be filled. This one is filled with sound, and sound has its swimmers.

They move through the warm air in loose shoals, though no current carries them. They are long and narrow, made of thin fired clay or beaten bronze, with bodies hollow enough to ring when touched and fins of stiff mica that flicker as they turn. They have no eyes. They have slits along their flanks, like gills, but these are acoustic apertures. They swim by listening. They orient toward tones, recoil from discords, gather wherever the bronze dishes give off their deepest warmth. When a keeper taps a tuning rod, the shoal turns as one body and darts through the gloom, tracing invisible channels of vibration. When the room is quiet, they hang motionless near the ceiling, their fins trembling just enough to remain aloft.

They are beautiful and unnerving.

Their bodies are covered in fine inscriptions, some so small they can only be felt with a fingertip. Each mark is a stored sound. A dry fish may carry in its flank the echo of a tool dropped three centuries ago, the particular scrape of a repair that was never finished, the first tone used to wake a new keeper. If you were to catch one—if you could catch one without breaking it—you would not find flesh inside. You would find a little chamber of polished salt, and in that chamber a single grain of mineral glass trembling in response to the nearest sound. The fish are not alive in the way the keepers are alive. They are alive in the way a memory is alive when it has been given a body.

They breed, if that is the word, when two tones meet perfectly.

The keepers sometimes arrange this deliberately. They stand around a bronze dish and strike a sequence of rods, calling down a convergence of pitches. The fish circle faster and faster until their bodies begin to sing in sympathy. At the moment the chord locks into itself, one of the fish shivers, opens its mouth—a small, dry, soundless mouth—and releases a bead of salt-glass. The bead falls into a prepared pan of warm ash. If the chord remains unbroken, the bead will begin, over many days, to grow a hollow center. If the chord is broken, it becomes only a bead, and the keepers use it for repair.

These fish are the Cistern’s living archive. They are also its immune system. When an unpermitted sound enters the chamber—a crack, a wet cough, a footfall too heavy, a voice too full of breath—the shoal scatters, then regroups around the intrusion. They circle it. They thin it. They take the offending vibration into their inscribed bodies and carry it away into high corners, where they release it as a faint, harmless whisper against the ceiling. They do not destroy sound. They redistribute it.

Above them, in the niches, stand the choir.

They are harder to describe because they resemble what they have ceased to be.

They were once perhaps human, or near enough to human that the shape remains. Now they are tall, narrowed figures, more vessel than body. Their limbs are long and still, wrapped in fired cloth that has become stiff as pottery. Their skin, if it can be called skin, is a pale mineral crust, smooth in places, crazed in others, the color of old bone warmed by lamplight. Their mouths are open, but not in agony. The open mouth is their instrument. Their throats are hollow ceramic shafts. Their chests contain nested resonators: small clay ampullae, bronze diaphragms, beads of salt suspended in cages of wire. When they sing, no breath moves. No moist tongue shapes the sound. Instead, the whole body vibrates, and from the open mouth comes a tone so dry it seems to subtract humidity from the air.

They do not all sing at once.

Most of the time they stand motionless in their alcoves, facing inward toward the center of the Cistern, their mica eyes catching the lamp-glow in slivers. The keepers tend them with more reverence than they show one another, polishing their crusts, tightening the wire cages in their chests, replacing cracked resonators with new ones fired in the lower rooms. Sometimes a keeper will climb onto another’s shoulders to adjust the angle of a singer’s jaw by a fraction so minute it would be invisible to an outsider. When the adjustment is complete, the singer may exhale a single, toneless click. The keepers freeze. Then they move away, satisfied.

When the choir sings, the Cistern changes.

It does not become less still. It becomes more contained. The warm air seems to draw closer to the walls. The fish slow in midair. The mites stop moving. Even the keepers lower their tools and become part of the architecture. The sound is not loud. It is not melodious in any comfortable sense. It is a chord of dryness: a high, mineral ringing underlaid by a pressure too low to hear, a sensation like standing inside a kiln after the fire has gone out and hearing the heat remember itself. The choir sings the absence of water. They sing the absence of sap, saliva, rain, tears, blood, milk, dew, mud, rot, green, birth. They do not sing these things as loss. They sing them as completion.

Some of the singers are older than the visible Cistern.

Their bodies have been repaired beyond original recognition. One has no arms, only sealed shoulder-plates, and yet its chest still holds three intact resonators. Another has been built into its niche so thoroughly that its lower body is continuous with the wall; it no longer stands, it is installed. A third has a face of almost pure salt-glass, translucent and blurred, behind which something moves when the lamps are brightest. These oldest singers rarely sound. When they do, the keepers prostrate themselves, not in worship but in alignment, pressing their bodies flat to the floor plates so that the vibration can pass through them without resistance.

Below the floor plates there are older lives still.

If you pried one up, as the description warns, you would not find soil or bedrock. You would find more making. Older brick. Older metal. Older clay. Older seams of bitumen and salt-glass. But those are not only materials. They are compressed ancestors.

The Cistern has been made over itself so many times that earlier inhabitants have become substrate. They are not dead. They have been reduced to function. Whole keepers of older patterns lie flattened beneath the present floor, their bodies integrated into the foundations, their listening-cores still faintly active, transmitting pressure upward through layers of clay and metal.
