What lives there is not, at first glance, a creature. It is a keeping. The basin has a resident the way a bell has a resonance: not separate from the whole, but noticeable when the whole is struck. If one must give it a name, the best name might be the Green Fitter, because it fits things together, and because it is green, and because it remembers the old work of fitting iron to iron, pipe to valve, bolt to plate, even though its materials now are root, moss, steam, and patience.

It appears, when it appears at all, as a low figure no taller than a person kneeling, though it may also be mistaken for a mound of roots, a fallen section of catwalk, or a burl swollen with moss. It has shoulders, or what seem to be shoulders: rounded humps of bark and corrugated metal healed into one another, furred with ferns. Its back may look like a piece of the wall, because the wall has grown over it, or because it has grown over the wall. It does not stand apart from the place. It is tucked into the place. To see it, one must wait until a drip falls in just the right rhythm and the figure answers by shifting a leaf-valve with a hand made of rootlets.

Its body is a composite of the basin’s two ancestries: factory and swamp. There are plates in it, old boiler plates softened by rust and laced with pale root-fibers, but they are not armor so much as healed skin. Moss grows along the seams like stitching. Where flesh would be, if flesh were required, there are packed layers of leaf-mold, shredded bark, and living tissue dense enough to hold shape. Copper threads run through it in fine lines, some entering its limbs, some vanishing into its chest, so that after rain it gives off a faint hum, as though its nerves were remembering electricity. Water moves inside it visibly, at times, through translucent root-tubes under leaf-skin: slow beads rising, pausing, dividing, descending. It has no blood, unless sap is blood. It has no breath, unless the mist that leaks from its joints is breath.

Its head, if the word is not too generous, is a rounded pressure-burl, smooth in places where bark has healed over metal. There is no face in the ordinary sense. There are gauges. Two small ceramic lenses, nested in moss, collect condensation and hold it like eyes; they do not reflect the world so much as register its wetness. Between them, a needle trembles in a brass socket grown over at the edges. Below, where a mouth might be, there is a short pipe, copper-green and fringed with root-hairs, from which a thin vapor escapes when the figure is adjusting the pressure of something nearby. Its expression, if it has one, is the expression of a dial: patient, exact, unreadable until one notices how the needle moves.

Its hands are its most deliberate parts. They are bundles of root-fingers, finer than wire, capable of parting into threads or thickening into grips. When it touches a leaking joint, the fingers spread over the leak and begin to grow into the seam. When it touches a dry valve, moisture gathers around the touch. When it touches an old tool left half-buried in moss, the tool seems less abandoned afterward, less lost, more like an organ placed on a shelf for later use. The wrench green with algae is not merely lying there; it is being kept. The row of ceramic fuse-holders with their pale seedlings is not accidental; it is a nursery, or a reliquary, or both.

It moves as the place moves: without hurry, without visible decision. It does not step so much as root forward. One limb becomes anchored; the body follows by slow contraction; the previous anchor releases and becomes a limb again. A footprint left behind fills with water before the next movement is complete. If observed directly, it may seem still for a long time, a sculpture of green and rust. If observed long enough, one realizes that the sculpture has changed position by inches, and that the pool beside it is now a little clearer, the dripping a little more regular, the moss over a bare pipe a little thicker. It does not pass through the basin; it continues the basin.

It is not an animal. It has no hunger in the animal sense, no fear, no quickening pulse. Yet it is not a plant either, if a plant is a thing content merely to grow. It tends. That is what makes it a resident rather than a growth. It tends the water. It tends the metal. It tends the sound. It moves through the crowded green fabric and makes small corrections: narrowing a channel that has begun to flow too freely, wrapping a newly exposed wire in a leaf-sheath, pressing moss into a crack where steam has started to whistle too sharply, folding a fallen branch into the floor so that it becomes a support instead of debris. Where it has worked, the place feels more itself. Where it has not yet worked, there may be a slight rawness, a drip out of time, a patch of metal still too bright, a leaf-valve opening and closing with unnecessary agitation.

It feeds, if feeding is the word, by maintenance. It takes rust into its root-hands and draws from it a mineral sharpness that strengthens its tissues. It absorbs warmth from the slow fermentation of old stems and the gentle heat of sealed vessels. It drinks condensation from the air, or perhaps lets the air drink through it. It may also take light, but not eagerly; light is for the upper leaves, the canopy, the floating disks of marsh plants. The Green Fitter lives below that, in the dim green middle distance where white columns of daylight arrive only occasionally and make the mist look architectural. When a beam touches it, the figure does not turn toward the light. It becomes briefly visible: every hair of moss illuminated, every droplet on its shoulders made silver, every copper thread in its body shining like a line of memory.

Its thoughts are measured in drips. One might imagine that such a being has no mind, but that is because its mind is too slow to overhear. It thinks in intervals. It compares one sound with another. It remembers how the basin sounded after the last rain, and how it sounded before the water level rose by the width of a leaf, and how the flywheel hummed when the air was thick enough to carry vibration from one wall to the other. It notices when a drop falls too soon. It notices when a root creaks in the wrong key. It does not panic. It answers. A hiss from a pipe is met by the closing of a leaf-valve. A hollow knock from a buried drum is met by a slow pressure shift through neighboring stems. A visitor’s footfall, if a visitor is foolish or fortunate enough to enter, is met by silence first, then by a change in the pattern of condensation, then by the subtle rearrangement of nearby roots, as if the floor is learning the shape of the intrusion.

It does not speak, but it has grammar. Its grammar is sequence. Drip, pause, drip. Hiss, creak, settle. Thump, long silence, deeper thump. To hear it for long is to feel one’s own heartbeat become embarrassed by its speed. The place already has a rhythm stretched over minutes; the Green Fitter is that rhythm given local intention. It is the part of the basin that listens back.

There are other living things in the basin, but they are not companions in any ordinary sense. The translucent snail on a wet leaf may be merely a snail, or it may be one of the Fitter’s slower senses, carrying a film of moisture across surfaces so that the whole can taste them. The pale water-strider trembling in a bowl-sized pool may be a tiny animal, or it may be a brief thought skimming the surface of a larger mind. The white moth that appears after rain is almost certainly not an animal at all; it looks too much like a scrap of bark given temporary flight, too much like a loose membrane released from some high leaf-valve. It flies without urgency, brushes against a hanging chain, and disappears into the green. If these are creatures, they are minor ones, punctuation rather than speech. The true inhabitant is larger than any single body and smaller than the whole place. It is distributed. It is local. It is both.

Sometimes it seems that there is more than one Green Fitter. In heavy rains, when the basin is full of weeping and every surface runs with water, small forms can be seen moving through the lower channels: hand-sized knots of root and moss pulling themselves along cables, entering pipes, disappearing into the hollow interiors of old boilers. They are not animals. They are detached repairs, temporary organs budded off from the main body when too much needs tending at once. They clean algae from insulators, stitch torn leaf-banners, push seed-cases into soft ground, and carry droplets from overfull depressions to places that have gone dry. When the rain ends and the water table settles, they slow, root themselves, and become part of the wall, or the floor, or a new burl where a gauge may later appear. Some of the knobs that look like sleeping faces on the trunk-walls may be former attendants. Some of the roots shaped like fingers may be their descendants.

The Green Fitter itself may be old, but age means little here. It may have begun when the first root found a seam in a boiler and chose to grow through it rather than around it. It may have begun when the first flywheel slowed enough for moss to take hold of its spokes. It may have begun when the factory stopped being a factory and started becoming a memory that refused to be inert. It is not the ghost of a worker, exactly, though it has the habits of one. It is not the spirit of a machine, exactly, though it has the patience of an engine at idle. It is what happens when the need to maintain meets the need to grow, and the two discover that they are not opposites.

It has rituals, if rituals can be made of maintenance. It returns to certain gauges at intervals measured by light and drip. It touches them with root-fingers, not to move the needles but to feel their trembling. It places objects in shallow niches: a ceramic fuse-holder here, a rusted washer there, a fragment of copper pipe angled so that condensation falls through it and makes a bell-like note. It does not worship these things. It keeps them in circulation. A tool is not dead because it no longer cuts; it may become a seedbed. A door is not useless because it lies flat under ferns; it may be a floor for spores, a plate for moisture, a dark surface under which roots can braid themselves. The Green Fitter understands, in its slow way, that objects are not finished. They are only waiting for a new function.

If something is removed from the basin, the Green Fitter does not pursue it. It fills the absence. This is one of its strangest habits. Where a wrench once lay, a root-burl swells into a wrench-like shape, green and damp. Where a fuse-holder has been taken, a cluster of pale seedlings rises in a neat row. Where a gauge has been pried from its niche, a living knot forms in the wall with a circular depression at its center, and a needle of pale wood eventually grows there, trembling whenever steam passes nearby. These replacements are not perfect. They are too close. They have the uncanny accuracy of memory rather than manufacture. They suggest that the basin remembers its contents not as objects but as roles, and that the Green Fitter is the one who keeps the roles filled.

It is not hostile. It is not welcoming either. It does not recognize a stranger as a stranger so much as a disturbance to be incorporated. If someone stands too long in the basin, the warmth seems to gather around them. Moisture condenses on their clothes. Moss presses faintly into the soles of their boots. A root-tip may appear near a footprint, not reaching, but present. The sounds change: the dripping becomes slightly more deliberate, the flywheel’s hum a little more noticeable, the thump from deep in the plant mass arriving sooner than expected.
