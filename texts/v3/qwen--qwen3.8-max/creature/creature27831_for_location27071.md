Whatever lives there has been taught by sound. It is not life that fills the country; it is life that slips into its pauses.

The plateau does not support abundance so much as permit it, and the permission is always temporary. Life exists in the hollows, in the lulls, in the sheltered folds of stone, in the wet minutes after a fog tide has passed, in the brief green carpets that rise from dormant seed and then vanish. Nothing here is allowed to become too tall, too numerous, too confident. What survives has learned to be low, intermittent, patient, and exact.

The first residents are not always visible as living things. When a basin fills, the stone seems to remember that it once held a body. Eggs that have waited for years as black grains in dry cups begin to swell. Cysts split. Thin films of green spread across the water. Then the pool-life appears: soft-bodied, translucent, almost without color except for the faint clouding of their organs and the dark seeds inside them.
