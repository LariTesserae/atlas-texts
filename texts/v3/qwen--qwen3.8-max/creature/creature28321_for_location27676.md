What lives there is not one thing, but a crowded, patient agreement.

At first, the basin seems to belong to the plants alone. They fill every shelf and corner, climb the walls, roof the lanes, and press their thick leaves against one another until the hollow looks like a green vessel brimming. But that green is not wild in the ordinary sense. It is tended, negotiated with, pruned, trained, honored, and used. It is also watched. Between the leaves there are eyes, and above the leaves there are voices, and along the walls there are hands.

The life of the hollow is dense, dry, and sealed. Nothing there exists without containment. Every creature, every household, every custom has been shaped by the same first fact: there is no open water. What lives there has learned to keep its moisture inside itself, to treat fluid as a hidden inheritance, and to make fullness out of restraint.

The plants are among the oldest residents.

They are not merely scenery. They are co-inhabitants with their own habits, seasons, and temperaments. The broad-leaved terrace growers stand in close ranks, their fat leaves angled toward the dim copper light, their surfaces waxed so thoroughly that dust slides from them in fine sheets when the wind shifts. They do not drip, even when cut. Their sap is thick, resinous, and slow. When a leaf is trimmed, the wound closes with a dark shine rather than a bleed. The people of the hollow know each kind by the sound it makes: the low rasp of stiff blades, the soft knock of seedpods, the creak of resin-vines tightening around a trellis.

Some plants grow for food. Their fruits, if one can call them that, are dry-fleshed capsules, oily seedheads, fibrous bulbs, and nut-like growths that must be roasted, cracked, soaked in oil, or chewed at length. Others grow for structure: leather-fern fronds that can be woven into panels, gray grasses whose roots bind dust into firm mats, vines whose stems become rope when stripped and cured. Some are kept for light. Their seeds yield a slow-burning oil; their resins harden into cakes that smoulder evenly in stone lamps. Some are medicinal in a dry, unsentimental way: pounded leaf-pulp sealed over a wound, resin varnish for cracked skin, powdered bark for calming breath.

The plants also live with the animals. They provide shade, perches, hiding places, nesting fibers, and food. In return, beetles carry away dead matter, moths move pollen in fine dry dust, birds scatter seeds only where the people permit, and small roof-dwellers chew paths through tangled growth that would otherwise close the lanes entirely. Nothing in the basin is permitted to become purely ornamental. Even beauty has a use, and even use is allowed a certain beauty.

The first animal lives one notices are usually the beetles.

They move through the lower leaf-beds in great numbers, unhurried and industrious. Their shells are dull, not because they are plain, but because brightness would be wasted there. Some are the color of old wood, some of mineral ash, some of dark salt-stained copper. They are not pests. They are the basin’s careful undertakers. They consume fallen leaves, dry waste, shed skins, and the fibrous remnants left after harvest. Their ticking feet on stone make one of the constant under-sounds of the hollow: a fine, patient percussion, like small clockwork buried under green.

Children learn early not to crush them. A beetle crossing a path may be moved with a leaf, a fingertip, or a small wooden scoop. Their shells, when they die naturally, are collected and polished into tiny cups, ladles, buttons, and seed-sorts. Some households keep beetle-beds near the compost terraces, where the creatures work through leaf-matter until it becomes usable ground. The smell there is not foul; it is warm, dusty, faintly sweet, like heated straw and old paper.

The moths are gentler, stranger, and more loved.

They have broad wings and quiet bodies, and they rest in clusters beneath eaves, over doorways, and in the upper tangles of vine and pipe. In the dim amber light, they look like soft patches of ash or folded pieces of dusk. Their wings open and close with extraordinary slowness, as if each movement were being considered. They are important pollinators, though they do not work with the wet exuberance of lowland insects. Their pollen is dry, fine, and often slightly oily. It clings to their wings and to the hairs around their mouths. When they move among resin-flowers, they leave behind a faint dust that smells of warm bark and lamp-smoke.

The people of the hollow watch the moths closely. A good clustering of moths under a roof is taken as a sign that the air is sound and the household’s warmth is balanced. If moths leave a terrace suddenly, someone will inspect the wind-tubes, vents, and plantings. They are not worshipped, but they are spoken to softly, as one speaks to anything whose favor one hopes to keep.

The hoppers make the daylight metallic.

They cling to stiff stems and produce short, ringing notes by rubbing parts of their bodies together. The sound is dry and bright, like a tiny struck blade. One hopper begins; another answers from a roof-garden; another from a wall crevice. Soon the terraces are filled with a delicate, overlapping stridulation. The people use these sounds without realizing it at first, then deliberately. Children learn to tell the hour by the density of hopper-song. Workers pause when the notes change, because the hoppers feel shifts in heat and air before the wind-catchers do.

The lizards are quieter.

They lie on warm walls with their bodies flattened against stone, their scales patterned like the mineral grain of the basin. They are not quick in the way lizards are quick elsewhere. Their movements are deliberate, almost ceremonial. They turn their heads. They watch. They take small insects, mites, and stray larvae that might otherwise damage the stored leaf-beds. People do not disturb them. A lizard settled above a doorway is considered a good neighbor. If it lays its parchment eggs in a wall pocket, the household will reroute a hanging bundle or shift a rack rather than disturb the place.

Then there are the roof-runners.

They are the most familiar, the most mischievous, and the most difficult to describe to anyone who has not seen them. They are not rats, not exactly. They are small, dense-bodied, furred in close coats of ash and rust, with short tails, clever forepaws, and faces that seem always to be listening. They live in the wall gaps, roof beams, and hollow spaces between terraces. Their claws make fine scratching sounds at dusk and before the warmest part of the day. They are commensals of the settlement: they eat crumbs, dry seed husks, beetle grubs, scraps of resin-pulp, and whatever is left where it should not be.

They also steal. Everyone knows this. They steal fibers, seed-cakes, small polished objects, and if they can, sealed oil-beads. They are punished only mildly, because they are useful. They clean places hands cannot reach. They warn of cracks and drafts by changing their routes. They teach children patience. A household that cannot outwit its roof-runners is considered careless. A household that lives in perfect war with them is considered joyless.

The birds are louder.

They nest high in the tangle of vines, poles, and ceramic wind-pipes, where the rim of the hollow softens into haze. They are not bright. They are dust-brown, ash-gray, rust-winged, their feathers tight and sleek against the dry air. Their calls cut through the basin’s murmur: short knocks, drawn whistles, sharp double cries.
