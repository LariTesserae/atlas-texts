What lives there is not easy to separate from the place itself. In that high, warm, saturated world, life has not merely settled on the ground. It has become the ground. The first and largest inhabitant is the wetland as a whole: the breathing, water-holding, slowly digesting body of moss and root and leaf-felt that makes up its living substance. Yet within that vastness there are others—beings condensed enough to move, to tend, to hunger in their patient way, though even they are less like separate animals than like organs grown aware.

If they need a name, the closest one might be the Softened, because that is what they are: forms from which all hardness has been patiently removed. They are what happens when age, moisture, and warmth continue long enough that even the idea of a boundary becomes too brittle to keep.

At rest, the Softened resemble low breathing mounds in the living surface. They might be mistaken for thickened patches of moss, folded leaf-skins, or rounded cushions of root-felt until one notices the slow rhythm of their swelling and settling. Their bodies are made of layered wet tissue, dense and springy, threaded through with capillary channels through which water moves by slow suction. They have no bones. Bones would be an offense there, too dry, too final, too much like a wall. Where support is needed, they use pressure, fullness, interwoven fibers, and the tension of saturated membranes. Their surfaces are green-brown, plush with tiny growths, darkened along seams where moisture gathers. They are never dry. Even the idea of dryness seems distant to them.

When they move, they do not stride. They spread, gather, and draw themselves forward through countless fine contacts. Their limbs, if they choose to have limbs, are blunt and soft, ending in fringes that root momentarily into the surface and then release. They move as if asking permission of the ground, and the ground answers by yielding just enough. A Softened crossing the mire leaves no wound behind. It leaves only a temporary smoothing, a faint compression that the wetland remembers for a little while and then forgets.

They have no eyes in the ordinary sense. The light there is too dim and diffuse for sharp sight to be useful. Instead, they have shallow light-cups: moist depressions in their upper surfaces that sense brightening and shadow, gradients of greenish illumination falling through the vapor-ceiling. They perceive mostly by taste, pressure, humidity, and the behavior of water. They can smell a change in mist before it arrives. They can feel the difference between a healthy seep and a clogged root-channel. They know where the water table is rising by a faint increase in sweetness in the air, or by the way droplets form more eagerly on nearby fringes. To them, a dry patch is not merely a place lacking water. It is a pain, a thinness, a wrong note in the world.

Their feeding is quiet and intimate. They do not tear or chew. They lie over places where decay has become rich and soluble, extend absorbent rosettes into the surface, and draw up softened nutrients through their tissues. They also host within themselves colonies of minute organisms that transform warmth, moisture, and dissolved plant-matter into forms usable to the larger body. In this way, they are both feeders and fed-upon, both garden and gardener. Sometimes they lift mist into internal bladders and hold it there, letting it condense into a warm internal fluid. Sometimes they remain motionless for long periods while their outer surfaces participate in the general life of the mat, blurring into the ground so completely that it is impossible to say where the dweller ends and the wetland begins.

They are caretakers, though not in the sense of masters. They do not rule the place. They maintain it. They open blocked capillary threads by inserting soft probing lobes and waiting until the water remembers its path. They guide tiny flows from one root-bowl to another. They clear fallen matter from shallow pools before it becomes too thick. They shade vulnerable hollows when a thin place in the canopy lets in too much weak light. When a surface begins to tighten or dry, they gather there and cover it with their bodies, breathing moisture onto it until it softens again. Their work is slow, but nothing in that world is in a hurry except water, and even water moves there as if it has all the time it needs.

Their communication is made of droplets, ripples, taps, and chemical changes in moisture. If they have voices, those voices are the small sounds already present in the fen: a bead slipping from a frond, a bubble forming in a root-pit, a soft tick against a stretched membrane. They speak by adding intention to these sounds. A single drop falling into a cup-hollow can carry greeting. A sequence of surface ripples can signal need. A change in the taste of shared water can convey memory. When two Softened meet, they press their margins together and exchange a bead of fluid, each one reading the other's condition in that tiny taste. Sometimes they remain joined for a long while, sharing nutrients or simply resting as one broader body. Sometimes, after a long enough union, they separate again, though it is never entirely clear whether the same beings have parted or whether a single awareness has merely folded itself back into two.

They can merge more completely when needed. If a wide area of the mat becomes damaged, or if an unfamiliar dryness threatens to spread, several Softened may flatten themselves over it and become a temporary living skin. In that state, they think more slowly but more broadly. Their individual concerns dim. They become a shared pressure, a common warmth, a collective attention. When the danger passes, they separate, carrying traces of one another in their tissues. This is why no Softened is ever entirely solitary. Each one contains residues of others, layered memories of contact, faint chemical histories of places it has touched and been touched in return.

Their cities, if they can be called that, are not built from separate materials. They are maintained patterns in the living mass: circles of carefully tended pools, low ridges of moss that guide condensation, hanging curtains of root-fiber where mist collects, broad resting hollows worn smooth by generations of soft bodies. Nothing is angular. Nothing is cut or erected. Their settlements look like places where the wetland has become especially attentive to itself. A gathering place may be recognized by the richness of its water-sounds, the density of its capillary channels, and the way the air there feels thicker, more fragrant, more crowded with gentle intention.

They do not build tools of stone or wood, because there is little hard material to use and no need for force. Instead, they cultivate living structures. They persuade stems to bend. They train root-hairs into nets. They encourage certain mosses to form absorbent borders around pools. They coax leaves into cupping water. Their technology is patience. Their engineering is encouragement. Their art is keeping the world soft.

Their young are not born in the usual way. They arise when the living mat thickens into a knot of unusual activity, often in places where moisture, warmth, and chemical memory have gathered for a long time. Such a knot may remain closed for seasons, or longer, swelling gently as water and tissue organize around some new need. When it finally opens, what emerges is not an infant but a small, simple Softened, already damp, already patient, already capable of joining the work of the place. It grows by becoming more intricate, not larger and harder. Its body adds layers, channels, membranes, and memories. It learns by contact, by absorption, by being corrected by older forms. There is no childhood there as drier worlds would understand it, only a long unfolding into usefulness.

Death, too, is not a sudden departure. A Softened that has grown old does not fall or stiffen. It becomes softer, broader, less distinct. Its motions slow. Its edges blur into the mat. It may remain half-aware for a long while, resting in a shallow basin it has tended for generations. Eventually it settles, spreads, and loses separation. Its tissues become part of the peat. Its water joins the slow circulation. Its chemical memories remain in the layers it leaves behind. Other Softened can taste those layers when they press close to the ground. They do not resurrect the dead, but they can recognize a place where someone once was, and they can read something of that former being in the quality of the moisture there. In this way, the past is not buried. It is saturated into the present.

The Softened are not the only beings there, though they are the closest thing the place has to a people. Above them, in the low internal cloud layer, drift the mist-herds. These are vast translucent veils, almost without flesh, living sheets of condensing tissue that move through the vapor like slow sails. Their bodies are so thin that they seem made of weather rather than life. They trail long filaments that comb moisture from the air, collecting droplets, fine organic particles, and microscopic spores. As they feed, they swell faintly and grow heavier. When they become full, they release a warm drizzle that falls back into the upper hollows and leaf-cups below.
