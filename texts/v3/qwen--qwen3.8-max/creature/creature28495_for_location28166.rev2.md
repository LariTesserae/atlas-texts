What lives there has been rinsed of green. The basin does not permit roots, leaves, grass, or the soft shade of ordinary land. It accepts only what can cling, filter, endure glare, and become patient under mineral skin. Life is not absent from that bright wet place; it is redistributed. It is in the slick film on stone, the drip from a ledge, the slow turn of a valve, the white crab lifting a leg over a salt seam, the low throb of a pump beneath a vault, the still figure standing knee-deep in a pool with its face tilted toward rain.

The first inhabitants are not people, or not people in any simple sense. They are the microbial and fungal skins that coat the basin: black felts clinging to warm rock, rust-dark mats breathing in thermal runnels, pale filamentous growths threading shaded cracks, and iridescent mineral films that make the inside of slow channels look like wet porcelain. These living surfaces are the foundation of everything else. They feed on heat, salt, sulfur, dissolved iron, and the faint chemistry of the air. They do not root. They adhere. They spread in sheets and crusts, in slick veils and granular blooms, and they are constantly being eaten, scraped, regrown, and harvested. In places they are so thick and warm that they seem less like organisms than like the basin’s own dark flesh.

Upon these living surfaces move the small grazers. Clouds of brine flies rise from shallow pools, their wings mica-bright, their bodies thin and quick. When they settle, they make a sound like fine glass being shaken. Translucent shrimp-like swimmers dart through warm water, their organs visible as tiny moving shadows inside their bodies. Flat white crabs with porcelain shells patrol the crusts, tapping and clicking when they meet, their legs spread wide to keep from breaking through brittle salt. Segmented crawlers move in slow processions along wet walls, leaving faint mineral trails behind them. In the deeper runnels and covered channels, long eel-bodied things slip through the heat, their scales smooth as glazed ceramic, their movements so quiet that one sees only a ripple and then a pale curve disappearing around a bend.

Larger animals live among these smaller ones, using the city and the basin alike. Heron-like waders stand in the shallows with bare throats and long legs, their eyes shaded by pale membranes that slide across the glare. They are patient hunters, waiting for shrimp-swimmers or young eels to move within reach. Low-bodied amphibious hunters lie at the water’s edge, breathing through moist skin, their broad flat bodies almost indistinguishable from wet stone until they shift. Pale serpents swim silently through pools and channels, their tails ribboning through water that is too hot for most creatures. Broad-winged scavengers ride the thermal columns above the basin, their wings white and salt-crusted, their shadows passing over terraces and rooftops like slow clouds. They feed on what dies in the shallows, on discarded crab shells, on the small casualties of the industrial waterways. They are part of the basin’s cleanliness, part of its quiet balance.

And then there are the people.

They are the makers of the pump-houses, the causeways, the cooling halls, the pipe-bridges, the low arcaded yards. They are not soft beings. Nothing soft could remain soft there for long. They have been shaped by glare, wetness, heat, and the endless labor of keeping water moving. They are tall enough to see across open basin-distance, but light-boned and careful in their movements. Their limbs are long, their hands precise, their feet broad and flat with spread toes and tough, gripping soles made for slick stone and fragile crust. Their skin is smooth and pale, sometimes gray-white, sometimes warmed by copper and rust tones, often shining with moisture. Along their shoulders, throats, and collarbones run lines of salt glands that excrete fine crystals after long work near brine. They brush these crystals away with a thumb or wrist as often as one might blink.

Their eyes are large and dark, built to see through glare and steam. Clear inner lids slide across them when they work near spraying water or lean into hot vents. Their noses are narrow but valved; their mouths can seal tightly against mineral air. Their throats and chests are adapted to wet heat, and they can remain submerged in warm shallows for long periods, though they are not fully aquatic. They need moisture. If their skin dries too long, they become sluggish and irritable, and their joints stiffen. They rinse themselves often, pressing palms into channels, letting condensation run over wrists and faces, standing still in brief rain with their arms lifted slightly as if to receive a blessing.

They wear little, because the heat is strong and the air is heavy. Their clothing is practical and mineral: aprons of fired clay, harnesses of woven fungal felt, shoulder straps strengthened with copper, kiln-hardened caps, and sometimes long capes of pale, salt-glazed cloth made from compacted microbial fiber. Nothing about them comes from wood or leaf. Their ornaments are made of porcelain beads, polished black volcanic glass, copper rings, iron toggles, and salt crystals grown in careful molds. Some wear small ceramic discs that chime softly when they move, tuned to particular notes so that a household can recognize one of its own approaching through the mist. Their tools are carried in frames on the back or hips: scrapers, brushes, chisels, valve keys, tasting spoons, coils of mineral cord, small mirrors for signaling across glare.

They live scattered across the basin rather than packed into one dense city. A district may be only a cluster of low vaulted dwellings around a pump-house, a cistern, a condensation tower, or a row of cooling baths. Between districts lie empty pools, salt flats, steam vents, and long shining stretches of wet mineral ground. Their houses are thick-walled and rounded, built from basalt, fired brick, glazed stone, and iron-reinforced ceramic. Roofs are not merely shelters; they are water-gathering surfaces, sloped and grooved to guide condensation into cisterns. Floors are often wet by design, with shallow drains and curved edges, because dryness inside would be uncomfortable and dangerous. Many dwellings have a central soak-pool, fed by a controlled trickle of clean condensation, where the inhabitants rest and cool their skin.

The people of the basin are, above all, water-sorters. They do not treat water as one substance. To them, water is a family of different materials, each with its own use and danger. There is sweet air-water, gathered from high condensation towers and stone veils. There is bitter mineral water, heavy with dissolved salts and metals. There is hot deep-water, sulfurous and scalding, drawn from geothermal vents and used for power, heating, and certain industrial baths. There is dead brine, too saturated to sustain much life, which must be moved carefully into evaporation pans. There is rain-water, sudden and precious, which must be caught before it is lost or contaminated. Their entire civilization is organized around knowing which water is which, where it should go, how fast it should move, and what must be kept out of it.

They taste water from small ceramic spoons, but they also listen to it, feel its heat, watch its bead and sheet on stone. Specialized workers can tell a blocked pipe by the change in a hum, a weakening crust by the note it makes under a tap, a dangerous rise in mineral load by the way droplets dry on a wall. Pipe-listeners press polished discs of ceramic or copper against conduits and stand motionless for long moments, their eyes half closed, hearing the slow internal weather of the city. Descalers go into channels and vaults with brushes, chisels, and carefully measured acids made from controlled microbial fermentations. They scrape pale crust from surfaces that would otherwise seal shut. They clear valves, clean insulators, reseat gaskets, and re-glaze walls. Maintenance is not a temporary task. It is the center of their life.

Their machines are powerful but not loud. Engines sit in stone chambers half filled with water, their movements softened by liquid jackets and heavy masonry. Turbines turn slowly beneath grated floors. Boilers and condensers breathe in long, quiet rhythms. Electric lines run along pale porcelain knobs, and the current they carry is steady enough to power lamps, pumps, kilns, and rail systems without making the air feel torn. Where wheels roll, they are wrapped or set on dampened tracks. Where valves open, they open with balanced pressure. Where pipes meet, they are curved and tuned to avoid shrieking. The people understand that noise is a kind of violence in their basin. It startles creatures, cracks attention, and seems to offend the bright stillness. They build their machines as if they were making instruments for a music that must never become too loud.

Rain is one of the few times the basin permits greater sound. When clouds gather and the first heavy drops strike hot stone, the people turn upward. Covers are drawn back from cisterns. Sluice gates are adjusted. Children step into open courtyards to feel the water run over their shoulders. For a short time, there is a drumming on ceramic roofs, a rushing across causeways, a bright communal alertness. Then the rain passes, as it often does, and the basin returns to its hushed cycle of evaporation and glare. But the cisterns are fuller, the air is cleaner, and the mineral skins on the rocks shine as if newly made.

They eat what the basin gives. Their food is drawn from microbial mats, fungal growths, brine pools, warm runnels, and carefully tended shallow gardens of edible film. They cultivate dark felts in troughs, feeding them with measured mineral solutions and controlled heat. They harvest shrimp-like swimmers with fine nets of woven fiber. They collect brine-fly larvae from crust pockets, gather crab eggs from safe ledges, and raise flat white crabs in enclosed terraces where the crust can be maintained. Eel-bodied creatures are caught in channels or guided into ceramic traps. Eggs from wading birds are taken sparingly, with rules and seasons, because the basin is abundant but not inexhaustible. Food is steamed over thermal vents, baked in kilns, dried into thin salt-cakes, fermented in sealed jars, or pressed into dense cakes of microbial matter. There is no green softness in their meals. Their flavors are salt, mineral richness, heat, smokeless roast, sulfur in small amounts, the clean bite of condensation, the texture of slick film and crisp shell.

Their language matches their world. It is low, precise, and full of small sounds: tongue-clicks like drops falling into shallow pools, soft trills from the back of the throat, breathy consonants made behind sealed lips, and long pauses in which meaning seems to settle like sediment. They also speak with their hands, because hand-signs carry well across glare and distance without breaking silence. A curled finger may mean slow the flow. A flat palm lifted may mean wait. A tap against the collarbone may mean listen. A sweep of the hand near the ground may mean the crust is thin. Children learn these signs before they learn to walk safely across open salt. They are taught that loudness is careless, that haste can break a crust, and that attention is a form of respect.

Their music, when they make it, is quiet and water-shaped. They build tuned channels where condensation falls into stone bowls at different intervals. They strike hollow ceramic drums with padded hands. They run water over ribbed metal plates and listen to the changing pitch. They sing in low choruses that resemble the hum of pumps or the breath of a cooling hall. Their dances are performed in shallow pools, where the feet make soft splashes and the body moves with the careful balance of someone crossing uncertain ground. To watch them dance is to see labor made beautiful without losing its caution.

They organize themselves by water circuits more than by simple bloodlines. A household may be formed around a condensation tower, a pump, a set of evaporation pans, or a stretch of causeway that must be kept clear. Responsibilities are inherited, negotiated, and exchanged. A child belongs not only to parents but to the cistern-clan that tends its first pool, the workshop that trains its hands, and the maintenance route where it learns to listen. Marriages and alliances often involve the joining of water rights, the sharing of scraping duties, or the repair of a common sluice. To be wealthy there is not to possess many things, but to control reliable clean water, stable heat, and well-maintained channels. To be poor is to live near dead brine, cracked crust, or failing pipes. Yet even the poorest district is cared for to some degree, because a failure in one part of the basin can spread mineral blockage, heat imbalance, or dangerous flooding to another.

Their elders are valued for memory and attention. The basin changes slowly, but it changes constantly. Crust thickens. Vents shift. Water turns bitter or sweet. A channel that was safe for a generation may become brittle. Elders keep records on glazed ceramic tablets, but they also keep the deeper knowledge of sound and habit. They remember which pump used to throb in a certain rhythm, which terrace flooded after heavy rain, which wall wept more in one season than another. Some elders become almost landscape themselves, sitting for long hours beside warm pools with salt whitening their shoulders, their eyes half closed, their breathing slow. They are not abandoned. They are consulted, fed, rinsed, and honored. It is said, in their quiet way, that a person who has learned enough begins to resemble the basin.

Death, too, is mineral. There is no soil for burial, no wood for pyres. The dead are carried to the lowest evaporation pans at the basin’s edge and laid in shallow water. Over time, salt and mineral crust form around them. Small creatures clean what flesh remains. The bones are later removed, purified by brine and sun, and either placed in walls, set into memorial chambers, or returned to the kilns to be fused into new ceramic. In some districts, a person is remembered by a thin salt cast made from the place where they lay. In others, the family keeps a small polished bone bead or a ceramic tablet bearing the deceased’s name and water-route. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is made unclean. The dead become part of the basin’s long patience.

They keep no great beasts of burden. There are no grazing animals to harness, no pasture, no fodder. Heavy work is done by water pressure, counterweights, steam, geared mechanisms, rail carts, and their own careful hands. Where transport is needed across shallow pools, they use broad flat-bottomed floats of fired clay and metal, poled slowly over water. Where goods must move between districts, they use rail spurs and pipe-bridges, small carts with wrapped wheels, and conveyors powered by geothermal engines. Their industry is real, but it has been tamed into quietness. It does not roar. It hums, drips, exhales, and turns.

They have a complex relationship with the nonhuman life around them. They harvest, but they also tend. They clear paths for migratory eel-things through the runnels. They leave ledges open for wading birds to nest. They protect certain crab beds from overharvest by rotating districts and closing shallow pools for seasons. They discourage scavengers from nesting too close to condensers, but they do not drive them away entirely, because the broad-winged creatures keep the basin clean. They watch the brine flies as indicators of salinity. They watch the heron-like waders as indicators of pool health. If the small shrimp-swimmers vanish from a channel, the people know something has changed in the water. Their science is not abstract. It is intimate, sensory, and continuous.

Their days begin before the glare becomes severe. They wake in dim, vaulted rooms, rinse their skin, check the cistern level, and step out onto wet causeways. Work starts with inspection: valves, pipes, crust thickness, condensation surfaces, pump temperature, electrical insulators, channel flow. They move in small groups, barefoot or in thin-soled mineral shoes, carrying tools and tasting spoons. By midday, the light is too severe for prolonged open labor. They retreat into cooling halls, shaded arcades, or half-submerged workshops. Some sleep. Some repair tools. Some tend nurseries where eggs or infants are kept warm above gentle thermal currents. In the late afternoon, when the glare softens and the air begins to tremble with rising moisture, they return to the open basin.

Night does not empty the place. The white lamps come on, steady and clean, reflected in every pool. The city becomes a field of small lights and dark water. Nocturnal creatures move through the shallows. Some workers prefer the night because the stone is cooler and the condensation harvest is strongest. Others keep only watch, listening for leaks, watching pressure gauges, observing the behavior of the animals. In the dark, the basin feels even more like a living mechanism: water whispering, pumps beating slowly, steam lifting in thin veils, lamps trembling in wet stone, distant signals flashing from tower to tower.

Their beauty is not the beauty of softness. It is the beauty of polished surfaces, balanced pressures, clean channels, and patient repair. They admire a valve that opens without noise, a crust that has formed evenly, a cistern that catches every drop, a causeway that has been worn smooth by generations of careful feet. They find pleasure in the smell of hot flint and clean electrified metal, in the sight of steam making the distance tremble, in the sound of a single drop falling into a stone bowl tuned to receive it. They do not long for green things. To them, green would seem untidy, soft, perishable. Their world is made of mineral patience, and they have made themselves worthy of it.

If one must say who lives there, the answer is not only the people. It is the microbial felts that coat the warm rocks. It is the flat white crabs and translucent shrimp-swimmers. It is the eel-bodied things in the runnels, the heron-like waders, the pale serpents, the broad-winged scavengers. It is the engines sunk in vaults and the condensers breathing slowly behind glazed walls. It is the water itself, sorted and moved and cooled and returned. It is the crust forming, being scraped, forming again. It is the bright wet silence, so full of motion that silence becomes not emptiness but control.

The true inhabitant of that place is the basin’s whole life together: a distributed, mineral, industrial ecology that thinks in channels, breathes in steam, remembers in crust, and survives by maintenance. It has no single face. If you saw it at a distance, you might mistake it for only landscape, only city, only weather. But look closer. There is a figure on a causeway, pausing to taste a drop from a copper fitting. There is a crab lifting one leg over salt. There is a pump beating under stone. There is a child learning to sign “listen” while standing barefoot on warm, wet rock. There is a wader motionless in a pool. There is rain gathering again above the glare.

Everything there lives by being washed, sorted, and renewed. Everything there has learned to be bright without softness, active without uproar, alive without green.
