What lives there are the inheritors of that gesture.

Not one creature, not one species, not even one kind of life, but a long-continued habitation made of many bodies. The High Lattice Marsh is occupied the way an old song is occupied: by voices that have entered it one by one, by pauses that belong to no single singer, by the accumulated discipline of everyone who has learned not to break the rhythm.

At first, the inhabitants seem to be only the small hidden animals already hinted at by the structure itself. Bark-patterned amphibians sleep inside leaf-funnels, their bellies pale against the darker green, their throats moving with the same slow pulse as condensation gathering on a waxy stem. They do not leap so much as transfer themselves from wetness to wetness, choosing each landing place with an almost ceremonial caution. Flat insects skim the black pools, not disturbing the surface, leaving behind only a faint trembling ring that fades before it reaches the edge. In the deeper funnels, translucent larvae drift like living glass, their bodies full of tiny bright grains that may be food, may be light, may be some symbiotic passenger no one has named.

Gliding grazers move between terraces on broad membranes mottled like lichen. They are not graceful in the way of birds. They are graceful in the way of falling leaves that have decided, over many centuries, to fall in a controlled direction. They ride the warm updrafts with very little effort, sinking slowly, correcting with subtle shifts of membrane, vanishing into mist as if they have remembered another place and chosen to be there instead. Blind browsers crawl along the inner surfaces of root-walls, mouthparts working fungal threads with a soft, continuous whisper. They are slow enough that moss can begin to grow on their backs. They do not seem to mind. They are part of the maintenance: eating where eating is needed, thinning where growth has become too thick, leaving behind polished channels and little heaps of fibrous waste that other creatures later gather.

In the pools there are eel-like cleaners, long and ribbon-bodied, whose work is so constant that the water itself seems to owe them something. They pass through capillary channels, through leaf-basins, through the shallow troughs where moisture is directed from one terrace to another. They eat decay before it can sour into rot. They remove the first films of mold, the clogged spore-mats, the settled dust of old bark. Where they have passed, water runs clear again, and the faint smell of green sweetness returns. Flattened creatures rest on the pool bottoms looking like wet leaves until a shadow moves over them; then they unfold, reveal soft undersides, and glide away into the dark plumbing of the marsh.

These lives are numerous, but they are not loud. They have adapted to the quiet of the place. Their calls, when they have calls, are low pressure changes, floor-borne tremors, brief vibrations in a stem. Some species barely make sound at all. They signal by bending antennae, by shifting the angle of wings, by releasing scent into the humid air. Predation exists, but even predation has become subdued. A predator here does not crash through vegetation. It arrives as a slight rearrangement of shadows. A stillness changes. A leaf-funnel empties. Later, one may notice that something has been eaten, but the act itself seems to have been folded into the silence before anyone could witness it.

Yet all these creatures are only the outermost layer of what lives in the Lattice Marsh.

Beneath them, or rather interwoven with them, are the tenders.

They are not easy to see from a distance. In the green-gray light, wrapped in layered garments of leaf-fiber and living moss, they resemble upright growths, pruned shoots, bundles of vine waiting to be trained. Only when one of them moves does the eye accept that the shape has intention. They are the ones who maintain the repeated gesture. They are the ones who open the leaf-gates when the mist-tide rises, who close the resin-sealed louvers when cold air gathers too thickly in a hollow, who clear the capillary channels when spore-fall clogs them, who press their palms to bridge-roots and listen for the deep complaint of overstrained wood.

If you must give them a name, they are often called the Marshwrights, though they would be more likely to answer to the sound of water being correctly redirected.

They are near-human in shape, but changed by long ancestry in the Lattice. Their bodies are light, not fragile but sparingly made, as if excess weight became a discourtesy among bridges that must not be asked to bear too much. Their limbs are long, especially the fingers and toes, with broad, soft pads that grip wet cane and polished root without slipping. Their feet are wide, the toes spreading and curling with a separate intelligence, able to feel the difference between firm walking-stem, spongy moss-shelf, and a root that has begun to soften with age. Their chests are deep, adapted to the thin, warm, breathed-thick air of the heights. Their skin is mottled in greens, grays, browns, and dusky blues, not as decoration but as a record of moisture, light, fungal contact, and the slow staining of plant juices. It is not dry skin. It is not wet skin exactly. It is skin that has learned to live with film and bead and vapor, skin that knows how to be touched by mist without recoiling.

Their eyes are large and quiet, made for the dimness. They do not glitter sharply. They gather light. In the darker recesses, where phosphorescent fungus makes the handholds glow faintly, their gaze seems to continue the glow a little longer than it should. They blink slowly. Their faces are expressive in restrained ways: a tightening at the mouth, a slight flare of the nostrils, a settling of the eyelids, a turning of the head that means attention has been given. They do not need broad gestures. The place has trained them out of broad gestures.

They wear garments that are half clothing and half garden. Cloaks of overlapping leaf-scales shed moisture along their edges. Shoulder-wraps of woven root-fiber are seeded with moss and tiny epiphytes, so that when a tender stands still for long enough, the garment begins to blur into the architecture. Hoods are fringed with dangling rootlets that catch droplets and guide them away from the face. Belts are braided from bark cord and hung with small tools: bone grafting knives, thorn pins, resin plugs, hollow seed-cases, clay valves, coils of living vine that will be tied and later allowed to grow into their new purpose. Some carry slings woven from aerial root fiber, not for throwing stones—there are no stones to throw—but for carrying cuttings, young fungal cultures, sleeping larvae, or water-filled leaf-pouches.

They smell of the place: warm leaf, wet bark, faint resin, soft fungal sweetness, the clean mineral absence of water that has been filtered through living fiber for generations. Their hands are always marked. Resin darkens their fingertips. Plant stains line the creases of their palms. Moss spores powder their sleeves. They do not wash these marks away in any thorough sense; they renew them. A clean hand, in their understanding, is not a hand untouched by work but a hand that knows what it recently touched.

Their voices are low. They speak in soft, breathy tones, because loudness is wasted where wet vegetation swallows sound. Much of their communication passes through the body of the Lattice itself. They tap on roots. They press palms to bridges and feel answering vibrations. They can distinguish the creak of settling from the creak of strain, the hollow note of healthy wood from the duller complaint of interior decay. Their language has many words for moisture: moisture that arrives, moisture that waits, moisture that must be moved, moisture that has become dangerous, moisture that is holy in the sense of being exactly enough. They have fewer words for ownership and many for obligation. A person does not say, “This terrace is mine.” A person says, “I am the one who listens to this terrace,” or, “The third root-shaft has begun to speak to me,” or, in older phrasing, “I have been permitted to be useful here.”

They live sparsely, because the Lattice is sparse. Their settlements are not towns but clusters of related habits arranged around water flow, structural stress, and the needs of growing walls. A household may occupy a hollow inside a cistern-column, sleeping in curved alcoves where the inner bark has been encouraged to form smooth, breathing surfaces. Another may live in a suspended chamber beneath a terrace, a pod-shaped nest of woven root and leaf-skin that sways almost imperceptibly in the updrafts. Another may inhabit a long, low gallery along a weather-screen, where hanging gardens are trimmed and renewed. Between these places there may be distances so great that a person walking from one to another passes through hours of mist and silence, seeing no one, hearing only the faint internal creaking of the world adjusting to its own weight.

Their homes are not built so much as convinced. A wall is persuaded outward by patient tying. A floor is thickened by layers of moss coaxed to knit together. A doorway is kept open by living clamps of root that grow slowly around a shaped frame. If a room becomes too cold, channels of warm water are guided through the wall. If it becomes too damp, new leaf-louvers are trained to open and close with the shifting air. If a family grows, the home grows too, not by sudden addition but by gradual expansion, a new chamber swelling from an old one like a bud that has been promised space.

They do not keep open fires. Fire is almost unimaginable in the ordinary life of the Lattice, and when it appears in stories it appears as catastrophe: a dry violence, a bright eater, the enemy of all patient growth. For warmth they use fermentation chambers filled with shredded bark and moist leaf-matter, carefully tended until they give off steady heat. They use channels of warm water drawn from deeper vascular shafts. They use body warmth, layered garments, and the closeness of sleeping alcoves. For light they cultivate luminescence: fungi trained along handrails, seed-lanterns with pale blue interiors, jars of glowing spores suspended in still air, moss that brightens under touch. Their illumination is never harsh. It suggests rather than declares. It makes their faces look like faces seen in dreams, softened by green and shadow.

Their food comes from the Lattice’s own abundance, gathered with care. They cultivate fruiting walls where edible brackets form in shelf-like rows. They tend algae films in shallow light-basins. They harvest soft shoots, flower buds, sweetened root-sap, and the pale tender growths that appear after mist-tides. They encourage certain insects and discourage others, never with total mastery, always with negotiation. The translucent larvae are especially valued, not merely for the waterproof silk they spin but because their presence indicates that a waterway is healthy. Children learn to carry larvae gently in leaf-cups, to whisper to them as if they were small kin, to release them at joints and seams where their silk will seal a leak. Some families are famous for their larvae-lines, their spore-strains, their cultivated fungal gardens, their patience in coaxing a stubborn arch to close over three generations.

Work among them is quiet and continual. There is no great separation between labor and worship, between maintenance and memory. A tender rises, listens to the walls, tastes the air, checks the bead-lines along the terrace edge. If a leaf-gate is clogged, it is cleared. If a root-rail has begun to split, it is bound with resin-soaked cord and watched. If a channel flows too quickly, a moss plug is adjusted until the water slows. If a bridge has grown in the wrong direction, a new tie is set, not to force it but to remind it of the desired path. Every action is small. Every action is repeated. The landscape persists because the smallness is not despised.

They measure time in growth, not in sharp divisions. A child’s first task may be to tend a single shoot for a season. If the shoot survives and grows true, the child is said to have entered into conversation with the Lattice. Adolescents are given more difficult work: repairing a weather-screen, clearing a vertical shaft, learning to read the tension in a spanning root. Adulthood is marked less by age than by responsibility. One becomes fully trusted when one can be left alone with a fragile part of the structure and not be seen until the work is done. Elders are those whose hands have become slow enough to be trusted with the oldest growth. They no longer climb the high bridges. They sit in root-chairs, in alcoves polished by years, and teach by demonstration, by touch, by placing a younger hand on the correct place and saying, “Here. Do you feel that?”

Their law is woven into practice. One does not cut without leaving a path for regrowth. One does not take water from a channel without asking what downstream will thirst. One does not tighten a tie until the living tissue protests. One does not leave a tool where rain will gather and rot begins. One does not shout unless the structure itself is in danger, and even then the shout is brief. The worst thing a person can do is make an unnecessary wound. The second worst is to hide a wound. For wounds in the Lattice are never private. A crack neglected becomes a leak. A leak neglected becomes a softening. A softening neglected becomes a collapse into the void below, and the silence after such a collapse is worse than any sound.

They have ceremonies, but the ceremonies are restrained. At the height of mist-tide, when the whole structure weeps and every channel runs full, they open all the leaf-gates and stand in the rain with bare hands. They do not dance wildly. They turn their palms upward. They let water gather in the lines of their skin. They sing low notes into the wet air, not to be heard by one another but to feel the sound move through the chest and into the nearby wood.
