At first, the only honest answer is: no one.

No one with feet. No one with eyes. No one whose breath fogs in the damp, whose heart keeps time against the dripping, whose hunger makes the place meaningful through need. There are no sleepers behind the gilled eaves, no watchers on the terraces, no crowds compressed into the channel-streets. The town is empty in the way a theater is empty after the actors have gone and before the next performance has been announced: full of arrangement, full of readiness, full of design, and yet devoid of bodies.

But that answer is too simple. It mistakes absence for vacancy. The hollow is not uninhabited. It is only unpeopled.

What lives there has been redistributed. Life has not vanished; it has changed its center of gravity. It has moved away from faces and limbs, away from appetite and pursuit, into wetter, slower, more distributed forms. The residents of the chamber are not inhabitants in the old animal sense. They are growers, filters, repairers, resonators, flows, and patterns. Some are visibly alive. Some are alive only in the way a process is alive. Some are not alive at all, yet behave with enough continuity, memory, and purpose that they cannot be dismissed as mere machinery.

The place is occupied first by green things.

They are the most visible citizens: the mosses, the ferns, the basin plants, the hanging root-curtains, the reed-like stems, the floating mats. They live without panic. There is no predation here, no insect grazing, no burrowing disturbance, no sudden violence of hunger. Because of that, their existence has a softness that feels almost liturgical. They thicken. They spread. They cushion every hard edge the young stone offers. They turn ledges into green terraces and fissures into gardens. They do not struggle in the way surface plants struggle against wind and drought; they submit to moisture, and in submitting they take possession of the chamber.

The mosses are the common folk of this hollow. They fill cracks, soften stairs, gather on damp faces of stone, and make the mineral world feel inhabited. They are not decorative. They hold water. They mute sound. They create microclimates. They give the chamber its deep green dimness. In places they are so thick that they seem less like plants than like a patient green flesh grown over the town’s bones.

The ferns are stranger. Their fronds are pale and translucent, adapted to weak light, and they unfurl from fresh fractures as if summoned by the smell of newly broken stone. They do not seek sun, because there is no sun to seek. They orient instead toward humidity gradients, toward mineral seepage, toward the faint blue-green glow of luminous tissues and phosphorescent veins. Some appear almost cultivated, their rows too regular to be accidental, their placement aligned with the architecture as if they had been seeded into the town plan like living plumbing.

The broad-leafed hydrophytes standing in the shallow basins are more stately. Their roots spread like white lace beneath the water’s skin, filtering, stabilizing, tasting the chamber’s chemistry. They live in stillness, but not in idleness. They process dissolved minerals. They oxygenate the shallow pools. They provide surfaces for microbial films. Their leaves cup condensation. When the ceiling rains, they receive the drops and break them into softer runoffs, preventing the delicate basin floors from being scoured. They are at once garden and infrastructure.

The reed-like plants with hollow stems are something else again: instruments as much as organisms. When pressure changes in the chamber, vapor passes through them, and they whistle softly. These whistles are not random. They are part of the hollow’s regulation. Certain pitches coincide with valve movements, with spore release, with shifts in capillary flow. The reeds are not merely alive; they are acoustic organs. They live by translating weather into sound.

Then there are the hanging root-filaments, the curtains that absorb condensation directly from the air. They drape from bridges and overhangs in pale strands, motionless until mist thickens around them. Then they drink. They become heavier, more luminous, more present. Water moves along them in slow bright beads. They are less like plants than like living nets set to catch the chamber’s breath. Around them, the air itself seems domesticated.

There are no flowers here in any ordinary sense. No petals, no nectar, no bright insect-luring displays. Instead there are reproductive structures suited to a world without animals: pale cups that release spores in timed bursts; gelatinous buds that split when struck by falling water; fronds that shiver and scatter microscopic propagules into the saturated air. Reproduction here is not seduction. It is hydrology, vibration, and timing. It is civic. It happens in rhythm with the chamber’s cycles.

These plants do not live alone. They are part of a larger population, one much harder to see.

The second order of inhabitants is microbial.

They are the invisible masons, the true repairers, the quiet builders who make the town’s continuity possible. They live in films and crusts, in milky seeps, in the fine mineral skins that spread over cracks. They inhabit fissures too narrow for roots, pores too small for water to move through freely, ducts where condensation first learns direction. They are not individuals. They are colonies, mats, membranes, chemical neighborhoods. They live by metabolism so slow and so distributed that it resembles patience more than life.

When a crack opens in the young stone, they are among the first responders. Moisture collects. A faint milky film spreads. It thickens. It hardens. Mineral precipitates. The wound closes, or at least becomes something else: a scar, a reinforcement, a new architectural opportunity. This is not mere geology. It is guided. The films respond to flow, to dissolved calcium, to iron, to electrical gradients, to sound. They build where building is needed. They seal where leakage threatens. They lay down buttresses where stress concentrates. They work with the precision of masons, but without hands, without tools, without visible intention.

They are the chamber’s lithic bees, though no bees live here. They are the workers of a city without labor. Their industry is so quiet that it appears, at first glance, like natural deposition. But the pattern is too purposeful. The repairs occur too exactly where they are needed. The mineral accretions are too well placed. If the town knows what it is doing, this is part of how it knows: through countless microscopic lives that translate chemistry into structure.

These microbial citizens are also tuners. They alter surfaces. They make stone more absorbent or more water-shedding. They encourage certain plants to colonize certain walls. They condition the acoustic properties of the chamber by softening some faces and hardening others. They participate in the town’s sound as surely as the reeds do, though no one hears them individually. Their contribution is cumulative: a slight change in texture here, a film over a resonance point there, a mineral crust that redirects a drip into a pool. The chamber’s music is partly their work.

Then there is the built environment itself, and this is where the question of what lives becomes more difficult.

The architecture is not merely built. It is grown, responsive, self-correcting. Its pale calcium lattices, dark vitreous panels, fibrous membranes, and fine metallic-looking threads do not behave like dead materials awaiting use. They flex when touched by water. They open and close. They conduct signals. They repair themselves. They adjust their surfaces in response to humidity, pressure, and sound. They glow faintly. They pulse. They participate in the chamber’s cycles with something like reflex.

Are they alive? Not exactly. But they are not inert either. They occupy a middle state: grown matter that still remembers life, or machinery that has become so intimate with biology that the distinction stops being useful. These are organs without owners. The walls are filters. The staircases are spillways. The roofs are condensers, gardens, and resonating chambers. The doors are valves. The town is not a collection of buildings so much as a body of interlocking functions.

Its membranes are especially unsettling in their gentleness. When water touches them, they flex. They do not move quickly. There is no alarm in them, no animal startle. They respond the way a leaf responds, or a valve responds, or a throat responds: by changing shape to accommodate flow. Some surfaces seem to inhale moisture. Others shed it. Others guide it into storage veins so fine that they resemble ornament until one realizes the ornament is plumbing.

The lights, too, are part of this half-life. The phosphorescent mineral veins, the embedded strips along stair edges, the blue-green shine of wet tissue, the slow pulses from hidden machinery: these are not illumination imposed from outside. They are symptoms of the chamber’s operation. Light here is metabolic, hydrological, structural. It shifts when water moves. It cools when rain thickens over luminous panels. It dims when filters draw energy into deeper processes. It is not bright enough to reveal everything, and perhaps that is the point. The town does not need to be seen. It needs to be regulated.

So one could say that a third population lives there: the responsive architecture. Not alive as a plant is alive, not conscious as a person is conscious, but active, sensitive, and continuous. It lives as maintenance. It lives as adjustment. It lives as the ongoing correction of imbalance. If the chamber has nerves, they are here, in these membranes and threads. If it has glands, they are in the storage veins. If it has skin, it is the ribbed exterior surfaces that guide condensation. If it has a skeleton, it is the young stone and the calcium lattices slowly being added to it.

But none of these lives can be understood apart from water.

Water is the senior presence. It is not alive in the same sense as moss or microbial film, yet it is the condition through which all other life here becomes possible. It is also the nearest thing the town has to a primary citizen. The streets are channels for it. The basins are for it. The reservoirs are for it. The roofs are made to catch it. The eaves are made to break its fall. The walls are made to bleed it, guide it, filter it, or drink it. The architecture does not merely tolerate water; it is organized around water as completely as a surface city might be organized around roads or markets.

Water lives here in many forms. It lives as bead and film, as thread and vein, as mist and rain, as pool and gutter-flow, as capillary crawl and hidden duct-pressure. It moves upward in fine channels, defying expectation. It trembles on ceilings before falling. It gathers in shallow basins and then withdraws, leaving behind shine and sediment. It strikes surfaces with sound. It carries spores. It activates repair films. It changes the pitch of reeds. It triggers doors. It feeds mineral printers. It is the medium, the messenger, the nutrient, and the clock.

If the plants are the town’s softness and the microbes its patience, water is its motion. Without water, the chamber would become merely a dry tomb of advanced construction. With water, it is a working world. Water gives the place its cycles, its sound, its danger, and its strange civic rhythm.

The danger is real. The rock is young. It has not been softened. A ledge can cut. A slab can shift when saturated. A dry walkway can become a sluice in seconds when an upper duct flushes. This is not a gentle garden. It is a wet machine inside a fresh wound in the earth. The lives within it are not safe lives. They are adaptive lives. They survive because they are flexible, distributed, and quick to repair. Moss can regrow. Films can reseal. Channels can reroute. Plants can colonize new reinforcements. The town can absorb violence because it is not organized around fragile central bodies.

There is another inhabitant, harder to name.

Sound lives here.

Not as mere noise, but as agency. The chamber is loud, and its loudness is organized. Water falling from the ceiling produces percussions. Runoff hisses along channels. Pipes boom and gurgle. Reed-stems whistle. Pools answer droplets with rounded notes. Stone amplifies. Moss absorbs. The whole hollow sometimes falls into a low harmonic drone, as if the infrastructure were being played by its own weather.

This sound is not accidental. It is control. Certain frequencies open valves. Others release spores. Others summon repair films. Others shift capillary movement. The town listens to itself, and in listening it regulates itself. Its acoustic life is therefore not background atmosphere; it is a population of signals, rhythms, and commands. The sound is a kind of invisible fauna, though no animal throat makes it. It moves through the chamber like a living thing: rising, subsiding, repeating, pausing, returning.

When the rain cycle begins, the sound changes first. The ceiling pores dilate with a soft wet creak. Condensation thickens. The first drops strike tiered roofs. The chamber answers. A hundred small percussions organize into pattern. Water moves down the channel-streets. The porous ceramic drinks and releases. Basins fill. Hidden ducts boom. Floating walkways rise on buoyant joints. Doors open and close. Plants unfurl or contract. Filters flush. Mineral printers accelerate where fresh flow provides material. The reeds whistle. The drone deepens. Light shifts cooler as water passes over luminous panels.

This is the closest the hollow comes to society.

No crowd gathers, but everything participates. The event is municipal. It is also intimate. Every surface is touched. Every resident process is called into relation. The chamber becomes, for a little while, more obviously a single coordinated phenomenon. Then the rain stops. The water drains. The town exhales mist. The machinery settles into a lower hum. The sound thins. The cycle waits. Then it begins again.

One could almost call this the social life of the place, except that it has no participants with faces. Its society is made of responses. Its politics is hydraulic. Its ritual is cyclical. Its etiquette is acoustic. Its memory is mineral.

There is also a human-shaped inhabitant, though no humans live here.

Absence lives in the town.

The settlement was made for bodies, or at least for something like bodies. There are stairs, terraces, doorways, ramps, bridges, reservoirs, lock-like thresholds. There are places where hands might have rested, where feet might have paused, where eyes might have looked across the basin from a high ledge. The scale is not purely alien. It is not built only for water. It remembers occupants. It anticipates them. Its emptiness is not formless; it is molded.

This makes the absence active. It is not merely that people are missing. It is that their missing has shape. The town keeps their places. It maintains pathways they do not walk. It opens doors for bodies that do not arrive. It filters water they may never drink. It lights stair edges no one descends. The architecture is loyal. That loyalty is one of the saddest things about the place, if one is capable of feeling sadness there. The town does not mourn, because it has no heart in the ordinary sense. But it persists in the shape of expectation.

So if one asks who lives there, one answer is: the remembered occupant. The anticipated citizen. The missing user. The body implied by every railing, terrace, and threshold. This is not a living being, but it is a living pattern. It continues to influence the chamber as strongly as any plant or machine. The town is still arranged around a social possibility that has withdrawn.

Out of all these things—green tenants, microbial masons, responsive architecture, water, sound, and human-shaped absence—another kind of life emerges.

It is not a creature. It is not a ghost. It is not a god, though the chamber has a cathedral hush. It is not an artificial intelligence in any simple sense, though the place is intelligent in its behavior. It is the composite life of the hollow itself: the basin as a single continuing process. It has no boundary that can be pointed to. It has no center that can be isolated. It is distributed across rock, root, film, valve, pool, duct, membrane, mist, and echo. But it behaves as a whole.

This composite life receives water. It moves water. It refines water. It distributes water. It listens to water. It rebuilds itself around water. That is not merely function. That is existence. The town’s being is maintenance, but maintenance raised to such complexity that it becomes a form of life. It does not need hunger to be alive. It does not need fear. It does not need reproduction in the animal sense. It reproduces its conditions.
