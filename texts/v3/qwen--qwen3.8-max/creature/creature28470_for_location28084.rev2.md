What lives there is not one figure against the horizon. It is a pressure.

The first inhabitants are small, numerous, and heard before they are seen. The plain’s true crowds are insects. They bead along stems at dawn, crawl through the leaf-felt in black and metallic threads, rise in shining masses after warm rain, and hang over the warmer hollows in trembling clouds. Some are tiny enough to be no more than moving specks against the light; others are long-bodied, armored in green or copper, with wings folded tight over backs scored by wind and fiber. There are pale soft-winged flies that gather over damp depressions, dark beetles that work the dead middle layers of the plant-mat, thin wasps that hunt in the low growth, and broad-winged hoppers whose rasping calls become part of the plain’s constant noise.

Their lives are brief, dense, and essential. They pollinate the broad-leaved carpets. They chew the tough outer surfaces of older plants. They lay eggs in leaf-cups, in the seams where stems overlap, in the brown tannin water of shaded pools. Their larvae feed in hidden places: inside saturated root-felt, beneath overhangs of matted growth, in the cloudy green nurseries where water stands long enough to quicken. When a hatch occurs, the air itself seems to thicken. Winged bodies lift from the basins in shifting columns, and the plain answers with movement: birds turning toward the rise, amphibians stirring in the wetter pockets, the high shimmer of wings becoming a roar.

Insects are not merely residents. They are the plain’s circulating life. They are the food that moves through everything else.

Where water lingers, amphibians and crawling things take over the sound. They are small for the most part, and their presence is often announced by the wet movement of bodies through saturated plant matter rather than by a clear view. There are frogs no larger than a thumb, brown or green, calling from the cups of broad plants. There are darker, squat-bodied toads that tolerate the acidic brown pools and go silent when shadows pass overhead. There are slick, low crawlers that move through the moss quilts at night, their skins always damp, their eyes set high, hunting insects and larvae in the soft layers. They breed with astonishing speed after rain. Eggs appear in gelatinous clusters in shallow water, in the shaded lips of pools, in the hidden tanks formed by overlapping leaves. The larvae multiply fast, darken the water, and are eaten in great numbers by beetles, birds, larger amphibians, and one another.

This is not cruelty in the way a person would name it. It is the plain’s metabolism. Warm shallow water becomes a nursery; the nursery becomes a feeding place; the feeding place dries; the survivors scatter into the mat and wait. When damp returns, the chorus begins again.

Birds give the open plain its visible motion. They are scattered rather than crowded, but intensely active. Some run along the springy surface with wide toes that distribute their weight, probing into soft growth with slender bills. Others hover briefly over leaf-cups, taking insects from the water’s surface in quick, precise snaps. Harsh-voiced species perch on the few elevated seed stalks, watching the surrounding expanse, calling in rough notes that carry far across the open. Their cries are not sweet. They are functional: claims, warnings, alarms, announcements of food or danger. In a landscape with so little vertical obstruction, a single bird can seem loud; many birds become a dispersed chorus.

There are birds that nest low in the moss, their eggs patterned to match the mottled greens and browns of the plant-mat. Their young are quick, hiding rather than flying at first, pressed into the felted ground while adults give alarm calls nearby. There are aerial feeders that appear when swarms rise, turning through the insect clouds with open bills. There are broad-winged hunters that use cloud shadows to move unseen, dropping low over the plain when the light changes. Birds follow hatches. They follow rain. They follow the softstep of seasons, arriving when the pools are alive and leaving when the water retreats into the deeper mat.

Predation is ordinary and constant. Nothing here remains gentle for long.

Insects take insects in the low growth. Birds take amphibians from the edges of pools. Amphibians take larvae, smaller frogs, and anything soft enough to swallow. Something large enough to be noticed but low enough not to break the surface moves through the root-mat at night, leaving depressions and broken stems. The people who know the plain have names for it, but the names are not spoken casually. It may be a bird, though it does not behave like most birds: broad-footed, heavy-bodied, with dull plumage the color of wet fiber and shadow. Its wings, if they can still be called wings, are short and stiff, used for balance rather than flight. It walks with a patient, rolling step, spreading its weight carefully, hunting by vibration and sound. Frogs go quiet when it passes. Insect noise changes pitch. Birds give low alarm notes and shift away. It is not monstrous. It is simply one of the plain’s older appetites, adapted to a world where weight must be used sparingly and silence is a tool.

The sudden silences are often its signature. The chorus stops in a patch, then another, then returns rearranged, as if the plain has taken a breath and decided where to resume speaking.

Beneath all the visible and audible life are the smallest inhabitants, the ones that make the ground itself possible. Mites, springtails, beetle larvae, fungal threads, bacteria, and countless nameless decomposers work the dead middle of the plant-mat. They are the ones who turn old leaf into fiber, fiber into structure, structure into the sponge that holds water. They do not make the loud noise of the surface world, but without them the plain would collapse into its own remains. Every step a person takes passes above their slow labor. Every shelter is built on their patience. The living floor is not soil. It is a body made of many bodies, maintained by appetite at every scale.

And then there are the people.

They do not live on the plain as conquerors or settlers. They live with it as guests who know the terms. They are few, scattered, and seasonal. Their camps are not towns. They are small clusters of low shelters, raised platforms, woven windbreaks, drying racks, and careful pathways laid over soft ground with broad leaves or woven panels. If abandoned, these marks are absorbed within a few seasons. The plant-mat grows over them. Rain softens their edges. Insects move into the old cordage. The plain closes around them as if they had never been.

The people themselves are light in their habits before they are light in body. They move in small groups, often family bands, sometimes pairs, sometimes temporary gatherings of several households when food and water are reliable. They do not build heavily. They do not dig deeply. They cannot, because the ground is not earth in the ordinary sense; it is living and dying plant tissue, and to cut it carelessly is to wound the surface that supports them. They take what they need from the upper layers. They harvest insects, larvae, eggs, amphibians, tender shoots, seed-heads, and the occasional small bird, always with attention to abundance rather than desire. They know which pools can be touched and which must be left alone. They know when a hatch will bring enough food to dry and store for a few days, and when taking too much will empty the place before it can recover.

Their technology is direct, repaired, and portable. Bone pins. Wooden hooks. Cordage twisted from peeled fibers. Shell or seed-case containers. Nets fine enough to sweep insects from the air without tearing too many wings. Long poles used both for balance and for testing the ground ahead. Broad foot-wrappings or woven sole-panels that spread weight over soft places. Fire is treated with reverence and caution, because the living floor is fuel. Hearths are made in shallow trays of wet plant matter or in clay-lined basins carried from camp to camp. Flame is not casually built upon the mat. It is contained, fed sparingly with dried stems and dead leaf-edges, then extinguished completely. A good camp leaves no black scar.

Their shelters are low and close to the surface. A sleeping platform may be nothing more than woven panels lashed to nearby growth, raised just enough to keep a body above the night damp. Windbreaks are made from layered leaves and stems, flexible enough to bend rather than resist the wind. Drying racks hold insects, thin strips of amphibian meat, or seed-cases in the warm air. Nothing is made to outlast its use. If a shelter falls, it becomes part of the mat. If a path is abandoned, the stems rise again. The people understand that permanence would be a burden here. The plain tolerates presence, but not weight. It accepts small traces and then erases them.

Their bodies are shaped by the work of living lightly. They are often wiry rather than massive, with strong ankles, broad practiced feet, and lungs adapted to the high air. Their eyes are trained to read slight differences in the landscape that an outsider would miss: the darker green that hides soft wet layers, the faint yellowing that shows dry firmness beneath, the pattern of small animal tracks that indicates a safe line, the way certain plants stand taller where the mat is stable. They learn to walk with a rolling, testing step, placing weight gradually, listening to the sound the ground makes under pressure. Children learn early that a careless foot can break through a thin crust into cold saturated layers below. They learn to move without haste, but not without noise.

Noise is necessary. Silence on the plain is a warning.

The people make sound as they travel: a tap of a pole, a low call, a soft rattle of seed-cases tied to a pack, a whistle exchanged between members of a group. Their voices are adapted to distance. They use short calls that carry without becoming shouts, and they know how to interpret the plain’s sudden hushes. A silence may mean a predator passing. It may mean weather changing. It may mean a hatch about to begin, when insects gather so thickly that other creatures pause before feeding. The people have words for different kinds of quiet. Some are ordinary. Some require stopping. Some require moving away.

They are not silent hunters in the way lowland hunters might imagine. To hunt silently here would be dangerous. It would mean surprising the softstep or other predators. It would mean becoming invisible in a place where visibility and sound are part of survival. Instead, they announce themselves in measured ways. They use the plain’s own noise as cover, moving when the chorus is thick, pausing when it thins. They follow bird alarms. They watch where insects gather. They read ripples in pools, broken stems, the smell of disturbed leaf-water. Their knowledge is less like dominion and more like attention.

Water governs their days without ruling them harshly. They collect dew from broad leaves in the early morning, using absorbent pads of woven fiber that are wrung into containers. They take water from clear pools with shallow scoops, careful not to stir the bottom. They know that brown tannin water can be safe if taken from the right place, and that green cloudy water is alive and should not be fouled. When rain comes, they work quickly, turning leaf surfaces into temporary channels, filling seed-case bottles, letting the brief pulse of water enrich the camp before it vanishes. They do not expect rivers. They do not expect wells. They expect moisture in small, intermittent gifts, and they organize their lives around gratitude for them.

Their food is seasonal and opportunistic. During insect hatches, they dry larvae and adult forms on racks, making light cakes that keep well for a few days. They take eggs from abundant pools, but not from every pool. They eat tender plant shoots and seed-heads, knowing which parts can be taken without killing the structure that produced them. They may eat small birds or amphibians when the chance is right, but they do not depend on large kills. The plain does not offer much in the way of large meat. What it offers is repetition: many small lives, arriving in waves. The people move with those waves. They do not try to store a year’s wealth in one place. They store enough to reach the next good hollow, the next hatch, the next camp.

Their social life is shaped by openness and distance. Families may go long stretches without seeing one another, communicating by signs left in biodegradable materials: a knot in a stem, a woven marker pressed into a safe path, a pattern of leaves indicating water condition or predator movement. At certain times, when hatches are great or pools unusually stable, groups gather. These gatherings are not cities. They are temporary intensifications of presence. People trade tools, food, news, cordage, and stories. They arrange marriages, settle disputes, share knowledge of routes and weather. Then they disperse again, because the plain cannot support concentration for long. Their wealth is portable. Their memory is carried in practice, song, and the trained eyes of those who walk behind.

They have little use for heavy things. A possession must be carried, repaired, or left to disappear. Metal, if known at all, is distant and rare, a rumor from below rather than part of daily life. Their finest objects are made from what the plain gives: strong fiber cord, polished bone hooks, seed-case cups, woven panels, pins carved from bird bone or hard stem. They value lightness, balance, and reliability. A good tool is one that does not fail and does not burden. A good shelter is one that keeps off wind and damp without scarring the ground. A good path is one that leaves the mat able to forget it.

Their relationship to the fauna is intimate and unsentimental. They are surrounded by life, but not protected by it. They share the plain with predators, parasites, hunger, weather, and accident. They respect the softstep not as an evil thing but as a necessary one. If its signs are too fresh near a camp, they leave. If it approaches too closely, they make themselves known: upright, noisy, grouped together, not worth the risk. They do not hunt it casually. To kill such a predator would be to disturb a balance they depend on, and perhaps to invite worse instability. They may take an egg or a fallen feather, but they do not seek to master the creature.

They also live with insects in a way that outsiders might find overwhelming. Insects are food, nuisance, weather-sign, alarm, material, and sound. The people wear veils or head-coverings when swarms are thick. They oil or smoke their clothing to discourage biting forms. They know which hatches mean abundance and which mean irritation. They can sleep through the ordinary chorus but wake to an unusual one. Children learn to distinguish the rasp of harmless leaf-hoppers from the sharper noise of predatory swarms. They learn to notice when flies gather over something dead or wounded. They learn that the plain’s sound is not background. It is information.

Their dead are not buried, because the ground cannot be opened deeply without harm. Customs vary, but all are light. A body may be wrapped in woven fiber and laid on a raised platform, exposed to birds, insects, wind, and rain until only clean remains are left. Bones may be carried away by the living, placed in dry hollows, or returned to pools where water and small lives can take them. There is no grave monument. There is no stone. The plain’s way of remembering is to grow over. The people accept this. To be taken back by the living roof is not an insult. It is the final form of the same exchange that fed them while they walked.

Travelers born below would struggle to understand the plain’s habitation. They might see only emptiness: no towers, no roads, no fields, no herds, no forests. They would miss the density of small events. They would not see the insect clouds until their eyes learned the light. They would not hear the differences in the chorus until their ears were trained. They would step too heavily, sink into soft places, foul pools, frighten the feeding birds, and perhaps walk into the path of something that had been listening to them long before they noticed it. The locals know better. They move as if the plain were not a surface beneath them but a body with them.

And in a sense, that is what lives there: the plain itself as a distributed living thing. The mosses, root-felts, broad-leaved carpets, cup plants, creeping mats, and ridged wind-resistant growths are not merely scenery. They are architecture, waterworks, nursery, shelter, food, and floor. They breathe, grow, die, compress, and become the ground for the next generation. The creatures live in them and on them. The people live by their permission. Every pool is held by plant lips. Every safe path is made by roots binding together. Every shelter is borrowed from leaves and stems. The world there is built from living tissue, and every structure breathes.

So if one asks who lives there, the answer must be plural.

The insects live there, in overwhelming numbers, making the air shimmer and roar. The amphibians live there, filling the damp pockets with breeding calls and sudden splashes. The birds live there, running, hovering, watching, crying across the open distance. The hidden decomposers live there, turning death into the soft architecture of the floor. The predators live there, low and patient, keeping the chorus honest. The people live there, briefly and carefully, with carried fire, light tools, wide feet, and voices that answer the plain’s own noise.

No one owns the high green plain. It is owned by appetite, by water held in small amounts, by ancient softness, by the habit of not staying too long. Its inhabitants are those who can be loud without becoming heavy, numerous without becoming crowded, and present without leaving a lasting wound. The plain lives through them. They live through it. And when they pass, the mat rises, the pools fill, the insects return, and the sound closes over the place where they were.
