Who lives there is not one thing.

It is a pressure. It is a warmth. It is a sound so continuous that it becomes a kind of weather. The first answer is always too many: too many wings, too many mouths, too many legs, too many soft bodies folded into seams and hollows. The second answer is that nothing lives there alone. The third answer, the one you arrive at after listening long enough, is that the place is inhabited by a single, layered, breathing fullness.

If you tried to name the dwellers, you would not name them as species first. You would name them by what they do.

There are the stitchers. They are small and tireless and everywhere. Ants, larvae, spiders, silk-spinning grubs, tiny weavers whose bodies are their only instruments. They draw fibers together. They fasten leaf to leaf, feather to stem, fungal thread to old nest wall. They do not know they are making architecture. They know only the next pull, the next attachment, the next closing of a gap. Yet their work holds the loose world together. Where a surface might fray, they arrive. Where a seam opens, they thicken it. Their labor is so fine and so numerous that it seems less like building than like healing.

There are the chewers. Termites, beetle larvae, wood-borers, soft-bodied grubs, and countless other mouth-workers reduce tough matter to pulp. They eat what is fallen, what is shed, what is dead but not yet gone. Their mandibles make the oldest material new again. They soften stems, break down husks, grind old feathers into fibers, turn dry droppings into powder that can be carried and reused. Out of their chewing comes paste. Out of paste comes wall. Out of wall comes shelf, tunnel, terrace, and mound. They are not conscious builders, but they are builders nonetheless, because the place has made their hunger into construction.

There are the pellet carriers. They move in lines and streams, each one bearing a scrap, a seed, a bit of bark, a flake of dried fungus, a grain of something once alive. They do not pause to admire the whole. They only carry. Their bodies make paths by repetition. Their feet wear ramps smooth. Their burdens become layers. A thousand pellets make a bump; ten thousand make a ledge; generations make a slope. They are the ones who turn small motions into landforms.

There are the wax-makers and paper-makers. Bees and wasps and their kin add sealed cells, thin partitions, glossy coatings, and water-resistant surfaces. Their work smells faintly sweet, faintly sharp, like resin and warmth. They fill cracks with wax, line nurseries with smooth cells, and make the inner chambers of the basin safer for eggs and larvae. Their contribution is not vast in scale compared with the mountains of guano and fiber, but it is crucial. They give the place finishes: the sealed edge, the waterproof seam, the polished cell, the protected brood.

There are the guano masons, though they do not call themselves that. They are the bats. They hang in the high vaults and hidden ceilings, folded into their own wings, breathing together in hot darkness. Their bodies are a stored weather. When they roost, warmth rises. When they leave, the air feels emptied for a moment, as if the basin has exhaled. When they return, the sound is like rain taking flight. Their droppings fall through the dark and become floor, fertilizer, fuel, and foundation. Entire slopes are made from their accumulated presence. They do not build with intention, but their bodies make ground. In this place, even waste becomes geography.

There are the winged threaders: swifts, nightjars, small nocturnal birds, and others whose names would dissolve into their habits. They nest in the woven masses, in crevices of old fiber, beneath eaves of leaf and feather. Some stitch with beaks, some press with breasts, some plaster with saliva, some simply occupy a hollow until their bodies and droppings make it permanent. Their wings are constantly brushing the architecture, polishing it, loosening it, rearranging it. They are the restless surface of the place, the moving layer that makes the stiller layers possible.

There are the root walkers. These are not animals but plants, and yet they are inhabitants too, perhaps among the most important. Their roots move slowly through old nests, through compressed feathers, through decayed wood, through insect carton and fungal mats. They bind. They anchor. They drink. They split apart soft masses without violence, finding the spaces between lives. Where the basin might otherwise crumble, roots hold it in a patient grip. They are the slow fingers that make the accumulated world stable. They do not hurry, but they do not stop.

There are the water keepers. They are frogs mostly, but also tadpoles, larvae, beetles, worms, and the small trembling lives that exist in leaf cups, hollow stems, wax basins, and sealed chambers of resin and saliva. The frogs sit in their tiny reservoirs and call. Their voices are liquid, overlapping, ceaseless. They do not know they are announcing the presence of water; they simply are water made audible. Around them, other lives gather. Mosquito larvae wriggle in thin films. Tadpoles graze on algae. Beetles skim the surface. Fungi fringe the damp edges. These cisterns are not mere puddles. They are neighborhoods, nurseries, hunting grounds, and acoustic centers. The water keepers give the basin its wet voice.

There are the heat makers. Heat comes from many bodies, but especially from decomposition and packed roosts. The deep layers ferment gently. Old nests warm from within. Fungal beds radiate a low, living heat. Bats clustered overhead make the air thicker. The young in nurseries generate warmth simply by being alive and crowded together. The heat makers are not a single guild; they are a condition of the whole place. Every mouth that eats, every body that digests, every pile that breaks down adds to the temperature. This is why the basin feels feverish in places, why the air can press against the skin, why moisture hangs so close. The inhabitants are not merely living in the place; they are metabolizing it into climate.

There are the gardeners of decay. Fungi are among the quietest inhabitants, but they are essential. Their threads move through droppings, feathers, fruit, dead insects, shed skins, and collapsed plant matter. They knit debris together. They convert loss into fertility. They make pale mats where nothing else could grow. They fruit in damp seams, offering flesh to beetles, flies, and small mammals. Their glow, when it appears, is not decoration but evidence: the darkness is being worked. Without the gardeners of decay, the basin would choke on its own history. Instead, death is continually taken apart and returned.

There are the cleaners. Beetles, mites, flies, ants, worms, and countless unnamed small bodies move through the place with endless purpose. They remove what falls. They bury what softens. They strip carcasses down to memory. They enter old nests and carry away the unusable. They groom living feathers and fur, sometimes helping, sometimes taking, always participating in the economy of surfaces. Cleanliness here does not mean emptiness. It means circulation. Nothing is allowed to remain inert for long. Every death becomes a dispersal of material into new uses.

There are the predators, though they are not separate from the crowd. Snakes move slowly through the roosting masses, their scales dry and quiet amid damp fiber. Small owls wait in hollow chambers, their eyes tuned to the faintest movement. Spiders stretch webs across flight lanes and dark seams, catching moths, flies, and stray bits of pollen. Lizards flick their tongues in crevices and take insects from warm surfaces. These predators do not create panic because they are not exceptions to the place; they are part of its regulation. They keep any one life from overflowing into ruin. They are the restraint inside the abundance.

There are the small warm ones: rodents, climbing mammals, soft-furred nocturnal creatures with grasping feet and sensitive whiskers. They do not dominate the basin, but they move through it with familiar urgency. They eat fruit, seeds, insects, eggs, fungus, and whatever else the place offers in passing. They carry warmth from chamber to chamber. They nest in compressed feathers and chewed fiber. Their bodies leave scent trails, worn routes, and tiny disturbances. They are among the closest things to ordinary mammalian life here, and yet even they seem absorbed into the larger body of the basin. They do not stand apart. They are woven into the mass as surely as the birds and beetles.

There are the riders, the tenants of tenants. Mites live in feathers. Lice move through fur. Tiny flies cling to bats. Worms inhabit the gut. Fungal spores rest in skin folds and leaf axils. Every large creature here is also a landscape. A bird is not merely a bird; it is a moving thicket with its own hidden population. A bat is not merely a winged body; it is a warm cave that flies. A mammal is a heated terrain of scent and shelter. This nestedness is one of the deepest qualities of the place: life lives upon life lives within life. There is no simple individual, only arrangements of lives.

There are the larvae. They are the soft, pale, ceaseless middle of the place. They hatch in fruit, in dung, in carrion, in fungal flesh, in damp fiber, in hidden brood cells. They eat and grow and are eaten. They turn stillness into motion. They are the living currency of the basin, because they translate waste into bodies and bodies into wings. Without them, the place would slow. With them, it hums.

There are the singers, though none of them sings for an audience. Frogs call to claim moisture and attract mates. Insects rasp and buzz and click. Birds give sharp cries that cut through the upper galleries. Bats pour out in high, thin streams of sound. The singers make the darkness navigable. Sound is not ornament here; it is structure. A call can mark a territory, a roost, a cistern, a mate, a danger, a readiness. The basin’s architecture is as much acoustic as physical. Each level has its pitch. The lower hollows boom and drip. The middle terraces rustle and chatter. The upper galleries snap and whistle. The inhabitants live inside one another’s noises until the noise becomes a shared body.

There are the sleepers. This is easy to forget, because the place never becomes silent. But sleep is everywhere: bats folded in high dark vaults, birds tucked into woven pockets, insects motionless beneath leaves, mammals curled in warm chambers, snakes coiled in dry seams, frogs still in wet cups. The basin does not rest all at once. It rests in layers. While some sleep, others feed, call, mate, clean, hatch, and die. There is no single night and day here, only overlapping cycles. The place is always half-asleep and half-awake, like a vast animal breathing in dreams.

There are the young. Eggs, hatchlings, nestlings, larvae, pups, fledglings, nymphs, and soft newly emerged insects fill the nurseries. The nurseries are some of the warmest and most protected parts of the basin. They smell sweet, sour, milky, close. They are crowded with small mouths and patient attendants. The young are not merely offspring; they are the future shape of the place. Every repaired wall, every widened tunnel, every softened slope exists because there are bodies coming after the current ones. The basin is patient because it is always being inherited.

There are the old. Not many individuals grow old in the human sense, because so many lives are short and quickly recycled. But oldness exists as structure: ancient nest layers, hardened guano floors, root systems thickened over generations, worn paths polished by countless feet, chambers whose walls have been repaired so often that they have become something neither built nor grown but simply continuous. The old ones here are not necessarily individual creatures. Sometimes the oldest inhabitant is a wall. Sometimes it is a tunnel. Sometimes it is a roosting ledge whose shape has been maintained for longer than any living creature can remember.

There are the lost and the stray. Occasionally something arrives from beyond the rim: a seed carried by wind, a bird blown off course, a spore drifting through a high crack, an insect escaping a storm. The place receives these arrivals without ceremony. If they can live, they are absorbed. If they cannot, they are eaten or buried. The basin does not remain open to the outside for long. It turns even accident into material.

There are no rulers. There are no masters. There is no council, no throne, no singular intelligence directing the whole. Yet there is order. The order is made of appetite, habit, signal, and repetition. A frog calls because its body calls. An ant follows scent because scent leads. A bird nests where nesting has succeeded before. A bat returns to the roost where its warmth was once held. A beetle moves toward decay because decay feeds its young. Each creature obeys its own narrow need, and together these needs create the basin’s immense stability. The rules are not spoken. They are enacted.

If one insists on asking who lives there in the sense of “who” as a person, the answer becomes stranger. There is no one in the likeness of a separate self. There is no being that stands outside the crowd and calls it mine. But there is something like a collective presence. It has no face, but it has rhythms. It has no voice, but it has a chorus. It has no memory in words, but it has memory in shape: the curve of a worn path, the thickness of a wall, the position of a roost, the depth of a cistern, the location of a feeding ledge. Its memory is built.

This collective presence might be called the Fullness, though no one there would call it anything. It is the sense that every niche is already spoken for, every surface already touched, every silence already filled. It is the feeling that the basin is not merely inhabited but occupied to the point of being a body. The roosts are its lungs. The water pockets are its fluids. The fungal threads are its slow nerves. The flight lanes are its circulation. The nurseries are its soft interior. The waste chambers are its digestion. The sound is its pulse.

And yet it is not a single organism in the ordinary sense. It is a relationship so dense that it has become terrain. It is a multitude that has worn the shape of landscape. The inhabitants do not merely live in the place; they live as the place. Their bodies have made walls, floors, ceilings, bridges, terraces, and towers. Their deaths have made soil where there was no soil. Their waste has made ground where there was no ground. Their movements have made paths where no road was planned. Their voices have made chambers out of darkness.

What lives there, then, is not a list of animals, though animals are beyond number.

What lives there is the stitcher in the seam.

The chewer in the wall.

The carrier on the path.

The singer in the cistern.

The sleeper in the folded wing.

The predator in the crowd.

The larva in the fruit.

The mite in the feather.

The fungus in the dark.

The root in the old nest.

The bat in the vault.

The frog in the leaf cup.

The bird in the woven bridge.

The warmth in the decomposing mound.

The sound in the fibrous chamber.

The pressure of countless small lives packed together until they become world.

If you stand there, you will not meet a single inhabitant. You will be met by many small contacts: moisture on skin, wings near the face, legs across your sleeve, a beak testing a fold of cloth, a beetle mistaking you for bark, a frog’s call vibrating through your bones. You will become, for a time, a surface in the basin. A temporary ledge. A warm obstruction. A strange branch. The place will not hate you. It will not welcome you. It will simply include you in its calculations of shelter, warmth, moisture, and space.

And if you remain still long enough, you may feel the true answer settle over you like damp:

The high basin is not lived in by one creature.

It is lived by everything that has learned to make a home out of other lives.

It is lived by the accumulation.

It is lived by the chorus.

It is lived by the endless, patient, toolless multitude that has turned bodies into architecture and architecture into ground.

Whoever lives there is everyone, layered.

Whatever lives there is the place itself, awake in every part.
