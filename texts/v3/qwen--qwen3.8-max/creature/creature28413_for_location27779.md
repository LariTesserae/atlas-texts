What lives there is not yet numerous; it is early, various, and almost all of it is listening.

The first inhabitants are the ones that can make a life on a surface no wider than a breath: films of algae and cold-water microbes, green-brown skins over mineral dust, threads and cells so fine they look more like staining than growth. They live wherever wetness lingers long enough, especially on the pale stone that has only just begun to hold a surface. They bind grit, slow the drying of thin films, and begin the patient work of turning new rock into something less absolute. In the still pools they bloom in faint clouds; along the running channels they make clay slick and dark. They are not ornamental. They are necessary.

Then come the low plants, because water has made a possibility of every crack. Moss lives there as a slow congregation, thick cushions in shaded hollows, thin stitches along ledges, green seals over joints where stone has been newly fitted. It does not rush. It gathers rain like a held breath and gives it back slowly, making the basin more patient than it would otherwise be. Liverworts cling to weeping faces in flat, dark coins, their edges curled with damp. Ferns live in pockets of accumulated grit and unfurl with an almost excessive tenderness, their fronds glossy and heavy. Sedges and dwarf grasses fringe the marshy margins, trembling under droplets, their roots threading mineral soil that is barely soil yet. In wetter depressions, creeping plants form dense mats, and pale rootlets spread through gravel as if feeling for a promise.

These plants are not scenery. They are residents with work to do. Moss holds edges. Sedge slows runoff. Root-fibers bind loose grains. Where wooden scaffolds have stood long enough, algae darkens the grain and moss begins to seal the joints, so that even the tools of making are being quietly taken over by living tenants. The greenery is lush, but it is a lushness of texture rather than height: close, cold, tenacious, pressed against the ground as if aware that the sky above the basin is too open and too windy for tall ambitions.

Animals are rare, and those that are present seem tentative, as though they have arrived early and are not yet sure they wish to stay. Insects hover over the still pools: small midges, pale flies, perhaps caddisflies in cooler water, their movements quick and isolated. Some live only briefly above the surface; others have hidden larval lives under stones, in the thin current, in the submerged hair of moss. Small amphibians shelter under wet rocks, blinking when disturbed, their bodies glossy with the same moisture that covers everything else. They are the first obvious animals of the basin, and they seem to know that the place is not yet finished. They do not multiply into crowds. They keep to margins, to damp shadows, to the spaces between stone and water.

Occasionally a bird passes high overhead, drawn by light or air-currents rather than by any intention to remain. It does not linger. There are no roofs yet, no sheltered eaves, no abundance of food, no thicket to hide in. The basin is not ready for birdsong in any settled sense. It is ready only for brief crossings, for a wingbeat over wet stone, for the suggestion that later, when the city has edges and hollows, other lives may come.

The human figures are few enough that, from a distance, they can be mistaken for markers or tools left upright among the works. They move slowly among the terraces, cisterns, channels, and foundation walls, small against the open sky. They are not numerous, and they do not try to make themselves numerous. Their presence is careful rather than crowded. They live in the place as people live in a sentence that has not yet been completed: aware of the spaces that remain, aware that what is absent is part of the meaning.
