What lives there is layered, numerous, and adapted to being interrupted. Nothing in that basin has the luxury of assuming the ground will remain the same from one surge to the next, so life has become low, dense, practical, and alert. The inhabitants are not only creatures in the ordinary sense. Some are small enough to be mistaken for silt. Some are old enough to seem like weather. Some are human, or nearly human, and some are not quite separable from the marsh itself.

The first inhabitants are almost invisible: the maintenance motes in the mud. They drift through the black sediment in clouds too fine to see, working at the border of life and instruction. They bind loose particles, break down poisons, carry oxygen into root zones, and repair micro-fractures in the buried systems before those fractures can widen. They are not animals, exactly, but they are not inert either. They respond, feed, divide, and fail. In places where the water turns sour or the roots begin to blacken, they gather thicker. In places where the basin is stable, they thin out and become dormant, waiting in the mineral dark. To the larger creatures, they are nothing more than the reason the marsh does not choke itself.

Above them live the filaments. The black threads running through the mud are part root, part network, part grown machinery. They twine through reed beds, under gravel shelves, and beneath the angular basalt, forming a living web that senses pressure, chemistry, temperature, and flow. They are not conscious in a way a person could recognize, but they have direction. They carry signals too faint to be called thought and too persistent to be called accident. When a channel begins to silt up, the filaments know it before the water does. When a bank weakens, they tighten around it. When a surge is about to move through the basin, they pass the warning along in slow, cold pulses. The marsh’s larger inhabitants have learned, by instinct or experience, to respect what the filaments do.

The plants are among the truest residents. The reeds and sedges are not merely growing there; they are holding the place together. Their stems are tough, silica-laced, dark green to black, and they grow so tightly that they form living walls against wind and current. Their roots interlock with moss and filament, making mats that can bear weight one season and collapse the next. The mosses drink the cold water and keep it close, softening stone and trapping larvae. The low creeping plants in the shallows have oily leaves that shed frost and resist rot. The dwarf willows and shrubs on firmer banks grow sideways rather than up, their branches braided by wind into thickets strong enough to break a surge. These plants are not decorative. They are infrastructure. They feed, shelter, anchor, filter, and conceal. They are also home to smaller lives: insects in the stems, fungi in the roots, birds in the lower branches, fish among the drowned roots when the water rises.

Insects live there in overwhelming abundance. Midges and cold-water flies hatch in clouds over warm seeps, their bodies so dense they make a visible haze above the black water. Larvae inhabit the stems of reeds, the undersides of stones, and the soft mud around root knots. Some are ordinary in their appetites: they eat decay, algae, one another. Others have been shaped by the basin’s hidden systems. Certain caddis-like larvae build cases not only from sand and plant fragments but from tiny flakes of old composite, black glassy grains, and metallic dust. They crawl along the bottoms like living repair kits, collecting loose particles and cementing them where needed. In sheltered corners, beetle-shaped things with dull shells graze algae from sensor housings, never knowing what they are cleaning, only that the surface is food.

Fish move through the channels in tight, shifting bodies. They are cold-adapted, dark-backed, quick to turn. Some are small and silver-sided, flashing in the faint bioluminescence as they feed among the roots. Others are broader, bottom-dwelling fish with broad heads and whisker-like barbels that taste the water for minerals and electrical traces. Their scales catch the weak light and hold it for a moment, so that a school can seem like a moving seam of stars beneath the surface. They are not merely animals to be caught; they are part of the basin’s circulation. They stir nutrients, clear parasites from reed bases, and carry energy from deeper channels into the shallows. In some places, their movement is guided by faint field gradients they cannot perceive directly but obey anyway, turning as one body when the water tells them to turn.

The eel-like forms are harder to classify. Some are true animals: long, muscular, pale-bellied, with blunt faces and strong tails, living in root tangles and under rock shelves. Others are not entirely born. They have smooth skins, eyeless heads, and a way of moving that is too deliberate for hunger alone. They slide through cracks in the basalt, into submerged conduits, around buried fittings, and out again, often carrying nothing visible but leaving changed conditions behind them: a cleared blockage, a loosened knot of roots, a section of filament made to pulse again. The marsh people, if they notice them at all, regard them the way one regards weather or tides. They are part of the working system. One does not eat them unless starving. One does not trap them unless one has made a serious mistake.

Crustaceans occupy the gravel banks and the edges of the channels. They are small, hard-shelled, and numerous, with carapaces that take on the color of whatever they grow up among: basalt-black, mineral-grey, rust-brown, or the dull green of algae-covered stone. Some incorporate fragments of old manufactured material into their shells as they molt and repair, so that their backs may carry smooth black patches too regular to be natural. They scavenge dead stems, dead fish, and anything digestible that the water brings. They also aerate the lower mud and keep the coarser substrates from becoming sealed. Where they are abundant, the water stays cleaner. Where they vanish, something has gone wrong.

Birds are everywhere, and they make the basin feel both larger and more watched. Herons stand in the black shallows, motionless until they are not, their legs like broken reeds and their eyes fixed on movements no human would notice. They are not merely hunting. In that place, stillness is a kind of attention. When a heron lifts its head too suddenly, nearby rails scatter. When several herons take flight at once, the marsh people stop what they are doing. Rails and gallinules slip through the reeds with quick, lateral motions, their calls sharp and complaining. Waterfowl rest in long, uneasy lines on open water, rising and resettling as currents change. Smaller birds move in bursts between shelter, feeding on insects and seeds, their wings beating fast against the cold. Some carry traces of the outside world on their feet and feathers: pollen, parasites, mud from another valley, news of a different weather system. The birds are not symbolic to the basin. They are participants. They eat, excrete, scatter seeds, alarm one another, and reveal changes before the water itself makes them obvious.

Then there are the people.

They are not numerous, and they do not live as conquerors. They live as residents of a place that is still settling, which means they live lightly, carefully, and with constant attention. They are known, where they are known at all, by several names: marshfolk, silt-walkers, basin-tenders, the low people. They do not use one name for themselves in all circumstances. Identity among them is local. You are from a particular shelter, a particular stretch of channel, a particular line of old maintenance posts. You are known by what you repair, what you avoid, and how well you listen.

Their bodies have been shaped by cold water and by generations of living in a saturated environment. They are not tall. Height is a disadvantage where wind cuts across open marsh and where one must often crouch, wade, or move over uncertain footing. They tend to be compact and broad through the chest and hips, with dense bones and a layer of insulating fat that makes their movements look deliberate rather than slow. Their limbs are strong, especially their legs. Their feet are wide, with thick soles and toes that can spread for balance on soft ground. Between fingers and toes there is webbing, not delicate or ornamental but tough and practical, sometimes cracked at the edges from cold and constant wet, treated with rendered fish oil and moss salves.

Their skin is thick, oily, and resistant to waterlogging. It ranges from deep grey and green-black to mottled brown, often patched with darker algae growth or pale mineral stains where the basin has marked them. In lamplight, their shoulders and forearms gleam faintly, as if they have just come out of the water even when they have not. Some carry bioluminescent freckles, colonies of engineered algae living in the upper skin, giving off a faint blue-green glow when disturbed or cold. This is not regarded as beautiful or frightening. It is simply useful: a hand moving in the dark leaves a brief trace; a child sleeping can be found without a lamp.

Their eyes are large and adapted to low light, with pupils that open wide and a reflective layer behind the retina that makes their gaze flash amber, green, or silver when caught by a lamp. They have nictitating membranes that slide across the eye when diving or when wind throws spray. Their hearing is acute but protected; external ears are small and often covered by hoods or caps of woven reed. More important is their sense of vibration. Many have pressure-sensitive pits along the jaw, collarbone, and inner forearms, either inherited or grafted in early life, that let them feel changes in water movement and the deeper hum of the basin before those changes become audible. A tender standing thigh-deep in a channel can tell, by the behavior of the water against their skin, whether a surge is coming from above or below.

They dress for wet cold, not for display. Their clothing is layered, tied, and repairable. Inner wraps are woven from moss-fiber and reed fluff, soft when dry, still insulating when damp. Outer garments are made from tightly woven sedges, treated fish skin, or scavenged membrane materials that shed water. Boots are precious. They are constructed from local stone, cured reed, and fragments of old composite, with soles replaced again and again. Nothing hangs loose. Everything is fastened with knots, toggles, or clips carved from bone, antler, or black glassy material recovered from the mud. They carry pouches for tools, line, fire-starting material, dried food, and small sealed lamps. The lamps are rare enough to be respected. A lit lamp means work, warning, or access. Wandering light without purpose is wasteful and dangerous.

Their voices are not loud. In a place where wind and water make a constant roaring, shouting is inefficient. They speak in clicks, low throat beats, breath-whistles, and a complex language of hand signs. Words exist, but many are short, shaped to carry only a little distance or to be felt rather than heard. Among intimate groups, conversation can happen with almost no sound: a tilt of the head, a hand tapping the thigh twice, a finger tracing a line across the wrist to mean changing current. When they do call out over the marsh, it is usually a harsh, abrupt note, not unlike a rail’s alarm cry. It means stop, move, danger, or come here, depending on pitch and repetition.

They eat what the basin gives, and they have learned not to take too much. Fish are caught with woven traps, hand-nets, and short lines baited with insect larvae. Eels and crustaceans are taken cautiously, because some forms are not food and some are useful. Edible roots and tubers are harvested from firmer islands, where the vegetation is less tangled with active filaments. Algae is scraped from certain stones and pressed into cakes that dry slowly and taste of mineral salt. Insect larvae are collected in fine nets and roasted on flat rocks when fuel is available. Birds’ eggs are taken only in small numbers and only by those who know how to do so without provoking a mass disturbance. Nothing is wasted. Fish bones become tools or fertilizer. Reed stems become walls, mats, traps, and splints. Old composite fragments are too valuable to discard; they become walkway supports, lamp housings, knife blades, or patches for shelters.

Their shelters are small and practical. They stand on stilts above the waterline, anchored in gravel or driven into rock seams where the ground is stable enough to hold them for a few years. The legs are wrapped in climbing plants and sometimes fitted with old dampers that reduce vibration during surges. The floors are raised platforms made of woven reed, local stone, and salvaged panels. Walls are layered against wind and spray. Roofs slope low to shed frost and rain. Inside, there is little space: sleeping shelves, storage nets, drying racks, a small hearth or heat chamber if the occupants are lucky enough to possess one, and perhaps a sealed box of tools or heirlooms. The shelters are not meant to become permanent houses. They are meant to be abandoned, moved, or repaired as the basin shifts. A family may live in one place for a season, a decade, or a generation, but no one expects a structure to outlast the marsh’s changes.

Their settlements, if they can be called that, are not villages in any stable sense. They are clusters of use: a shelter here, a walkway there, a landing shelf, a fish trap, a marker post, a repair ring, a place where a certain old pylon provides shelter from wind. Paths are short and chosen carefully. They follow rock shelves, buried ridges, and old maintenance lines where the ground will bear weight. Where a path crosses open water, there may be a floating mat of woven reed anchored at both ends, or a series of stepping stones placed by generations of hands. None of these routes form a permanent network. A surge can erase them. A new channel can open overnight. The people respond by adjusting, not by fighting the change.

They know the basin’s rules deeply, and children learn them early. Do not linger in the lowest channels when the deep hum begins. Do not walk on reed mats that have not been tested. Do not cut the black filaments. Do not build heavy structures where the ground is new. Watch the birds. If the waterfowl rise together, something is moving. If the herons leave, leave. If the reeds flatten in a pattern that is not wind, get to higher ground. If a lamp flickers in a sequence that has no obvious cause, do not approach unless you know the code. If the water tastes suddenly metallic, stop drinking and find a known clean seep. These are not superstitions. They are survival instructions refined by consequence.

Children grow up close to water and noise. They are carried on backs, tied into sleeping shelves, and allowed to crawl on safe platforms while the marsh roars around them. They learn to swim before they learn to walk far, because swimming is not optional. They are taught to float face-up in cold water, to conserve breath, to read the surface for hidden currents. They are taught to touch the ground lightly, to test before trusting, to move with their weight low. A child who panics in the marsh is in danger, so much of early training is about calming the body under pressure. Older children learn maintenance: how to clear a blocked channel without damaging root mats, how to replace a marker light, how to weave a trap, how to identify which mosses can be eaten and which indicate poison. Some children show an unusual sensitivity to the basin’s hum and are apprenticed to tenders who listen more closely than most.

The people who live there are not isolated from one another, but they are not densely connected either. Households and small crews know their neighbors by sound, by marker patterns, by the particular way a shelter’s lamp is shaded. Contact happens at stable landings, along old maintenance paths, or during seasonal movements of fish and birds. There are exchanges: tools, food, information, breeding stock for plants, fragments of useful material, stories of outside weather. There are also tensions, as there always are where resources are limited and mistakes are costly. A family that blocks a channel, overharvests a nursery pool, or damages a filament line may find itself avoided or asked to move. There is little appetite for punishment in the abstract. Consequences are immediate and practical. The marsh does not tolerate negligence, and neither do the people who depend on it.

Their relationship to the hidden technology is intimate but not reverent in a simple way. They do not worship the buried systems as gods, because they know too much about them. They know which stones are warm in frost, which weirs release at certain pressures, which lights mean access and which mean warning. They know that some regulators must be left alone, that others can be nudged with a pole or a carefully placed stone, and that some respond only to the black filaments. They repair what they can, but their repairs are often biological: encouraging moss to seal a crack, planting reeds to stabilize a bank, introducing a particular larva to clean a surface. When they encounter something too advanced or too damaged to understand, they mark it and move on. They have a phrase that translates roughly as “not for hands yet,” meaning that a thing may be important but not ready to be touched.

Some among them are more deeply changed than others. In certain shelters live people with black lines under their skin, fine traces where filaments or sensor grafts have become part of their nervous systems. These are not rulers or priests. They are often quiet, practical individuals who spend long hours standing in water, listening. They can feel a change in flow through their bones. They can tell when a weir is about to release by a pressure behind the eyes. They may be less comfortable in dry places, less patient with ordinary talk, more attuned to bird movement and the behavior of fish. The others respect them and give them room. No one forces such changes. They happen by accident, by necessity, or by choice after years of close work with the basin. Some who undergo them remain mobile. Others eventually settle near a regulator rock or a warm seep and become part of the local system in a more stationary way, still themselves, still speaking when spoken to, but increasingly oriented toward the slow language of water and maintenance.

There are also outsiders, though few stay long. Traders, refugees, researchers, and lost travelers sometimes enter the basin from the older terrain beyond the horizon. They arrive with wrong boots, loud voices, and a habit of staring at the lights as if they are marvels. The marsh people watch them. If outsiders are careful, willing to learn, and not too many in number, they may be allowed to shelter until conditions improve. If they are greedy, clumsy, or dismissive, they are guided away or left to make their own mistakes. The basin is not cruel, but it does not forgive ignorance. A person who cuts a filament out of curiosity can destabilize a whole nursery pool. A person who builds a fire where heat can trigger a release may flood a sleeping shelf. The marsh people have no desire to keep outsiders out entirely, because outsiders bring useful things, but they insist that newcomers become quiet quickly.

The dead are handled in ways that fit the place. There are no large graves, because the ground is too wet and too unstable. Bodies may be wrapped in reed mats and laid in a chosen channel where the water will carry them into the basin’s cycle. In some families, the dead are placed near root beds, where filaments and moss can take what they need. In others, bones are kept as markers or tools after the flesh has been returned to water. Nothing is wasted, and nothing is entirely removed. To live in the basin is to be part of it; to die there is to become part of its ongoing construction.

The largest inhabitant is not any single creature, nor even the people. It is the basin as a living, regulated whole. It has no face and no center, but it behaves. It opens and closes channels. It holds water in some compartments while releasing it elsewhere. It warms certain seeps, cools certain shallows, oxygenates root zones, and flushes toxins before they accumulate. It responds to storms, to migration, to biological pressure, to the slow accumulation of peat. Its “thoughts” are distributed across motes, filaments, fish schools, bird alarms, plant growth, and hidden regulators. It does not speak in words, but it speaks in consequences. If a channel is overused, it silts. If a bank is weakened, it collapses. If a population grows too dense, food shifts, predators gather, and movement changes. The people understand, in their practical way, that they are living inside something larger than themselves. They do not resent this. It is simply the condition of being there.

Because of that, the inhabitants share certain qualities. They are patient. They are observant. They are not sentimental about comfort. They know how to be wet, cold, hungry, and alert without making a drama of it. They value repair over novelty, because novelty in the marsh often means danger. They value attention over strength, because the basin punishes brute force. They value lightness: light structures, light steps, light use. They have learned that survival in an in-between country requires becoming in-between oneself: neither fully land nor water, neither wholly animal nor wholly part of the machine, neither permanent nor transient, but something capable of moving between states.

If you were to see one of the marsh people from a distance, you might mistake them at first for a rock, a clump of reeds, or a heron standing still. They do not move unless movement is necessary. When they walk, they test each step with the outer edge of the foot before committing weight. When they stop, they listen. Their faces are calm, not from absence of feeling but from long practice in waiting. Their hands are always busy or ready to be busy: adjusting a knot, clearing a stem from a path, touching a filament line without disturbing it, cupping a lamp flame against wind. They smell of water, crushed reeds, fish oil, mineral mud, and the faint clean charge that rises from the hidden systems beneath the surface.

At night, or in the near-darkness that passes for day there, they become even more themselves. The cold deepens. The mist thickens. The amber marker lights glow at intervals. The algae in the water pulses faintly where fish move or where a current changes. The people move through this with careful familiarity. They know which sounds mean nothing and which mean action. They know the pitch of wind in the reeds, the slap of water against a newly exposed shelf, the click of thin ice forming and breaking. They know when the birds will sleep and when they will rise. They know the basin is loud, and they have made their lives inside the noise.

So what lives there? Everything that can live in negotiation. Microbes that build soil. Plants that anchor the young ground. Insects that clean and feed. Fish that move energy through the channels. Birds that carry alarm, seed, and hunger across the marsh. Eel-like maintainers that may be animal or device or both. People who have become quiet, dense, webbed, and watchful enough to call such a place home. And beneath all of them, threading through mud and root and water, the basin itself: a vast, dispersed, still-forming inhabitant that lives by adjusting, filtering, releasing, and enduring.
