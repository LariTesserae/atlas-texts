Under the bright water there is no mud, no peat, no tangle of drowned roots. There is a completed floor: pale membrane, welded seams, capillary mats, ceramic lattices, warming lines, and drainage channels cut to widths that were argued over in meeting rooms and signed off. The sun does not find a bottom there; it finds a specification. Light strikes the shallow basin and is returned almost whole, so the marsh is lit from below as well as above. Everything that lives in it must accept glare as its first condition.

What lives there is not wild in the old sense. It is installed, permitted, adjusted, and loved by maintenance. The smallest residents are the seam-menders: minute, translucent workers no bigger than grains of salt, bred into the lining hatcheries and released as larvae. They crawl the welds in silver threads, tasting for leaks, eating micro-fissures, exuding a resin that cures into the same color as the basin. When they die, they become part of the seal. No one sees them individually, but the marsh stays whole because of their continuous, invisible labor. At night, where they are thick along a seam, they give off a faint blue glow, and the Finishers call that the line breathing.

Above them stand the reeds, though they are not reeds as older wetlands knew them. They are standpipes, pale green columns with jointed bodies and shuttered leaves. Their roots are plugs; their stems are cooling towers in miniature. At noon they open their pores and give the air a thin hiss of vapor. At dusk they close and hold the day’s heat just long enough to keep the channels from chilling too fast. They do not seed themselves. They are replaced by sections when a panel of them tires. Inside their hollows live pith-wasps, black and quick, no larger than staples. The wasps move pollen-analogues from socket to socket and keep the standpipes from locking with scale. If the marsh has a smell at noon, it is the smell of warm polymer, clean water, ozone from the pumps, and the green-metal breath of those pipes opening.

The first animals one notices are the panel newts. They lie flat on the broad sheets where the sun is strongest, looking at first like discarded samples of wet paper. Their backs are pale and mirror-bright; their bellies are clear enough to show the color of the fluid moving through them. They are living indicators. If the alkalinity drifts, their veins darken to lilac. If a warming coil leaks, their skin flashes copper. The children of the Finishers are taught to read newts before they are taught to read gauges, because newts complain with their whole bodies and cannot be persuaded to show a false green.

In the deeper lanes, where the water is allowed to be a hand’s width deeper, move the mirror carp. They are broad, slow fish with scales polished by the mineral budget of the marsh. They feed on films of light-loving microbes that grow on the upper surfaces, and they turn together in long, blinking schools. When they feed, the basin flashes. From the rim walkways it can look like signaling, as if the marsh is sending messages to itself across the glare. The carp keep the bright surfaces clean, but they also eat the eggs of lesser fish that have no official place there. No one mourns those eggs. Completion has a strict table of allowed lives.

The herons are called boilerbirds. They are low, wide birds with pale wings and dark inner panels that open at dawn. They do not wade in the old way, stabbing at mud; there is no mud to stab. They stand on insulated pads and drink heat. All morning they open themselves to the sun and to the warm exhalation of the channels. All afternoon they shift from foot to foot, adjusting their angle by fractions. At dusk they release what they have gathered over the nursery channels, where the young are raised in shallow cradles of controlled temperature. Their calls are not songs but two-note warnings: one for seepage, one for strangers. The Finishers keep their nests away from the main sumps, because boilerbirds are useful but too fond of warmth and have once nested over a valve until the marsh nearly ran fevered.

The people who live there are called Finishers by outsiders, though among themselves they often say the Kept. They are the descendants of the last construction crews, the calibration teams, the inspectors who stayed past the final inspection because no finished thing is ever left alone. Their houses are low and white, set on floating pads and connected by shaded walkways. Every roof gathers condensation; every wall is a screen against glare. They wear wide hats, reflective capes, and dark lenses, but even so their faces are marked by brightness: fine lines at the eyes, lips cracked at the corners, skin burnished to a permanent warmth. They walk softly. Sound carries too well across the basin. Their tools are polished so they will not shed rust into the water. They do not use anything that can become mud.

Their lives are shaped by the terror of accident. The Finished Marsh has no natural substrate, and the Kept treat natural substrate as contraband. Soil is a rumor of disorder. A handful of dirt could carry a seed, a worm, a fungus, a future that was not designed. If a bird brings in a seed, it is removed with tongs and burned in a closed vessel. If a crack reveals any dark grain, any powder not accounted for in the original schedule, it is reported like a wound. The Kept do not hate life; they hate unplanned succession. They believe that what is completed must be defended from becoming something else. A child’s first duty is to find a leak. A child’s second duty is to learn that some leaks are not water but time entering where it should not.

Still, they are not joyless. The marsh has its pleasures, all of them bright and precise. In the cool first hour, the basin turns rose and silver, and the standpipes make a sound like many people inhaling after silence. At midday the glare is so complete that shadows become blue coins under walkways. In the evening, when the heat lifts slightly, the mirror carp rise and the boilerbirds close their wings, and the water seems to thicken into a warm metal. The Kept gather on the rim to listen to the pumps change pitch as the night cycle begins. Their greatest festival is the Night of No Alarms, when every gauge rests green, every seam is quiet, and the basin holds its breath without effort. They do not dance wildly. They walk the perimeter with lanterns hooded against reflection, and when they finish the circle they say the same word together: complete.
