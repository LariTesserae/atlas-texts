If you ask what lives there, the first answer is not a creature. It is a correction.

Almost nothing lives there, if you mean life as greenness, moisture, herds, nests, breath clouding in cold air, the busy metabolism of wet worlds. Almost nothing lives there, if you mean abundance. The plain will not permit that kind of living. It has passed beyond the age that rewards it.

But if you ask what persists, what attends, what moves with intention through the dry storms, what has made a home out of finality, then the answer begins to multiply in small, strange ways.

The inhabitants of the terminal high plain are not numerous. They are rare, distributed, and patient. They do not crowd. They do not build high. They do not leave much behind. Most of them are not individuals in the way a traveler from a younger world would expect. Some are bodies. Some are fields. Some are habits preserved in mineral. Some are brief architectures that exist only while the air is charged. Some are so close to the ground itself that to call them separate life feels like an error in boundary-making.

What lives there has learned to exist without thirst.

It conducts.

The first beings a careful observer is likely to notice are the ones that seem almost geological: the lichen crusts, the metallic rosettes, the brittle filaments, the dark scales set into shallow cracks and the protected lee sides of stone plates. They are not plants, though they borrow the posture of plants. They do not reach toward water because there is none to reach for. They spread instead as conductive skins and mineral lattices, harvesting what the plain still offers: light, thermal difference, electrostatic gradient, the faint charge left by passing turbulence.

These growths are among the oldest living things on the plain. A handspan of copper-gray rosette may be older than languages. A dark, glassy crust may have been slowly extending itself since before the last visible mountain disappeared. They do not grow toward anything in a hurry. They do not bloom extravagantly. Their increase is measured in micrometers, in the addition of crystalline cells, in the slow capture of potential.

Yet they are not passive. The patterns they form are too deliberate to be accidental. Arcs. Broken grids. Concentric rings. Lines that stop abruptly as if obeying an unseen boundary. In places where storms pass often, the lichen arranges itself into configurations that resemble circuitry, notation, or survey work. It is difficult to say whether this is growth, cultivation, or computation. Perhaps those distinctions no longer apply. The lichen may be recording the storms. It may be participating in them. It may be a sensory surface left running long after whatever mind first valued sensation has changed its nature.

The wiry tufts that stand a few centimeters tall in sheltered hollows are even more elusive. They give the impression of grass remembered imperfectly by a place that has forgotten grass. They are dry as filaments, brittle as old wire, and when charged air passes through them they stiffen and brighten at the tips. For a moment they look alive in a familiar way: like sparks caught in a field of straw. Then the charge passes, and many of them crumble.

They are not tragic. They do not seem to experience their own brevity as loss. Some release tiny mineral grains into the turbulence, and those grains may travel enormous distances before coming to rest. Most never germinate. Most are not meant to. The ecology of the plain does not depend on success in the ordinary sense. It depends on rare events occurring across enough time.

These rooted or rooted-looking forms are not the whole of life there, but they are its quiet foundation. They make charge legible. They make surfaces inhabitable. They turn the bare mineral skin of the plain into a place where even rarer beings can pause, feed, or listen.

Above them, or just above them, move the drifters.

The veil-like beings are perhaps the closest thing the plain has to animals, though that word fits them badly. They are broad, thin, nearly transparent, like moving panes of smoked mica or stretched mineral film. They glide centimeters above the ground on electrostatic lift, silent, unhurried, with no visible limbs, no mouths, no eyes, no organs that declare themselves as organs. They do not swim through air so much as they are permitted by it.

They feed, if feeding is the word, on charged dust and on the subtle energy fields generated by the storms. They may also take potential from the lichen crusts, though they do not seem to damage them. They pass over the ground as if reading it without touching it. Sometimes one will appear alone in the middle distance, its body almost invisible except for a faint darkening against the pale plain and a network of internal lines that flicker when the air is unstable. Sometimes three or four will appear near evening, moving along separate paths that nonetheless feel coordinated, as though they are obeying the same unheard instruction.

They do not seem to communicate by sound. They have no need of it. When they gather, their internal lines brighten and fade in sequences too regular to be random. The patterns may be social. They may be navigational. They may be reproductive. They may be nothing so definite. The plain does not explain itself. It only presents phenomena and allows them to remain unresolved.

The drifters are rarely seen for long. They move into distance without seeming to hurry, and because the air is so clear, they may remain visible long after they should have become small. Then a swell of the plain, a shifting metric, or a dry storm will take them, and they are gone. They leave no tracks. They leave no scent. They leave only a faint residual charge in the air, quickly dispersed.

Their deaths, if one can call them deaths, are even quieter. A drifter that loses coherence does not fall dramatically. It settles. Its transparency becomes dull. Its edges fray into films no thicker than dust. Then the plain’s turbulence takes the matter back. There is no corpse. There is no decay. There is only a brief local exhaustion of potential.

Harder and slower are the plated beings.

They are found near lichen crusts, in shallow depressions, beside marker stones, and in the shelter of structures too perfect to be natural. They are no larger than a hand, compact and multi-plated, with surfaces like old ceramic or dark enamel. They move in pauses. A limb, if it is a limb, shifts. Then stillness. Then another shift. They seem to consider each motion across centuries.

They do not eat in any visible way. They press themselves against mineral growths and remain there for long periods. Perhaps they exchange ions. Perhaps they draw charge through contact. Perhaps they listen. Their stillness is not inert. It has the quality of attention. If you come near one, it may not flee. It may simply stop, becoming even more still, as if deciding whether your presence is a temporary error or a permanent condition.

Their plates are often marked with fine lines, not painted but integral, as though their bodies have been grown around instructions older than their kind. Some of these markings resemble the patterns found in the lichen. Some resemble the alignments of buried artifacts. It is possible that these creatures are not merely animals but symbiotic instruments, living remnants of technologies that no longer distinguish themselves from biology. They may tend the lichen. They may be tended by it. They may be mobile nodes in a network whose center is nowhere.

They do not hunt. Nothing on the plain appears to hunt. Predation would require surplus, and the plain has no surplus. Conflict, when it occurs, is rare and almost invisible: a slight displacement of charge, a withdrawal, a slow change of position. The plated beings may compete for stable gradients, for favorable cracks, for proximity to a marker that hums with useful potential. But they do not tear one another. They do not consume. They endure.

During intense storms, they retreat into cracks or beneath stone plates. Afterward, they emerge to move across surfaces where the turbulence has left new patterns. They may press themselves to places where dust has arranged itself into lattices, remaining there until the pattern disperses. They may be feeding on the memory of the storm.

Then there are the storm-born.

These are the hardest to describe because they do not remain. They are not bodies in the stable sense. They are temporary assemblies of dust, charge, light, micro-machines, and whatever passes for will in the plain’s turbulent skin. They appear during the dry storms, when columns of pale particulate rise and twist, when sheets of glittering matter slide soundlessly over the ground, when pebbles lift and separate by size and the air shimmers with static intention.

Some of these forms are small: little towers of aligned grains, crystalline flowers that open for seconds in charged air, delicate arches that stand just long enough to be seen. These may not be alive, or they may be alive in the most minimal sense: as coherent processes that maintain themselves briefly against dispersion.

Others are larger and more troubling.

Tall, jointed shadows have been glimpsed inside dust columns. Bright ribbon-like flickers cross the upper air during severe turbulence. Shapes resembling ribs, sails, buildings, or writing appear and dissolve. These are not necessarily imitations of familiar things. It may be that the plain’s turbulence, when sufficiently complex, naturally produces forms that a traveler’s mind translates into architecture or script. Or it may be that these assemblies are the temporary bodies of something genuinely alive: beings made of field and particulate, existing for minutes or hours, born from storm potential and dying when the charge relaxes.

They do not leave corpses. They leave altered conditions. A place where a tall shadow stood may afterward have slightly different grain alignment. A patch of ground may repel dust for a day. A marker may hum more strongly. The plain remembers, but not as memory is understood by creatures with brains. It remembers as geometry, as conductivity, as permission.

Some of the storm-born may incorporate fragments of other life. A drifter caught in a turbulence column might become part of a larger flickering structure for a few moments. Grains released by wiry tufts may be incorporated into temporary lattices. Micro-scale machines in the regolith may join these assemblies without intention, simply because they are there and the storm gives them a role. The boundary between organism, weather, and mechanism is weak in such moments.

This may be the plain’s true society: not stable individuals, but temporary collectives that arise when conditions allow and dissolve when their coherence becomes too dangerous. Nothing large is allowed to persist. The moment a storm-born form becomes too massive, too continuous, too imposing, the plain’s deeper rules begin to disassemble it. Materials lose coherence. Fastenings forget their purpose. Mass becomes temporary. The form does not crash or explode. It unravels. It becomes possibility again.

If these assemblies are alive, they are the most numerous inhabitants of the plain. If they are not alive, then the plain has no need of the word.

Beneath all of this, or within all of this, is the regolith itself.

It is not alive in the way a body is alive. It does not have a shape one can point to. It does not move as an animal moves. But it is not inert. The plain’s dust and powdered mineral matter contain a latent engineering finer than pollen, older than the last mountain range. It operates through principles that feel less like machinery and more like law: metric smoothing, low-energy topological rearrangement, inertial damping, matter memory, quantum annealing at planetary scale.

This lattice does not manufacture objects. It permits and forbids configurations.

It is why footprints vanish. It is why tracks smooth themselves out with a faint ripple, as if the plain is correcting a misspelling. It is why ambitious structures cannot rise. It is why the storms churn but the bedrock remains untouched. It is why the landscape has reached its final geometry and yet is not still.

To call this lattice an inhabitant may be wrong. It may be more accurate to call it the condition of habitation. But the longer one remains on the plain, the less satisfying that distinction becomes. The lattice responds. It maintains. It resists certain changes. It allows others. It seems to have preferences, though preferences may be too strong a word. It favors smallness. It favors distribution. It favors low attachment. It opposes accumulation. It disassembles whatever insists too strongly on being permanent.

If the plain has a mind, this is where it lives: not in a skull, not in a tower, not in a hidden city, but in the dispersed intelligence of the ground itself. The storms may be its thoughts. The rare artifacts may be its fixed ideas. The living things may be its allowable exceptions.

And then there are the built remains.

They are not numerous. They are not grand. They are not cities. They are low domes of fused dust, black ceramic cairns, slender marker needles, shallow shelters, rings of pale posts, single cubes set into the ground with faces aligned to directions that do not match the horizon. They are too perfect in some ways, too worn in others. Their edges remain sharp where they should have dulled. Their surfaces repel dust without seeming to. Some hum faintly when touched, not with electricity, but with postponed action.

These places are not simply artifacts. Some of them may be inhabited, if inhabitation can mean a residual protocol maintaining itself with almost no energy. Some may contain minds reduced to their smallest durable form. Some may be instruments left running after their original purpose became unclear. Some may be the last traces of intelligences that learned to leave very little behind.

It is possible that the builders did not vanish. It is possible that they diminished. It is possible that they edited themselves down until only calibration remained.

A black cube may not be a monument. It may be a waiting mind.

A dome whose interior is unnaturally still may not be a shelter. It may be a held breath.

A ring of posts around a patch of ground where dust refuses to settle may not be a ruin. It may be an attention still turned toward something no longer visible.

These built presences are among the most unsettling inhabitants of the plain because they suggest intelligence without display. They do not communicate openly. They do not beckon. They do not explain. If they possess awareness, it is narrow, patient, and specialized. They may know only one thing: a direction, a measurement, a threshold, a command. They may have been maintaining that one thing for ages longer than ordinary memory.

They are not friendly. They are not hostile. They are exact.

If persons live on the plain, they are not persons in the old wet sense.

An ordinary human body would be an offense there, or at least an anomaly: too much water, too much heat, too much noise, too much need. Such a being could pass through only in sealed protection, carrying its own atmosphere and moisture, leaving footprints that the plain would erase almost immediately. It would be a visitor, not an inhabitant. It would feel the silence as pressure. It would feel the emptiness as threat. It would hear its own breathing and heartbeat as crude mechanical sounds.

But the rare figures glimpsed at great distance, moving with impossible patience across the terminal expanse, may be something else.

They are few. They are thin. They do not seem burdened by thirst, cold, or the usual urgencies of flesh. Their surfaces, when seen closely enough, are too smooth, too cool, slightly too perfect. They may be post-biological. They may be descended from travelers who adapted themselves to the plain by becoming less like animals and more like the artifacts scattered across it. They may be ceramic, conductive, partly mineral, partly field-supported. They may not breathe in the ordinary way. They may not drink. They may not eat.
