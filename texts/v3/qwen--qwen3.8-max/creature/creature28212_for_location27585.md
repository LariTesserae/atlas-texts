Pools are where the hollows first confess life.

They are shallow, clear, cold, and easily mistaken for empty. But in the thin margins where water meets rock, something is always breathing. A greenish film darkens the damp side of a basin. A faint rust bloom gathers in a crack. A white crust forms where evaporation leaves mineral lace. These are the first inhabitants: algae, cyanobacteria, diatoms, fungi, and bacteria living together in skins so thin they seem less like organisms than like weather made edible. They bind dust into a surface. They turn wet stone into a beginning. They are patient, patient, patient, and they ask almost nothing: a little seepage, a little gray light, a little time.

In the brightest places—“bright” meaning only that the twilight there has a direction—these films become richer. They make a soft green stain along bead-lines of dripping water, especially beneath vertical throats where daylight seeps down through mist. Mosses and liverworts take hold in cracks where the seep is steady, their leaves small and dark, their roots threading the stone like black hairs. A few fern fronds may unfurl where a shaft admits enough light, but they remain stunted, leaning upward as if remembering a sky they will never reach. There is almost no soil. What soil there will be has not yet been made. Life here is still in the act of manufacturing ground.

Then the film is grazed.

The first animals are small enough to seem like motes in the wet air. Springtails and mites move through the microbial crusts, pale or translucent, some no larger than a grain of mineral dust. They crop algae and fungal threads with mouths too fine to see. Tiny snails with shells like boiled skin slide over damp faces of rock, leaving trails that glimmer briefly before drying into silver. Larvae of gnats and midges live in the dampest films, their bodies clear enough that the darkness inside them looks like a swallowed shadow. Where fungal hyphae stitch the dust into felt, small beetles and beetle-like things tunnel, eating the weave and leaving behind fine pellets that become the first true crumbs of future soil.

Their numbers are not obvious, but they are vast. The aerosol in the hollows is partly their making: condensation on cold stone, yes, but also the exhalation of countless small bodies. In the dimmer basins, one can almost feel the air thickened by living. It is not a smell of rot. It is a clean, mineral dampness with a faint living edge, like cold water held in the mouth too long.

There are more visible lives in the air and along the break-lines. Where openings reach far overhead, insects fall or descend by accident: moths with torn wings, beetles, flies, occasional caterpillars still clinging to a leaf from the upper world. These are treasures. They land on shelves and die, or they survive long enough to become prey. Fungus gnats hover near damp crusts, their legs dangling like threads. Some of the larger winged things are cave crickets or relatives of them, long-antennae sweeping the air, bodies pale and humped. They do not sing loudly. Their sound is a dry ticking, a rubbing, a small hesitation in the silence.
