During a heavy burst of rain, the bowl does not empty. It fills.

Not with panic, not with flight, but with use. The inhabitants draw inward and upward, shift along covered ways, lift covers from cisterns, test gutters with their hands, shout warnings down the channels. What lives there is best understood not as one creature, one tribe, or one species, but as a crowded wet congregation: a people braided into their place, their animals braided into them, and the plants holding all of it together like a living net.

The dominant human life in the bowl is a small, dense, rain-shaped people. Outsiders, if any ever climb far enough through mist and slipping stone to find them, might call them moss-folk, greenfolk, drip-born. They do not use such names for themselves except in stories or trade-talk. They call themselves the Bowl-folk, or simply the People of the Wet House. They are human in face, voice, grief, and laughter, but they have been made strange by generations of living in a place where nothing is ever truly dry.

They are not tall. The bowl does not encourage largeness. Most are compact and broad through the chest, with strong shoulders, long arms, and hands made for gripping wet wood. Their fingers are not delicate; they are practical, thick-knuckled, callused, with nails kept short and hard. Their feet are their most telling feature. The toes are long, widely spaced, and strong, the nails curved and toughened until they seem almost claw-like on the stair-roots and bridge rails. Between toe and heel the skin is rough, patterned with callus, accustomed to wet planks, moss slicks, and the give of living floors. They walk with a rolling, deliberate balance, never in a hurry unless there is urgent cause, because haste in the bowl is how one falls.

Their skin is cool to the touch, not slimy but always faintly damp. It takes on the colors of their life: green stains from crushed moss, brown from bark tannin, gray from stone dust, blue-black from bird dung and pond scum. They are often mottled without intending to be. A child’s knees may be permanently green. An elder’s forearms may carry a map of pale scars from thorn, awl, splinter, and pruning knife. Their hair is usually dark, sometimes with a greenish cast, often worn short or tightly braided to keep it from catching in tools, nests, and woven walls. Some twist it with fibers, feathers, or strips of bark until it becomes almost architectural. In the wet, hair that hangs loose is a nuisance, so it is bound, tucked, or covered.

Their eyes are used to gray light and sudden green-gold shafts. They are not luminous, not uncanny, but quick. They notice movement: a trembling leaf, a swelling channel, a bird shifting in the eaves, a crack in a root-rail. They listen as much as they look. Their ears are trained to separate water-sounds, because in the bowl a change in the sound of dripping can mean a blocked gutter, a failing cistern, or a storm about to overload the upper rim.

They smell of the place: wet bark, moss, smoke held under roofs, frog-muck, fish oil, herbs, damp wool or hair, cold stone. A stranger might find the smell overwhelming at first, because it is not the smell of dry houses or swept streets. It is the smell of growth and decay kept in balance. The Bowl-folk do not notice it after the first few breaths. To them, it is simply the smell of home.

They dress for water, not against it. They know they will be wet; they do not waste effort pretending otherwise. Their clothing is light when the air is warm, layered when the mist turns cold. They wear woven sedge capes, bark-cloth wraps, softened hides from the small mountain deer or goats kept on the outer terraces, and sometimes cloaks made from molted feathers stitched into netting. In heavy rain they use broad leaf-hoods and overlapping bark capes that shed water like shingles. Beneath these, they wear simple kilts, wraps, aprons, or close-fitting garments of woven fiber. Children often wear very little until they are old enough to work the channels, because children in the bowl are always wet anyway.

Ornament is practical or earned. A bone awl at the belt, a polished stone weight, a pouch of pitch, a netted bag for snails, a feather quill tucked behind the ear, a string of snail shells, a whistle carved from a bird bone—these are common. They paint themselves with clay, ash, plant dyes, and charcoal, not for vanity alone but for work marks, season marks, kin marks, and rain protections. Some paint thin lines under their eyes to cut glare from pale mist-light. Some mark their hands with patterns that show which ward or household they belong to. In festivals, they wear bird feathers, dried beetle shells, and garlands of moss, but even then the decorations are materials that could return to the bowl’s cycles without leaving waste.

Their technology is intimate rather than imposing. They do not force the bowl into shape. They negotiate with it. Stone adzes, bone awls, wooden mallets, fire-hardened stakes, twisted cordage, clay lamps, pitch-lined vessels, reed baskets, hardwood levers, and simple pulleys serve them. Metal is rare: a traded hook, a needle, a small blade, kept oiled and wrapped, passed down through households. A metal edge is not a symbol of power so much as a memory of outside contact, a thing that came up through mist and steep paths and was valued because it could not be made locally. Most work is done by knowing how to bend, tie, prune, feed, thin, and repair.

Their houses are not built and then left. They are raised and then tended. A Bowl-folk house is a living wall, a roof of leaves, a floor of root and plank, a gutter of hollow wood, a cistern of clay and stone, a nursery of bundled stems. The people live inside organisms. Their walls thicken. Their roofs shed leaf-shingles and grow new ones. Their staircases become smoother with use. Their bridges sway. Their homes breathe, creak, drink, and stretch. A family does not merely occupy a house; it maintains a relationship with it. A neglected house does not simply fall into ruin. It grows wild, loses its shape, becomes too leafy, too root-bound, too open to rain, too soft underfoot. A good house shows care. A great house shows generations.

Their society is organized around maintenance. There are wards, lanes, terraces, roosts, basins, and bridges, but the deepest divisions are duties. There are water-tenders who watch the channels and know the sound of a rising flow. Roof-thatchers who climb after storms to replace leaf-shingles and clear moss combs. Fog-combers who keep the upper fringes of the settlement trimmed so mist will catch and drip into cisterns. Root-wardens who understand which roots may be cut, which must be fed, and which have begun to lift a walkway. Bird-keepers who know nesting seasons, egg limits, guano shelves, and the tempers of different species. Frog-herders, though that word is too grand for the careful work of encouraging frogs in gutters, pools, and leaf-tanks. Compost-turners who manage the dark wealth of decay: bird dung, fish scraps, spoiled vegetation, shed feathers, food waste, human waste, everything returned to the sponge ground in measured ways. Bridge-lashers who know cordage tension and the slow strength of living wood. Weavers, potters, fish-trap makers, netters, herb-dryers, snail-tenders, fungal gardeners.

No one rules the bowl by simple command. It would not answer to that. Authority is distributed like water. The eldest water-tender may have great respect, but a young bridge-lasher can overrule her if a crossing is unsafe. A bird-keeper can halt work in a ward during nesting season. A compost-turner can shame a household for wasting nutrients. Decisions are made in small councils on walkways, at basin edges, under eaves, by the overflow pool where the bowl spills into mist. Arguments can be loud because the bowl itself is loud. Voices carry over frog chorus, drip, and bird-call. Whistles cut across gaps. Hands move in signs when rain is too heavy for speech. Consensus is often reached not because everyone agrees, but because the bowl makes consequences immediate. If a sluice is badly managed, the lower ward floods. If a roof is poorly pruned, a house leaks. If guano is harvested too greedily, the plants weaken. The place teaches by result.

Their daily life begins before dawn in the cold blue-gray of mist. Breath shows. Water sounds everywhere. The first workers rise to check channels, lift fish covers, collect eggs, empty night jars, feed fires from dry wood stored under bark roofs. Fire is precious and carefully housed. The bowl is wet, but not fireless; dry fuel is kept in raised granaries, and hearths are lined with stone, clay, and wet moss to prevent escape. Cooking smells rise with mist: steamed fern shoots, roasted snails, fish broth, bird eggs, mashed tubers, algae cakes, fungi from tended logs, bitter leaf teas. They eat hot food when they can, because wet cold enters the joints. They also eat many things cold or preserved: dried larvae, smoked fish, fermented bromeliad hearts, pickled shoots, moss cakes, dried berries from outer slopes, nuts traded from below.

They are farmers of dampness. Their fields are not open grain plots but layered gardens: moss beds, sedge banks, bromeliad shelves, hanging leaf-tanks, shaded fungal logs, duckweed pools, root terraces, snail walls. They encourage the plants that hold water and discourage those that choke channels. They harvest without killing. They cut where regrowth is desired. They bend where strength is needed. They feed roots with compost teas and fish water. They thin shoots before caterpillars strip them. They know which vines will become railings, which leaves will become thatch, which bark can be stripped without harming the tree, which fallen trunk should be left as a bridge and which should be carved into a trough. Their agriculture is closer to gardening a living body than plowing a field.

They also manage animals as neighbors, workers, food, and nuisance. Birds are everywhere. Some are kept in woven towers for eggs and feathers. Some are tolerated because they eat caterpillars. Some are driven away from tender shoots with rattles and nets. Their droppings are wealth. Roost shelves are built so guano can be scraped into baskets and composted. Molted feathers are collected for fletching, pens, decoration, and insulation. Egg harvesting is done with care; a household that takes too many eggs earns blame, because birds are not livestock alone but pest control and sound and life. Children learn to climb to nests without frightening the parents. They learn which eggs may be taken, which must remain, and how to apologize to a scolding bird with a handful of grubs.

Frogs and toads are protected in many places because they eat insects. Tree frogs live in roof gutters and leaf-cups. Toads shelter under root steps and around compost pits. During mating season, their chorus can become so loud that the Bowl-folk speak mostly by gesture or shout directly into one another’s ears. The children imitate frog calls. The old people say you can tell the weather by whether the frogs sound eager or annoyed. Salamanders and newts are left in cool corners; their presence means the water is healthy. In cisterns, small fish and loaches eat larvae and scraps. Fish are fed, counted, and sometimes harvested. Their silver-brown flash when a cover is lifted is ordinary to the Bowl-folk, a sign that the tank is doing its work.

Insects are the bowl’s constant pressure and protein. They are not all enemies. Some larvae are harvested in season. Beetles are allowed to work certain rotting logs where their tunnels help composting. Ants highways are protected under walkways because ants clean carcasses and carry waste. Caterpillar waves are both danger and opportunity: they can strip tender growth, but they also feed birds and can be collected when abundant. Mosquitoes are managed by fish, frogs, newts, and careful drainage; still, every damp corner has them, and every sleeping platform has some kind of net, screen, or smoked bundle. Flies, midges, gnats, and leafhoppers are part of the air itself. The Bowl-folk do not expect a world without insects. They expect to be smarter than insects, and occasionally to lose.

Small mammals test every boundary. Rodents try to enter granaries and must be thwarted with tight lids, raised posts, and weasel-hunted beams. Bats are welcomed because they eat night insects, though their droppings must also be managed. Weasel-like hunters live in the upper structure, half-wild, tolerated for their work against rats. Mountain goats or small deer are kept on outer terraces where their hooves cannot ruin the sponge floor. Their yards are plank-lined and root-fenced. Their manure goes to compost. Their milk, if they give it, is used sparingly. Their hair is twisted into cord. Their bones become tools. Larger predators rarely enter the bowl because it is small, steep, and crowded with noise, but hawks pass over the rim, and sometimes a careless bird, child, or young goat is taken. This is not tragedy so much as fact. The Bowl-folk watch the sky. They teach children to freeze when the shadow comes.
