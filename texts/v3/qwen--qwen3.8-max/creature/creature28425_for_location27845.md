What lives in the High Cisterns of the First Build is not easy to answer in the singular, because the place does not offer the usual signs of habitation. There are no footpaths worn into the ground, no fires, no refuse, no tools abandoned beside a doorway. There are no beds, no hearths, no marks of hands. At first, the mind says: *no one lives here.* Then, after a little while, the mind begins to correct itself. The place is not empty. It is not even uninhabited. It is only uninhabited in the way a body is uninhabited when there is no separate soul standing apart from it, looking out.

The first answer, the simplest answer, is that the High Cisterns live in themselves.

The living floor lives. It is the broadest resident, the most continuous presence, the citizen spread thin over the whole basin. It is not ground in the old sense, not dirt, not mineral crust, but a low horizontal being made of compressed felts of leaf, interwoven root, sealed membranes, and spongy tissue that answers pressure with moisture. To step there is not to cross a surface but to be noticed by something patient. The floor yields faintly. Water rises near the place where weight is given. Somewhere a little distance away, a pore opens, a capillary thread tightens, a bead of warm water gathers and is passed inward. The floor does not walk. It does not see. But it is aware in the manner of living tissue: by gradient, by tension, by wetness, by the minute redistribution of itself. It keeps the world from becoming dry. It keeps the basin from becoming waste. It is the first and last inhabitant, the common body under all the others.

The cistern-forms live too. They stand spaced across the basin like solitary public buildings, each one a swelling of stored water, each one a thickened patience. Some are low and broad, their trunks compressed into reservoirs. Others rise as pale towers with segmented sides and open crowns. Others still are almost architectural in their stillness: hollow-stemmed, arched, ribbed, cupped. They are not individuals in the way trees elsewhere are individuals. They are more like organs of one vast distributed creature, though each holds its own water, its own tone, its own slow pulse. Their life is holding. Their life is receiving mist from the heights, catching dew from leaf roofs, drawing fine threads of water upward through translucent veins, and keeping all of it in warm, green-lit chambers where light passes through the walls as through old glass.

They do not hunger as animals hunger. They thirst structurally. Their desire is shape. When the air is humid, their surfaces brighten. When droplets gather along their rims, their crowns become shallow bowls of trembling weight. When water enters their internal channels, they make sound: a low filling, a faint glug, a resonant rise. They live by being filled without overflowing. They are the elders of the basin, though none of them are old in the sense of decay. They are elders because they hold. They are elders because they make permanence out of arrival.

The hollow stems live. They are the singers of the place. Tall, reed-like, open at the top, perforated along their sides, they stand in sparse clusters and translate warmth into breath. Air rises through them in slow columns. Water climbs inside others in pulses too measured to seem accidental. Some whistle faintly when heat increases. Some hum with a membrane stretched across a joint. Some are dark and slick within, their inner surfaces shining with moisture; others are pale enough that daylight passes through them as a greenish glow, making them seem less like plants than like lanterns lit by the sky. They are not towers for watching. They are towers for drinking height. They live by being empty enough to let the world pass through them. Their emptiness is not absence. It is capacity.

The weeping rocks live, if one is willing to stretch the word *rock* until it becomes almost false. They are not stone. They are dense masses of lignified sap, compressed pollen, hardened root tissue, and growth pressure shaped into boulder-like forms. They sweat continuously. Their flanks glisten. Water comes from pores too fine to see and travels down their sides in slow threads. Around their bases, collars of absorbent moss take the moisture and pass it inward. These forms are among the slowest residents of the High Cisterns. Their life is exudation. Their life is making wetness appear from apparent stillness. They seem heavy, but their heaviness is not dead weight. It is accumulated living pressure. They do not rest. They seep. They give off dampness as if their inner substance were too full to remain entirely contained. They are the patient masons of humidity.

The mosses live. The root-laces live. The fine green felts that cover every cooled underside live. They are the maintenance choirs, the small congregations, the countless workers without faces. They catch mist. They bind edges. They drink overflow. They close gaps. When a fragment loosens somewhere in the basin, it is not allowed to become debris. These smaller lives receive it, fold around it, reweave it into the living mass. They do not build by adding foreign material. They build by continuing. Their labor is so quiet that it resembles stillness. Yet the whole crispness of the place depends on them. Because of them, there are no gullies, no dust, no slumping, no ruin. The High Cisterns remain newborn because these tiny tissues refuse to let anything be lost.

The translucent creatures in the clear pools live. They are barely more than moving clarities, little drifts of life in the cisterns, feeding on films too fine for ordinary sight. They have no obvious eyes, no limbs, no urgency. They move like seeds that have learned to swim without effort. Sometimes they rise in a slow spiral; sometimes they hang suspended as if considering nothing. They do not disturb the water. They belong to its stillness. In them, the place has a kind of thought without mind: a faint animation inside the stored light. They are among the rare animals, and their rarity makes them more solemn. They do not belong to an economy of prey and predator. They are accents, small permitted guests in a vegetal grammar.

The pale snails live. They cross the wet spillways with a slowness that suits the basin. Their shells are thin whorls of secreted plant-lime, almost translucent, as if they had been made from the same condensed water as the cisterns. They leave no wound behind them. Their trails become shining seams, briefly visible before the surface reabsorbs them. They eat only what has already loosened, only films and residues that would otherwise gather. They are not numerous. One may watch a spillway for a long time and see none. Then, when one appears, it feels less like an animal passing than like a thought the basin has allowed itself to have. It moves as if it knows that hurry would be a kind of blasphemy here.

The wingless pollinators live, though they appear so rarely that they might almost be remembered into being. They pass between swollen buds that never quite open, moving over the living architecture with a soft, ceremonial certainty. They have no wings, no bright display, no buzzing importance. Their bodies are pale, low, jointed, made for close contact with closed petals and resinous seams. They carry out an exchange that feels less like feeding than like maintenance. They touch one bud, then another. Something is transferred. Something is confirmed. Then they are gone, and the buds remain swollen at the threshold of opening, exactly as before. Even this rare animal life does not change the pattern. It only testifies to it.

There are also lives too fine to name comfortably. Films on the inner surfaces of pools. Membranes that breathe underfoot. Threads of tissue in the capillary grooves. Veil-thin growths on the undersides of roofs. These are not individuals, but they are alive. They are the basin’s most intimate workers, the ones that turn vapor into substance, residue into tissue, film into structure. They live at the edge between water and form. They make the boundary between liquid and body feel less like a wall and more like a negotiation. Without them, the High Cisterns would dry into mere shapes. With them, every surface remains on the point of becoming.

But if one asks who lives there in the larger sense, the answer must become stranger.

The First Build itself lives.

Not as a person lives. Not as a beast lives. Not as a god lives, if gods are imagined as beings with faces and wills. The First Build lives as a pattern lives when it has become thick enough to hold water, sound, and continuity. It is the whole basin considered as one body. It has rooms instead of organs. It has channels instead of veins. It has resonance instead of speech. It has thresholds instead of intentions. Its life is not concentrated in a head or a heart. It is distributed across cisterns, causeways, root shelves, hollow towers, breathing floors, and weeping masses of sap-stone. It is alive in the way a vast instrument is alive when every part of it has been tuned to the same sustained chord.

It has no face, yet it has expression. Its expression is the loud, patient sound that fills the open spaces. The dripping is not random. The plinking is not empty. The glugging, humming, creaking, and soft drum-beating are not signs of confusion. They are the voice of the First Build maintaining itself. The place speaks because its shapes have reached equilibrium with water and heat. Every falling drop has a surface to receive it. Every channel has a tone. Every hollow stem has a breath. Every pool has a note. The sound is not made for ears. It is made by the fact that the basin exists. Yet to hear it is to understand that the emptiness is inhabited. The loudness lives there. It resides in the intervals between the scattered forms. It is one of the deepest residents of the High Cisterns.

The water lives too, though not in the same way as tissue lives. Water here is not merely substance. It is principal mason, chief architect, circulating will. It decides where thickening occurs. It teaches forms where to swell. It makes channels by insistence. It makes sound by falling. It makes stillness by pooling. It makes growth possible by remaining near the surface. It is warm, intimate, everywhere near the skin of things. It beads on membranes. It gathers on leaf teeth. It runs down the flanks of weeping forms. It rises by tension, descends by spillways, rests in cups, trembles in shallow troughs. If the First Build is a body, water is its animating breath. If the basin is a city, water is its law. If there is any ruler in the High Cisterns, it is water without throne, water without crown, water moving through living plumbing and making every form obey the gentle severity of its nature.

What lives there, then, is a civilization of functions rather than a population of beings. There are citizens of holding, citizens of lifting, citizens of catching mist, citizens of sealing joints, citizens of resonance, citizens of overflow. Some live as roofs. Some live as stairs. Some live as valves. Some live as cups. Some live as bridges. Some live as membranes that breathe when touched. Some live as pollen-stone walls that exist only to make condensation bead in a certain pattern. Some live as thresholds. Some live as pauses. The emptiness itself is a kind of life, because it is maintained. The open spaces are not neglected. They are stretched, kept moist, kept smooth, kept as constructed silence between presences. They are rooms without furniture, courts without crowds, plazas without centers, and yet they are not dead. They are the held breath of the basin.

There is no ordinary society there because there is no need. No one must eat another in order to continue. No one must flee. No one must hoard. The economy is circulation. Nutrients come from condensation, air, shed tissue, and the slow internal movement of the living floor. Waste is not cast out; it is reabsorbed. Death, if it happens at all, is not a fall into decay but a folding back into construction. A fragment loosens, and the basin receives it. A membrane thins, and new tissue rises beneath it. A root tip brightens, and the same design continues. Change occurs, but it does not accumulate into history. The pattern remains. The basin does not develop from infancy to maturity to ruin. It remains forever at the first moment of formation, pale tips extending, buds swelling, droplets gathering weight, surfaces just becoming visible to themselves.

This is why the place feels both inhabited and unborn. What lives there lives as beginning lives. Not as a child lives, moving toward adulthood, but as the first instant of rain lives when it is asked to remain forever. The High Cisterns are filled with the energy of arrival, yet arrival never passes into completion. Everything seems about to become something else, and yet nothing betrays its own shape. The cisterns seem about to fill beyond their rims, but they do not. The buds seem about to open, but they remain closed. The root tips seem about to reach a new edge, but the edge is already part of the pattern. The towers seem about to sound a new note, but the note has already been sounding for as long as the basin has existed. The inhabitants, if they can be called that, live inside this perpetual pre-formation. They are creatures of the first hour made permanent.

If one insists on imagining a single being there, it might be this: a vast, warm, wet, vegetal body with no center, no face, and no desire except continuation. It would not look at you. It would not speak to you. It would not need you. It would feel your footfall as a pressure, answer it with a hydraulic sigh, and then continue its loud, patient work. Your breath would add a little moisture to its air. Your warmth would be no stranger to its warmth. Your listening would become one more small witness to its resonance, but the basin would not be completed by you. It would remain itself: total, sparse, made, alive.

There are doorways in the High Cisterns, but they open onto more living floor. There are stairways, but they lead to water. There are colonnades, but no assemblies gather beneath them. There are causeways, but no processions cross them. There are rings of fused trunks, but no rituals are performed within them except the ritual of water returning to water. The mind searches for people because the forms resemble civic life, but the resemblance is not evidence of persons. It is evidence that living construction can arrive at the shapes of gathering without gatherers, of ceremony without priests, of architecture without architects. The place is built for itself. It is inhabited by its own being.

So what lives in the High Cisterns of the First Build?

The floor lives. The cisterns live. The towers live. The weeping sap-boulders live. The mosses live. The root-laces live. The films and membranes live. The rare translucent drifters live. The pale snails live. The wingless pollinators live. The sound lives. The water lives. The pattern lives. The first hour lives. The basin lives as one distributed body, green and warm and loud, holding itself at the edge of beginning without ever falling into completion.

And beneath all these lives, quieter than all of them, there is another answer: the High Cisterns are lived by their own becoming. They are not a place where something lives so much as a place that lives by being a place. Every drop is a citizen. Every hum is a resident. Every pale root tip is a presence. Every pool is a held thought. Every empty space is a breath not yet released. Nothing there is merely background. Nothing is merely empty. Even silence, when it seems near, is only the loudness resting inside itself.

The place is inhabited by the fact that it continues.
