What lives there is not numerous, not loud, and not eager to be seen. The first life of the basin is water; the second is plant and fungus; the third is a sparse people who have made themselves almost into weather.

They are the only warm moving things the place permits, and even that seems granted conditionally. They do not swarm, do not herd, do not hunt, do not ride, do not drive beasts before them. They come and go along the causeways with such restraint that a watcher might mistake them for part of the basin’s slow circulation: a figure bending at a sluice, a shadow passing along a terrace edge, a pair of hands adjusting reed matting against seepage. If they are human, they have been changed by the wetness into something quieter than humanity usually is elsewhere. They live not upon the city, but within its water-right logic.

They are few. Not so few that the basin feels abandoned, but few enough that emptiness remains the rule. A household may hold a terrace for generations without filling it. A plaza may stand wet and clean for days with no one crossing it. The population is scattered like seeds in a marsh: present, viable, but widely spaced. The city does not need crowds to be maintained. It needs attention, and attention here is a slow art.

Their bodies have taken the shape of the place. They are human-sized, but not heavy. Most are slender, though their hands, forearms, shoulders, and legs carry the dense strength of people who work constantly with rope, stone, wet wood, and their own balance. They are not the thick-limbed strength of plowmen or horse-riders, because there are no plows and no horses. Their strength is patient: the strength of pulling a loaded barge by hand, of lowering stone with ropes, of kneeling at a channel edge and working for hours with a wooden spade. Their feet are broad and sure, often bare or wrapped in woven sedge sandals, the soles toughened but soft enough to feel the slickness of stone. Their toes spread for grip. They almost never slip. Even children learn to walk on wet ledges without splashing.

Their skin is rarely fully dry. It takes on the look of the basin: smooth, faintly glossy, sometimes stained at the fingertips and palms by green algae, black peat, or rust-colored mineral water. They do not smell of sweat, dung, smoke, or animals. They smell of wet leaves, moss, clay, warm stone, and faint salt. In cooler light their skin may look pale; in the silver glow of standing water it may seem green-grey or warm brown depending on lineage and work. Their hair is usually dark and kept practical: cropped, braided close, bound under woven caps, or oiled with pressed seeds to shed water. They do not decorate themselves with feathers, shells, bone, or hide. Such things would feel alien there, perhaps even obscene.

Their clothing is made entirely from plants and, in some cases, cultivated fungus. They wear layered garments suited to damp warmth: close underlayers of soft bast fiber, sleeveless vests of woven reed, long shoulder capes that shed condensation, and wide sashes used to carry tools or small baskets. Some garments are pale and undyed; others take the colors of the basin itself: moss green, peat brown, mineral rust, blackened leaf. In the wettest districts they wear cloaks of layered bark or tightly woven mats with stone weights at the hem so the fabric does not lift in the faint airs that cross the marsh. Their fasteners are wooden pins, knots, copper clasps, or carved toggles of polished basalt. Nothing about them is borrowed from an animal. They have no wool, no leather, no horn, no bone buttons, no sinew thread. If a stranger arrived wearing hide, the basin people would likely stare not with anger but with unease, as if the stranger carried a dead noise on their back.

They move differently from people in drier lands. They do not hurry unless water demands it. Their steps are placed, not dropped. They know which stones are stable, which are treacherous when wet, which edges have begun to weep more than usual. They walk with their weight low, their knees loose, their hands often free. When they carry burdens, they balance them carefully or use sledges, yokes, and backpacks rather than carts. On water they use flat-bottomed barges and reed bundles, poling quietly through channels with long wooden staffs. They never beat the water. They enter it, cross it, and leave it with as little disturbance as possible.

Their senses have sharpened toward moisture. They hear water better than they hear each other. A spring opening slightly, a cistern filling too fast, a blocked runnel changing the pitch of its trickle — these are events they notice immediately. They can tell the level of a hidden channel by the sound under stone. They touch walls and read damp the way others read faces. An elder may place a palm against a retaining wall and say, after a long pause, that the third weir needs to be opened a finger’s width. They are often right. Their knowledge is not mystical. It is accumulated attention.

They speak softly. Voices carry strangely across the basin, and loud speech feels intrusive, almost violent. Their language is rich in terms for water and wetness: bead, film, seep, sheet, weep, gloss, breath, drip, pool, runnel, still-head, overflow, dark-saturation, green-shallow, mineral-sweat, clay-slick. They have fewer words for ownership and many for tending. They have several verbs meaning “to let pass,” each with a slightly different sense: to let water pass, to let a task pass to another, to let anger pass, to let the dead pass into ground. They also use a developed sign language of hands, especially when working near channels where speech might distract or where sound would travel too far. A hand opening slowly at the wrist means ease a gate. Fingers rippling over a palm means seepage. A closed fist laid gently on a wall means stop, listen. Children learn these signs before they are trusted near open water.

Their names are usually short and soft, often drawn from places or conditions rather than animals. A person might be named after the sound of a particular cistern, the color of a terrace in low light, a weir repaired in the year of their birth, or the way rain beads on a certain stone. Names may change over life. A child called Shallow Reed might later become Moss-at-the-Sluice, then Grey Channel, then simply the name of the place they tend longest. The basin people do not like to speak their own names loudly. They introduce themselves with a gesture and a quiet phrase, as if naming should not disturb the air more than necessary.

They live in small households, but households here are not tightly packed compounds. A house may stand alone beside a cistern, or three houses may share a platform separated from the next by a broad sheet of standing water. Their dwellings are low, open enough to breathe, raised on stone or timber where the ground is soft. Roofs are layered reed, bark, and woven mats, weighted smooth with dark stones. Floors drain. Walls have weep holes. Doorways are curtained with hanging reeds rather than sealed with heavy doors, though some inner rooms can be closed against damp with fitted wooden panels. Every house accepts water in some controlled way. A home that tried to be perfectly dry would be considered foolish, perhaps even dangerous, because it would push water elsewhere. Their architecture is not a defiance of wetness but a negotiation with it.

Inside, their rooms are spare and orderly. Sleeping platforms are raised and covered with thick woven mats, dried reed layers, and soft cushions filled with moss or seed down. Storage jars of fired clay hold dried tubers, seeds, algae cakes, fiber, and tools. Low tables and benches are made from split wood or flat stone. There are no beds of wool, no rugs of hide, no animal-fat lamps. Light comes from open sky, from reflected water, from small clay lamps burning plant oil where needed. Some homes have tiny braziers of clay filled with charcoal made from reed or wood, but fires are managed carefully because smoke and damp do not mix easily. Much of their cooking is done in communal steam pits, hot springs, or shared stone troughs where water can be heated without open flame.

Their work is mostly maintenance, and they do not consider maintenance lesser labor. The basin survives because they keep it open. They clear silt from channels, prune reeds where they thicken too much, scrape algal mats from drinking cisterns, oil wooden sluice gates with plant resin, replace rotting ropes, refit stones loosened by seepage, weave new mats for roofs, and listen to springs for changes in flow. Some are specialists: sluice-tenders, spring-listeners, cistern-keepers, reed-cutters, moss-farmers, rope-makers, stone-fitters, barge-pullers, mat-menders, and those who watch the cliff faces for fresh cracks. These roles are not castes. A person may move between them over a lifetime. A child who loves the sound of water may become a spring-listener; a person with strong hands and patient shoulders may become a stone-fitter.

Their tools are simple but finely understood. They use polished basalt hammers, wooden spades shod with stone or soft metal, levers, wedges, ropes, baskets, clay trowels, copper pins, bronze fittings where available, and cords marked for measuring depth. They have no great machines. They rely on gravity, water pressure, counterweights, ramps, and many hands working together. When a stone must be moved, they do not imagine conquering it; they imagine persuading it downhill. When water must be redirected, they do not think of stopping it, only of giving it a better path. Their engineering is quiet and cumulative. A hundred small corrections each generation keeps the basin alive.

They eat what the wetness can give without animals. Their gardens are marshes, ponds, terraces, and shaded fungal beds. They cultivate tubers in black mud, reed shoots in shallow water, seed-bearing grasses on saturated margins, edible algae in warm green pools, and mosses that thicken into soft cakes after long cooking. In shaded undercrofts they grow pale fungi on composted reed stems. They harvest sparingly and rotate beds so that no place is stripped bare. Their food is mostly steamed, boiled, baked in clay shells, dried into flat cakes, or fermented in sealed jars. They make warm broths from tubers and mineral salts, pressed algae sheets, roasted seed cakes, and soft porridges of moss or fungal meal. They have no meat, no fish, no eggs, no milk, no honey. If they need fat, they press seeds or nuts from water-loving trees. If they need seasoning, they use mineral salts, bitter leaves, fermented reed sap, or clay-rich earths carefully prepared. Their meals are quiet, often eaten warm, often shared, but never festive in a loud sense. Celebration here is closer to satisfaction: a channel running clear, a storehouse dry enough, a child learning to tie a proper knot.

They are very clean, but cleanliness in the basin does not mean dryness. They bathe often in designated pools, using soft clays and plant ashes to cleanse skin and hair. They wash clothing and mats in graywater channels that lead through reed beds so waste is filtered before returning to lower marshes. They are careful not to foul the cisterns or springs. Their laws about water purity are strict. To spoil clean water is one of the gravest wrongs they can imagine. They separate drinking water, washing water, work water, and water for the dead. A visitor who unknowingly stepped into a spring-fed pool might be stopped not with shouting but with a quick, firm hand and a serious face.

Their society is organized around responsibility rather than ownership. They do not say, “This water is mine.” They say, “This water passes through my keeping.” Families may hold the right and duty to tend a particular terrace, weir, or cistern, but that is a burden more than a possession. When disputes arise — and they do, because even quiet people can disagree — they are usually settled by measurement and observation. Which gate caused the overflow? Which path was blocked? Which repair was delayed? Elders who remember previous flows are consulted, but memory is tested against present evidence. They mark water levels with notched stones and keep records in knots, carved clay tablets, or patterns of beads strung on plant cord. Their archives are not books but infrastructure: a repaired wall, a widened channel, a cistern lid replaced after a certain event.

They have no kings, no generals, no priests who stand above water. If they have leaders, they are those who can listen longest and decide last. A cistern-clerk, a weir-mother, a sluice-father, a terrace-steward — such people are respected because they know the basin’s moods. Authority is shown by work, not ornament. A leader might be the person who first notices a hairline crack in a new scarp or who remembers how an old channel was redirected three generations ago. When urgent decisions are needed, they gather in small numbers on a dry platform or causeway and speak in low voices. They do not orate. They describe. They propose. They listen. They test. Then they act.

Their law is simple in principle and demanding in practice: water has right of way. Every structure must drain. Every wall must breathe. Every floor must accept the possibility of damp. Every new path must not block a hidden seep. Every roof must give water somewhere to go. Every field must be harvestable without killing the marsh. Every child must learn not to throw stones into still pools for mere amusement. To live in the basin is to accept that one’s desires are secondary to flow. This does not make them joyless. It makes them careful. Their joy is shaped by care.

Children are few, loved, and taught early. They are not kept in closed rooms for long; the basin is their school. They learn to walk without splashing, to carry a cup without spilling, to hear the difference between a full channel and a blocked one. They play with floating tokens, miniature canals, woven boats, and clay models of sluice gates. They make little dams and then learn why the dams fail. They are taught to watch leaves drift and to ask where they gather. They are taught to touch moss without tearing it. They are taught that silence is not emptiness but attention. A child who speaks too loudly is not beaten; they are taken to a still pool and asked to listen until they can hear their own breathing settle. They are given responsibilities young: mending mats, carrying dry reeds, watching a slow drip, remembering which stones are loose. By the time they are grown, they carry the basin in their bodies.

They have little use for speed. Their games, if they can be called games, involve balance, patience, accuracy, and subtlety. A group of young people might compete to cross a wet causeway without making a sound, or to adjust a sluice gate so that the overflow changes pitch without rising visibly, or to weave a mat tight enough to hold water for a set time yet still let it breathe when needed. They do not cheer. They smile with their eyes. They may laugh softly, a sound like water moving through reeds. Their humor is dry in the only way a wet place allows: understated, delayed, often based on human pretension. A person who tries to keep their feet dry while doing impossible work becomes a gentle story for years.

Their art is inseparable from maintenance. Beauty is not added to the city; it is discovered in rightness. A well-cut weir has beauty. A stone fitted so precisely that water beads along its seam but does not erode it has beauty. A reed mat woven in a pattern that strengthens its edge has beauty. A causeway whose curve slows floodwater without appearing to resist it has beauty. They do make decorative things, but sparingly: carved channel-maps, polished basalt markers, pins of soft metal shaped like ripples, cloaks woven with subtle green and grey patterns, clay vessels impressed with leaves. They do not carve animals, because animals are absent from their world. Their motifs are water veins, root lines, reed bundles, terrace steps, cracks in young stone, and the spiral paths of overflow. To them, these are the living forms.

Music exists, but it is hushed. They make instruments from the basin itself: hollow reeds that sigh when air passes through them, stone bowls that drip in tuned rhythms, stretched plant membranes that hum faintly when touched, wooden boards under sluice pressure that creak in predictable notes. They do not perform often, and never loudly. Their music sounds like the basin thinking. Sometimes a person alone at a cistern will tap a wooden staff against stone in a slow pattern, not to summon anyone, but to mark the hour or steady their own heart. Singing is rare. If someone sings, it is low, wordless, and brief. They prefer the sound water already makes.

Their relationship to the dead is shaped by the absence of animal decay. They do not leave bodies to scavengers, because there are none. They do not bury in dry ground, because dry ground is scarce and the dead belong to the basin. Instead, they wrap the dead in woven mats of reed and bast, often with personal tools laid nearby, and lower them into chosen pools, peat beds, or mineral springs. In some districts the dead are sunk into dark marshes where plant matter accumulates slowly; in others they are laid in shallow stone troughs where mineral water darkens and preserves the outline of the body while softening it over time. The basin does not consume its dead quickly. It folds them in. After many years, what was a person becomes part of the ground that holds water. Their bones, if they remain, are not displayed. They become hidden, mineralized, quiet. The living mark them not with tall monuments but with repaired infrastructure: a sluice renamed, a cistern rededicated, a stair channel smoothed in their memory. To maintain something well is to remember someone well.

Because of this, their city is full of invisible ancestors. Not as ghosts, but as habits. A certain turn in a path may be called by an old keeper’s name. A wall may be spoken of as “the one your mother fitted.” A spring may be remembered because a child first spoke there. The past is not distant; it is in use. They do not long for a golden age. They understand that the basin is young, the stone raw, the channels still finding their best forms. Their reverence is practical. They honor what works and what endures.

They are not without feeling. They love in quiet ways: mending a roof before someone asks, leaving a dry bundle of reeds by a door, saving a portion of warm food, walking with a friend along a causeway without needing to speak. Their grief is slow and deep, like water rising in a closed cistern. They may become stiller after loss, not colder. They do not expect sorrow to vanish. They expect it to be carried carefully. Anger exists too, but it is most often aroused by waste, cruelty to plants, fouled water, broken trust, or thoughtless noise. When they punish, they prefer repair over imprisonment. A person who has harmed the basin may be assigned to tend a difficult stretch of channel for a year, or to rebuild a damaged terrace by hand. Exile is the severest penalty, because outside the basin there is dust, dryness, animal noise, and a world that does not listen.

They are wary of outsiders, but not hostile by nature. If strangers come down from the ridges, the basin people observe them first. They notice whether the strangers walk heavily, whether they carry animal skins, whether they speak too loudly, whether they look at water as something to be seized or feared. If the strangers are peaceful, the basin people may meet them at a causeway edge and offer clean water in a clay cup. They are generous with water, because water is their law. But they are slow to invite outsiders inward. They do not like dogs, pack animals, or beasts of burden; such creatures would feel like living noise. They may trade at the margins: plant fiber, woven goods, polished stone, mineral salts, dried food, medicinal mosses, perhaps fine clay vessels. In return they might accept copper, bronze, salt from distant flats, or tools of outside make, but they prefer goods that can be cleaned and used without animal traces. They do not wish to conquer the world beyond the rim. They wish to keep their basin in balance.

Their minds are shaped by steadiness. Because the basin has no harsh seasons, they do not think in dramatic cycles of feast and famine. Their time is measured in maintenance: reed-cutting, algae-skimming, sluice-oiling, rope-renewal, cistern-cleaning, stone-checking. They have a long patience for tasks that barely show. They do not ask, “What will this change tomorrow?” They ask, “What will this keep?” This makes them conservative in the deepest sense, not politically, but ecologically. They distrust innovation that increases noise or blocks flow. They accept small improvements if the water agrees. Their conservatism is not stupidity. It is survival in a place where one careless blockage can spread damp into walls, rot into roofs, and silence into resentment.

They have myths, but they are not like the myths of hunters or herders. They tell of the First Opening, when water found its way into the basin and did not leave. They tell of the People Who Tried to Dry the Stone and Lost Their Feet. They tell of the Child Who Listened to a Spring and Learned Where to Cut the First Channel. They tell of the Reed That Bent and Did Not Break. They tell of the Stone That Wept Until Someone Made It a Path. Their stories rarely have villains. They have obstacles: blockage, haste, pride, forgetting. Their heroes are those who clear, who listen, who make room. In one story, a person saves the city not by building a wall, but by opening a gate. In another, the greatest gift is a bare footprint that finds the loose stone before anyone falls. In another, a woman teaches the people to leave empty spaces so water can rest. These stories are told softly, often while working.

Their emotional atmosphere matches the light: ordinary, steady, not mystical. They do not believe themselves chosen. They do not imagine the basin as a paradise. It is wet, warm, demanding, and quiet. It requires constant care. But it also gives them a world without predation. Nothing there hunts. Nothing flees. Nothing screams in the night. This has shaped their souls. They are not innocent; they know conflict, loss, and error. But they do not know the constant tension of predator and prey. Their stillness is not merely environmental. It is moral. They have learned to live without making other creatures suffer. That knowledge gives them a peculiar dignity.

They are not all alike. Some are more solitary, tending high cliff seeps where few others go. Some are communal, living near the central wetlands where barges cross and tasks are shared. Some love the reed beds and spend their days waist-deep in green shallows. Some love stone and climb fresh escarpments to watch for cracks. Some are young and curious about the rim; some are old and distrust any change in water level. There are those who laugh easily in low voices, and those who have become as silent as the pools they tend. But all of them share a common discipline: they do not take more than the basin can absorb.

If you were to see one of them, you might not notice them at first. They would likely be crouched beside a channel, a reed cloak dark with damp around their shoulders, one hand resting on a wooden sluice lever. Their face would be calm, not blank. Their eyes would move slowly across the water, reading small changes. When they stood, they would rise without hurry. Their feet would make almost no sound on the wet stone. They might glance at you, not with alarm, but with assessment: Are you heavy? Are you thirsty? Will you muddy this place? If you were quiet, they might nod. If you were careless, they might turn away and begin repairing whatever you had disturbed. If you asked them who they were, they might answer with the name of the channel they keep. If you asked them what they want, they might say something as simple and enormous as: for the water to pass.

They are the basin’s living attention. They are the hands that keep the ways open, the voices that do not need to rise, the feet that have learned not to bruise the wet ground. They live in the gaps left by stone and water, and they leave gaps in return. Their presence is not a conquest of the landscape but a continuation of it. In a place where water is everywhere but never violent, they have become like water: unhurried, persistent, shaping without force, present without noise.

They are not many, but they are enough. They tend the cisterns, the weirs, the terraces, the reed beds, the causeways, and the silence. They eat what grows in wet ground. They wear what grows in wet ground. They build with what the young stone gives. They remember the dead by repairing the living city. They leave room for water, for plants, for emptiness, for time.

And so, in that high, warm, newly formed basin-city, what lives is not a crowd, not a kingdom, not a machine, but a quiet people who have learned the hardest lesson a living thing can learn in such a place: how to remain without disturbing.
