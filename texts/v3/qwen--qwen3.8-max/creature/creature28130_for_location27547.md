What lives there does not merely occupy the place. It answers it.

Every living thing in that marsh is a listener first and a body second. Life has been shaped around the fact that the world arrives as pressure, heat, wetness, texture, smell, and above all sound. Nothing there turns its face toward light, because there is no light to face. Instead, creatures face one another, face the engines, face the water, face the long slow breathing of the land itself. To live there is to be constantly interpreted by what surrounds you, and to interpret in return.

The smallest inhabitants are not the least present. In the peat and warm mud there are thread-thin worms that move by thermal gradient, rising through saturated fibers when a vent shifts and sinking when the stone cools. They are eaten by grubbing beetles with blunt, heat-sensitive heads, and those grubs are eaten in turn by soft-mouthed amphibians that spend their lives half-buried in moss. Rust-grazers scrape mineral films from pipes and engine housings with mouths like coarse files. They cling to curved metal, shell tight against vibration, and when a pump cycle begins they flatten themselves and ride out the tremor like barnacles on the hide of a breathing animal. Their shells become so pitted and crusted that they are difficult to tell from bolt-heads or scabs of ore.

Around vents and steam seams the air is thick with small winged things. Their wings are not delicate in the way sunlit insects are delicate; they are damp, heavy, ribbed, and they beat with a thick humming that seems to come from the throat of the marsh itself. They do not gather because of brightness. They gather because warmth and chemistry call them. They court in clicking spirals, collide softly with one another, and settle on hot surfaces to lay eggs in the condensation that gathers there. When a pressure change comes, whole clouds of them lift at once, a moving texture in the dark felt as much as heard.

The shallow pools belong to blind amphibians with throats that swell into resonant sacs. Their calls are not pretty in any simple sense. They are deep, carrying, sometimes so low that they are felt in the belly before they are registered as sound. One begins, and then another answers across the flooded sheet, and then a dozen, and then the whole basin is filled with overlapping pulses that make the water twitch in concentric rings. They breed after engine shifts, when warm runoff floods the lower channels and the marsh smells strongly of iron and stirred peat. Their eggs are laid in clusters against vibrating surfaces because vibration keeps the eggs oxygenated and because the rhythm of the machinery has, over long time, become a kind of surrogate heartbeat.

Eel-like forms move through flooded root tunnels and narrow waterways, their bodies muscular and sinuous, their heads equipped with whiskers that read electrical disturbances in the water. They hunt by following the faint fields given off by struggling prey or damaged machine parts. Some are small and nervous, living in dense tangles of roots where their bodies can coil and hide. Others grow long enough to bridge a runnel, thick as a leg, and when they move through standing water they displace it with a slow, deliberate pressure that nearby creatures recognize and avoid.

There are crustacean analogues that live on rock faces and engine casings, scraping films, cleaning surfaces, clicking to one another with territorial impatience. Their limbs are adapted to wet metal and slick basalt. They are not merely animals in the marsh; they are part of its maintenance. Where they congregate, surfaces stay cleaner, heat exchange improves, valves remain less clogged. The machines tolerate them. In some cases the machines seem to encourage them, warming certain ledges, releasing mineral-rich fluid at intervals that draw the scrapers into useful patterns.

Filterers anchor themselves where currents are reliable. They are soft-bodied, patient, and strangely beautiful in their function. Their mouths open like slow valves, drawing in water, expelling it, drawing again. They do not chase food. They wait for the marsh to bring it. When a pump cycle changes, they close in unison, their bodies sealing themselves against sudden turbulence. Afterward, when the water settles and carries fresh particles, they open again. Larger animals sometimes rest beside them, taking advantage of the cleaner flow, and predators sometimes wait near them for the same reason.

The larger fauna moves through the marsh as moving terrain. Grazers with broad, rasping mouths crop heat-moss and fungal growth from terraces and banks. They walk on stilt-like limbs with wide, spreading feet that distribute their weight over soft ground. Their bodies are warm, fibrous, slow to anger, and their digestive processes give off a deep fermentation heat. Small creatures follow them, living in the litter that falls from their mouths or sheltering beneath their bodies when a steam squall passes. The grazers communicate by low chest-rumbles and by stamping, sending vibrations through the ground that can be felt long before the animals themselves are near.

Predators are abundant because life is abundant. Some hunt by stillness, lying half-buried in warm mud with only a breathing slit exposed. Some hunt by suction, drawing prey into expandable throats with a sudden collapse of pressure. Some emit focused pulses of sound that stun or disorient. Others use ambush in the dense thickets, where a brush of filament can mean food or death depending on which body moves first. Violence in the marsh is not an interruption. It is one of the regular sounds: a splash, a tearing rush, a choked vibration, then the smaller noises of the world resuming around the place where something has been taken.

Yet among all these lives, the ones who make the place most fully a home are the tool-bearing peoples.

They are not one kind. They are several lineages or species or modes of being, shaped by different parts of the marsh and by different relationships to the engines. What they share is this: they have no use for eyes, they build, they remember, they maintain, and they live inside sound the way other beings might live inside air.

The marsh-edge peoples are perhaps the most widespread. They move in small bands across peat banks, moss fields, and shallow runnels, following warmth, water level, and the seasonal behavior of edible growth. Their bodies are tall enough to reach over reed thickets, but narrow and supple, with broad feet and long toes that spread their weight. Their skin is slick and constantly moist, secreting a protective film that smells of mineral salt and living fiber. They have no eyes.
