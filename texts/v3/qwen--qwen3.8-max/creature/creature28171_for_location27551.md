The first animal lives are almost invisible: translucent worms, fine as threads of spun glass, that move through the cyanobacterial felt without disturbing it. Their bodies are nearly water themselves; only a green or rust-colored gut marks where they have fed. They are the first animal presence here, barely separate from the mats they graze, small enough to live on the skin of the lake rather than in its depths.

Around them, in the warmer crevices and on the white pumice banks, are other sparse creatures: pale mites armored with silica, translucent larvae that fold themselves into leaf-cases, minute crustaceans no larger than seeds, each carrying a tiny shell pitted by mineral deposition. They do not swarm. They appear singly or in brief clusters, as if animal life here is still learning its permitted amount. There are no fish. There are no large predators. The basin has not had time for the long, hungry chains of older waters. Its chemistry is generous but severe: heat where the veins open, silica enough to stiffen tissue, mineral films that coat soft mouths, oxygen in uneven pockets, and a buoyancy that makes every movement strange.

Because animals are few, the place is inhabited above all by what grows. The mats are not mere surfaces but living towns. They spread over basalt shelves in emerald, olive, and copper, holding mineral grains in place with fine sticky threads. Around warm vents, frond-like growths rise in soft plumes, their tissues reinforced with silica, leaning toward the current as if they had preference. Rooted aquatic plants grip fresh lava with tough cord-like roots, sending acid-fine filaments into glassy seams and widening the rock over years. Their stems are flexible, black-green, adapted to pressure and current; their leaves are broad ribbons where water is still, finely divided filaments where flow is stronger. Some carry small gas bladders, pale and translucent, pulling their upper parts toward the light.

These growths are not passive scenery. They extend, test, withdraw, repair, overgrow. The floating ceiling mends its gaps. When a storm tears a section of leaf and bladder, neighboring stems lean into the wound, buoyant shoots rise, and within days a green scar closes. The rooted plants choose cracks. The vent-plumes fold when flow becomes too strong. The microbial films thicken where minerals feed them and thin where water becomes too hot. The lake is populated by intention without brains, by a vegetal attention distributed through leaves, films, roots, and chemical signals.

Between bottom and canopy move the drift-gardens. They are not single organisms but mobile thickets: detached leaf-rosettes, bladder-plants, tangled root-beards, fungal threads, epiphytic algae, and trapped pumice, all held together by slime and buoyancy. They turn slowly in the light, rising when sun warms their bladders, sinking when storms cool the upper water. Small worms and mites ride them. Spores settle on them. They are nurseries, rafts, and traveling islands. To see one pass through a column of turquoise water is to see a piece of the lake’s population migrate without feet.

On the fault shelves live the terrace-builders. They begin as microbial mats but become something larger and more deliberate. Layer upon layer of cyanobacteria, root filaments, mineral crusts, and gas-filled tissue accumulates into living ridges. They are soft at their growing edges, stony where they have stood. They can seal a too-violent vent by growing a lattice across it; they can open channels by letting tissue die back in lines. Their bodies are part reef, part garden, part slow animal colony. They do not move in any visible sense, yet they occupy the basin like patient landlords. If the lake has memory, it is stored in their layered skins: a thin dark line for a season of ash, a pale silica band for a year of strong seepage, a green bloom-line for a warm calm summer.

But if one asks who lives here, not merely what grows, the answer becomes stranger.

Among all these green and mineral lives, the most nearly personal dwellers are the ones who live in the hanging roots. They are not human, and perhaps not animal in the ordinary sense, though they move, attend, and seem to choose. They are the canopy-keepers, the root-walkers, the green quiet of the upper water.

They live mostly in the inverted forest just below the surface, where the floating ceiling of broad leaves and buoyant stems sends roots downward in long curtains. There, in the broken light, they move with a slow, unhurried grace. They are long-limbed and buoyant, their bodies more like flexible water-plants than flesh. The outer skin is dark green where light touches it, pale and almost translucent underneath, threaded with silica veins that give it a faint glitter. Instead of hair, they trail root-fibers. Instead of hands, their fingers divide into fine clinging tendrils that can part root-tangles without tearing them. Along their backs and shoulders are clusters of gas bladders, clear or amber, which they adjust by subtle chemistry, rising or sinking with the slowness of leaves.

Their support comes from turgid tissues and a lattice of flexible fibers, not bone. They can bend into narrow root-chambers and unfold again. Their eyes are large, mineral-pale, adapted to green distances and the slatted light under the floating roof. They have gill-like folds along neck and sides, but also a simple internal bladder that can take air at the surface if needed. They are never forced to leave the water, but they can endure the thin highland air for a short time. Even then, they do not look at home outside it. The air is too cool, too dry, too empty. When they surface at dawn, their leaf-dark skin steams faintly, and they seem part of the mist rather than separate from it.

They do not build with dead materials. They persuade living tissue to grow. A dwelling among them is a knot of roots coaxed into a hollow, a chamber of leaves whose edges have been chemically softened until they adhere, a nursery bladder warmed by a nearby vent and shaded by a trained frond. When a home is no longer needed, it is released back into the general growth. There are no walls, no tools, no carved objects, no stored possessions. Their only architecture is living, and their only craft is cultivation.
