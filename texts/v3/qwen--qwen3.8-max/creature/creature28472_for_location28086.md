It is wrong to say nothing lives there. It is only wrong if one expects life to arrive in the old animal way: with wings, with appetite, with tracks, with the nervous quickness of something that can flee. The sunken intake court has been emptied of that kind of life. The marsh above may have its insects, its birds, its fish, its small secret mammals moving through the reeds, but down the raw steps, past the slumped earth and the broken concrete, the ordinary animal economy has stopped. What lives there lives in another register. It lives slowly, hotly, mineral-slow, in the language of seep and pressure and sound.

The first lives are the smallest and the quietest. They are not creatures in the common sense but communities: the orange-brown mats that skin the stone where warm water runs, the dark green films lying over the shallows like old varnish, the white crusts that gather along evaporation lines and glitter faintly when the chamber dries for an hour. These are the true natives of the pocket. They do not ask for much. They ask for heat, for moisture, for iron, for the faint chemical food carried in the vapor. They build their bodies out of seepage. They make a living from what would otherwise be corrosion.

They are patient beyond animal patience. When a drip strikes them, they do not flinch. When the pumps begin and the shallow pans tremble, they hold their place until the water shifts them. They grow in the corners where the heat is most constant, along the weeping faces of the walls, under ledges where condensation gathers and falls in slow irregular beats. If they can be said to have a season, it is not spring or winter but the rhythm of the machinery: the hours when the pipes warm, the hours when the valves release, the long nights when the chamber exhales its stored heat into the cooler air above. They are not beautiful in a lush way. They are beautiful as evidence. They prove that even here, in a place stripped of fauna, life finds a way to become surface, stain, and film.

But if you stay long enough, the pocket begins to feel less like a colony of films and crusts and more like a room that is being kept. That is when the question changes. It is no longer only “what lives here?” but “who lives here?” And once that question forms, the place seems to answer in the only way it can: by making the machinery sound slightly more intentional.

Call it the Attendant, though that is more an office than a species. Call it the keeper of the intake, the tenant of the control shed, the one who turns the wheels no one else is seen turning. It is not an animal, or at least not now. If it was ever human, it has been converted by the chamber into something more like a function with posture. It belongs to the heat and the drip and the industrial drone. It belongs to the ordinary light on corroded brackets, to the yellow-white glow over valve wheels and gauges, to the warm metal surfaces that hold heat longer than they should. It is the missing laborer suggested by the labor.

You do not meet it at once. At first it is only a sense of arrangement. The chain in the puddle is coiled too neatly. The pressure gauge lying face-down in the silt has been placed, not dropped. The rusted drum half-filled with clean rainwater stands where it can catch the purest condensation from the upper rim. The door of the little control house is not shut, not open, but held at the exact angle that lets the damp air move through without letting the worst of the spray reach the warped wooden bench inside. Nothing looks inhabited, yet nothing looks abandoned. That is the first sign.

Then you feel the attention.

It is not the attention of eyes. There are no eyes in the ordinary sense. It is the attention of pressure. The room seems to notice your weight on the raised path, your breath in the saturated air, the small heat you add to the already hot chamber. A drip changes rhythm. A panel creaks when no wind is present. One of the pipes gives a single hollow knock, not loud, not threatening, but decisive, as if something has registered you and passed the information along. The Attendant is not necessarily visible in that moment. It may be only a change in the acoustic field, a shift in the layers of sound that fill the pocket. The water keeps slipping down the walls, the transformers keep up their low hum, but underneath them there is a new note: the sense that the place is being listened to from inside.

When it does take shape, it does so as a figure seen almost casually. Near the control house, where the roof sags and the corrugated panels are streaked with rust and mineral white, there is a shadow that seems too dense for the light. It may resemble a person wearing a heavy coat, though the coat has the stiffness of something left in damp for years, then dried, then wetted again until the fabric has become more like wall than cloth. The color is not quite yellow, not quite gray, not quite rust. It is the color of old paint under mineral bloom. The hood may be up. The face may be hidden by vapor, by shadow, by the fact that the chamber’s light is ordinary and flat and does not grant clarity to everything at once.

Its body is difficult to remember because it is made of the room’s materials. Its shoulders have the blunt weight of wet concrete. Its hands are pale and heat-roughened, with fingers thick at the joints, fingertips polished like worn porcelain. Where skin might be, there is the shine of condensation on metal. Where hair might be, there is something like fine rust or dark filament, moving faintly when the vents exhale. It does not seem to breathe in the way a person breathes, but it does pause, and when it pauses, the chamber pauses with it. Then a valve releases its hot ragged hiss, and the figure seems to resume its task.

It moves along the fragments of catwalk and the dry concrete islands with a slow, practiced balance. It knows which stones are firm and which patches of sediment will turn dark and liquid under pressure. It does not avoid the shallow black water out of fear, exactly, but out of knowledge. It knows when the pumps are about to start. It can feel the change in the floor before the sound becomes audible. Sometimes, just before the deep suction-throb begins, the Attendant will stand still beside one of the valve kiosks, one hand resting on the wheel, not turning it yet, only waiting for the pipes to knock their first warning. When the knock comes, it may turn the wheel a fraction, or tap the pipe once with something metal, or simply step back. The machinery starts. The water in the pans trembles. The room takes on its heavier voice. The Attendant has done what was needed, though no one could say exactly what that was.

It tends the chamber in the way a body tends a wound: not to heal it back to its original condition, but to manage its ongoing. The intake is young, raw, fractured. The recent collapse or scour has left sharp edges and fresh gouges in the clay. Blocks of broken concrete jut at angles. Black ballast stones and split basalt fragments lie unrounded where they fell. The Attendant does not try to smooth these things. It works around them. It directs moisture where it can be directed. It scrapes the microbial mats away from certain gauge faces and sight-glasses, not because it dislikes them, but because some surfaces must remain legible. It leaves the mats elsewhere, where they can slow the seep or filter the mineral water into their slow skins. It does not garden. It balances.

Its tools are the scattered artifacts of the chamber. The heavy chain coiled in the puddle is not decoration; when the water rises beyond a certain point, the chain is pulled taut against something hidden below. The broken ceramic insulator has been fitted under a leaking conduit so that the drip strikes it and breaks into a finer spray before reaching a warm cable junction. The rusted drum catching clean rainwater is one of the few sources of sweetness in the place, and the Attendant guards it in its own way. It may use that water to cool a valve, to mark a level, to make a small clear mirror in which the upper opening can be watched without looking directly up. The pressure gauge face-down in the silt is not garbage. It has been silenced, perhaps because it told a pressure that was no longer useful, or perhaps because its face was cracked and the reading became a lie. The Attendant does not throw things away. It reassigns them.

It does not speak. If it communicates, it does so through the room’s own voice. A sudden thinning of the transformer hum may be a warning. A rise in heat may be an instruction. A loose panel may begin to chatter when you have stood too long in one place. The red warning lamp on its ledge, unlit and small, is the clearest word it has. As long as the lamp remains dark, the Attendant may tolerate you. It may even, in its impersonal way, guide you: by leaving a dry path lit by the work lamps, by letting the fan above turn in a rhythm that makes the air easier to breathe, by allowing the condensation to run down a particular wall where it can be touched and drunk if you are foolish or thirsty enough. But if the red lamp lights, then something has crossed a threshold. The water may heat. The pumps may change their suction. The black pans may deepen in places that were shallow a moment before. The sound may become so layered, so pressurized, that your own heartbeat feels foreign to you. The Attendant does not need to attack. It only needs to adjust the room until you are no longer compatible with it.

It is hard to say whether it is one being or several. Sometimes the chamber seems to contain a single presence: the stooped figure in the mineral-stiff coat, moving from valve to gauge to cracked glass panel. At other times, the sounds suggest a crew. There is a knock here, a drip there, the creak of expanding metal on the far wall, the gritty whisper of the fan changing speed, the faint scrape of something being dragged across stone just beyond hearing. These may be separate actions performed by one entity, or they may be fragments of a distributed life, a maintenance colony without bodies. The place compresses too much into too small a space for ordinary individuation. A whole industrial process has been folded into a pocket, and whatever lives there has been folded with it. It may be singular the way a machine is singular. It may be plural the way a system is plural.

Its metabolism is not eating, not exactly. It feeds on differentials. It draws life from the gap between the cooler marsh air above and the hot close air below. It takes what it can from the stray warmth of buried machinery, from the faint electrical leakage in old cables, from the mineral charge carried by the sweating walls. When the chamber is at its hottest, the Attendant seems more solid. When cooler air slips down from above and the puddles take on their faint steaming look, it becomes more active, moving through the vapor with a sense of purpose. It may stand over a vent when the warm air exhales, not bathing in it, but receiving it, as if the vent is a mouth and the Attendant is listening to the breath of something far below. It may press a hand to a pipe and hold it there until the metal stops trembling. It may collect condensation from the rails in cupped palms, not to drink like a person, but to return the water elsewhere, to complete a circuit only it understands.

It does not sleep, but it has modes. There are periods when it seems to stand down, when the chamber is only water falling, heat gathering, light shifting. In those hours, it may be indistinguishable from the control house itself: a dark shape under the sagging roof, a coat on a hook, a stillness in the corner where the breaker panel waits with its dead switches. Then a pump starts, or a valve opens somewhere behind the walls, and the stillness resolves into attention. The Attendant is not awakened by noise. It is awakened by fault. It exists for the moment when something must be adjusted. Between those moments, it remains in a state that is less rest than readiness.

It has no nest, no bed, no midden. The place does not accumulate life’s refuse in the ordinary way. There are no bones, no shells, no feathers, no insect casings. What remains are industrial relics: corroded brackets, porcelain fittings, broken gauges, split cable sheathing, mineral-streaked wood. The Attendant keeps these things in a kind of order, but it does not hoard them sentimentally. They are organs and tools. If a piece of equipment fails beyond use, the chamber absorbs it. Rust advances. Water works the edges. Mineral crust seals a crack. The object becomes geology. In this way, the Attendant also becomes geology, little by little. Its coat stiffens. Its hands take on the sheen of wet stone. Its outline loses certainty in the silver haze of condensation. It is not immortal so much as continuous. It persists as long as the chamber has need of attention.
