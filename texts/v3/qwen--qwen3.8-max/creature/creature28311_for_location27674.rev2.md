The first mistake is to look for creatures.  
The second is to look for people in the old, bounded sense: separate bodies with clear edges, warm and hurried and animal in their needs. Neither search is entirely wrong, but both are too narrow. What lives there lives as part of a wider condition. In that high, cool, green-extending world, habitation is not only something that happens inside skin. It can be a schedule, a function, a tendency, a responsibility, a pattern that has grown dense enough to answer when called.

The beings you would first call inhabitants are the ones who move along the grooved avenues: upright, deliberate, tool-bearing, not quite human but close enough to an old idea of the human to unsettle anyone expecting something wholly alien. They are the city’s cuttings, its trained and tending forms, its walkers and workers and surveyors. Their bodies are made of the same substance as the place itself: dense fiber under bark-like skin, joints swelling like healed knots, veins carrying sap, resin, and heat rather than blood in the old sense. They are taller and thinner than most lowland bodies, stretched by distance and sparsity. Their faces are less expressive than orienting: broad planes of leaf-laminate, dark seed-case eyes, mouths that part like split sheaths. They do not smile as a lowlander would understand smiling. They turn toward moisture, toward sound, toward pressure, toward need.

Some of them are new enough to show their making: iron pins in their shoulders, cable braces along their ribs, sap-lines still visibly running beside them like temporary veins. Others are ancient. Their lower legs have thickened into the ground; their feet have become anchor-routes; moss has grown over their hips and backs. They may stand for hours in one place, not sleeping, not waiting in the ordinary sense, but continuing a civic function through stillness. A figure planted at a corner may be a sentry, a weather-gauge, a memory-post, or all three. If it speaks, its voice comes as a creak from inside the stem, or as a vibration passed along root-felt to the soles of your boots.

These walking inhabitants are not decorative dryads or gentle forest spirits. They are municipal. They wear practical things: capes of felted moss, harnesses of braided stem, belts with metal tools gone green at the edges, pouches for seed, resin, cuttings, and small instruments. Their hands are strong and precise, fingers of cane and fiber, sometimes tipped with copper or polished wood. They smell of crushed leaf, wet bark, warm oil, rust, and steam. Their movements are neither quick nor slow; they have the economy of growth. They do not waste gestures. When they work, they work with the patience of roots and the sudden decisiveness of pruning shears.

They are not one people in the simple sense. There are lineages, graft-lines, nursery cycles, district habits. Some are grown from municipal trunks in broad cisterns where pale shoots stand in dark water, each inspected, each staked, each given a first orientation. Some are budded from older structures: a wall may produce a caretaker, a bridge may put out a supervisor, a boiler may grow a tender from its own heat-stressed bark. Some are assigned rather than born: a street that has begun to fold may develop a keeper; a door that opens at the wrong hour may find itself attended by a figure that did not exist the day before. In this place, a life can begin as an appointment.

No one there asks first, “What are you?”  
They ask, “What are you keeping?”  
Or, “What has grown into you?”  
Or, “What route do you maintain?”

Children, if that word can be used, are not small versions of adults. They are supported cuttings, tender shoots trained along stakes, taught to hold moisture, to distinguish useful fog from useless damp, to recognize the sound of an engine about to misfeed. They are protected not from work but from premature fixation. A young inhabitant may be moved from district to district so it does not root too early into one habit. Some become walkers. Some become house-forms. Some become cistern-guards. Some become the quiet, half-motionless presences that stand at thresholds and do nothing visible for years, yet are essential to the balance of a street.

Names are not fixed labels. They are growth records. A person may have a seed-name, a training-name, a work-name, a pruning-name, and a final notch-name carved into a root-wall when they have become part of the city’s permanent structure. Names are often situational: “Third Mist on the Eastern Rise,” “Knot Under Boiler,” “Leaf That Corrects Rain,” “Hand That Holds the Rail,” “Door Refused Until Surveyed.” To know someone fully is to know their sequence of names and the conditions under which each was given. In ordinary speech, they may use shorter forms, or sounds: a click of seed-shell, a hiss of steam, a particular creak that carries the memory of a place.

Their language has changed to fit the world. It includes tenses for becoming, for overgrowth, for dormancy, for scheduled disappearance. It has words for dampness that arrives briefly and must be caught, for silence that means danger, for a path that has altered without malice, for a building that has migrated overnight. It also has many terms for pruning, because pruning is not one act there. There is pruning as care, pruning as punishment, pruning as memory, pruning as mercy, pruning as civic correction, pruning as grief, and pruning as invitation.

They eat, but not in the way a lowland animal eats. Some draw moisture and light directly through prepared surfaces. Some consume pressed seed-cakes, fermented pulp, mineral broths drawn from old stone, resin pastilles, and fiber loaves softened in cistern water. Others feed machinery more than themselves, tasting sap for heat-value, testing green liquor for fermentation, carrying nutrient sludge to engines that thump behind hedges. Meals are less social performances than maintenance intervals, though festivals exist. During condensation festivals, when every surface beads with water, they gather at cisterns and roof-hollows with vessels of leaf and old metal. They do not celebrate abundance by waste; they celebrate precision. Every drop caught is a small victory.

Their work is everywhere, but it is not frantic. The loudness of the landscape—the wind-drag of leaves, the creak of structures, the thud of sap-engines, the percussion of falling droplets—is not background to them. It is the medium in which their labor is organized. Fog-combers walk the open rises with long wands, dragging moisture from the air into catch-leaves. Cistern-keepers test pools for false reflections. Engine-feeders climb to boiler mouths with baskets of resin and compressed fiber. Pruning crews move through districts with saws, hooks, binding straps, and small living graft-kits. Surveyors carry notched rods and reels of fiber line, measuring streets that have changed length, staircases that have altered their rise, walls that have drifted a few meters in the night.

Surveyors are among the most important inhabitants. Reality there is not hostile, but it is unreliable in settled, administrative ways. A street may fold back on itself. A nearby factory may be an hour away. A painted leaf-surface may pretend to be a wall. Rain may briefly fall upward. A stair may arrive at different levels depending on the direction of approach. These are not emergencies; they are municipal facts. The surveyors record them. They do not rage against the instability. They file it, mark it, reroute traffic, adjust schedules. If a path changes direction, they survey the new route. If a building grows a door where none existed, they assign someone to watch it until it proves itself. If a district becomes thin at dusk, they post markers and wait for it to thicken again.

This gives the inhabitants a particular temperament. They are patient, but not passive. They are tolerant of strangeness, but strict about procedure. They do not panic easily because most anomalies have precedents. They are not superstitious in a wild sense; they are ritualized. They know that silence is more alarming than noise. In a place where leaves and engines and pipes are always sounding, a sudden quiet means something has stopped breathing, stopped turning, stopped condensing, or stopped being remembered. When silence falls over a district, the inhabitants become alert. They move toward it carefully, tools in hand, ready to repair, prune, feed, or record.

Their social life is sparse, like their buildings. They are not crowded into neighborhoods. Many live far apart, connected by long green intervals and avenues of compressed leaf. Solitude is normal to them. A person may spend days in a house-kilometers from another without loneliness as a lowlander would understand it. But they are not isolated. They communicate through messengers, through marked stems, through vibrations passed along root-lines, through steam-signals from boiler towers, through the rare beetles trained to carry sealed seed-capsules. Distance is not empty to them; it is potential. The open spaces between districts are not wastelands. They are reserves, future rooms, latent forests, possible machines. To live among such intervals is to live surrounded by things that have not yet decided what they will become.

Their houses are among the most important inhabitants, and it is not always clear where the resident ends and the dwelling begins. A house there is not a container for life. It is a slow colleague. Some houses are trained trees with hollowed trunks, resin windows, and roofs of overlapping seed-scales. Some are ancient structures whose lower floors have been swallowed by the green mass, so that doors open at ground level only because the ground has risen to meet them. Some are newer scaffolds with leaf-panel walls, still visibly assembled, still adjusting to their occupants.

A house may choose its residents by growth. It may thicken a wall around a person who sleeps often in the same place. It may grow a shelf where tools are kept. It may form a window facing the direction its resident most often looks. In time, the house becomes a bodily extension of the person, and the person becomes part of the house’s routine. If a resident leaves, the house may remain vacant for a long time, not empty so much as remembering. Some houses grow doors for people who have not yet arrived. Some grow rooms for people who are no longer there. These rooms are not avoided; they are treated with caution and respect. Door-watchers inspect them. If the room opens into a memory, it is cataloged. If it opens into an unbuilt future, it is scheduled.

Machines also live there, though their life is of another order. The engines, boilers, dynamos, presses, vats, waterwheels, and sorting mechanisms are not inert technology mastered by inhabitants. They are partners, dependents, hazards, and civic organs. They thump, hiss, whine, knock, and vibrate. They need heat, water, fiber, sap, grease, and sometimes pollination. Yes, pollination: some machines have flowering joints, some have seed-valves, some have heat-stalks that must be brushed at certain intervals or they clog and grow feverish. Machine-tenders speak of engines as if they were large, slow, temperamental creatures. A boiler can be sullen after a cold night. A dynamo can become over-excited if fed too richly. A press can develop a stubborn rhythm and refuse to release its load until a particular vibration is tapped against its frame.

The machines are not worshiped. They are maintained. But they are also listened to. An engine’s knock may indicate a blockage in a sap-line, or it may indicate that the ground beneath has shifted and the machine is trying to adjust its foundation. A humming dynamo may be healthy, or it may be drawing too much current from a root-network and starving a nearby district. The inhabitants who work with machines are part mechanic, part gardener. They carry wrenches and pruning knives. They know when to tighten a bolt and when to let bark grow over it. They understand that metal in this place is not permanent. It is a temporary skeleton. Sooner or later, the plant wins. But the plant needs the metal, too. The civilization is not soft; it is reinforced. It is industrial and vegetable at once.

Water is sacred in the practical sense. The inhabitants do not treat water as romance; they treat it as responsibility. Every roof, leaf, rail, groove, and cistern has a role in catching and holding dampness. A person who wastes water is not merely careless; they are injuring the civic body. Pools are protected organs, not ornaments. Some are cool and dark, used to stabilize engines. Some are shallow nurseries where replacement structures begin as floating shoots. Some are archives. The inhabitants know that certain pools hold images longer than they should. They approach them with care. A pool that shows another sky is not automatically dangerous, but it is not to be drunk from until the water remembers its own weather.

Pool-readers are specialists. They lean over still surfaces and interpret what appears: the settlement as it was before the current growth-cycle, a district after a future pruning, a street that has not yet folded but will. They do not treat these images as prophecy in a mystical sense. They treat them as pressure readings from time. If a pool repeatedly shows a collapsed terrace, surveyors inspect the terrace. If it shows a door that no longer exists, door-watchers check the old foundation. If it shows a person no one recognizes, the image is recorded. Sometimes the person later appears. Sometimes the person has already appeared and no one remembers them. This does not cause alarm. Memory there is not a sealed room; it is compost, always breaking down and re-entering use.

The remembered are among the strangest inhabitants. They are not ghosts in the sentimental sense. They do not moan, they do not beg, they do not haunt for revenge. They are residual habits, growth-memories, shapes the city has repeated so often that they persist after the original conditions have gone. A remembered figure may sweep a path that is now lawn. A remembered child may run along the edge of a vanished terrace. A remembered worker may stand at a machine whose place has been composted into a nursery bed. These figures are translucent at times, or made of leaf-shadows, or visible only in certain weather. They are not pitied. They are managed. If a remembered person interferes with traffic, they are gently pruned. If they are harmless, they are allowed to continue. Sometimes they are assigned small duties, because even a memory can be useful if it holds a routine.

The temporarily remembered can become citizens if they thicken. This happens rarely, but it is not unheard of. A figure that appears at dusk in a two-dimensional district may, over many cycles, gain depth. It may begin to carry tools. It may respond to questions. It may be given a notch-name and a water ration. The inhabitants do not ask whether such a person has a soul. They ask whether the person can keep a schedule. If the answer is yes, they belong.

The predator-plants are also inhabitants, though not neighbors in any comforting sense. They live in the quieter margins: low rosettes with trigger-spines, hanging pitchers the size of cabinets, root-snares that close around anything warm that struggles too long. Because true animals are rare, these predators have developed a strange, patient hunger. They do not feed often. They wait. They catch drifting seeds, fallen leaves, fibers, spore-clumps, occasional beetles, and sometimes errors: a glove left too long, a surveyor’s marker, a child’s training-stake. The inhabitants do not destroy them without reason. They are part of the civic ecology. They clean the margins. They process what strays. But they are fenced, watched, and pruned when they grow too near public paths. Some are used deliberately: a root-snare may be placed near an unstable fold in the ground to catch debris before it blocks a capillary channel. The relationship is not affectionate. It is administrative.

Fauna, where it exists at all, is rare enough to be treated as a sign rather than a population. The pale moths that drift between condenser towers are watched with quiet attention. They are almost translucent in the thin light, moving like steam that has decided to remember wings. They are not hunted. They are noted. Their appearance often coincides with changes in humidity, engine cycles, or thin places in the world. The black beetles in the lower bark are more common, but still not numerous. They perform essential work: cleaning dead tissue, breaking down old resin, carrying small messages, sometimes unintentionally pollinating machine-joints. Children are taught not to disturb them. Beetles are considered minor officials of the lower city.

True animals are another matter. If one is seen—something with breath, warm blood, quick eyes, an animal’s urgency—the inhabitants do not chase it. They note its direction. They record the place where it appeared. They check for nearby folds, upward rain, doors that have opened without permission. Animals are not hated. They are unsettling because they belong to an older logic of life, one that the plateau has mostly composted. Their rarity gives them a strange dignity. Some inhabitants believe animals appear where the world is thinning. Others believe they are remnants of the cities beneath the current city, surfacing when old layers are metabolized. Others say they are merely lost. No one says they are common. No one treats them as ordinary.

Love among the inhabitants is not absent, but it is not primarily romantic in the lowland sense. It is closer to grafting: the willingness to join, to share moisture, to grow around another form without crushing it. Bonds are made by long proximity, by exchanged cuttings, by shared maintenance. Two inhabitants may become attached by tending the same engine for years. Others may join households that are also root-networks. Some love houses. Some love machines. Some love pools. Some love paths. This is not considered strange. In a world where buildings breathe and streets can change their minds, affection is not limited to persons. It is extended to whatever endures, helps, or returns care.

They are capable of severity. Their world cannot afford sentimentality. If a structure grows into a public path, it is pruned. If a machine overheats and threatens a cistern, it is starved of fuel until it calms. If an inhabitant becomes overgrown, losing the ability to move or speak or keep their assigned route, they are not left to choke the ground. They are helped into stillness. Their living parts may be grafted elsewhere. Their mass may become a wall, a bench, a terrace lip, a support for a young bridge. This is not considered death in the final sense. It is promotion into infrastructure. The person does not vanish; they become a place. Friends may rest their hands on the place later and feel a slow answering warmth.

Conflict exists, but it is rarely dramatic. Competition for light, moisture, heat, and anchorage shapes politics as surely as any ideology. Canopy-forms may grow too tall and starve lower districts. Engine-tenders may demand more sap than moss-fields can spare. A factory may expand too greedily and need to be cut back. These disputes are handled through survey, quota, pruning, and negotiation among growth-lines. There are assemblies, but not always in the form of people speaking in a hall. Sometimes an assembly is a circle of roots sharing chemical signals. Sometimes it is a row of houses adjusting their roof-pitches until a compromise is reached. Sometimes it is a boiler lowering its pressure after a long complaint. The inhabitants understand governance as cultivation. To rule is to prune without causing collapse.

They have festivals, but these are practical and atmospheric. During spore release, the air thickens with green-gold clouds and the sound becomes a low roar. The inhabitants wear masks of woven fiber and resin, protect sensitive joints, and work in shifts to keep filters and breathing-fronds clear. It is not a joyful season in a simple way, but it is honored. Spore is future. Too much of it is danger; enough of it is continuation. During condensation festivals, every surface beads with water, and the cisterns fill. The inhabitants move quietly from catch-point to catch-point, collecting, measuring, sharing. There is music, but it is made from tuned stems, dripping metal, stretched root-strings, and the hollow percussion of seed-capsules. The music sounds like the city describing itself.

Dormancy intervals are more solemn. Whole sectors grow quiet. Machinery runs at reduced pressure. Some inhabitants root themselves and slow their functions. Others maintain minimal routes. Dormancy is not death, but it is a rehearsal of absence. The city becomes spacious and hushed. The few who remain active move carefully, listening for silence. When the interval ends, growth resumes with a sound like many joints loosening at once. The world does not leap back to life; it remembers how to continue.

They know they live on top of older settlements. They know the current city has composted previous ones into itself. Sometimes a root-wall reveals the shape of an older brick wall. Sometimes a terrace is found to be flattened roofs from a forgotten cycle. Sometimes sap-pipes run through obsolete conduits whose purpose no one can reconstruct. The inhabitants do not treat these discoveries as losses. They treat them as inheritance. The past is not beneath them as a tomb; it is within them as structure. They may feel a strange tenderness when they find a worn stone patch where plant-cover has peeled back. The stone is smooth, ancient, dull as a mirror. It will not remain bare long. The green mass is patient. The inhabitants understand that they, too, will one day be metabolized.

They are not gentle in the way a soft world would be gentle. Their kindness is careful, not mild. They prune because they know what happens when growth is left unattended. They catch water because they know what happens when dampness escapes. They survey because they know what happens when a folded street is ignored. They feed machines because they know what happens when heat becomes lonely and seeks its own fuel. Their civilization is not a paradise. It is a maintenance pact between living matter, old metal, weather, memory, and time.

If you were to meet one of them, you might not realize at first that it was a person. You might see a figure standing in municipal grass, motionless, face lifted toward mist. You might hear a creak and think it was a branch settling. You might see a house breathe and think your eyes were adjusting to light. You might lean over a pool and see a face that is not your own. You might find a door grown overnight in a wall that had no door yesterday. You might follow a street that repeats itself with slight variations and then meet a surveyor marking the repetition as if it were ordinary weather.

Do not expect them to be frightened by you. They will observe you as they observe any new presence: with attention, without rush, assessing whether you are a danger, a resource, a memory, or a mistake. If you are thirsty, they may offer water, because wasting water is wrong and because drinking is a civic act. If you ask where you are, they may answer with a route rather than a name. If you ask who lives there, they may look around at the houses, the engines, the pools, the rare beetles, the fog-combers, the remembered figures, the predator-plants, the distant chimneys, and the empty intervals, and say something that translates poorly but means:

All of us that are needed.  
All of us that have not been pruned away.  
All of us that still hold a shape.

That answer would be true. The inhabitants are not only the walking figures with tools and names. They are the trained tree-houses and the breathing fronds, the boiler-beasts and the cisterns, the folded streets and the surveyors who mark them, the pools that remember and the pool-readers who interpret them, the rare moths and the beetles, the remembered tenants and the doors that appear without permission. Some lives are bodies. Some lives are functions. Some lives are habits thickened into form. Some lives are absences kept in schedule.

What lives there is not a population placed inside a landscape.  
It is a landscape that has learned to inhabit itself.

And under the ordinary daylight, with clouds passing close enough to seem municipal, the whole green-extending world continues its ancient, loud, vegetable work: engines thump, leaves rise, condensation gathers, streets fold gently, houses breathe, surveyors mark new distances, and the inhabitants — grown, assigned, remembered, pruned, and still becoming — go on with their patient, impossible lives.
