Whoever lives there is shaped first of all by dryness.

Not merely adapted to it, but formed from the same discipline as the world itself: sealed surfaces, internal chemistry, minimal loss, hard edges, patient motion. Life there does not rely on open moisture. It does not drink from pools, sweat through skin, or soften in humid air. It carries its solvent locked in cells, bladders, fibers, and slow metabolic cycles. It is life made of vessels and valves, of cuticle and resin, of pressure and resonance.

The great plant-forms are alive, but they are less like individual creatures than like organs of the world. The columnar stems, the tower-forms, the rib-cages of living wood, the disc canopies spread over bright air — these are the dominant living masses. They breathe the thin air in vast cycles. They make wind, charge, sound, and weather. They are not scenery. They are the architecture of the place, and everything else lives inside their consequences.

But if one asks who lives there in the sense of who moves, perceives, remembers, and belongs, the answer is not the towers alone.

The answer is the herds.

They are the armored grazers of the fiber plains, the polished, slow-moving presences that cross the pale plant-stone in long processions and disappear into distance. They are not human, and nothing about them is human in habit or desire. They do not build. They do not make. They do not carve, tie, stack, fasten, or assemble. They have no concept of a tool as a separate object. They are not makers. They are participants.

They are large, but not heavy in the way a human would understand heaviness. Their bodies are broad and low, distributed across multiple columnar limbs that press the resonant ground with wide, fibrous foot-pads. Their backs and flanks are covered in overlapping plates of polished cuticle, dry as ceramic, hard as mineralized wood.
