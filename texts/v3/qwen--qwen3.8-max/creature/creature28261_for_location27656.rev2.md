What lives there is not, at first, easy to separate from the place itself. The pocket does not contain inhabitants so much as it secretes them, argues them into shape, keeps them warm and wet and unfinished. To ask who lives there is to be answered by a crowd of small presences: hands without bodies, water with memory, plants that behave like artisans, fungi that keep the dim light, and a few sparse animals that seem less born than permitted.

The first residents, if you can call them that, are the hands.

They are the five-fingered impressions in the clay walls, but they are not marks. They are not memorials of some vanished builder. They are still working. When water runs over them, they flex faintly, not enough to be obvious, but enough to make the wall seem to breathe through its skin. They press where a seam has begun to open. They smooth a ridge before it hardens too sharply. They widen a pore for steam when the heat thickens. They close tiny drains when the basin needs to keep more water. They do not move across the surface the way a crab moves; they migrate by becoming more prominent here and less present there, as if the room remembers touch wherever touch is needed.

They have no faces, no mouths, no eyes. They do not need them. Their knowledge is in the fingertips. They know the difference between a wall that must stay soft and one that must take weight. They know when a reed pin is about to slip, when a cord is too tight, when the ash-clay bricks are drying unevenly. They are the room’s oldest young things, the continuing verbs of whoever made it. Perhaps the builders left and their hands stayed. Perhaps the builders never existed apart from the hands. In that hot, crowded dark, it is easier to believe that making came first and bodies were only temporary handles.

The basin has another kind of life.

In the blackened clay pool, over the mosaic of tiny blue shards, lives the pale salamander. It is the closest thing the pocket has to a heart that has decided to wander outside the body. Its skin is translucent enough that you can see the dark pulse moving inside it, not hurried, not calm, but deliberate in the way a thought is deliberate when it has not yet become words. It moves with terrible slowness through the hot clear water, lifting one limb, pausing, letting the water decide whether the limb has been accepted, then setting the limb down as if placing a tool on a shelf.

It has no eyes, or perhaps it has not yet chosen whether eyes are necessary. In a place lit by resin-lamps, fungus-glow, and the dull breathing red of a hidden brazier, sight may be too crude a sense. The salamander seems to know by pressure, by heat, by the minute alterations of the water’s taste. When a drop falls from the ceiling, it may turn its head a fraction toward the sound. When the reed pipes rise in their mournful chord, it becomes still, as if listening through its spine. Its shadows do not agree with one another. Sometimes there are several, each bent in a different future posture, as though the salamander is not one animal but a small council of possible animals temporarily sharing the same pale body.

It does not seem to eat in any ordinary way. Maybe it drinks warmth. Maybe it feeds on the mineral sweetness of the blue shards, on spores that settle on the water, on the first heat of the rain when the small cloud enters and darkens. It is not hungry in the manner of beasts. It is hungry in the manner of a lamp: it needs to be kept supplied with something invisible. When it sinks slowly out of sight, the basin’s whisper changes pitch, becoming deeper and more interior, as if the room has swallowed its own tongue.

Around the salamander, in the channels and troughs and wet seams, live the thread-lives.

The white worms are almost too simple to be called animals. They are moving filaments, pale as root tips, drawing themselves through the shallow water with a patience that makes the salamander seem restless. They are the pocket’s stitchers. Wherever two materials meet—clay to shell, root-mat to cloth, kiln-dust to seed-husk—the worms pass through the wetness between them, leaving behind no visible trail but a sense that the joint has become more coherent. They do not build as hands build. They bind by presence. If a hairline crack appears in a channel lip, a worm may enter it and remain there, its body becoming a living thread until the surrounding clay remembers itself and closes.

They are also the room’s handwriting. Their movements make brief sentences in the water. A curl means the heat is rising. A straight, slow draw means the air is about to thicken. When they knot themselves together near a drain, the water will soon change direction. They are not intelligent in any shape you could recognize, but they are exact. They belong to the pocket the way stitches belong to a wound: small, necessary, almost invisible, holding what would otherwise fall apart.

The springtails are different. They are the room’s sudden thoughts.

They live in the moss cushions, on the undersides of broad leaves, in the damp folds where reed stems press against woven cloth. They are tiny enough to seem like accidents until they move, and then they are accidents with intention. They jump from one leaf to another, vanish, reappear elsewhere, vanish again. They are not abundant, but they are everywhere in quick flashes, like sparks struck from a wet fire. They test the air. They test the steam. They test the tension of a droplet before it falls. When the room is about to shift from hush to rain, they become restless and leap more often, as if trying to outrun the weather before it arrives.

They are prey, but not in a simple wild sense. The pocket has so little animal life that predation feels less like violence and more like calibration. The eyeless centipede is the one who keeps the count.

It emerges from behind the reed matting only when necessary. Its body is long, pale, segmented, and moves with a mechanical patience that makes it seem like a tool given appetite. Its legs are a row of tiny decisions. It does not hunt with anger. It hunts with exactness. It waits near a moss ledge while springtails flicker above it. It becomes still in the way that only artificial things can be still: not resting, but withheld. Then it moves, and the movement is precise enough to seem rehearsed long before it happened. A springtail disappears. The centipede withdraws. The room’s balance settles again.

It is not clear where the centipede lives when it is not visible. Perhaps it folds itself into the wall like a needle into cloth. Perhaps it waits in some narrow space behind the clay, dormant until the number of springtails exceeds the room’s patience. It feels less like a creature than a corrective, a living instrument placed there to keep the smaller lives from multiplying into disorder.

The fungi are quieter, but no less important.

They live in the darker corners, along the wettest seams, on the shaded undersides of shelves and inside the damp hollows where the resin-lamps do not reach. Some are round as buttons. Some are folded like ears. Some are veined with faint light, pale greenish-white, and seem to glow not because they wish to be seen but because their living is a kind of slow combustion. They are the lamp-tenders of the pocket. They do not carry flames; they soften illumination. They make the dimness inhabitable.

They release their spores in fine glitters when the steam rises. The spores hang in the air among the droplets, catching the low light, and for a moment the crowded chamber seems to contain a tiny festival of suspended stars. Then they settle on the wet clay, on the moss, on the leaves, on the blue shards in the basin. Some of them will become new fungi. Some will be absorbed. Some will only make the air thicker, sweeter, more alive. The fungi do not decay anything here, because nothing has yet become old enough to decay. Instead, they seem to digest excess possibility. They eat overfullness. They soften what is too intense. They make the room’s constant becoming bearable.

The plants are not scenery. They are residents too, though their lives are pressed into the architecture so tightly that they seem half organ, half inhabitant.

The moss is the pocket’s skin. It swells when the air wets and tightens when the air cools. It covers every horizontal seam, not merely growing there but holding the seams shut with its dense green patience. The ferns are more restless. They unfurl in tight fists, then open too quickly, as if time is being pushed through them from behind. Their leaves rasp against one another when the humidity changes, and they lean toward the hidden brazier’s warmth with a slow, blind eagerness.

The orchids are stranger. Their leaves are thick, heat-blistered, and pressed against the wall as if they are listening to the clay’s inner temperature. They do not bloom often, but when they do, the flowers are small, hot-colored, and brief. They open when steam rises, as though the steam were a summons, and close when the air momentarily cools, as though disappointment had passed over them. They are not decorative. They are valves of some kind, perhaps emotional valves, perhaps organs for expressing the pocket’s pressure in color.

The reeds are voices. They cluster around the water channels, their roots bound in strips of woven cloth, and when air passes through their hollow stems they make the low mournful chord that bends upward into a whistle and collapses into a gurgle. They are not merely sounding. They are calling weather. When their note rises, the water sometimes runs upward along the clay channels. When their note thins, the hanging droplets pause in the air, reconsidering. The reeds are the room’s incomplete language: enough music to make the elements obey, not enough grammar to explain why.

The vine that has threaded itself through the handle of the bone trowel is perhaps the most deliberate of the plant-lives. It has not merely grown around the tool. It has adopted it. The green fist holds the trowel in place, but not as a trap holds something. It holds the way a hand holds a spoon while resting between uses. Sometimes, when the wall near the basin needs smoothing, the vine seems to shift almost imperceptibly, and the trowel’s edge catches the clay. It is not clear whether the vine is using the tool or becoming the tool. In that place, the difference may be meaningless. It is a living grip, a green intention wrapped around a made object, and together they form a small compound worker.

Even the weather lives there.

The small cloud enters through a slit in the upper wall as if squeezing itself through a wound. It is no larger than a pillow, but it behaves like an animal. It darkens. It thickens. It hesitates. Then it releases a brief hissing rain that beads on the leaves and runs into the channels. After that it thins, unravels, and is drawn upward into the steam, gone but not gone, because the pocket’s cycles bring it back again. It is the room’s tame sky, a pocket-sized season, a visitor that is also an organ. It lives in transformation: cloud, rain, steam, bead, drip, hush, then cloud again.

Water is the most numerous life of all.

It is not one body but many. It beads on leaf undersides. It weeps from the tiny holes in the baked clay lump. It threads down copper pins. It gathers in marshy ledges an inch deep. It slips into drains no wider than a finger. It ticks into hollow seed-cases with a bright resonant sound. It sweats from the walls. It drips from the ceiling. It remembers heat. It pauses in the air when the reeds sing. It runs upward when sound persuades it. It is the room’s blood, breath, saliva, and thought. If the pocket has a mind, water is the medium in which that mind moves.

Heat lives there too, under the grille.

The covered brazier is not an animal in any ordinary sense, but its dull red breathing gives it the air of one. It exhales warmth into the hidden flues. It is fed by the leather bellows, which expand and contract with the gravity of sleeping lungs. The heat it makes is not wild. It is housed, directed, coaxed, measured. It moves through clay cells and tile channels. It warms the basin from below. It makes the walls sweat. It coaxes the orchids into their brief hot flowers. It turns rain into steam and steam into condensation and condensation into drip. In the dimness, the brazier’s red glow feels like the pulse of some buried creature curled beneath the floor, patient and warm and endlessly awake.

The tools have a half-life as well.

They are not alive as the salamander is alive, nor as the hands are alive, but they are not dead either. They roost. They wait. They are placed with such care that they seem to be resting rather than stored. The bone trowel, held by the vine, has become a captive and a collaborator at once. The copper pins hum faintly when the humidity swells. The shell-and-stone counterweights click together like small beaks. The fish-spine needles seem to twitch when a seam needs closing. The ash-hammer heads hold the memory of blows and seem ready to continue them if a hand appears. The clay valves opened and closed with cord are like sleeping joints. The leather bellows breathe with the slow seriousness of organs. In this place, a tool is only one step away from being a limb.

The sounds live there too, though they have no bodies.

The water slapping into clay is a percussion. The hissing over hot tiles is a breath. The drums on stretched bark membranes are small heartbeats set at different distances. The reed chord is a cry that belongs to no throat. The muffled boiling beneath the floor is a stirring that suggests a patient cook, or a slow creature turning in sleep, or the pocket’s own interior being mixed into being. The copper strips click like the chatter of tiny metal birds. The leaves rasp. The air creaks. Sound is not decoration here. It is construction. The room is being tuned into existence. Nothing is silent because nothing has finished becoming.

And yet, for all these lives, the pocket is not crowded in the way a market is crowded. It is crowded in the way a womb is crowded: every presence touches another, every movement has consequence, every breath changes the whole. There is no distance. A step forward requires turning sideways. A breath brings leaves against the cheek. To stand there is to be included whether you wish to be or not. Your warmth enters the air. Your moisture joins the condensation. Your voice alters the drips. If you touch a wall, it hardens under your fingers, incorporating your contact. If you speak, the room listens structurally, and somewhere a new bead of clay forms as if your words had weight enough to be built from.

So if you ask finally who lives there, the answer is not one creature.

It is the pale salamander, slow and eyeless, carrying its pulse like a lamp.  
It is the wall-hands, flexing faintly, still finishing what they began.  
It is the white worms stitching wetness into form.  
It is the springtails leaping like sparks from damp fire.  
It is the eyeless centipede moving as a corrective moves.  
It is the fungi glowing in the seams, making the darkness gentle.  
It is the moss, ferns, orchids, reeds, and the vine that holds the trowel.  
It is the small cloud that enters, rains, thins, and returns.  
It is the water in all its states and moods.  
It is the heat breathing beneath the tiles.  
It is the tools waiting between uses.  
It is the sound that builds as it speaks.  

And beyond all these, it is the pocket itself: a hot, wet, artificial being no larger than a closet, compressed with more life than its dimensions should allow, still being born, still not settled, still deciding what it will be. It lives as an argument lives. It lives as a birth lives. It lives as a hand lives when it has not yet released the thing it is shaping.

It is not a place that has inhabitants.

It is a place that is inhabiting itself.
