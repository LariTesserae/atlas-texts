**What Lives in the Underleaf Works**

What lives in the Underleaf Works is not one thing, and not even a simple collection of things. It is a congregation of lives arranged around warmth, water, pressure, and appetite. Some of them move. Some of them grow. Some of them wait. Some of them only become visible when the lamps pulse, when the rain begins, or when the engines change their rhythm. To ask who lives there is to ask a question the place itself does not fully recognize. The Underleaf Works does not divide resident from room very cleanly.

The first thing that lives there is the chamber.

Not the walls alone, not the roots alone, not the machines alone, but the whole breathing arrangement: the root-ceiling, the felted floor, the leaf-bowls, the stem-pipes, the swollen engines, the resin lamps, the cisterns, the valves, the walkways, the ducts, the hanging lofts, and the slow pressure moving through all of it. The Works is a body with functions. It sweats. It drinks. It warms. It opens and closes. It makes light when its sap rises. It makes rain when its ceiling heats. It makes sound when air and pressure pass through hollow stems. It notices weight, warmth, moisture, and vibration, not with a mind exactly, but with a vast distributed attention. A handrail thickens under a grip. A floor mat softens where a body has stood too long. A flower turns toward breath. A root explores a vibration until it decides whether to grow around it, seal it, or withdraw from it.

In that sense, the Underleaf Works is not merely inhabited. It is an inhabitant.

It is the oldest resident, the largest resident, and the one most difficult to see as a single creature because it is so spread out. Its thoughts are hydraulic. Its moods are pressure systems. Its memory is stored in worn paths, healed pipe-scars, polished root columns, and the thickened places where repeated touch has taught the plant what must be strengthened. It does not speak, but it signals. It does not rule, but it permits. It allows passage where the paths are hollowed. It closes off spaces where damage has occurred. It answers certain sounds with other sounds. It is not benevolent, and it is not hostile. It is simply continuing.

Within that continuing body lives the animal weather.

The fauna of the Underleaf Works is so dense that it cannot be understood as a population scattered through a habitat. It is closer to climate. Life there gathers, thins, rises, settles, darkens lamps, fills ducts, coats walkways, and moves in tides governed by heat, moisture, hunger, and reproduction. Animals are not merely present in the Works; they are one of the ways the Works thinks about itself.

The midges come first to the eye, or rather to the air. They are so numerous that they stop behaving like individual insects and become gray veils, shifting curtains, soft columns that rise from the root-felt when the chamber warms. Around the lamps they form halos. Around the flower-throats they become a trembling mist. Their wingbeats are not one sound but many sounds layered until they press against the skin. When the air charges with static, the midges lift as if pulled by invisible strings. When a predator moves through them, they fold inward, scatter, then slowly knit themselves back together. They are the Works’ smallest common currency: food, motion, noise, and death all at once. Their lives are brief, and their bodies are never wasted. When they fall, the beetles find them. When they sink into the basins, the water-hunters take them. When they die in dry heaps near the lamps, mites and larvae reduce them to powder, and the powder becomes part of the floor.

The beetles are everywhere, but they are never random. They move like hardware given purpose. Some are black and hard-shelled, like rivets loosened from a panel, crawling in tight processions across bark walkways and root panels. They process waste. They carry fragments so small that one can see the line moving before one understands what is being carried. Others are broader, flatter, and slower, scraping old growth from damp surfaces, cleaning the undersides of leaf-troughs, or turning seed-cases in their racks so the heat reaches them evenly. Some beetles are dull and dusty, almost indistinguishable from bark; others shine with a dark resinous gloss, as if they have been dipped in the Works’ own sap and cured. Their presence is one of the reasons the place remains balanced. They are the patient laborers of decay and repair. Without them, the Underleaf Works would choke on its own shed matter.

The moths are larger, slower, and stranger in their dignity. Some are as broad as hands, with dusty wings and heavy bodies, moving through the warm air like pollination engines that have forgotten urgency. They are drawn to the glowing flower-throats, but not in the mindless way of surface insects around flame. Their attraction is more liturgical. They approach, hover, drink, dust themselves with pollen, and carry that pollen to other blooms. Their wings shed fine scales that sometimes catch the lamp-light and hang in the air like dim green snow. When many moths fly at once, they can block the lamps and throw the chamber into flickering shadow. When they gather at the nectar valves, their wings make a soft, wet percussion, like cloth breathing. They are not delicate. They are warm, blunt, and purposeful. In the Underleaf Works, even beauty is a kind of labor.

There are ants, or creatures so like ants that the word is useful even if it is not precise. They farm resin. They tend scale-insects along the inner bark. They patrol the edges of the nectar vats with nervous precision, antennae tapping, mandibles ready. Some of them build living fences from softened seed-fiber and dried honeydew. Some of them harvest spilled sap and carry it back in shining beads. Their colonies are not cities in the human sense, but they are organized enough to look like departments of the Works itself. When a valve leaks, they often arrive before any larger creature does, not out of intelligence but out of appetite. Still, their appetite serves the whole. They seal small wounds. They clear sweet obstructions. They keep the nectar economy moving.

In the high lofts, where the root-ceiling knots into darkness, there are larvae. Fat, pale, slow, and faintly luminous, they occupy the bark honeycombs and papery seed cells in such numbers that their chewing becomes audible whenever the chamber quiets. They are not disgusting. They are necessary. They process pulp, soften fiber, break down shed husks, and generate heat with their dense, patient metabolism. Some of them spin wax or silk used by other creatures to seal cracks and bind weak joints. Some of them are tended by beetles or ants, protected and moved when the local temperature changes. Their glow is soft and yellowish, more like fermentation than light. In the dim upper galleries, they make the lofts seem inhabited by small lanterns, each lantern busy with eating.

The root-crabs live along the walls and in the damp folds between root columns. Their shells are soft, not because they are young but because mineral armor would be foreign there. They move sideways with surprising certainty, front legs strong enough to grip root bark or scrape thin films of organic growth from wet surfaces. They are shy, but not timid in the way of prey that knows only flight. They pause, assess, and then decide whether to retreat into a knot or climb higher. When they molt, they hide deep in root hollows, vulnerable and pale, and the Works seems to ignore them, as if respecting an old schedule. They are cleaners and scavengers, but they are also food. Nothing in the chamber is allowed to be only one thing.

The cisterns have their own crowded life. Water arrives in pulses, but when it gathers in leaf-bowls, root-cups, and pitcher-shaped basins, it becomes intensely inhabited. Water-beetles row across the surface with quick, dark purpose. Caddis-like larvae build cases from fiber, seed fragments, and bits of shed bark, carrying their homes with them like mobile rooms. There are glass-bellied frogs or toad-shaped things, hard to name because the chamber does not encourage taxonomy, only recognition. Their calls are hollow, metallic, resonant. They bounce off the root walls and give the place a sense of measured time. When one frog answers another, the sound seems to organize the air. Their bellies are translucent enough that, in certain lights, one can see swallowed illumination moving inside them, as if they have eaten pieces of the lamps.

In the same cisterns live the hunters.

Predation in the Underleaf Works is constant, visible, and strangely formal. It does not feel cruel in the way surface violence feels cruel. It feels scheduled. Root-mantids wait on stems, shaped like thorns, utterly still until a midge cloud passes too near. Then they unfold with a speed that seems to tear the air, and one more small body is removed from the swarm. Robber beetles patrol the walkways, armored and quick, taking weakened insects, dead ones, and those unlucky enough to be slow during a pressure change. In the water, hunters lurk just below the surface film. They are not large, but they are efficient. A struggling insect breaks the glow of a cistern, rings spread outward, and then the surface smooths again. The event is over. The chamber continues.

The plants participate in predation. Some blossoms are lamps and valves, but others are traps. They smell sweet, open slowly, and close with a patience that makes the violence almost invisible. Small bodies are held in warm fluid and digested while the flower continues to glow. Some vines retract when touched by the wrong weight, withdrawing not in fear but in selection. Some leaves curl inward, sheltering nursery colonies or enclosing prey depending on the chemistry of the contact. Nothing in the Underleaf Works is only decorative. Even a flower’s beauty is a mechanism.

Then come the cleaners.

They are the quiet majority, though most of them are too small to see clearly. Mites work the polished root-felt in pale waves. Bark-lice strip old films from surfaces. Tiny worms inhabit the wettest channels, breaking down pulp and dead matter before it can sour. There are unseen fermenters in the floor, heat-makers in the compressed root-mat, microscopic lives whose labor is felt as warmth, smell, and slow chemical change. They clean the lamps, too. Certain small creatures feed on the dull film that gathers over resin bladders and seed-case bulbs, leaving the light clearer after they pass. Death in the Underleaf Works is not allowed to accumulate. A shed skin, an empty egg case, a broken wing, a dead midge, a crushed beetle—all are found quickly. They are eaten, carried, dissolved, or absorbed. The place is loud partly because life is dense, but it is clean because death is mechanical.

If one asks who lives there, the answer must also include those who might be called people, though the word fits them only after being softened, widened, and made less certain.

They are the Underleaf Kin.

They are few. They have to be few. The chamber could not support many large-bodied residents without throwing its water, its swarms, and its delicate heat balance into distress. The Kin live in the intervals: in bark honeycombs, root lofts, hollows behind pipe lattices, and suspended platforms woven from living stems. They do not build houses so much as occupy permitted spaces. Their homes are grown, shaped, maintained, and eventually reabsorbed. When a dwelling becomes too heavy, too damp, or too close to a valve that needs room, they move it or let it be composted into the architecture.

They are small, warm, and quiet. Their bodies have adapted to the chamber over a long time, or perhaps the chamber adapted them; in the Underleaf Works, cause and effect are braided like roots. They are light-boned and sure-footed on the yielding floor. Their skin is damp and warm, often marked with faint stains of resin, pollen, or luminescent dust. Their eyes are large, suited to low amber and green light. Their ears are sensitive not only to sound but to pressure. They can feel a coming rain-cycle in the floor before the ceiling begins to sweat. They can tell from the thump of a sap engine whether the rhythm is healthy or beginning to strain.

They do not resemble surface rulers. They do not own the Works. They are not priests, though their actions often look like ritual. They are closer to organ-tenders. They listen, adjust, wait, and repair. They know which pipe-note means the nectar valves should open. They know which frog-calls indicate the cisterns are nearing fullness. They know which flicker in the resin lamps means pressure is rising too fast. They move through the chamber with a patience learned from the place itself. They step around beetle processions. They duck beneath moth flights. They stand still when a swarm is deciding where to go. They do not rush unless a fault demands it, and even then their urgency is precise.

Their tools are grown rather than mined. They use hollow reeds as probes and wrenches, seed-case containers, leaf-leather bindings, sap adhesives, tensioned grass belts, and living fasteners that tighten in response to warmth. Metal is rare, mostly ornamental, and often suspicious. If they use anything metallic at all, it is in tiny fragments worked into ornaments or resonant parts, not as the substance of daily labor. Their light comes from cultivated organisms: seed-case bulbs, resin bladders, pale leaf-lamps, and carefully maintained flower-throats. They do not carry fire. The thought of carrying fire is almost a form of madness to them. Fire is not a tool in the Underleaf Works. Fire is the wound that does not close.

Their language reflects this. It is soft, full of clicks, breath-tones, taps on hollow stems, and hand-shapes meant to be read in dim light. They speak loudly only in emergencies, and even then they prefer pipe-signals because the chamber carries them better. Their vocabulary is rich in states of becoming: to wet, to warm, to swell, to leak, to thicken, to ripen, to thin, to clog, to heal, to darken, to quicken, to quiet. They have many words for water timing and almost none for ownership. They do not say, “This pool is mine.” They say, “This pool is coming,” or “This pool has been drunk,” or “This pool will return.”

They eat carefully. They harvest nectar, condensed sap, edible pulp, pollen cakes, cistern growths, and certain insect larvae, but only where the system can bear the loss. Much of their food is fermented, dried in warm air ducts, or cured in sap. They do not cook with flame. Their kitchens are low-walled fermentation hollows and seed-pod stores where heat is applied slowly through living channels. They chew resin to dull thirst during dry intervals. They share meals in small groups, often while listening to the engines, because eating without attending to the Works feels careless to them.

Their work is organized by function, not rank. When a pipe sings a fault-note, whoever is nearest and trained to answer becomes the leader of the mending until the fault is resolved. There are Valve-listeners who press their palms and cheeks to living stems and read pressure through vibration. There are Rain-counters who track the ceiling’s sweating cycles and warn the terraces when a heavy pulse is coming. There are Lamp-wrights who tend resin bladders, clear dimmed surfaces, and coax bioluminescent blooms back into rhythm. There are Swarm-turners who use scent, heat, and sound to guide midge clouds away from critical machinery. There are Husk-gatherers who follow beetle processions and collect what can be reused. There are Menders who weave new roots into sagging walkways and seal split pipes with living callus-stuff. There are also those who do the hardest task: they decide when something must be allowed to fail so that something else may continue.

Their relationship with the fauna is intimate and unsentimental. They know that the moths must feed, that the beetles must process, that the predators must kill, and that the larvae must chew. They do not try to make the chamber gentle. They try to keep it continuous. A Kin child learns early to stand still while root-crabs cross near their feet, to breathe shallowly so as not to scatter a pollination flight, to step over a beetle seam without breaking it, and to recognize the sound of a trap-bloom closing. Children are taught that fear is useful but panic is expensive. Panic wastes heat. Panic disturbs swarms. Panic can make a small fault into a large one.

They also have customs for death, though they do not build tombs. In a place without mineral permanence, burial in the surface sense would be a foreign act. When one of the Kin dies, the body is washed with condensed water, wrapped in leaf-leather, and carried to a root basin prepared for such return. The roots come slowly, then quickly. The chamber absorbs what was warm. Small lives arrive to do their work. The Kin do not pretend this is tragedy-free, but they understand it as part of the same metabolism that fed the person while living. They keep small tokens instead of monuments: a polished seed-case, a knot of root that grew around a loved one’s resting place, a beetle-shell ornament, a pipe-song associated with a name. Their grief is practical. It is carried in maintenance. They repair what the dead once tended.

Their deepest taboo is fire.

They fear it more than they fear predators, more than they fear hunger, more than they fear the collapse of a walkway or the failure of a cistern. Fire in the Underleaf Works would not be a localized disaster. It would be a spreading unmaking. The air is humid, yes, but the place is also full of resin, dried fiber, sap pressure, oxygen-rich pockets, and living tissue that has never been taught to survive flame. A spark could ignite a lamp bladder. A lamp bladder could ignite a vine-wire coating. A vine-wire coating could carry heat into a seed-tank. A seed-tank could rupture. The whole chamber might become a wound of light and heat, destroying not just bodies but the ancient agreement between growth and wear. The Kin teach their children that fire is not power. Fire is forgetting.

Because of this, their technology has developed along other lines. They cultivate electricity rather than combustion. They encourage static buildup in pollen-rich air. They maintain coppery vines that carry small charges. They use resin capacitors to store bioelectric potential, releasing it in controlled sparks to stimulate valves, signal across distances, or coax dormant organs into activity. They know the dangers of charge as well. When the air prickles and pollen lifts, they move carefully. They avoid standing near antennae hairs and exposed seed-case bulbs. They know that a spark in the wrong place can become the thing they fear most. Their mastery is not domination; it is timing.

They are not the only intelligent presence, however one defines intelligence.

The Works itself has something like intention. Not human intention. Not a face, not a voice, not a desire that could be translated into words. But it chooses. It reinforces where reinforcement is needed. It abandons what cannot be sustained. It opens passages for certain creatures and closes them for others. It rewards maintenance with stability. It punishes imbalance by making itself difficult to live in: lamps dim, rain falls wrongly, cisterns sour, predators overfeed, engines labor. The Kin understand that they are not the masters of this place. They are its most self-aware symbiotes.

Outsiders would find the Underleaf Works overwhelming. A surface person entering the chamber would first be struck by the heat and the smell: green, sweet, sour, resinous, animal, alive. Then by the sound, which is not background but architecture. Then by the impossibility of standing anywhere without becoming part of the system’s calculations. Your breath would attract flowers. Your heat would draw midges. Your weight would soften the floor. Your movement would alter beetle routes. If you carried smoke on your clothes, the Kin would recoil as if you had brought a weapon. If you spoke too loudly, the sound would bounce through root walls and disturb frog-signals. If you reached for a handrail without knowing it was alive, you would feel it test you back.

The Works does not hate outsiders. It simply has no space for carelessness.

What lives there, then, is not a list but a relationship.

The chamber lives. The root-ceiling lives. The leaf-lamps live. The engines live in their slow, muscular way. The pipes live when pressure passes through them. The cisterns live when water arrives. The midges live as weather. The beetles live as maintenance. The moths live as pollination and shadow. The ants live as resin-herders and leak-sealers. The larvae live as heat and digestion. The root-crabs live as wall-walkers and scrapers. The frogs live as sound and hunger. The predators live as balance. The cleaners live as disappearance. The Kin live as attention, repair, and taboo.

No one there is innocent. No one there is unnecessary.

The Underleaf Works produces life the way it produces rain: by pressure, warmth, recurrence, and need. It does not merely contain animals. It makes them necessary. It does not merely shelter people, if the Kin can still be called people. It shapes them into the kind of beings who can hear it. The place asks for no worship. It does not demand understanding. It asks only that its residents move with its rhythms, take water when water comes, mend what weakens, eat what is permitted, return what is finished, and never, under any circumstance, bring fire into the dark green breathing room of the world.
