At noon, if the surface wind is thin, the Court of Small Rains lives in layers.

Light comes down in pale blades through the shafts, and those blades reveal not one inhabitant but many: wings in the upper dark, feet on the middle galleries, hands on ropes and valves, eyes in the alcoves, small lives moving along the floor, slower lives working invisibly inside the walls. The court is crowded not because every ledge is packed, but because every life there has a task, a sound, a place, and a claim. Nothing merely occurs. Everything is kept.

The people who live there are the first answer, though they are not the whole answer.

They are the Court-born, or so they call themselves in their own clipped, carrying speech, though the name is less a boast than a statement of maintenance. They are born into echo. They are born into damp air, bird cries, warm plaster, and the sound of water deciding where to go. Their first lesson is not to walk quietly but to answer when called. In the court, silence is not peace. Silence is a crack somewhere. Silence is a shutter stuck, a child ill, a drain blocked, a rope about to fail. So their voices are trained early: not loud, never wasted, but clear. They speak in calls that bounce cleanly off tile, in clicks that test hollows, in short repeated phrases that can be passed from gallery to gallery without becoming noise.

They are small in number compared with the birds, but large in presence. A dozen people can fill the court with intention. Their bodies are marked by the place: lime-dust in the creases of their fingers, sun-brown shoulders where the shafts touch them at certain hours, feet made sure by wet sherds and rounded tile. They walk as if the floor were a sleeping animal they do not wish to startle. Their hands are never entirely empty. Even when resting, they are likely to be rubbing a smooth patch of plaster, loosening a knot, feeling a rope for rot, or holding a shard up to hear whether it rings soundly.

Their clothing is practical and endlessly repaired: soft tunics, wrapped skirts, trousers patched with reed-cloth or old sail-fiber, sashes with loops for tools. They carry pouches of lime dust, shell scrapers, bone needles, little clay whistles, and twist-ties made from sinew or grass. Copper is worn not as ornament but as memory: a pin in a collar, a staple on a belt, a flattened hook hanging at the hip. Metal is old there, old and beloved, because it has survived enough to be useful. A copper clasp may have been a fishhook, then a net-needle, then a hinge, then a child’s whistle, then a clasp again. To wear such a thing is to carry a chain of repairs.

Their homes are not grand. They are alcoves raised above the damp, closed with woven mats that smell of reeds, smoke, and feathers. Inside, the floor is smooth and slightly springy, layered with packed ash, lime, and old rush mats. Sleeping ledges are shallow, shelves are built into the wall, and every household keeps at least one listening jar: a vessel half-buried in plaster, used to hear weather moving in the upper channels or to tell whether the basin is rising too fast. A home in the court is not a place to escape the room. It is a place to hear the room more carefully.

They live by duty as much as by blood.

Some are shutter-keepers, who climb at dawn to raise the wooden vanes and read the light. Some are gutter-walkers, who scrape mineral crust from channels and clear dead moths from the duck-billed spouts. Some are egg-keepers, who know which nests may be touched and which must be left, and who mark counted clutches with tiny dabs of chalk. Some are net-menders, some rope-turners, some fungus-tenders, some beetle-stewards, some water-tasters. The titles are not ornate. They are spoken the way another place might say “baker” or “herdsman,” though the work is stranger.

There are families, but the court blurs them. A child may sleep in one alcove and spend most of the day in another, learning a craft or following an elder whose voice has gone thin but whose ear remains exact. Parentage is known, yet care is distributed. If a woman is hauling wet reeds, another person may carry her bucket the last few steps. If a child falls from a low ladder, three hands reach before the body lands. If someone is ill, the court knows by the change in sound: a missing answer, a late step, a cough that belongs in the wrong gallery. Illness is not private. It becomes an acoustic fact.

The children are especially vivid, because they are the court’s future ears.

They are small, quick, lime-dusted, and fearless in a careful way. They learn to balance on railings before they learn to read marks on clay. They play games that are secretly training: knocking on jars to find cracks, throwing pebbles into the basin and guessing the water level by the echo, racing along the ramps while imitating bird calls, catching drips in cups without spilling. They wear little pouches around their necks containing a piece of chalk, a smooth seed, and a shard from their first broken pot. The shard is not a punishment. It is a reminder that breaking is not the end of a thing’s usefulness.

A child in the Court of Small Rains learns early that every surface has a claim. You may not lean idly against a wall if your warmth will loosen moss. You may not kick a loose tile because the tile may become a patch tomorrow. You may not shout near the roosting niches during the high heat, because panicked birds bruise their wings. You may not take the last drop from a pan without asking whether the frogs have need of it. These rules are not taught as commandments so much as rhythms. They are repeated until they feel like balance.

The elders are the court’s memory, and some of them seem half-made of sound themselves.

They sit in the warmer recesses where the lamps are kept, mending nets or shaping reed ties, their eyes often half-closed. They remember when a certain beam was set, when a particular channel was cut, when a storm came so sudden that the basin rose to a hand’s height above its normal line. Some become listeners more than workers. They press their ears to pipes and jars and can say, “There is a hair-crack in the west gallery,” or “The upper cistern is breathing wrong,” or “Too many swifts roosted low last night; damp is coming.” Their authority is quiet but absolute. If an elder says, “Open the third flute,” people climb to open it. If an elder says, “That jar no longer sings,” it is taken down and made into something else.

But the people are not alone. They are only the most articulate of the court’s inhabitants.

Above them live the birds.

The swallows and swifts are the court’s visible sky. They fill the upper galleries with nests, cries, and the sharp smell of warm feathers. Their homes are built into the architecture: ceramic niches, rows of open jars, shallow boxes of tile and reed, plastered lips beneath eaves of salvaged wood. The nests are not accidental. They are encouraged, repaired, sometimes moved, always counted. A nest is a small engine of life: it produces feathers, guano, sound, hunger, and eventually new wings. It is also a sign of trust. Birds return because the court has become dependable in its strangeness.

At dawn, the birds leave in bursts. The shafts become rivers of wings, and the court seems suddenly too large and too quiet. Then the air is full of absence, and the people move more freely through the upper galleries to scrape, mend, and collect. Eggs are taken carefully, never too many from one nest. Feathers are gathered for fletching, insulation, and signal bundles. Guano is scraped into pans and carried down to feed fungal beds or mixed into mortar where old recipes require it. Nothing is taken greedily. The court’s abundance depends on restraint.

At dusk the birds return, and the court becomes almost unbearably alive. The shafts darken with wings. Cries multiply until they are less individual sounds than a single bright pressure against the walls. People move quickly then, adjusting nets, closing fragile panels, calling warnings to one another in short notes. The smell of feathers and warm dust fills the room. A swallow may brush a person’s hair; a swift may strike a beam and tumble briefly before righting itself. No one romanticizes this. They know the birds are wild, but they also know wildness can be housed if it is not insulted.

Doves live lower and warmer, near lamp shelves and dry recesses. They are softer presences, rounder in sound, slower in movement. They are kept partly for eggs, partly for their calm, partly because their cooing tells the keepers that the air in a certain corner is stable. A dove shelf that suddenly goes silent may mean a snake has found a way in, or a draft has turned cold. The doves are loved in a practical way. Children stroke them when permitted, but only after checking whether the birds are nesting.

Bats belong to the upper dark.

They hang in the highest shadows, in the crown of the sideways cathedral, where the air is warmer and the light rarely reaches. At dusk they leave through the shafts in soft, hurried streams, and at night their absence is felt as a faint emptiness overhead. They return before full dark, folding themselves back into the black with tiny dry sounds. The people do not fuss over them, but they do not disturb them either. Bats are the court’s night hunters, taking moths and biting flies that would otherwise gather near lamps and food stores. In some seasons, bat guano is carefully harvested from special catchment boards; in others, it is left to accumulate because the colony is weak. The rules change according to need.

Moths live near flame and pale light. They are not cherished, exactly, but they are understood. They are food, warning, and weather. When moths cluster thick around a lamp, someone checks whether the reeds are too damp. When they vanish, the frogs grow restless. When their wings beat against clay jars in quick dry taps, children learn to imitate the sound on their fingers.

The basin has its own residents.

Frogs live along its edge, in the wet dark where tile meets lime and the floor sweats. They blink slowly, their throats pulsing, their bodies bright or mottled depending on the line. They are among the court’s most trusted prophets. Before rain, they gather and call, and their calls are not ignored. If the frogs sound from the southern pocket, the keepers open the southern channels. If they go silent while the air is damp, something is wrong with the water itself: too much salt, too much rot, a dead fish in a hidden hollow. Children learn not to catch them for play. A frog is not a toy. It is a listener that answers.

In the deeper pockets of water live the fish, eels, and pale crustaceans brought long ago and now treated as residents rather than possessions. The fish are small, quick, silver-bellied, rising when droplets disturb the surface. They eat larvae, scraps, and the tiny lives that hatch in wet plaster. They are not often eaten, except in lean seasons and under strict rules. To eat a basin fish is to admit that the court has narrowed. Better to let them live and work.

The eels are stranger and more respected. They slip between stones and submerged sherds, appearing only when they choose. Some are said to be very old, though no one can prove it. The keepers know certain ones by the way they move or by old scars: a crooked jaw, a missing fin tip, a copper-colored streak along the flank. Children whisper that the oldest eel remembers the first rain the court ever drank. The elders neither confirm nor deny this, but they do say, “Do not put your hand where an eel has chosen to rest.” That is not superstition. It is respect for a creature that has found a place in the machinery.

The crustaceans are pale and patient, living in the coolest hollows. They clean what sinks. They are seldom seen, but their work is visible: fewer foul smells, clearer pockets, fewer blockages in the lower drains. When they appear in numbers near the basin’s lip, the water-tasters grow thoughtful. Something is shifting below.

On the floor and along the wall bases live the beetles.

They are the court’s quiet clergy. They move through dung, feathers, spilled grain, dead insects, and old nesting material, turning waste into smaller forms that can be carried, composted, or worked into plaster. Some are dark and heavy-shelled; others are quick and rust-colored. Children sometimes race them, but rarely touch them. Beetles are useful in a way that demands dignity. If beetles vanish from a corner, that corner begins to fail. If they gather too thickly near food stores, they are redirected with brush and pan, not crushed. Their lives are small, but they are part of the court’s digestion.

Behind the walls live the rats.

No one pretends otherwise. The Court of Small Rains has rats, as it has ropes, damp, and echoes. They move through hidden runs, behind reed panels, under old amphorae, along passages made by centuries of repair and re-repair. They are clever, hungry, and dangerous when allowed to multiply too freely. They gnaw nets, steal eggs, foul dry stores, and undermine plaster. Yet they are also food for the hunters that keep them in check, and their presence tests the court’s vigilance. A court without rats would be a court with no secrets; a court overwhelmed by rats would be a court that has stopped listening.

So there are hunters.

Weasels move in the narrow places, sleek and sharp-eyed. Snakes appear in certain seasons, slipping along ledges after eggs or young rats, and the keepers decide case by case whether to drive them out or let them work. Some snakes are tolerated near the grain shelves; others are removed from the roosting galleries. There are also ferrets, trained or half-trained, wearing little collars of soft fiber and sometimes a tiny copper bell. The bells are not for charm. They tell the hunters where their small allies have gone. A ferret returning with a rat is praised in a low voice and given a scrap of fish. A ferret that lingers too long behind the egg shelves is called back with a sharp clapper sound. The ecology is not gentle. It is governed.

Even the green lives have their place, though the prompt might call them less alive than the rest. The court disagrees.

Moss is kept like a slow animal. It holds moisture along the mortar lines and tells the keepers where damp is settling. Liverworts grow in the shadow of drip channels and are harvested sparingly for poultices and packing. Rushes are cultivated in troughs of silt, their roots binding the floor, their stems becoming mats, ties, filters, and baskets. Salt-tolerant grass is allowed on ledges where condensation gathers, because its roots stabilize surfaces that would otherwise crumble. The vine in the light shaft is guided by loops tied by human hands, and every leaf is known. If the vine yellows, someone asks whether the shaft is drawing too much cold air. If it flowers, children are forbidden to pick the blossoms, because the vine’s purpose is not beauty alone.

Fungi are perhaps the most carefully misunderstood of all the court’s residents. They spread in darker alcoves in pale mats, soft shelves, and fine white threads. They are not weeds. They are workers. They break down waste, soften old reeds, prepare material for mortar, and sometimes produce edible caps in careful seasons. But fungi can also devour timber if left unwatched. So there are fungus-tenders, usually patient people with cool hands and good noses. They prune, dry, transplant, and taste. They know which mats smell healthy, which smell sour, and which should be smothered with ash before they spread too far. In the court, even decay must be assigned a role.

All these lives are braided by sound.

The inhabitants live inside an instrument, and they know it. The walls amplify, swallow, bend, and carry. A dropped shell scraper can ring from floor to ceiling. A whisper through a clay pipe can reach a gallery that should be out of earshot. A bucket knocked once against a beam means one thing; knocked twice, another. Wooden clappers mark the opening of water gates. A low hum from a child testing a jar can reveal a crack no eye has seen. The people do not merely make sound; they inhabit it. They trust it. They fear its absence.

Their language has grown accordingly. There are calls for ordinary speech, calls for warning, calls for work, and calls that are almost songs. Work chants keep rhythm when many hands are hauling reeds or turning a stubborn valve. Lullabies imitate dripping water, teaching infants the court’s pulse before they can speak. There are even insult sounds: a flat slap on wet clay meaning “You have blocked a drain,” or a short double-click meaning “You spoke when you should have listened.” Humor exists, but it is compact. A joke must be able to travel across a courtyard without causing panic.

Their rules are as lived as their speech.

No surface may remain unclaimed. If a crack appears, it is patched. If a patch fails, the failure becomes part of the next patch. If a pot breaks, its shards are sorted: large pieces for flooring, curved pieces for nest supports, sharp pieces for scraping, small pieces for mosaic. Nothing is thrown away in the ordinary sense. To throw away would require an outside, and the court’s logic has little room for outside. Waste moves upward or downward, but never into stillness. Guano becomes mortar or garden feed. Feathers become insulation or signal bundles. Dead insects become beetle work. Old rope becomes packing, then ash, then lime mix. Even spilled water is not simply lost; it darkens a joint, feeds a moss line, or warns of a slope gone wrong.

Water belongs first to the basin. Then to the jars. Then to the gardens, if gardens can be called the narrow green concessions they are. Then to washing. No one fouls the central pool. No one lets salt stores sit where condensation can ruin them. No one takes more than a share during the days after rain, because abundance is temporary and memory is long. If a family draws too much, they are not punished with cruelty. They are reminded with sound: a slow clapper pattern that means “The basin remembers.” Shame in the court is public, but it is also repairable. One can clean a gutter, mend a net, or carry water for an elder to restore balance.

The court’s strangeness is not hidden. It is simply worn smooth.

It is strange that sky-animals live below the dry ground. It is strange that rain is choreographed, that damp is farmed from air, that a room can be read by echo better than by sight. It is strange that the jars hum when wind crosses their mouths, that the keepers can tell the state of a cistern by throwing a pebble, that a child can turn a clay valve and change the path of a storm. But no one there speaks of these things as marvels. Marvels are for outsiders who come down the ramp with wide eyes and uncertain feet. To the Court-born, marvel is just maintenance delayed. If the jars hum, you listen. If the valve sticks, you grease it. If the rain comes, you move the salt.

At noon, if the surface wind is steady, the court breathes in long soft drafts. The hanging gourds knock gently. The ropes creak. The lamps burn low in the day, but their niches still smell of oil. People work in the middle light: scraping gutters, checking eggs, weaving reed mats, rubbing resin into cracked wood, teaching children how to tie a knot that will hold when wet. Birds wheel above the shafts, unseen but audible as they hunt beyond the dry terrace. The basin glimmers faintly. Frogs blink. Beetles patrol. A ferret sleeps in a hammock of old netting, one paw twitching. An old woman presses her ear to a jar and frowns, because the sound inside is too hollow. Someone else climbs to see what has shifted.

At noon, if the surface wind fails, the court grows warmer and closer. Sweat appears on glazed tiles. The smell of guano sharpens. The frogs begin their early questions. People move more slowly, conserving effort, listening harder. The air is not foul, but it is thick. It holds. When the flues breathe less, every sound becomes more bodily: wingbeats, footsteps, the wet click of a frog’s mouth, the small hiss of damp plaster accepting heat. The keepers open extra shutters and draw screens to encourage what little air there is. They speak less. The room listens for weather.

At dusk, the court becomes crowded with return.

The birds pour back through the shafts, and for a time the air is so full of wings that the lamps seem to flicker with shadows. People call to one another in quick notes, guiding nets away from fragile panels, closing egg shelves that must not be disturbed. Predators wake. An owl may drop through a shaft, silent and terrible, taking a bird in a burst of feathers and alarm. When that happens, the court does not pretend grief is absent. The keepers may curse softly, but they do not drive the owl away if it leaves quickly. The owl is also part of the balance. A snake may slip along a ledge toward eggs; a child may be sent with a stick to make noise, not to kill, unless the snake has become too bold. Life presses against life. The court’s gentleness is not softness. It is the decision to keep many lives from devouring one another entirely.

Rain changes everything.

Before rain, the court hears it: a duller echo, a change in the flues, a smell of wet dust from above. The keepers move with sudden economy. Clay valves turn. Woven screens are drawn. Salt, eggs, feathers, and dry stores rise above the damp line. Children are given small tasks to keep them from becoming fearful: hold this rope, count these drips, tell me when the basin’s voice changes. The first drops strike the floor like thrown seeds. Then the walls begin to run. Water slides down broad channels, spills from duck-billed spouts, gathers in pans, finds the basin. The sound becomes enormous, not because the court is large, but because it is built to answer. Every surface has a note. Every pipe has a throat.

In rain, the inhabitants become one creature with many hands. People shout in clipped calls, not in panic but in coordination. Frogs chorus. Fish move into new pockets. Beetles climb. Bats press deeper into the dark. Birds hush in the niches until the worst passes. The air fills with the smell of wet clay, copper, feathers, and lime. If the rain is small, it is folded quickly into use. If it is great, the court becomes a place of urgent joy and careful fear, because abundance can drown as easily as drought can starve.

After rain, the court steams.

Moisture beads on tile, copper, shell, and skin. Moss swells green-black along the joints. Reeds lift. Fungal edges soften. The basin is wider for a little while, dark and trembling with reflected light. People taste the water, measure it, argue over it, laugh over it. Children press their faces near the pans until they are told not to foul them. The animals drink and bathe and fight over the best wet ledges.
