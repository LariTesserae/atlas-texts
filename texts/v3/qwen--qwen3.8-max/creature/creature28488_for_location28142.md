Keep your soles soft, because the ground there is listening.

What lives in the Drip-Room is not one thing, nor even one kind of thing. It is a congregation of damp lives, a city held together by moisture, silk, patience, and restraint. If you ask who dwells there, the first answer is: the people. But that is only the outermost skin of the truth. Beneath them, within them, around them, above them, in the seams and cisterns and mossy mouths of the walls, there are many other inhabitants. Some were invited. Some were discovered. Some arrived as spores or eggs on fog. Some seem to have been produced by the basin itself, as if the young rock, still uncertain what it wanted to become, decided to try becoming alive in several ways at once.

The people who live there are few, and they are quiet in the way that people become quiet when they have spent their lives in a place that punishes noise by smothering it. They are highlanders, or descended from highlanders, but not in the hard, wind-burned manner of open ridge folk. They are shaded people. Their bodies have learned the basin’s economy: they move without waste, speak without volume, breathe without sharpness. They have wide eyes suited to the green-gray twilight, pupils quick to open in the deeper niches where the only light is the cold glow of fungal threads. Their hands are always a little cool, their fingertips roughened by moss, rope, stone dust, and larval silk, yet exquisitely gentle. In the Drip-Room, rough hands can be trusted. Clumsy hands cannot.

They dress for damp and silence. Their boots are layered things: woven grass beneath, felted moth hair within, sometimes a thin outer sole made from cured fungal skin or softened bark. The soles are replaceable, and replacing them is a constant small task. A worker with worn soles becomes a maker of unwanted sound. Their cloaks are hooded and heavy with use, but not heavy in weight; they are more like living garments, often furred with moisture or dusted with spores. In dry periods they are kept in sealed baskets so they do not steal water from the air. In wet periods they hang from hooks outside shelters, beading with condensation, tended like small animals. Under the cloaks they wear close layers of woven fiber, dyed in muted greens, grays, and ash-blues. Nothing bright. Brightness would be a kind of shouting.

Their faces often seem pale from the lack of direct sun, but not bloodless. There is a cool bloom to their skin, as if they carry the basin’s damp in their pores. They may mark themselves not with paint but with moisture: a touch of cistern clay on the wrist, a smear of luminescent algae on a hood, a knot of pale larval silk worn at the throat. These are not ornaments in the lowland sense. They are signs of duty, of recent work, of which part of the basin has claimed their attention. A fog-reader may have droplets caught in fine threads around the hood. A cistern-tender may smell of clean mineral water and old stone. A silk-keeper may have fingers faintly glazed from handling freshly drawn thread. A stone-listener may carry dust in the creases of their ears.

They are not numerous enough to fill the basin with human sound, and this is deliberate. The Drip-Room has more rooms than occupants, more stairs than feet, more cisterns than hands. The settlement feels built for many, but inhabited by a careful few. Some spaces are maintained without being used. Some towers are visited only when larvae need tending. Some terraces are swept not because they will be walked upon, but because dust there would disturb the balance of moss below. The people understand themselves as tenders of a place larger than their own immediate needs. They do not live in every room. They keep every room alive.

Their work is specialized, but not separated by pride. A fog-reader may also mend rope. A moss-gardener may also clean cistern filters. Still, certain callings are unmistakable. Fog-readers spend long hours near the eastern cleft, where the breath of the highlands enters. They watch the way threads of vapor bend against the rock, how moss swells, how certain beetles lift their antennae before moisture arrives. Some of them keep little frames strung with fine fibers, not unlike webs, and read the beading of condensation on them. They can tell whether fog will be thin and passing or thick and long by the way droplets gather on the third strand, the fifth strand, the lowest strand. They do not announce their readings loudly. They pass the information by hand signs: a closed fist drawn upward for rising fog, a flat palm tilted for condensation likely, two fingers touching the lips for “cisterns may gain,” one finger brushing the ear for “listen to the walls.”

Stone-listeners are stranger to outsiders. They press their ears to bound rock, to silked joints, to the sides of towers, and wait. They are listening for hollowness, for looseness, for the tiny internal shifts that mean a terrace may shed dust or a cistern wall may need reinforcement. They can also hear the mason-larvae. This is considered a practical skill, but it has the atmosphere of priestcraft. A stone-listener will sometimes remain motionless for a long time, cheek against cold stone, eyes closed, one hand resting nearby as if soothing the wall. When they speak of what they have heard, they say things like, “The seam below the third shelf is hungry,” or “There is knitting in the north tower,” or “Something has dropped inside the old cistern and the larvae are deciding whether to eat it or cover it.” They rarely speak such words aloud unless necessary. More often they sign them.

Silk-keepers are the ones who most directly mediate between the people and the mason-larvae. They harvest fiber without causing alarm. They know how to approach a working seam so the larvae do not mistake their presence for damage. They carry bone spatulas, smooth needles, little clay pots of mineral paste, and pouches of dried moss dust. Sometimes they place their hands flat near a larval reef and wait until the creatures accept the warmth of them. Sometimes they remove damaged silk with such delicacy that the larvae continue working around their fingers. Their own movements become slow to the point of ritual. An outsider might think them idle, standing with one hand resting on a tower for half an hour. In truth, they are negotiating. They are saying, without sound: I am not a wound. I am not a predator. I am part of the maintenance.

Cistern-tenders live closest to the basin’s thirst. They know the water tanks not as passive vessels but as living stomachs. Each cistern has a temperament. Some are cool and patient, accepting slow increases without changing taste. Some are eager and overgrow with algae if left untended. Some house filter-feeders that become sluggish in cold spells. Some contain amphibians whose skin secretions must be balanced so the water does not turn bitter. The tenders carry small clay lamps containing bioluminescent algae, enough to cast a dim blue-green glow over the surface without disturbing the dark too much. They lower nets of woven fiber, skim shed skins, remove dead insects, add certain larvae to consume excess growth, and sometimes introduce a new stone or plant fragment to alter a tank’s balance. They do not drink from a cistern without first touching its lip, listening, and checking the behavior of its creatures. Water, in the Drip-Room, is never merely water. It is a relationship.

Moss-gardeners are perhaps the most patient of all. They move from wall to wall with small baskets of living green, transplanting moss where it is needed, thinning it where it drinks too greedily, encouraging it where it is too shy. They know which mosses are suited to catch fog, which to hold rain, which to cushion stairways, which to silence joints between stones. They know which species grow too fast and smother others, which glow faintly when disturbed, which attract certain beetles, which repel certain mites. Their work is invisible at first. A patch moved today may not show its success until a season later, when a wall wakes wetter or a channel no longer sheds dust. They often kneel for long periods, palms pressed to moss as if checking for fever. In a place where moisture is labor, moss is both tool and colleague.

Children in the Drip-Room are taught restraint before they are taught most other things. Their games are quiet. They count drips. They weave tiny nets from shed moth fiber. They practice walking across gravel without making more than a certain number of sounds. They learn to read the cleft by watching adults, not by being lectured. They are given small jars with larvae inside and taught to keep the larvae calm, to offer the right dampness, to notice when silk becomes brittle or when the creature prepares to molt. A child who can keep a mason-larva content has learned much of what the basin requires. They are not forbidden to laugh or cry—such things cannot be forbidden without cruelty—but they learn early that strong feeling must be given shape. A child’s grief may be placed into a folded piece of felt and left near a cistern. A child’s excitement may be spent in weaving, in running messages, in helping to cover stores before a sporefall. Their voices remain soft not because they are silenced, but because the place teaches them that softness carries farther.

The elderly in the Drip-Room do not fade into uselessness. They become reservoirs. When hands grow stiff, they teach. When eyes grow dim, they listen. Some sit in sheltered niches where they can watch the fog enter and note changes that younger workers miss because they have seen the basin through many seasons. Some are no longer able to climb terraces, but they can sort seeds, mend nets, or sit beside nursery jars and keep the temperature of the dark rooms steady by opening or closing clay vents. Old age there is not a decline from labor; it is a refinement of it. The basin values memory. The rock is young, but the practices are old, and the old carry them.

Yet for all the presence of these people, the most numerous true inhabitants of the Drip-Room are not human.

The mason-larvae live there in every meaningful sense. They live in the walls, in the towers, in the seams, in the undersides of ledges, in the hollow cores of certain shrines, in the dark rooms where eggs are kept, in the netted scaffolds where molting occurs, in the very binding of the city. They are soft, pale, thick-bodied creatures with blunt heads and a slow, deliberate way of moving that suggests thought rather than instinct. They are not beautiful in the way lowland people usually mean beauty. They are moist, wrinkled, sometimes dusty with mineral powder, sometimes glossy from fresh secretion. Their bodies have the look of things that have not yet decided whether they are grubs, worms, or something else entirely. But their work is unmistakable. Wherever they pass, looseness becomes structure. Wherever they linger, dust becomes surface. Wherever they are encouraged, the basin becomes more itself.

They are not pets. They are not slaves. The people of the Drip-Room would not understand such words applied to them. The relationship is closer to a covenant with a creature that has its own nature and its own limits. The larvae need moisture, darkness, and calm. They need surfaces suitable for binding. They need periods of rest after molting. They need not be startled. In return, they produce the material that holds the young rock together. Their silk is not like spider silk or caterpillar silk alone. Mixed with chewed mineral paste, ash, powdered pumice, and fragments of plant matter, it becomes something between mortar, felt, and living tissue. It hardens enough to bear weight, yet remains flexible enough to absorb tremor. It drinks sound. It softens impact. It makes the basin capable of endurance without rigidity.

The people know that the larvae are sensitive to vibration, and they treat this knowledge as sacred practicality. A sharp sound can draw them from seams. A heavy footfall can cause them to rise in repair response. A dropped tool may be covered in silk before anyone returns for it. At first this seems like nuisance, but in the logic of the Drip-Room it is part of the basin’s self-defense. The place does not tolerate brokenness. It does not tolerate ringing emptiness. If something vibrates wrongly, the larvae attempt to still it. If a crack whistles, they seal it. If a stone shifts and leaves a gap, they fill it. If a wall begins to shed dust, they reinforce it. They are not conscious in a human way, but collectively they behave as if the basin has nerves and they are the endings of those nerves.

During molt, the mason-larvae become both vulnerable and strange. Their old skins hang from walls and towers like pale flowers. The air fills with drifting fragments, and the people cover their mouths and eyes not out of fear but out of respect for the delicacy of the moment. New larvae are bright and tender, almost translucent in the dimness, and they spin with anxious abundance. They cover themselves quickly, as if ashamed of being seen without a case. The people move slower then. Work is postponed if possible. The basin becomes a nursery. Even predators seem to withdraw, either because the people discourage them or because the smell of so much fresh silk changes the air. To be in the Drip-Room during a molt is to feel the city shedding one version of itself and becoming another.

Some larvae never fully leave the architecture. In certain towers, older colonies have become so integrated into the structure that it is difficult to say where creature ends and building begins. Their silk forms layered reefs. Their bodies move through hidden channels. Their presence is known by faint swelling in the walls, by the smell of wet flour and cold honey, by the way a stone-listener’s face changes when pressing an ear to a particular surface. The people do not speak of these deep colonies often. They do not need to. They know that some parts of the basin are more alive than others, and that such places should be approached with care.

The cisterns have their own citizens. They are not tanks but communities. In their dark water live filter-feeders with fringed mouths that open and close in slow rhythm, drawing in fine particles and leaving the water cleaner. There are amphibians with broad heads and velvet skin, pale gray or green-black, their gills like delicate moss. They wait in corners, motionless, then move with a sudden sideways glide that barely wrinkles the surface. There are snail-like grazers whose shells are built from ash and silk, rough outside and polished inside. There are translucent worms that consume dead silk and shed skins, preventing decay from fouling the reservoirs. Some cisterns contain small bivalve things that lie half-buried in gravel, opening like hands when the water is healthy, closing tight when something disturbs them.

The cistern-tenders know each tank’s population as a shepherd knows a flock. They know which creatures are signs of balance and which are signs of excess. If certain larvae multiply too quickly, the water may thicken with their waste. If filter-feeders die, the tank may grow cloudy. If amphibians become too numerous, they may prey too heavily on useful insects. If algae bloom too thickly, the light in the cistern changes and the glow becomes unsettling rather than useful. Nothing in the cisterns is accidental. Each resident has a task, even if that task is merely to be eaten, to shed, to die, and to feed another process. Water in the Drip-Room is kept alive by being filled with lives that keep one another in check.

The insects and insect-like creatures are everywhere, though often unnoticed. Midges rise from wet places in pale columns, silent as smoke. They are born in vast numbers and die in vast numbers, their bodies becoming food for larvae, fungi, moss, and the scavengers that move through seams. They are not pests so much as weather made visible. When their columns thicken, the fog-readers take note. When they vanish, the basin may be entering a dry pause. The people do not curse them. They adjust. They may cover silk stores, close certain vents, or move drying racks into sheltered niches. Midges are part of the moisture cycle, and moisture is labor.

The moths are softer and stranger. Their wings are furred rather than scaled, and their flight is so muffled that they seem less like flying creatures than like pieces of cloth remembering how to drift. They gather where spores are thick, where fungi breathe, where condensation collects. They shed fine fibers that float in the air and catch faint light, making the basin appear full of slow ash. These fibers are harvested when they settle, but never greedily. Moth hair becomes lining for boots, insulation for nursery jars, stuffing for dampening pads, and sometimes woven into cloaks so quiet they seem to absorb the sound of the wearer’s breath. The moths themselves are not tame, but they have become accustomed to the basin’s rhythms. They rise when fog thickens. They settle when the air stills. They do not sing. They do not call. They exist in a hush so complete that even their wingbeats seem borrowed from silence.

Predators live there too, because abundance without predation would devour itself. Some are small and quick: pale hunters with padded joints that move along walls and ceilings, taking midges, beetle larvae, and careless springtails. Some are ambush spiders whose webs are almost invisible in the dim light, strung between fern stems, across cistern lips, or beneath overhangs where droplets fall. Some are long-bodied, slow-moving things that hunt by patience rather than speed, waiting in moss until prey comes near. There are salamander-like creatures in the water, and perhaps other things that no outsider has fully seen, because they live in the deepest cisterns or in the hollow cores of towers. Even the predators observe the manners of the place. They do not cry after taking prey. They do not thrash. They kill quickly, eat quietly, and vanish into seams.

Occasionally, larger hunters enter from beyond the rim. Birds adapted to high cold may slip through the cleft or descend from the rim with folded wings, their feathers soft-edged and dull-colored. Climbing creatures, perhaps mammalian, perhaps something older and less nameable, may appear for a night and leave without trace except a disturbed patch of moss or a missing moth. The people do not drive them away unless they threaten the larvae or foul a cistern. Such visitors are part of the basin’s connection to the wider country of young mountains. The Drip-Room is hidden, but not sealed. It breathes through the cleft, through the rim, through the tiny passages that fog uses and rain finds. What lives there cannot be entirely separated from what passes through.

The plants also live there, and not merely as decoration. Moss is the first citizen of every wet surface. It is roof, sponge, skin, filter, and sound-deadening. It is transplanted, trimmed, encouraged, and sometimes mourned when a dry spell browns it. Liverworts cling to shaded ledges with slick leaves, drinking whatever touches them. Cushion plants swell with cold water, their bodies so dense they seem less like individual plants than like organs of the rock. Ferns unfurl in niches where light is weakest, their veins darkened for the dimness. Reed-thin plants with hollow stems carry condensation downward, acting as living pipes. Broad-leaved things no larger than a hand cup themselves to catch drips, their furred surfaces slowing evaporation. Epiphytic bladderworts hang from cracks with tiny traps, feeding on mist-born insects, adding a subtle predatory intelligence to the green.

Fungi are perhaps closer to the basin’s hidden mind than any single plant. They rise in shelves from shaded joints, soft as skin or hard as bone. They release spores in puffs that look like smoke in the dim light. Their threads glow in overhangs and deep niches, making cold veins of blue-green along the rock. They feed on decay, on shed skins, on dead moths, on old silk, on the accumulated life of the basin. In doing so, they return what has been spent to what will be used. During sporefall, when certain fungi release all at once, the air becomes a slow storm of particles and the whole basin seems to breathe green-gray. The people cover their faces, retreat under overhangs, and wait. The larvae lift their heads. The moths rise and fall. The towers fade. Afterwards, everything is dusted with fine powder that will feed moss, insects, and small scavengers. Sporefall is not disaster. It is one of the Drip-Room’s seasons, even if it lasts only hours.

There are lives too small to see clearly. Algae bloom in cisterns and glow when disturbed. Bacteria work in mineral paste. Mites move through moss. Springtails leap in damp corners. Eggs wait in seams. Dormant things wait for moisture. The basin is full of latency. A dry-looking wall may hold a thousand sleeping possibilities. A stone that seems bare may be covered in spores, egg cases, or microscopic threads that will awaken when fog returns. The Drip-Room is never empty, even when it appears empty. Emptiness there is not absence; it is pause.

The dead live there as well, though not in the manner of ghosts. They live as husks. They live as shed skins on tower netting. They live as old silk beneath new silk. They live as grooves worn into stone by generations of dragging baskets. They live as moss transplanted from one wall to another, carrying with it the memory of earlier tending. They live in the rules. The people do not need to speak constantly of ancestors to be ancestor-haunted. Every careful act is an inheritance. Every silence is a lesson passed hand to hand. A dropped tool half-covered by pale webbing is not merely a lost object; it is evidence that the basin remembers sound and responds to it. A wall sealed by larvae after a whistle is not merely repair; it is a continuation of an old argument against sharpness.

If you ask what single being lives there, the answer becomes stranger. The Drip-Room itself lives. Not as a body with a heart, not as a mind with a single thought, but as a composite organism made of stone, water, silk, moss, insect, fungus, human attention, and rule. It has moods. It has appetites. It has seasons. It has wounds and healings. It has a way of responding to disturbance. It notices sound. It notices dryness. It notices when too many larvae are removed or when a channel is blocked. It may not think in words, but it thinks in changes: a wall thickening here, a cistern clearing there, moss retreating from one ledge and advancing along another. The people are part of this organism, not outside it. Their labor is one of its metabolic processes. Their restraint is one of its defenses. Their crafts are its ways of knowing itself.

This is why the place feels observed without threat. There is attention in it. Not the attention of eyes alone—though there are eyes, many eyes, in moss and water and shadow—but the attention of surfaces. The way a leaf turns after fog. The way a larva pauses at a footstep. The way a cistern’s creatures shift when a plug is opened. The way moss swells before a drip becomes visible. The way towers seem to lean inward during sporefall. All of this gives the basin a presence. It does not speak. It does not need to. It teaches by atmosphere. It shapes those who enter. It asks them to become smaller, slower, softer.
