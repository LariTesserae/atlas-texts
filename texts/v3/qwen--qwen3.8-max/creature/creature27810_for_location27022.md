What lives there is not yet a simple population. It is a nursery of unfinished tenants, a wet first draft in which every resident is also a function.

The obvious citizens are the plants, but they should not be mistaken for scenery. They are the visible majority, the green pressure in every volume, the reason the air has weight and sweetness. They live in assigned troughs, basins, racks, and seams, but they do not remain merely placed. They test their borders in slow, hydraulic ways. Broad leaves tilt toward warm pump housings instead of light. Vines explore cable trays with the patience of fingers reading braille. Roots enter grating, find water, thicken. The mosses seal the floor joints like living felt, but when touched they contract by fractions, as if each green mat is a distributed ear. The flowers are stranger still: they do not seek the sun, because the sun is filtered and uncertain; they open toward heat, toward the small breath of machines, toward the warmth left by a passing body.

They are lush, but not free. Their colors are too saturated, their edges too regular, their growth too attentive. They seem to have been taught what “leaf” means and then asked to perform it with excessive devotion. Yet life is not obedient just because it was designed. Some plants have begun to lean beyond their troughs. Some floating basin leaves overlap so tightly that they form temporary green floors, trembling when water flushes beneath them. Some metallic-veined fronds vibrate faintly when the ventilation changes, as though they are listening for the next instruction. If the plants are not aware, they are at least responsive in a way that makes the room feel held. They are the first citizens, and they are already altering the plan.

Because the green is assigned, there are keepers that are not green.

The machines live there too, if life can be measured by motion, need, and habit. They are not animals, but they have animal timing. Flat fabricators slide along wall tracks, laying new surface in thin bands, sealing hairline seams, then pausing as if to inspect their work. Self-guided trolleys carry seedling trays with the patience of tortoises. Robotic arms descend from ceiling mounts, padded and slow, turning a leaf, lifting a vine, adjusting a support, then withdrawing with the careful dignity of surgeons. Small maintenance units gather in corners where leaf litter collects, wait, clean, and disappear into wall slots. Their bodies are warm. Their joints sigh. Their shells carry condensation, so that even the machines look dew-bright, as though they too are being grown.

They have no faces, but they have attention. When a person stands too long near a basin, a machine may drift closer, pause, orient itself, then move on. When a frond falls into a channel, a cleaner arrives before the water has finished carrying it away. When a patch of moss is damaged, a fabricator appears and lays down new green felt in a thin rectangle, the replacement so exact that the wound becomes a seam instead of a scar. The machines do not seem to serve the garden so much as belong to it. They are its metal fauna, its beetles of industry, its liturgical attendants. They do not sleep, but they enter low-power stillness in charging alcoves, where they tick softly as they cool.

Below both plant and machine lives the invisible commons.

The true first ecology is microbial. It exists in the warm water, in the mineral sweetness of the channels, in the film on glazed tile, in the green-black sheen of surfaces that are never fully dry. Engineered cultures live there: algae, filtration bacteria, synthetic biofilms, designed spores, nutrient workers, calcium fixers, odor makers. They are not individuals. They are colonies, fronts, blooms, crusts. They make the smell of the place: wet leaves, hot metal, sap, fresh plaster, and beneath that, the faint fermented sugar of living filters. They turn water cloudy, then clear. They darken a basin, then are filtered away. They leave pale rings around outlets and build fragile mineral ridges where the water drips.

They are the only residents that reproduce without a work order. Because of that, they are both essential and suspect. The system watches them the way a body watches its own appetite. Too much algae and the filtration panels tighten. Too much biofilm and a flush tide arrives with unusual force. Too little life and the water becomes sterile, thin, unhappy. The microbes negotiate with the room in silent chemical terms. They are digestion, decay, oxygen, sweetness, risk. Without them, the plants would not drink properly, the water would not taste of copper and life, and the artificial limestone ridge would never thicken. They are smaller than any permitted creature, but they may be the oldest true inhabitants.

Animals are rare because the place is not yet ready for hunger.

Hunger would change everything. Hunger would introduce pursuit, waste, fear, bones. The room is still arranging breath before appetite. Even so, there are exceptions.

Sometimes a pale moth, or something behaving like a moth, circles a misting nozzle. It is not quite convincing. Its wings are too soft, its flight too uncertain, its body too palely dusted with moisture. It may be a pollination proxy released too early, or an accident folded into a seedling tray, or a small machine taught to imitate an insect from an archive. It does not seem to eat. It only circles, trembles, and circles again. When it tires, it lands on a brass rail, leaves a faint powder mark, and is gone by the next flush. If it dies, it falls into a channel and is carried away with such efficiency that its death becomes part of the water cycle rather than a corpse.

There is also the dry skittering inside the vents. It may be thermal expansion. It may be a loose maintenance crawler. It may be something learning to climb. No one confirms it. The sound happens rarely enough to remain plausible as machinery, and frequently enough to resist being dismissed.

The most significant animal, if animal is the word, lives in one of the shallow basins beneath the floating leaves.

It is no larger than a thumb. It is translucent, almost gelatinous, with a body like blown glass that has not yet decided whether to become solid. Through its pale substance runs a dark thread, perhaps a nerve, perhaps a gut, perhaps a primitive vein. Something inside it pulses at irregular intervals. It moves with a shy, gliding motion, using tiny frills or cilia or some engineered analogue. It stays under the leaves where condensation furs their undersides, and it avoids the brighter patches where light comes through the upper lattice. When a pump changes pitch, it freezes. When a shadow passes over the basin, it sinks lower. It may be a pest that entered through a filter. It may be a prototype released by mistake. It may be an accidental birth from the water system itself, a knot of living culture that folded into motion because the room was warm, wet, and full of possibility.

The machines do not know what to do with it. That uncertainty makes it important.

They pause above its basin. A sensor cluster may descend, hover, shine a faint light through the water, then retract. A pruning arm once touched the surface nearby and withdrew without cutting. The translucent creature did not flee; it only became stiller, as if it understood that stillness was its only citizenship. If it survives, the room will have crossed a threshold. It will no longer be only a garden with machines. It will be a place where something moves by desire, not instruction.

People live there only as careful interruptions.

They come through vapor locks in pale boots and hooded slickers, carrying sensor wands, pruning tools, sample vials, folded cloths for wiping condensation from instruments. Their gloves are thin.
