It gathers there, but not as falling. In the Basin of Unfallen Water, moisture is not a traveler; it is a resident. It stands in hairline channels, beads along black veins, and appears on the surfaces of the tarns as a skin of cold light. Because it has not fallen, it has no memory of descent. Because it does not rush, everything that lives there has learned to feed on restraint.

The first and most widespread life is a film. Lowland eyes would call it lichen, but it is not lichen; it is a patient mineral skin that darkens stone by a shade or two when the damp threads move. It grows in the seams where quartzite meets schist, in the shadowed lips of channels, on the undersides of boulders whose stability has not been questioned for ages. It does not flower, does not fruit, does not decay. It thickens by accretion, one crystalline layer over another, and its color is not green but the violet-grey of light forced through ice. It eats temperature, iron, silence, and the faint electricity of stars. Where it has grown for a very long time, the rock beneath becomes hollowed into tiny basins, each no wider than a thumbprint, each capable of holding one bead of unfallen water.

Around this film the rest of the basin arranges itself.

There are grazers, though they do not graze eagerly. They are low, broad, stone-backed things, no larger than a shield and no quicker than the movement of scree in hot countries where scree still moves. Their shells are made of the same grey schist they rest upon, not by camouflage alone but by incorporation: each plate is crusted with mineral grains taken from its own feeding ground, so that a resting grazer becomes part of the slope’s settled angle. They have no eyes. They sense the film by tension and mineral scent, and they rasp it with mouths like worn polishing tools. They never strip a stone bare. They take only what has darkened fully, and in doing so they open space for new skin to form. Their waste is a fine dry powder that settles into cracks and becomes, over centuries, the bed for more film. When one of them dies, if death is the right word, it does not rot. It simply becomes a rounded object among rounded objects, a presence that the eye accepts without surprise.

In the lowest seams where damp gathers in thin, cold threads live the thread-dwellers. They are nearly invisible until light bends through them at the correct angle; then they appear as moving filaments, clear as water but more deliberate. They are not worms, not roots, not nerves, though they resemble all three. They follow the capillary paths of the unfallen water, widening a channel here, sealing a pore there, maintaining the thin circulation of the basin without ever making a wave. They are said—if anything in such a place can be said—to be the thoughts of the water itself, the forms it takes when it wishes to remain water.
