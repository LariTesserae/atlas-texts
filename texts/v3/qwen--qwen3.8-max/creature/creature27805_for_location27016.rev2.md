Whatever lives in the Intake Sea lives by admission.

Nothing there is simply native in the old sense, because the place has no old ground to be native to. Every life in that water arrived by stocking, escape, permission, accident, or design. The first residents were not fish, not crabs, not anything with eyes or hunger. They were microscopic: bacteria, spores, algal cells, diatoms, and the thin living films that learned to take hold of epoxy, ceramic, polymer, steel, and concrete. They still live there in their billions, invisible but fundamental, making the first skin of life over the manufactured world. They grow where warmth gathers on baffle plates, where light falls in stable rectangles, where slow currents bring dissolved food, where outflows offer chemistry they can eat. They are the lowest pasture and the first condition. Without them, the basin would remain only machinery and water. With them, it becomes a place that can support hunger.

Because vegetation is kept disciplined, these films are the nearest thing the Intake Sea has to grass. They are not meadows. They are controlled surfaces, permitted patches, growths tolerated where they do not interfere or cultivated where they are useful. Green fur gathers in low-flow light wells. Diatom frost feathers cold pipes. Engineered biofilms cling to reactor baffles, feeding less on sunlight than on warmth and mineral richness. In some channels, short dark filaments wave from polymer frames like living pile; in others, pale bacterial mats spread around warm effluents, pale as spilled milk, converting industrial discharge into food. These growths are grazed, scraped, electrified, dosed, and watched. They are never allowed to become a forest. They are kept as parts of the machine.

On top of these films live the attached animals: the settlers, the clingers, the living anchors that make their homes where flow carries food to them. Mussels form the most visible colonies, clustered on intake towers, grating edges, habitat modules, and the sheltered sides of pump housings. Their shells are not the soft, worn shells of old shores; they are new-looking, ridged, often bright with mineral staining, sometimes marked with the regularity of cultivated stock. They fasten themselves with byssus threads to manufactured surfaces, making small patch reefs of shell and leg and living tissue. When a pressure wave passes, or when a cleaning pulse is about to begin, whole beds can close at once, producing a faint hard sound like many small tools being set down.

Among them live smaller mollusks, limpet-like forms with shells that fit into seams and corners as though they had been measured for the place. They graze on illuminated panels, on diatom frost, on the first soft fouling that tries to claim new metal. Their lives are quiet but constant. They scrape, adhere, shift a little, scrape again. In low places along service roads, where current slows and artificial sediment collects in thin drifts, there are burrowing bivalves that live just beneath the surface of paint flecks and cement dust. They are not numerous enough to make a true bed, but they are present, their siphons opening when the water is calm, closing when the pumps change schedule. They too are part of the filtration of the place, living where the basin’s fine waste settles.

Crustaceans are more restless. They live wherever machines make warmth, leakage, or food-rich flow. Near effluent outlets, heat shrimp gather in translucent swarms, their bodies flashing when light catches them, their movements quick and nervous. They feed on microbial broth, on floc, on the fine organic snow that rises from processing halls and cooling galleries. They are eaten constantly, and they breed constantly, and their abundance is one of the reasons small fish stay near the warm zones. Their swarms are not permanent; they shift with the pump tides, thinning when the current changes, thickening again when feeding plumes are released.

Crabs are harder and more deliberate. Some are small, flat, and quick, living between habitat blocks and under grating where predators cannot easily reach them. Others are scavengers with metal-stained shells, dwelling among sacrificial anodes and eating corrosion scale, dead tissue, rubber fragments, and whatever organic matter the current drops into corners. They have learned, in whatever way such animals learn, that the anode clusters are safe places: rough, sheltered, chemically active, and often ignored by larger movement. Their carapaces may carry orange bloom, gray lime dust, or the dark gloss of oil-free machine wear. They move with a patient industrial rhythm: stop, test, cut, carry, retreat. When a feeding release occurs, they emerge in numbers and work the edges while fish churn the water above them.

There are also engineered crustaceans, though the line between engineered and adapted is not always clear in the Intake Sea. Some were made to clean. They have strong pincers or brushing mouthparts, tolerance for chlorinated faintness, and instincts shaped toward grates, rails, and service surfaces. They move over electrified boundaries that ordinary animals avoid, or they are permitted through them by signal. They are not pets, not tools in a simple sense, and not fully wild. They are living maintenance. When they molt, they hide in cable trenches or under habitat modules until their new shells harden, and in that vulnerable time they are prey to eels, rays, and larger crabs. Their lives are useful, but they are still eaten like everything else.

The fish are the most visible citizens of the basin, and they give the water its sense of population. The forage species form the largest living structures in the Intake Sea: shoals of slender, herring-like fish that move in curtains kilometers long but only tens of meters thick. These fish are silver, quick, and highly sensitive to pressure. They do not swim as individuals; they swim as a single distributed body, turning in unison when a sound pulse passes, compressing when a predator rises, spreading again when the water feels safe. Their bodies catch the ordinary light in a way that makes the basin seem to contain buried weather. Sometimes, from a distance, a shoal looks like a pale fog moving against the blue-green distance. Then a lamp catches it, and the fog becomes thousands of bright edges.

These shoals feed where the factories release food: manufactured plankton, nutrient floc, warm microbial broth, processed waste transformed into something edible. When a feeding plume is released, the basin responds. The shoals gather, not all at once but in stages, first a few scouts, then thin veils of fish, then dense revolving masses that turn over and over in the rising current. Their feeding sounds like fine rain on sheet metal: thousands of mouths, thousands of small body-turns, thousands of tails beating the same water. Predators come from below and from shadow. The shoals compress into spheres, then tear apart, then reform. The event may last minutes or hours. When it ends, the open water feels emptier than before.

Not all fish live in open water. There are bottom forms too, patient and deliberate, adapted to the hard manufactured floor. Cod-like hunters move over deck plates with slow fins, probing weld seams, access gaps, drainage channels, and the spaces beneath grating. They have heavy heads and sensitive barbels or chin feelers that read the manufactured terrain. They eat crabs, shrimp, small fish, dead matter, and whatever edible thing gathers in corners. They do not rush. They have learned that the floor gives food to those who inspect it methodically. In the shadow of turbine pits, they hang almost motionless, then move forward with one sudden burst when something small betrays itself.

Ray-like animals glide over the broad metal plains where the floor is smooth enough to reflect light. They are broad, flat, and silent in their movement, their edges lifting artificial sediment in soft gray clouds. They crush mollusks and crustaceans with slow grinding pressure, and they use the open grating as a hunting surface, sensing vibration through the lattice. When a pump tide begins, they settle lower and let the current pass over them, or they move into deeper channels until the pull weakens. They are not abundant, but when one appears from the haze, it feels large and consequential, like a piece of the floor that has decided to move.

The ambush predators are different. Eel-shaped forms live in cable trenches, conduit gaps, drainage galleries, and the narrow spaces between machine housings. They are dark, flexible, and patient, their bodies suited to the built geometry of the basin. They do not chase prey across open water. They wait in the seams of the Intake Sea, reading vibration through their sides, and then take passing fish with a fast strike. Some can fit into spaces that seem too small for them, folding themselves along service lines or resting inside abandoned pipe sections. They are not numerous enough to dominate the basin, but they make every shadow meaningful. A dark gap beside a junction box is not only a gap; it may contain hunger.

There are smaller fish too, quick darting forms that live near lamps, habitat frames, and slow-current corners. They feed on particulate, on tiny crustaceans, on the drifting young of other fish, on whatever edible thing the circulation brings. They are the basin’s ordinary traffic. They flash near lights, vanish when alarms test, and return when the water stabilizes. In lit zones, they may gather in loose clouds that move with the day cycle, thicker when illumination draws smaller life, thinner when maintenance darks fall across the sector. Their lives are short. They breed fast, die fast, and are recycled fast. The Intake Sea does not encourage long individual histories except in the slow, hidden places.

Cephalopods live where complexity allows them to work. Octopus-like colonists are especially drawn to service cavities, junction boxes, habitat modules, and the cluttered interiors of older machine platforms. They are not the soft, romantic creatures of natural reefs; they are practical, curious, and cautious, adapted to a world of bolt heads, hatches, cable bundles, and access panels. They investigate. They test edges. They learn which vibrations mean drones, which mean feeding releases, which mean gates opening far away. Some make dens in the spaces behind polymer panels, sealing the entrance with fragments of shell or scavenged debris. Others move from cavity to cavity, hunting crabs and shrimp, leaving no permanent trace except the occasional disturbed drift of artificial sediment.

They are restrained by the regularity of the place. A wild reef offers endless irregular hiding places; the Intake Sea offers permitted ones. The cephalopods that survive are those that do not trouble the machinery too much, or that are useful enough to be left alone. Sometimes they are tolerated because they eat pests. Sometimes they are removed by drones if they interfere with sealed systems. They know, in their limited way, the difference between a surface that can be occupied and a surface that will punish occupation. They are among the most individual lives in the basin, and that individuality makes them slightly unsettling to anyone watching them through cameras. A school is weather. An octopus in a junction box feels like a resident.

The largest biological presences are not single monsters. The Intake Sea is too disciplined for that. Its enormity is expressed instead through crowds and cultivated stocks. In some regions, acoustic fences hold broodfish: large, slow-moving fish kept for breeding, harvest, or genetic line maintenance. They move behind invisible barriers, their bodies thick and heavy compared to the quick forage schools. They are fed on schedule, monitored by sound, and protected from the basin’s ordinary hazards. They do not roam. They are part of production. Nearby, harvest protocols may bring flashing signals and guiding sounds into the water, turning open volume into a temporary corridor. Fish respond not because they understand the system, but because the system speaks to their senses in a language of pressure, light, and hunger.

Predation in the Intake Sea is not ornamental. It is a working process. When predators strike, the event is often heard before it is seen: a pressure disturbance, a sudden scatter-noise, a burst of small bodies breaking the surface of a shoal. Scavengers follow. Crabs emerge from corners. Shrimp flick into the disturbed water. Eels test the edges of panic. Sometimes the machines interrupt. An electric exclusion field may switch on, and whole populations bend away from an invisible boundary as though a wall had appeared. Sometimes an intake opens, and the current becomes a hazard: a slow, enormous draw that pulls small fish, particles, and debris toward dark lips. Sometimes a cleaning cycle begins, and the water becomes briefly hostile to anything slow. The ecology is active, but it moves inside rules set by industry.

One of the strangest inhabitants of the Intake Sea is not alive in the biological sense, yet it belongs there completely: the machine population. Cleaning drones move over grating and wall faces, scraping biofilm from surfaces where growth is not permitted. They are blunt, heavy, and practical, built for endurance rather than elegance. Their brushes and arms make regular sounds against metal, a small industrial rasp that fish learn to ignore. Autonomous tenders travel fixed rails or drift along acoustic lanes, inspecting pipes, testing valves, replacing small components, watching for fouling or corrosion. Sonar beacons mark channels with regular pulses, and those pulses become part of the basin’s audible geography. To the animals, they are not messages; they are weather, rhythm, boundary, and warning.

These machines are not alive, but they live there in the sense that they occupy space, follow schedules, alter behavior, and persist through time. The fish avoid them, hide from them, or ignore them. Crabs move out of their paths. Octopuses retreat from their lights. Even humans, when they enter the water, often do so with machines around them. The boundary between resident and equipment is thin in the Intake Sea. A pump is not a creature, but it breathes. A gate is not alive, but it decides when water moves. An alarm is not a voice, but it changes where thousands of bodies go. In such a place, to inhabit is to participate in a system of signals, and the machines participate as fully as the animals.

People live there too, but not in the water itself. They live in the dry parts of the machine: control galleries, sealed service rings, pressure habitats, dry bells, lock chambers, and monitoring rooms set behind concrete and steel. They are few relative to the size of the basin. They are divers, acousticians, stockwardens, drone pilots, engineers, harvest supervisors, and maintenance crews. Their presence is felt more through effect than sight: a light turning on in a sector that had been dark, a camera panning behind a flooded gantry, a cleaning cycle delayed because someone noticed a breeding shoal, a gate held open for an hour longer than scheduled. They do not own the Intake Sea. They tend it, adjust it, fear some parts of it, and listen to it constantly.

The people who know the basin best know it by sound. They learn that a low groan in the eastern galleries means thermal stress in a particular pipe run. They learn that a certain cavitation hiss means a turbine is beginning to work harder than it should. They learn that a dense fish drum can mask the signature of a failing bearing, and that a feeding release can make sonar returns confusing. They name currents, not with poetry but with operational shorthand: the morning pull, the cold wall, the blue lane, the rain curtain, the south intake breath. They know that some areas are safe only between pump cycles, and that some sounds mean leave now. Their work is not to conquer the place. It is to keep the manufactured sea from learning too much, too quickly, in the wrong direction.

Divers are the closest humans come to living inside the Intake Sea, and even they are temporary. They enter through moon pools, flooded access shafts, or dry-bell locks. They wear suits that carry the smell of treated water and warm machinery afterward. They move carefully, because loose objects become hazards in the current and because the basin does not forgive mistakes with sharp edges, pressure differentials, or sudden pump tides. They learn to read the water by feel: a temperature line against the face, a pressure change around an intake, a faint vibration that means something large is about to move. When they work near the fish, the fish watch them not with curiosity but with caution. The divers are not part of the food web. They are part of the maintenance regime.

The true population of the Intake Sea is therefore not one species or one community. It is a negotiated assembly. Every living thing there has to answer the same practical questions: Where can I attach? Where can I hide? Where is food released? Where is heat? Where is danger? Which sounds mean safety? Which sounds mean exclusion? Which surfaces are habitat, and which are machinery? The answers are not fixed. They change with pump schedules, production seasons, maintenance lows, surface rain events, and feeding pulses. The inhabitants do not merely survive in the basin. They keep recalibrating to it.

That is why the life there feels arranged rather than scattered. Around warm vents, fish hang in slow-turning crowds, taking advantage of heat and food-rich flow. Along current lanes, filter-feeders encrust the edges of grates, combing particulate from the stream. On intake towers, mussel beds and engineered crustaceans make rough living reefs over smooth industrial faces. In open water, forage fish move in vast thin curtains, their bodies catching light like silver weather. In cable trenches, eels wait. In service cavities, octopuses explore. On the floor, rays stir the manufactured sediment. Near waste heat, crabs and shrimp work endlessly. Everywhere, biofilms make the first edible skin over the built world.

The basin’s seasons are operational, and life follows them. In high-production periods, the water grows warmer, currents become more turbulent, and food releases increase. Shoals thicken. Predators move closer to the warm zones. Machines work harder. The whole basin feels busier, louder, more crowded even though its vastness remains. In maintenance lows, certain sectors dim, currents slow, and some animals disperse into quieter corridors. Cleaning drones become more active. Growth is scraped back. Surfaces are made clean and cold again. During flushing cycles, the basin seems to inhale and exhale through filtration walls, and loose life must hold fast or be carried into processing spaces that are not meant for it.

Surface-rain events are especially strange for the inhabitants. Cold freshwater enters through intake stacks and falls through the submerged sea as a moving curtain, bubble-laced and rich with surface memory. It changes salinity near the upper layers. It brings different particles. It dims some lights and brightens others with turbulence. Fish shift depth. Shrimp retreat into structure. Filter-feeders close briefly, then reopen when the layer passes. The rain does not fall on the Intake Sea from a sky; it is imported, translated, turned into underwater weather. To the creatures there, it is not a storm in the ordinary sense. It is a change in the taste of the water, a change in the light, and a signal to move.

Even the emptiness is lived in, though not densely. The open volume between towers and factory blocks is not barren in an absolute sense. It is acoustic space, circulation space, hunting space, migration space. It allows sound to travel and shoals to turn. It allows thermal plumes to rise and spread. It allows rare presences to become visible long before they arrive. When something crosses that open water, it matters. A school large enough to darken the light becomes an event. A predator moving through an acoustic shadow becomes an event. A maintenance platform drifting along its rails becomes an event. The low density of life is part of the basin’s scale. It makes every living presence consequential.

If one asks who lives in the Intake Sea, the answer is plural and procedural. The film lives there. The mussel lives there. The limpet, the crab, the shrimp, the eel, the cod, the ray, the herring-shoal, the broodfish, the octopus, the scavenger swarm, and the engineered cleaner all live there. The machines live there in their own way. The people in the dry galleries live there through attention, labor, and fear. The basin itself, as a working volume, lives in the sense that it changes, responds, remembers damage, and adjusts.

Nothing in the Intake Sea is innocent of the place. Every life has been shaped by the fact that the water is a manufactured medium, the floor is architecture, the currents are scheduled, and the light is ordinary but controlled. The inhabitants do not belong to a lost natural order. They belong to a first age of flooding, a new sea still sharp-edged and loud. They live inside the contract of the built: attachment is technical, growth is either permitted or removed, hunger is answered by release valves and feeding plumes, and survival depends on learning which sounds mean safety and which mean danger.

So what lives there is not a single creature. It is a population of permitted lives, all moving through cool, pressurized, galvanic water. It is a shoal turning in unison under a pale lamp. It is a crab cutting through corrosion scale beside a sacrificial anode. It is a ray lifting gray dust from a steel floor. It is an octopus feeling the edge of a junction box in the dark. It is a drone scraping a green line from a wall. It is a diver listening to a pump tide. It is a warden in a dry room hearing fish drum over a failing bearing and knowing both are part of the same weather.

The Intake Sea lives as a system of hunger, heat, sound, and permission. Its inhabitants are not free in the simple sense, but they are alive. They are abundant within limits. They are arranged by industry and still capable of wild movement. They are few and enormous, crowded and sparse, watched and unwatched, all at once. They live in the first years of a manufactured ocean, and they are learning its rules as the ocean learns them too.
