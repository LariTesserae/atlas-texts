What lives there is not a single creature, but a quiet assembly: a people, a fauna, a working ecology of wet mineral surfaces and low machines. If you watch the basin long enough, the emptiness begins to resolve into intention. Pale figures move along the causeways. Something slides through a warm runnel. A valve breathes. A flock lifts from a pool with soft, papery wings. The place is inhabited, but not in the way green countries are inhabited. Nothing here crowds. Nothing shouts. Everything that lives has learned to be sparse, patient, and careful under the glare.

The dominant inhabitants are the basin-born. They might once have been human in some older sense, but the caldera has worn that fact smooth. They are not numerous, and they do not gather in dense masses. The basin supports them in scattered groups around cisterns, pump-houses, condensation towers, kilns, and thermal baths. Their settlements are separated by pools, salt flats, steam veils, and long stretches of shining ground, so that even their society seems to have taken on the landscape’s openness. They live in households that are also maintenance crews, in districts that are also water systems, in a city that is closer to a network of careful tending than to a compact town.
