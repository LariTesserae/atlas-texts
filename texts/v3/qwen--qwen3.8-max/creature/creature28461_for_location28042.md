What lives there does not first appear as creatures. It appears as a pressure of existence: the sense that every pore, film, grain, and trembling pool is already taken. Nothing stands against the landscape as a separate figure. Nothing has the luxury of outline. The inhabitants of that wet, warm, ancient expanse are not so much beings placed upon a surface as conditions of the surface itself—knots, skins, pulses, burrows, cysts, vibrations, and slow heat-driven movements that belong to the plain as thoroughly as its mud and water.

The first and most numerous inhabitants are the living films. They are the true population of the place. Microbial skins, mucous sheets, gelatinous layers, fine laminations of slime and mineral—they cover the ground, line the pools, coat the weeping stones, and stretch over shallow water like breathing membranes. They are not green in any familiar sense. Most are gray, brown, black, bone-white, or the dull blue-gray of old bruises. Some give off a faint cold glow when disturbed, not enough to light the world, only enough to reveal how deeply the water is inhabited. These films are the primary living substance of the plain. They feed on warmth, dissolved salts, organic powder, pressure, and the slow chemistry of saturated sediment. They are eaten, grown over, torn, reformed, buried, and reawakened. They are not individuals. They are populations so continuous that to step anywhere is to interrupt a living skin.

They are also the plain’s memory. Each film leaves a lamination when it dies or is buried. Each lamination becomes a surface for the next. The mud is made of their compressed lives. Their history is not remembered as story but as layer, as mineral stain, as faint chemical difference in a grain of silt. In that sense, the ground itself is composed of inhabitants.

Plant life, such as it is, belongs to the same order of humility. The black leafless spikes are not forests or even true plants in any generous sense. They are isolated growths, rootless or barely rooted, clinging to rare patches where the surface stays stable long enough for them to persist. They do not wave in wind. There is no wind worthy of the word. They stand like burnt stakes in the wet. Some are hollow; some are packed with spores; some are merely hardened secretions mistaken for plants by the eye. The floating skin-mats are thicker and more alive, leathery or gelatinous discs that drift over shallow films and sometimes fuse when they touch. The liverwort-colored crusts on weeping rock are older and slower, living by condensation and mineral seepage. Green appears only as an accident: a thin film of accidental chlorophyll, quickly swallowed by brown and black and gray. These growths are not scenery. They are micro-habitats, anchors, shelters for cysts and spores, tiny stable places in a world that mostly refuses stability.

Beneath the visible surface lives the greatest density: cysts, spores, dormant cells, and compressed organic knots. They are packed into the mud like seeds into a seed bank, except that here they are not waiting for a season. They are waiting for pressure, heat, chemistry, or vibration. Some can be destroyed and reassembled. Some can be dried, crushed, buried, boiled by vent-water, frozen into temporary stillness by a passing cold film, and then return when the conditions shift. When a local storm of vapor bursts upward, or a sinkhole closes, or a warm exhalation rises through a new pore, entire fields of cysts may hatch at once. The water becomes clouded with tiny translucent bodies, no larger than flecks of slime, unfolding for minutes or hours before most of them are destroyed or settle again into dormancy. Their lives are not biographies. They are openings.

In the shell-pavement regions, where countless tiny shells, pellets, and rounded fragments are jammed together so tightly that they form a kind of trembling road, there are inhabitants even more interstitial than the cysts. They live between grains. They have no bodies that could be lifted whole. They are threads, films, capillary networks, soft mineralized strands, and compressed colonies occupying the gaps that are not really gaps. Every space between shell and shell is filled with water, mucus, and living filaments. These beings move only as the pavement shifts. They do not walk. They transmit themselves through contact. A pressure wave passes from shell to shell, and somewhere in that vibration a colony feeds, divides, secretes, or dies. They are the smallest citizens of the packed world, and they are everywhere. If you were to take up a handful of the pavement, you would not be holding shells alone. You would be holding a compressed society of mineral fragments, living threads, trapped spores, and ancient organic powder.

The larger visible animals are rare, but not absent.

The pale flat worms are among the most common of the rare. They move through the upper films like slow lightning, broad and translucent, their bodies only a few cells thick in places, spreading into sheets when they feed and contracting into ribbons when the surface trembles. They graze on microbial skin, leaving clean tracks that immediately begin to heal. Where they pass, the disturbed mat glows faintly, so that each worm seems to drag a pale afterimage behind it. They have no eyes, no heads in any recognizable sense, only a front that seeks and a rear that forgets. They are often torn by turbulence, and the torn pieces may survive, each becoming a cyst or a smaller worm. They are not social, but sometimes heat seams gather them into loose alignments, dozens of flat bodies moving in the same direction for a while before the plain reasserts itself and scatters them.
