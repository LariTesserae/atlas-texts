They are called the Drip-Kin, though that is an outside name, a convenience for people who need to speak of them in plains-language. In their own usage, they are something closer to a rhythm than a word: two soft taps on a cistern wall, then a low breathy syllable that means, roughly, *those who keep the water from falling wrong*. They are human, or near enough that the difference matters less than the shape of their days. The hollow has made them into its own image: compressed, careful, wet-minded, and always listening.

They are not tall as a rule. Height is a nuisance under overhangs, across low bridges, and along shelves where a straight spine would knock against rope, hide, or dripping stone. Their bodies are compact and dense, built more for balance than speed, with strong backs, patient shoulders, and hands that seem too large for their wrists. They move with a peculiar economy, never wasting a step, never making a gesture that might knock something loose. In a place where every surface is either wet, sharp, suspended, or precious, clumsiness is not a fault but a danger.

Their hands are their true faces. Palms are hard and mapped with old cuts. Fingers are sensitive enough to find a loose peg in the dark, strong enough to haul a soaked hide into position, delicate enough to tie wet rope without fraying it. The tips of their fingers are often stained with mineral film, pitch, algae, or the green bloom of copper. Scars heal pale and flat, sometimes with a faint gray shine, as if the water in the basin has left a little of itself under the skin. They touch everything as they pass: a wall, a rope, a beam, the edge of a jar. This is not nervousness. It is how they know the day.

Their feet are almost never bare. The young stone cuts too easily, and the lower rock is slick, cold, and unstable. Even small children wear some kind of sole strapped to them before they can walk properly. Their boots are thick, patched, and repaired so often that no original piece may remain. They are stitched from hide, reinforced with bark, stuffed with dry moss where possible, and fastened with bone toggles or copper pins gone green. A good pair of boots is a serious possession. A person who loses a boot in the lower channels may be rescued, but they will also be scolded, because carelessness with protection is a kind of theft from the whole settlement.

Their skin is cool more often than warm. It carries the basin’s pallor: not sickly, but muted, as if sunlight has to argue with cloud and wet stone before it reaches them. Some have a faint olive-gray cast from algae and mineral dust; others are brown or ruddy, but all tend to wear a film of damp. Their faces are often narrowed by glare reflected from water, their mouths habitually closed, their breathing shallow and quiet. They smell of pitch, fish oil, cold smoke, wet hide, and the clean sourness of stone that has been wet too long. It is not an unpleasant smell. It is close. It clings.

Their voices are low. Children are not hushed by fear so much as shaped by acoustics. In the basin, a sharp sound slaps from wall to wall and sets birds fleeing from the rim; a soft sound vanishes into hide and drip. So the people speak in tones that carry without breaking. They can whisper across a channel. They can make themselves understood with a lifted hand, a tap on a beam, a change in posture. They do not shout unless there is blood, fire, or falling stone, and even then the cry is short and purposeful.

They dress for water, not for display. Their garments are layered and practical: inner wraps of softened bark or woven rush, close to the body; outer vests or aprons of hide sealed at the seams; shoulder capes cut to shed rain into small side-pouches or channels. Every piece of clothing has loops, ties, or hooks. A person is rarely without a small knife, an awl, a length of cord, a scrap of pitch, or a folded patch of hide. Ornament exists, but it is modest and meaningful: a copper pin shaped like a fishhook, a bead of polished cistern stone, a charm made from an eel vertebra, a braid tied with a thread of old rope. Beauty here is not what shines from afar. Beauty is what has lasted through wet seasons.

They move through the settlement like water finding a path: not fast, but continuous. They do not stride when they can step, do not step when they can lean, do not lean when they can rest against something that will hold. Passing another person on a narrow walkway is a small ritual of adjustment. One shoulder turns. One vessel is lowered. One foot finds the safest patch of plank. A murmured word acknowledges the other: something like *drip-hold* or *pass-well*. No one blocks a drip-line. No one stands where overflow is meant to travel. To idle in the path of water is not merely rude; it is a violation of the settlement’s first law.

They are organized by water more than by blood.

The basic household is not simply a family but a catchment: a group of people responsible for a particular arrangement of roof, channel, cistern, sleeping shelf, and maintenance duty. A catchment may contain relatives, married pairs, widows, apprentices, orphans, and elders, but what binds them is shared obligation. They share the same roof-angle, the same overflow rights, the same morning tasks. They know which boards in their section need oil after a cold spell, which rope stretches too much when damp, which stone shard loosens if stepped on with a heavy heel. Their identity is partly architectural. A person may introduce themselves not only by name but by place: *I keep the second trough under the tilted roof*, or *I mend the rope near the eastern perch*.

Within each catchment, roles shift according to skill, season, and need. There are cistern-tappers who measure water levels by sound. There are roof-anglers who adjust catchment surfaces after storms. There are moss-tenders who scrape, encourage, and harvest the black-green growth in shaded troughs. There are stone-listeners, usually older, who press ears and palms to rock faces and know by some private calculus whether a shelf will hold through the next cold turn. There are channel-sweepers, trap-checkers, hide-sewers, rope-makers, fog-readers, and fire-keepers. No one is idle for long. Even the very old have work: remembering where a repair was made three seasons ago, knowing which household owes labor, teaching children the difference between a harmless drip and one that means a hidden crack.

There are no kings in the basin. There is no single chief, no throne, no monument, no title that gives a person authority over all the rest. Authority is distributed like water rights. Elders with long memory speak first in disputes. Skilled menders are deferred to when structures fail. The person who noticed a danger before others gains respect for a season or a lifetime, depending on how serious the danger was. Decisions are made by consultation, but not in the open, loud manner of larger towns. A matter is spoken of softly, often while hands are working. People listen. Taps on wood or stone punctuate agreement, doubt, or refusal. If consensus cannot be reached, the question is delayed until the next rain-cycle, because urgency is often a bad counselor.

The laws of the basin are simple, severe, and mostly about water.

No roof may be altered so that it steals rain from another catchment.  
No drip-line may be blocked by body, bundle, or laziness.  
Overflow follows the old order: cisterns first, then work troughs, then the moss beds and rush cracks, then the outer marsh.  
Waste water is not wasted.  
A channel fouled by carelessness must be cleaned by the one who fouled it, and if the fouling harms several levels, the labor is doubled.  
A structure may rise only if it does not burden a weaker one beneath.  
No goods may be stored where they impede movement or water.  
Fire must be kept in stone, fed small, and never left unwatched.

Punishment is rarely theatrical. There is no prison, because there is no room for one. A serious offender may lose first rights to a cistern during a dry stretch. A reckless builder may be required to repair public walkways until the work is judged complete. A thief may be made to carry water for the household they wronged. Shame is effective here because privacy is thin and everyone knows the routines of everyone else. In a place where one cannot hide a blocked trough for long, one cannot easily hide a dishonest heart either.

Their language has been shaped by the same pressures. It is rich in terms for water and poor in terms for open space. They have separate words for bead, film, weep, sweat, crawl, drip, glug, spill, gather, stand, sour, sweeten, and clear. They can describe the sound of rain on hide in six ways and the sound of water entering a jar in more. They have fewer words for distance than for interval: not “far” but “three jar-fillings,” not “near” but “within one drip-line.” Their numbers are tied to practical measures—handbreadths, rope-lengths, cistern marks, storm-cycles—rather than abstract distances.

They also have a secondary language of taps and gestures. A tapped cistern announces its level. A double knock on a beam warns of loose stone. Three pulls on a rope may mean weather coming.
