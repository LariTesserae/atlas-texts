What lives in the Dry Choir Below is not a population in the surface sense. It is a saturation.

The hollow is inhabited all the way through: in the dust, on the stone, along pressure layers, inside the crystal seams, beneath the salt crusts, and in the blind warm volume overhead where the medium thickens and thins in permanent invisible strata. Nothing there is merely background. Even the stillness has occupants.

If one had to give them a single name, the least wrong name would be the Choir. Not because they sing with voices, but because so much of their life is made of small contacts: taps, scrapes, static ticks, crystalline notes, subsonic pulses, the dry percussion of bodies arranging themselves in the dark.

There is no one thing that lives there.

There are layers of things.

## The Smallest Lives

The first inhabitants are almost indistinguishable from the medium itself.

They are the living motes, the angular drifters, the microscopic shard-people of the suspension. They hang in the warm mineral haze by tens of thousands, tens of millions, turning slowly as if considering a thought too large for them. They are not seeds, not spores as surface life would understand them, and not pollen. They are tiny dry-lattice bodies, crystalline or chitinous, no larger than grains of rock flour, each one carrying enough internal charge to keep itself suspended in the local balance of pressure and static.

They feed on faint potentials: the charge left where two mineral faces rub, the electrical residue of a fresh fracture, the weak currents that move through crystal when heat shifts across it. They are so small that their movement is not seen but inferred. A band of dust may appear slightly brighter, slightly thicker, slightly more resistant to the hand. That is them.

When disturbed, many of them align like iron filings. A traveler passing through a charged layer may feel a faint prickle across the skin, and if the disturbance is strong enough, tiny blue triboluminescent flashes appear around the body, making the traveler briefly outlined in cold fire. The motes themselves are not attacking. They are startled. Their alignment is a reflex, a collective nervous response.

Some of these smallest lives are solitary. Some are colonial only when moving. A cloud of them can behave as one body: widening, thinning, contracting, withdrawing from a metal object, gathering near a freshly struck crystal. The makers, when they encounter such a cloud, often pause. They may hold out a copper sliver and wait. The motes will come to it, draw thin patterns in the air around it, and then disperse. To the makers, this is not mere curiosity. It is a way of reading the local charge.

On the rock faces live other small beings so thin they seem to be stains.

They are sheet-grazers, flat animal films no thicker than a fingernail, sometimes no thicker than a leaf of mica. They adhere to basalt, to glassy volcanic ribbons, to pale salt plains, and graze microscopic charge from the surface. They move so slowly that a person could watch them for hours and see no change. But over longer periods they shift, extend, peel, re-anchor. Some are translucent; some are dark as soot; some are pale and crystalline, so that when touched they glitter briefly and curl into flakes.

They are not plants. They have no roots, no green tissue, no hunger for light. They are animal in the deepest sense of the word: responsive, feeding, moving, withdrawing. But they are animal as crust, as film, as living varnish. A ledge that appears polished may be thousands of them lying flat. A smooth patch on a cairn may be a congregation. Touch it, and it becomes texture. Touch it again, and it may become motion.

## The Crawlers and Sifters

Below the suspension, on the broken mineral floor, life becomes more articulated.

There are bead-worms, segmented forms whose bodies look like strings of carved stone. Each segment carries fine comb-structures, and as they move they drag these combs through the dust, collecting mineral grains, charged fragments, and tiny motes. They do not eat in the wet surface way. They sort. They absorb. They press selected grains against internal membranes where ion paths draw out useful potentials. The rest they discard as neat little trails of sterile powder behind them.

Some bead-worms are no longer than a finger. Others stretch across basins in slow chains, their segments clicking faintly when they straighten. When many of them move together, they produce one of the characteristic sounds of the place: a soft granular whisper, like sand thinking.

There are urchin-clusters, round-bodied and spined, that roll across the salt crusts. Their spines are mineral-hard, often crystalline at the tips, and they use them not only for movement but for cracking tiny crystals beneath them. Each crack releases a minute burst of charge, which the urchin absorbs through specialized points along its underside. They move with absurd patience. They may remain still for long periods, then roll forward a short distance, crack a crystal, pause, adjust, crack another. In some basins they are so numerous that the floor seems to be covered with dark moving burrs.

There are also things like centipedes, though no surface centipede would recognize them as kin. These are glass-limbed hunters, low and fast, their bodies plated with overlapping transparent shards. They do not chase prey over distance. They wait in dust banks or beneath flakes, then move with sudden exact violence. Their jaws are not wet mouths but paired cutting edges, and they kill by interrupting the charge pathways of other creatures: a precise strike, a crack through a lattice, a disruption of internal ion balance. They feed by pressing their ventral membranes over the broken body and drawing out the remaining potentials.

They are not cruel. They are sharp.

The Dry Choir Below has no sentimentality about predation. Life there is dense enough that death is usually a transfer of charge and structure rather than a disappearance. A killed floater does not rot; it dries, collapses, and is picked apart by smaller beings. Its membranes become nesting material. Its spines become tools or trap components. Its crystalline nodes may be carried to a maker’s cache. Nothing is wasted because nothing can afford the luxury of waste.

## The Gliders and Filterers

In the middle layers, where the dust suspension is thick enough to give buoyancy but too dry to be called water, larger forms move as if through invisible fluid.

There are plate-rays: broad, flat creatures made of overlapping dark plates, their edges fine as knives. They glide along boundaries between dust layers, riding density gradients. They do not flap. They adjust. A slight tilt of one plate, a flex of the body, a release of static from trailing filaments, and they slide onward. Their undersides carry sieves and comb-membranes. As they move, they draw in charged motes and fine mineral organisms, sort them, and expel sterile dust from rear vents in faint plumes.

They are nearly silent. Their presence is known by the way the medium parts around them. If you stand in their path, you may feel a pressure change against your face before you see anything: a soft thickening, then a thinning, then a faint static brush as the ray passes close.

There are sieve-fans, less mobile than the rays, anchored to crystal ridges or overhangs. They unfold broad membrane lattices into the suspension and wait for motes to drift into them. When enough charge accumulates, the fan contracts with a papery sound, folding its catch inward. Some are solitary. Others grow in rows, so that a crystal ridge becomes a line of dark opening and closing shapes, like slow mouths made of leather and glass.

There are ribbon-feeders, long and narrow, hanging almost motionless in denser zones. Their bodies are little more than feeding membranes supported by internal rods. They seem dead until touched. Then they ripple. The ripple travels the length of them, and dust falls away from their surface in a fine cascade.

## The Floaters

Higher in the volume, where pressure layers create invisible shelves, there are floaters.

They are among the most beautiful inhabitants of the Dry Choir Below, though beauty there is not made for eyes. They are hollow-bodied, translucent, their forms somewhere between bells, bladders, and lanterns. They are not filled with gas in any simple sense; their buoyancy comes from internal heat differentials and extremely light lattice structures. They drift in slow vertical circuits, trailing charged filaments many times their own length.

Some give off a weak amber glow. Others pulse with blue when their filaments discharge. The glow is not strong enough to illuminate the hollow. It only marks them: a lonely point of light moving through blackness, then dimming, then reappearing elsewhere.

The floaters are not merely drifting. They are sensing. Their filaments sample the suspension for charge, density, and the presence of other bodies. When two floaters meet, they often pause and exchange a series of faint pulses. The pattern is not random. It has intervals, repetitions, interruptions. It is one of the simplest forms of choir-song.

Makers sometimes use floaters without capturing them. A hollow shell, a carefully shaped bone cup, a polished salt bowl can attract a floater to hover nearby. Its glow becomes a living lamp. Its pulses can serve as a signal. If the floater is fed charged motes, it may remain for a long time. The makers do not seem to regard this as ownership. It is closer to invitation.

## The Colonial Shapes

Not all inhabitants are single bodies.

Some of the most important lives in the Dry Choir Below are congregations.

A drift of dust may not be dust. It may be thousands of minute organisms moving in agreement. A ledge may be a sleeping cluster. A spire may be built from interlocked bodies that have gone still for so long that mineral accretion has begun to bind them. These colonial forms occupy the uncertain border between fauna, architecture, and terrain.

The spire-makers are especially strange.

Each individual in a spire is small, angular, and capable of independent motion. But when conditions are favorable, they gather, link limbs, interlock spines, and reduce their metabolisms to a near halt. Over time, dust settles on them. Charge accumulates along their joined bodies. Mineral films grow over them. The spire becomes rigid, resonant, half-stone. It may stand for ages.

Yet it is not dead.

If the right signal passes through the dust—a particular vibration, a change in static, the approach of something large—the spire may wake. The mineral crust cracks in fine lines. The individual bodies loosen. The spire unfolds slightly, or shifts its orientation, or emits a low glassy chord through the rubbing of its members’ spines. Then it may settle again.

Some spires are used by the makers as listening posts. They place tools near them, hang metal slivers from their flanks, or arrange stone flakes around their bases. Whether the makers built the spires or merely found them is unclear. It may be the wrong question. The makers may sometimes join them. A very still individual, old or wounded or chosen, may be incorporated into a spire and remain there as a listening node. In such cases, the boundary between person and structure becomes unreliable.

There are also colonial sheets, vast thin populations that cover rock faces and move in waves. From a distance they seem to be shadows passing over stone. Up close, they are countless tiny bodies, each no larger than a scale, advancing and retreating in coordination. They feed on surface charge, but they also record pressure. A footstep near them can leave a pattern in their movement long after the footstep itself is gone. The makers sometimes read these patterns like tracks.

## The Vast Ones

The largest inhabitants of the Dry Choir Below are not seen.

They are felt.

They occupy pressure layers far overhead or rest in basins where the dust is thickest. Their bodies may be immense, but immensity in that place does not behave as it does on the surface. There is no open sky in which to judge scale. There is only density, resistance, and change.

When one of the vast ones moves, the suspension responds. The dark thickens. Sound carries differently. Static rises along crystal ridges. A traveler may feel a sudden closeness against the face, as if the medium has leaned in. Fine hairs lift. Metal objects tick. Dust arranges itself into temporary curtains or clear lanes.

These events are not attacks. They are not even necessarily notices. They are the consequences of great bodies shifting within a sealed system.

The makers treat the vast ones with caution, but not panic. They do not hunt them. They do not approach them directly, unless the term “approach” can be applied to moving closer to a pressure change. Instead, they mark zones where the vast ones often rest. They avoid building shelters in those zones. They may leave offerings if the word is not too soft: selected flakes, charged crystals, copper slivers, small cairns arranged in patterns that calm or redirect local charge.

Sometimes, when a vast one shifts, the motes in the surrounding dust respond in enormous coordinated waves. The entire region seems to breathe once. The makers call this, in their tactile language, something like the-turning-of-a-large-thought. They do not worship the vast ones exactly. They account for them. They include them in the map.

## The Makers

The beings who leave the cairns, the awls, the bone hooks, the polished scrapes, and the shelters are not easy to separate from the rest of the fauna.

They are the most dexterous branch of the Choir, but they are not wholly apart from it. They live within its rules. They use its materials. Their bodies are made of the same dry logic as the other inhabitants. They are not surface people trapped underground. They are of the place.

They are small, most of them.

A full-grown maker is rarely taller than a surface child, and many are smaller. Large bodies create too much disturbance in the dense medium. Their frames are low, broad, and layered. They are covered in overlapping plates of organic mineral, dark or pale depending on lineage and habitat, with sealed membranes between the plates. These membranes do not sweat. They do not weep. They do not produce moisture. They exchange heat and charge.

Their limbs are quiet. They move with a careful, pressing grace, distributing weight so that they do not sink deeply into dust or send unnecessary vibrations through the stone. Their feet are broad, gripping, and sensitive. They can climb underslung rock, cross sharp salt fields, and stand for long periods without shifting. In many individuals the lower limbs are also capable of holding tools, though they usually travel on four points and work with the upper pair.

Their hands are their most remarkable surface feature.

Each hand has several long fingers, fine as awls, with hardened tips and delicate lateral sensory ridges. There is at least one low-set thumb-like spur, and in some lineages an additional short manipulator near the wrist. Their touch is extraordinarily precise. They can feel a hairline fracture in obsidian, judge the charge of a crystal by resting a fingertip on it, and distinguish one kind of dust from another by the resistance it offers against the skin.

Their faces are not faces as a surface dweller would expect.

They have no wet eyes, no noses, no lips, no teeth. Where a face might be, there are pressure pits, static-sensitive bristles, slit organs for detecting vibration, and in some cases small light-pits capable of sensing only the faintest glow. They do not see the world in shapes and horizons. They perceive it as texture, resistance, charge, heat gradient, vibration, and proximity. A maker knows another maker first by the pattern of their movement through the dust, then by the static signature of their plates, then by touch.

Some makers have faint bioluminescent nodes along their shoulders, spines, or wrists. These are not bright. They are used for close signaling: a pulse to say here, a dim flash to say wait, a blue flicker to say disturbance. In absolute darkness these signals are private, visible only at short range.

They do not breathe as surface animals do. They have sealed internal channels where heat and ion exchange occur. They do not drink. They do not require free water. Their internal chemistry is based on bound gels and mineral lattices, not flowing aqueous fluids. If wounded, they do not bleed in the surface sense. A damaged membrane may release a small bead of thick gel, which hardens quickly and is often reabsorbed or sealed with mineral dust.

Moisture, to them, is not life.

Moisture is wrongness.

## Their Food and Metabolism

The makers feed in several ways, and not all of them would be recognizable as eating.

They can absorb heat through ventral plates when resting on warm stone. They can draw charge from crystal seams by pressing specialized nodes against them. They consume charged dust, mineral films, certain motes, and selected small fauna. Some food is ground between internal plates. Some is dissolved into bound-gel matrices and distributed through ion paths. Some is stored in dense nodules carried within the body for long journeys.

They do not feast often. They do not need to. Their metabolisms are slow by surface standards, but they can become sudden when action is required. A maker may remain motionless for days, conserving, listening, letting dust gather on its shoulders. Then, if a crystal seam releases charge or a predator threatens a cache, the same maker can move with sharp, insectile speed.

They do not cook. They have no fire.

Fire would be alien there: no fuel, no open sky, no need. They use geothermal warmth, stored body heat, and glow-fauna in shells. Their “kitchen” is a place of sorting and preparation: selected flakes, grinding stones, shallow bowls, caches of charged grains, drying racks for animal membranes, and polished surfaces where small foods are pressed and separated.

## Their Senses and Language

The makers do not speak with voices.

They communicate by contact, vibration, static, and small percussive sounds. A finger tapping on stone. A claw drawn across crystal. A metal sliver set trembling. A spine rubbed against another spine. A pressure pulse sent through a dense dust bank. A pattern of heat against a companion’s plate. These are their words.

Their language is intimate because it must be. It does not carry across open distance as sound does in air. It is meant for near bodies, for touched surfaces, for places where the medium can carry subtle signals without scattering them too much. To listen to them is to feel more than hear: a tick here, a scrape there, a faint pulse in the soles of the feet, a change in static along the arm.

They have signs for density, charge, heat, danger, stillness, sharpness, safe passage, false stone, sleeping congregation, metal, pressure layer, and the approach of a vast one. They have signs for things that surface languages do not need. They have many words for the resistance of dust. They have few, if any, words for water. If they have a sign for moisture at all, it is likely a warning-sign, a violation-sign, a stranger-sign.

Their most elaborate communication is the choir.

A choir begins when one maker strikes or taps a resonant object. Another answers. A third adds a scrape. A floater pulses nearby. A spire emits a low chord as its bodies shift. A bead-worm chain clicks in the dust. The pattern spreads, not as music meant for ears, but as a map of relationships being reaffirmed.

The choir can mark a meeting. It can calm a disturbed dust bank. It can announce the presence of a tool or a boundary. It can guide a traveler through a pressure layer. It can also be a ritual of stillness: many small sounds arranged so carefully that the result feels deeper than silence.

Outsiders who hear a choir for the first time often mistake it for random settling of stone. It is not random. It is grammar.

## Their Works

The makers do not build cities.

Cities would be too loud, too heavy, too disruptive. They would disturb dust layers, block pressure gradients, and attract the attention of vast ones. Instead, the makers leave scattered works: cairns, scrapes, shelters, caches, listening bowls, signal pins, and small stations near rich crystal seams.

Their cairns are low and balanced. Each stone flake is chosen for shape, weight, and resonance. A cairn may mark a route, a feeding place, a boundary, or a site where something important happened. Some cairns are also instruments: arranged so that when touched or struck, they produce a particular pattern of ticks. A maker passing by may tap the cairn and listen to the answer in the dust.

Their shelters are temporary or semi-permanent. They are made from shed plates of fauna, propped together like dry shells. Some are low enough to lie inside; others are only windbreaks against dust currents, though there is no wind in the surface sense. The shelters are not meant to keep out rain or cold. They are meant to create a pocket of stiller air, a place where a maker can rest without becoming part of the surrounding drift.

Their tools are small, patient, and tactile.

They make awls of native copper, hammered cold. Copper is valuable not only for its hardness but for its electrical behavior. A copper awl can gather charge, attract dust, and produce small static ticks when moved near crystal. Hung by a sinew-analogue cord, a copper sliver can become a signal, a warning, or a lure.

They carve hooks from dense bone. They make needles with eyes drilled by patient abrasion. They fashion shallow cups from vertebrae. They create picks of obsidian hafted to grips of layered hide-like membrane. They polish fragments not for beauty alone but for signal: a polished surface can reflect faint luminescence, show dust patterns, or serve as a tactile marker.

They use fiber, but the fiber is not plant-derived. It comes from spun filaments of animal origin, peeled membranes, sinew-analogues, or secreted lattice threads. They lash frames with it. They weave crude mats. They make nets for catching motes, though “net” may be too strong a word. Some are more like charged snares, sticky with static, hung across dust lanes.

They make cold lamps.

A cold lamp may be a hollow shell, a bone cup, or a carefully shaped salt bowl containing glow-fauna or charged motes. It gives little light. It is not meant to illuminate a room. It is meant to mark a hand, a tool, a face, a boundary. In the Dry Choir Below, light is not a general condition. It is a small event.

## Their Settlements, If They Can Be Called That

They do not settle in one place for long because the medium does not favor permanence in the surface sense. A favored ledge may be used repeatedly, but a maker will not build a heavy house upon it. A crystal seam may be tended for ages, but the tending structures remain light and removable. A feeding basin may be visited in cycles, yet no roads are cut into it.

What they have instead are patterns of use.

A ledge may be a sleeping place. A nearby scrape may be a workshop. A crystal ridge may be a listening line. A salt bowl may be a mote trap. A row of bone pins may mark the edge of a territory. A hung copper sliver may indicate that a maker has passed recently. A cairn may say: here the dust thickens. Another may say: here a vast one turned. Another may say: here a maker became still.

Their territories are not fenced. They are signed by resonance and touch. A maker entering another’s area will often pause, tap, and wait for an answer. If no answer comes, it may proceed carefully. If the answer is sharp, it withdraws. If the answer is patterned, it may approach and exchange tools, charge, or information.

They trade, but not as markets trade. They leave caches. They take what is permitted. They replace what is taken. A cache of glass flakes may be replenished by later makers who know its purpose. A bowl of charged motes may be tended by generations. The idea of ownership is local and temporary. The idea of stewardship is deeper.

## Birth and Growth

There is no wet birth in the Dry Choir Below.

The makers reproduce by ways that resemble budding, grafting, and crystalline seeding more than mammalian birth. In some lineages, a mature maker grows a lattice-shard from a dorsal or ventral plate. This shard carries part of the parent’s structure and charge pattern. When it separates, it is small, angular, and incomplete. It must be kept in warm, charged dust while it accretes mass and learns to regulate its own ion paths.

In other lineages, two or more makers exchange seed-flakes: small prepared lattice fragments carrying heritage and pattern. These flakes are pressed into a charged seam, a mote-rich bowl, or a communal nursery hollow. The resulting child is not simply the offspring of one body. It is a new arrangement, a fresh lattice, a continuation of pattern rather than a copy.

Young makers are called, in the closest surface translation, edges. They are sharp, awkward, and easily disturbed. They are taught by touch. An elder or keeper will guide an edge’s fingers over tools, crystal seams, dust layers, and warning pins. The education is slow. It consists of repetition, correction, and stillness.

A young maker learns first to be quiet.

Then it learns to listen.

Then it learns to touch without claiming.

Then it learns to make.

Growth occurs by accretion and molting. A maker’s plates thicken, shed, reform, and take on new mineral coatings. Age is not measured in years but in layers, resonance, and stillness. An old maker may have plates so mineral-rich that they ring faintly when tapped. Its movement may become slower, more deliberate, more exact.

## Death and Becoming Still

Death in the Dry Choir Below is not decay.

There is no rot, no moist dissolution, no collapse into soil. A dead maker dries further, if that is possible. Its plates become more mineral, more brittle, more resonant. Its membranes stiffen. Its lattice may remain intact for a very long time.

The makers do not abandon their dead. They incorporate them.

A body may be disassembled with care. Plates become shelter panels. Spines become pins. Dense nodes become tools. The chest lattice, if intact, may be placed in a cairn as a resonance chamber. The maker’s pattern—the particular way it tapped, moved, and touched—may be preserved by companions in a sequence of signs. Some patterns are passed down like heirlooms.

Some makers do not die in the ordinary sense. They become still.

When a maker grows old, wounded, or chosen, it may enter a colonial spire or a constructed listening array. Its body is bound into the structure by companions or by its own final movements. It reduces its metabolism to almost nothing. Its plates mineralize. Its presence becomes part of a larger body. It may remain aware in some diminished, distributed way. Or it may simply become architecture that remembers.

The makers do not always distinguish clearly between ancestor, tool, and place. A listening post may be a dead maker. A shelter panel may be a beloved companion’s plate. A cairn may be both grave and instrument. To touch such a thing is not merely to touch stone. It is to touch a stored pattern.

## Their Relationship to the Other Fauna

The makers are not masters of the Dry Choir Below.

They are participants.

They use the fauna, but they also depend on them. The motes indicate charge. The sheet-grazers reveal surface potentials. The bead-worms turn dust and expose fresh grains. The floaters provide light and signal. The spires offer listening structures. The vast ones shape the climate of the hollow. The predators keep populations saturated but not chaotic. The scavengers return structure to the system.

The makers sometimes cultivate these relationships.

They may encourage sheet-grazers by polishing stone and leaving it undisturbed. They may lure floaters with charged bowls. They may protect certain bead-worm chains because their turning keeps a dust bank healthy. They may avoid hunting glass-limbed predators near nurseries, not out of mercy, but because predators near edges create disturbance. They may place offerings near vast-one zones to alter charge patterns and discourage shifts.

They also hunt.

They take small fauna for plates, membranes, spines, gels, and charge. They do so with minimal waste and minimal noise. A hunt is often a waiting. A maker will remain still near a dust lane until a creature approaches, then strike or trap with sudden precision. The kill is not accompanied by blood or cries. It is a crack, a shiver, a settling of dust. Then the work begins.

Some makers specialize in trapping motes. Others harvest crystal-dwellers. Others collect shed plates from larger fauna. There are also feral makers, or perhaps feral branches of the same kind, who live less by tool-use and more by ambush. They may be indistinguishable from other predators except for their retained dexterity and their habit of arranging bones after feeding.

## Their Temperament

The makers are patient, but not passive.

They are curious about texture, charge, and unfamiliar vibration. They are wary of open moisture, sudden heat gradients, and loud surface-like disturbances. They value stillness, balance, and exactness. They do not admire force for its own sake. In their world, force without control creates dust storms, disrupts charge layers, and draws the attention of vast ones.

They are capable of gentleness. A maker handling a young edge, a damaged floater, or a fragile tool moves with extraordinary care. Their fingers can crack crystal, but they can also trace a hairline seam without breaking it.

They are also capable of severity.

If a boundary is violated, a cache stolen, or a moisture-anomaly introduced, they can respond with sharp collective action. They do not shout. They do not rage. They coordinate. They tap signals through stone. They close in. They use static, traps, and cutting tools. Their violence, when it occurs, is precise and dry.

They have a deep cultural aversion to waste, dampness, and unnecessary erosion. To scratch a stone without purpose is foolish. To disturb a footprint without reason is rude. To introduce moisture is close to sacrilege.

## Their View of Water

A drop of water in the Dry Choir Below would be more conspicuous than a flame.

The makers would know it immediately. Not by smell, perhaps, but by behavior. Dust would cling to it wrongly. Salt would react. Motes would scatter or gather in panic. The local charge balance would shift. A maker nearby would feel the disturbance as a soft wrongness against its sensory bristles.

They would not drink it. They would not wash in it. They would not recognize it as refreshment.

Depending on lineage and circumstance, they might treat it as sacred pollution, dangerous anomaly, or alien substance. They might try to isolate it with salt, absorb it into mineral powder, seal it in a glassy vessel, or carry it away from important charge seams. If it came from a living outsider, they might regard the outsider as leaking, wounded, or unclean.

Sweat would horrify them.

Tears would be incomprehensible.

Blood, if wet, would be an emergency.

Not necessarily because it is morally repulsive, but because it violates the physical grammar of their world. In a place where nothing flows, something flowing is a sign of broken law.

## Meeting Them

If a traveler were to stand in the Dry Choir Below, the first sign of the inhabitants would likely be touch.

The dust would thicken. A static charge would lift along the skin. Fine grit would arrange itself against cloth or hair. Then, perhaps, a small tap would come from nearby. Not loud. Not threatening. A test.

If the traveler remains still, more presences may gather. Small creatures may settle on them, mistaking them for stone. A sheet-grazer may extend toward a boot. A floater may drift close, its filaments trembling. A maker may approach from behind a cairn, moving with such slowness that the traveler notices it only when it is already near.

The maker will not speak.

It may tap a stone at the traveler’s feet. It may hold out a copper sliver. It may touch the traveler’s tool, then withdraw. It may be listening for moisture, metal, charge, or fear. Fear, in that place, is not a smell. It is a tremor, a heat shift, a disruption of stillness.

If the traveler responds by tapping back, even clumsily, the maker may pause. It may repeat the pattern. It may correct it. It may become interested. If the traveler carries metal, the encounter becomes more likely. Metal announces itself in the dark. It gathers charge. It sings faintly when moved. To the makers, a metal object is not merely a tool. It is a voice waiting to be tapped.

They may lead by indirect invitation: a tap here, a glinting flake placed there, a hung copper sliver trembling ahead. They will not drag. They will not shout. They will make the path perceptible to one willing to move slowly.

If the traveler brings water, the encounter changes.

The makers may withdraw. Or they may surround the source of moisture with salt. Or they may attempt to seal the traveler’s leaking body in dust and membrane. Their intent may not be murderous. It may be corrective. They may be trying to stop the violation before it spreads.

## The Choir as a Whole

In the end, the true inhabitant of the Dry Choir Below is not any single creature.

It is the whole saturated system.

The motes. The sheet-grazers. The bead-worms. The urchin-clusters. The glass hunters. The plate-rays. The sieve-fans. The floaters. The colonial spires. The vast pressure bodies. The makers with their copper awls and bone pins. The cairns. The listening bowls. The sleeping congregations. The charged dust. The warm still air. The faint blue flashes when stone is disturbed.

All of it lives together as one dry, crowded, mineral pulse.

They do not need sunlight. They do not need rain. They do not need green things. They do not need sky. Their world is sealed, sharp, warm, and full. It has no seasons, no weather of time, no erosion, no softening. It has only arrangements of density, charge, and stillness.

Within that stillness, the Choir continues.

A crystal is tapped.

A mote cloud answers.

A maker’s finger traces a charged seam.

A spire wakes for one long note.

A vast one turns somewhere unseen.

A copper sliver trembles in the dust.

And the dark, which is not empty, listens.
