What lives there lives by adhesion, pressure, and patience.

It does not arrive as greenness. It does not spread in meadows or forests, does not rise in fronds or bloom in soft drifting clouds. The first life in that high drowned sea is a skin: colorless films, thin as breath and tougher than paint, stretched over stone, concrete, salt-rimed metal, and the sharp faces of newly broken rock. They are the base of the world’s austere order. They feed on current, on dissolved iron, on sulfate, on calcium, on stray voltage, on the cold chemical gradients leaking from the machines that hold the sea in place. Where they grow, they make a sheen rather than a pasture: gray-white on basalt, rust-glossed on iron, chalky over concrete, black and oily over old steel, opalescent where electricity hums through buried conductors.

These films are not beautiful in any gentle sense. They are necessary. They make the mineral world edible.

On fault glass they grow almost geometrically, following microfractures and crystal stresses. On fresh scree they are patchy and tentative, colonizing the undersides of boulders where the current slows. On the sides of intake towers they thicken into velvet-like layers, not green but bone-pale, ash-gray, and sometimes a dull metallic bronze. They do not photosynthesize. The ordinary light falling through the water only reveals them; it does not feed them. Their energy comes from chemistry, from heat, from the slow digestion of stone and machine.

Threadlike fungal mats follow the films into cracks and seams. They are not plants, and they do not resemble leaves or roots except in the crude sense that they thread and bind. They are colorless to smoke-gray, sometimes black, sometimes the color of wet paper. They enter fissures in concrete, gaps between riveted plates, collapsed ducts, and the hollows of drowned machinery. They feed on whatever is difficult: leaked hydrocarbons, dead tissue, insulation, mineral-bound organics, the slowly settling dust of crushed stone. In places where the sea has swallowed industrial structures, these mats become the first soft thing, though “soft” is relative. They feel more like damp felt or compressed ash. They bind loose mineral dust into fragile crusts, creating tiny footholds for other life.

Where the films and fungal mats persist, the first animals appear.

They are sparse at first, and then suddenly obvious. A cliff face may seem bare for hundreds of meters, then reveal itself to be dotted with pale, conical shells no larger than a hand. They cling to the rock with muscular feet, their bodies sealed inside low angular cases built from precipitated silica, iron compounds, and grains of the surrounding stone. They graze the microbial films with rasping mouths hardened by metallic salts. Their tracks are bright scratches on darker surfaces: narrow scars where the film has been scraped away and the bare mineral beneath shows through. They move slowly, but not so slowly that they cannot clamp shut when a pressure front passes. When the water shudders, they lock themselves to the rock and become indistinguishable from limpets carved by a machine tool.
