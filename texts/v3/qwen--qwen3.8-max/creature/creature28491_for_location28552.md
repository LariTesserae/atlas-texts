But the most overwhelming fact of the basin is that it is inhabited. Not thinly, not provisionally, not as a refuge waiting for better weather, but inhabited completely. It is a wet fullness. Life has entered every seam and pool and has not asked for green permission. It has made an entire world out of mineral, heat, rot, water, and animal patience.

The people who live there are the first thing an outsider would notice, and the last thing they would understand.

They are human in shape, but shaped by the basin in a hundred subtle ways. They tend to be low and broad through the hips and shoulders, with long arms and careful feet. Their feet are broad indeed: toes spread, soles thick, skin toughened by sharp tufa, warm mud, and the constant slick of mineral slime. They move along wet ledges with a rolling, half-crouched balance, as if always expecting the stone to shift beneath them. They do not walk as people walk on dry land, with confidence in the ground’s indifference. They walk as if the ground is participating.

Their skin is rarely dry. It carries a faint sheen at all times, softened by moisture and stained in places by rust, chalk, fish oil, and pale mineral dust. Some have hands that look permanently steeped, the fingertips wrinkled and flattened, the nails worn short. Their palms are callused in strange patterns from stone, cord, shell, and the coarse drag of fungal matting. Their knuckles are often marked by work, their forearms by splashes of warm mineral water that leave whitish traces when they dry, if they ever truly dry.

Their eyes are adapted to the dimness, not in any monstrous way, but in the way of people who have lived long under soft light. They see well in gray. They notice small movements: a ripple where there should be none, a crack spreading through new crust, a fish turning under a ledge, a fungal knot swelling overnight. They blink slowly in the mist. Their faces often seem half-erased by vapor, features softened by the closeness of the air.

They smell of the basin: warm stone, fish, salt, wet hide, shell, fungal earth, and the faint alkaline bite of fresh mineral crust. It is not an unpleasant smell once one stops expecting the smell of leaves, soil, or dry sunlight. It is intimate. It clings.

Their clothes are never merely clothes. They are tools, insulation, and evidence of labor. They wear wraps and aprons made from softened fish skin, bird hides, and woven fungal felt. The fungal cloth is not like plant fabric; it is denser, more leather-like when worked, sometimes spongy when new, sometimes brittle if neglected. It must be oiled, scraped, stretched, and repaired. It holds moisture without becoming entirely heavy, and it can be patched with sinew and shell needles. The people often wear capes with layered strips that shed drips away from the neck and chest. Their belts are cords of twisted fungal fiber or braided sinew, hung with pouches, shell scrapers, bone awls, small clay lamps, nets, or little copper hooks.

Ornament exists, but it is practical first. Necklaces of snail shells, fish vertebrae, polished bone, and small crustacean carapaces are common. Copper, when available, is rare and valued; it appears as pins, tiny rings, awl caps, or thin hammered scales sewn onto ceremonial wraps. Some people mark their skin with clay pigments: rust-red, chalk-white, blackish mold-gray. These marks may show district, role, age, or mourning, but they also serve to identify a body in the haze. In a place where distances soften and outlines blur, to be known by color and pattern matters.

The people are not solitary. They live close, work close, sleep close, and think close. Their households are not always households in the dry-land sense of walls, doors, and private rooms. They are clusters of platforms, hanging shelves, sleeping hollows, hearth pits, drying racks, and water rights arranged around shared pools or channels. A family may be defined less by blood alone than by the flow it tends. The people who maintain the same sluice, the same weir, the same fungal trough, the same drip-line, become bound to one another by maintenance. To share labor with water is to share life.

Children are everywhere. They are small, sleek, and startlingly sure-footed. They learn the basin before they learn much language: which ledges are brittle, which channels run warm, which stones are sharp with new crust, where the crayfish hide, where not to put a hand in the dark. They are carried in slings of hide or fungal matting against the chest, where they listen to the heartbeat of an adult and the constant drip of the town. When they are old enough to crawl, they crawl on warm stone. When they walk, they walk beside water. They play with clay plugs, broken shell scrapers, fish bones, and little nets. They make dams. They undo dams. They are scolded and corrected and taught again.

They learn to swim early, not as recreation but as survival and labor. They learn to hold their breath in warm channels, to open their eyes under cloudy water, to feel for a trapped fish or a loosened weir stake with their fingers. Some children become especially good at listening to flow. They press an ear to wet stone and can tell whether a channel is blocked, leaking, or filling with new mineral deposit. Such children are valued. They may become water-listeners, gate-tenders, or wall-readers.

Elders live near warmth. Their joints ache in the damp, so they claim places close to vents, heated shelves, and cook-pits. They are not idle. They mend nets, scrape hides, sort shells, teach knotting, remember lineage, settle disputes, and watch the fungal beds. Their hands may be stiff, but their knowledge is precise. They know which white crust is forming into useful stone and which is a sign of bad seepage. They know the smell of a healthy fish pen and the first sourness of a stagnant pool. They know when a midge hatch will thicken the air and when the frogs will begin calling. In a place without sunlit hours, memory becomes a kind of clock.

The basin has no open fields, no pastures, no orchards, no gardens in the green sense. But it has cultivation. The people are farmers of wetness. They farm flow, heat, slime, fungus, insects, fish, and stone itself.

They tend bacterial films as carefully as other peoples might tend grain. They keep certain channels warm, certain surfaces rough, certain pools enriched with fish waste or shed fungal matter, so microbial mats thicken in controlled sheets. They scrape these mats into curds, press them into cakes, dry them over vents, or mix them with fat. Some mats are too bitter or mineral-heavy for direct eating, but they feed larvae, snails, and crustaceans, which then become food in turn. Nothing is understood as a single thing. Everything is a step in a wet chain.

Fungal beds are among their most important works. They cultivate edible brackets and soft white knots on damp shelves, in crevices, and inside low clay troughs. They feed the fungi with dead matter: fish guts, insect husks, shed skin, bird droppings, old mats, bones picked clean, and the remains of previous fungal harvests. The fungi return this decay as flesh. Some are eaten fresh. Some are dried into tough cakes. Some are boiled in stone pots with hot water carried from seeps. Some are not eaten at all but grown for fiber, rope, lining, or insulation. A particularly useful fungal strain may be guarded and divided over generations, like a living heirloom.

Insects are harvested in vast numbers. The people do not regard them as pests but as weather made edible. When midges hatch in thick clouds above the pools, workers and children stand with fine fungal nets, sweeping the air. The insects are shaken into clay bowls, roasted on hot stones, ground into paste, or pressed with fish fat into portable cakes. Larvae are raised in shallow troughs where microbial films are thickest. Some are allowed to mature into adults that feed birds and bats; others are killed while soft and fat, then cooked or dried. The people understand that an insect is not small enough to despise. In the basin, smallness is abundance.

Fish are the basin’s moving wealth. They are pale, quick, and numerous, their bellies bright in the dimness, their sides flashing when they turn in shoals. They move through channels and pens in living currents. The people know them by shape, speed, and behavior: the little open-water feeders, the bottom-grazers, the dark eel-like creatures that rest under ledges and strike when disturbed. Fish weirs are woven from fungal cord and reinforced with bone, placed where channels narrow. Fish are caught by net, trap, hand, or hook. Some are eaten immediately, split and laid over warm stones. Some are smoked or dried on racks above vents. Their bones become needles, pins, fishhooks, and small structural pegs. Their skins become coverings. Their fat becomes lamp oil.

Crustaceans are equally important. Pale shrimp and squat crayfish crowd the pools, picking over surfaces and hiding in woven baskets. The people keep crayfish in stone enclosures where they can be fed waste and harvested steadily. Shrimp are scooped from warmer shallows. Their shells are cracked, roasted, or crushed. The meat is rich and sweet. The carapaces are saved, pierced, and used as small cups, scrapers, ornaments, or reinforcement for clay. In a place without wood, shell and bone take on the dignity that timber might have elsewhere.

Snails and mussels cling to submerged stones and line the quieter troughs. They are slow food, patient food, food for times when the fish are shy or the insect hatch fails. Children collect them in shell baskets. The people know which pools produce clean-tasting mollusks and which taste too strongly of iron or mud. Empty shells are not discarded carelessly. They become tools, dippers, ladles, buttons, and tiny lamps.

Amphibians are loud citizens of the basin. Frogs and toad-like creatures squat on wet platforms, in drip-shadow corners, along pool lips, and under walkways. Their throats pulse. Their calls fill the haze with a wet, ringing chorus. The people do not always eat them, though some do, especially the larger ones; they also value them as indicators. When the frogs call strongly, the water is moving well. When they fall silent or gather oddly, something may be wrong: a blocked vent, a change in mineral flow, a poison seep, a coming shift in the basin’s temperature. Children sometimes catch them with nooses of fungal thread, not from cruelty but because every living thing invites a hand.

Birds live in the upper spaces, under eaves, along ledges, and in the gaps between roofs. Swift, sharp-winged flyers dart through the haze, taking insects from the air. Their nests are plastered to stone with mud, clay, and fungal fiber. Heavier waterbirds stand on platforms or swim in lower channels, feeding on fish, snails, crustaceans, and scraps. Some are kept in pens near the fish works, broad-billed and loud, living on offal and invertebrates. The people collect eggs carefully, never all of them. Bird bones are light and useful for awls, pipes, and fine needles. Feathers are used for fletching, insulation, and markers. Guano is scraped into fungal beds and warm pools where it feeds lower life.

Bats or bat-like creatures roost in darker crevices, crawlspaces, and under bridge undersides. They emerge in the dimness as if the dimness had peeled itself from the walls. They hunt swarms, their wings whispering through the vapor. The people tolerate them, encourage them, and sometimes harvest their droppings for fungal cultivation. In some districts, bat roosts are considered common property; in others, they are watched jealously. A good roost is a slow gift: it enriches the basin below it, one dropping at a time.

The people themselves are part of this food web, not above it. They eat fish, crustaceans, mollusks, insect cakes, fungal brackets, roasted bird eggs, amphibians when necessary, and rendered fat. They drink warm mineral water, sometimes filtered through clay and sand, sometimes drunk straight from trusted seeps. Their meals are salty, savory, oily, and dense. They preserve food by drying, smoking, salting with mineral crusts, and storing in cool clay vessels sunk into mud. Their cookery is shaped by heat that rises from below: steam vents, warm stones, clay ovens heated by seepage, and pits where water itself can be kept almost boiling without flame. Fire exists, but it is precious and awkward in a world without timber. Fuel comes from dried fungal mats, animal fat, bones, and whatever can be gathered from the basin’s animal life. The people do not burn carelessly.

Their technology is simple, but it is not crude. It is repeated, repaired, and known by touch. Stone hammers shape soft shelves. Shell scrapers clean hides. Bone needles sew. Clay is pressed into pipes, lamps, troughs, gates, and plugs. Copper is hammered into small, prized edges. Nets are woven from fungal cord. Lines are braided from sinew. Baskets are made from layered matting hardened with clay or fish glue. Every object has a second life: a broken lamp becomes a scraper, a worn net becomes insulation, a cracked trough becomes a fungal bed, a fish hook becomes a pin. The people do not throw things away easily because there is no surplus to throw away.

The settlement’s architecture is inseparable from the people’s bodies. They are always touching it, repairing it, scraping it, listening to it, redirecting it. Their hands know the difference between old stone and new crust. Their feet know which platforms will hold and which will crackle into powder.
