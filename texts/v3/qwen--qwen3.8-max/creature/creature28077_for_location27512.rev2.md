What lives there is plural. The hollow does not have one face, one voice, or one ruling kind. It has a population in the fullest sense: a packed, layered, wet-tempered community of people, birds, insects, amphibians, small climbers, fungi, mosses, trained trees, and moving water. To ask who lives there is to be answered first by dripping, then by rustling, then by voices, and only after that by human eyes looking back at you from under damp hoods.

The people of the hollow are its most obvious tenants, though not the most numerous. They are human, but their humanity has been shaped by the place the way a boat is shaped by the water it moves through. They are generally compact and sure-footed, not because they are born with any special grace but because the hollow teaches balance early. Children learn to walk on springy moss, slick root ridges, narrow branch walks, and stairs worn smooth by generations of wet feet. They learn to read a surface before trusting it: the difference between firm root and soft rot, between a railing that is merely damp and one that has begun to loosen at the joint, between a path that is muddy and one that is quietly becoming a stream.

Their bodies show their work. Hands are strong, calloused, and constantly occupied. They have the fingers of rope-makers, pruners, weavers, and climbers: good at tying knots, clearing leaf-blocked gutters, pinching beetles off cloth, testing cord, smoothing moss, and holding small tools. Their feet are broad and tough-soled, often bare inside the sheltered parts of the settlement, or protected by soft shoes made from woven sedge, layered bark, and oiled cloth. Many wear split-toe footwear or tied wrappings that dry quickly, because nothing here stays dry for long. Ankles become knowing. Calves become strong. Even old people move with a careful economy of balance, placing their weight where it will be received.

Their skin often has a cool sheen to it. Not sickness, but the look of those who live in constant moisture. They oil themselves in places where damp and wind could crack the skin: wrists, elbows, faces, the backs of hands, the edges of ears. The oils smell of pressed seeds, smoked fat, herbs, and sometimes the faintly green, animal sharpness of frog-fat or fish-fat, depending on what the season has given. They are not perfumed people, but they are not foul either. They smell of wet bark, moss, smoke, green leaves, clay, clean wool when they have any, and the mineral sourness of cold mountain water.

Their clothing is made for the weather of the hollow: cool, damp, unpredictable, and full of drips. They wear layers that can be shed, wrung out, dried, and repaired. Bark cloth, woven grasses, felted moss, split-cane capes, oiled leaf panels, and tightly plaited mats all appear in their dress. Heavy fabrics are dangerous if they soak through and stay heavy, so their garments tend to be light but numerous, with hoods, shoulder capes, and wide-brimmed caps of woven cane or softened bark to throw water away from the face. In cooler weather they bind their legs and arms with wrappings, tuck cloth into cord belts, and wear close-fitting caps under hoods. Their clothes are tied, not merely fastened. Everything is secured because everything can fall, blow away, rot, or be snatched by a bird.

They carry little but carry it well. A person in the hollow is likely to have a belt or sash fitted with hooks, loops, and pouches: a small knife, an awl, a waxed cord spool, a wooden or bone toggle, a spoon, a netted bag, a clay cup, a sharpened stick for clearing channels, a folded leaf for sudden rain, a twist of dried fungus for fire, a few seeds, a feather, a smooth stone. They do not burden themselves with ornament that can snag, but they are not plain. They tie bright cord into their hair. They polish seeds and beetle cases into beads. They wear feathers that were found, not plucked, and little carved tokens of root-wood or bone. A favorite tool may be smoother than jewelry from being handled for thirty years. An old woman’s hood may be patched with so many different kinds of bark that it becomes a record of storms survived.

They are not silent people. The hollow is too loud for silence to be useful. Water makes a constant base-note, birds interrupt, wind shakes leaves, ropes creak, shutters tap, people call from level to level. Because of this, their voices have developed a carrying quality: short calls, lifted phrases, repeated sounds that can cut through drip and hum without becoming shouting. They also have a rich language of gestures. A lifted hand can mean “wait,” a tap on a rail can mean “this path is slick,” a flicked leaf can mean “look above,” a cupped hand near the ear can mean “listen, something is wrong.” Children learn both speech and movement together, so that a warning can be given across the market even when the air is full of bird noise and wind.

Their language itself is said to be thick with words for wetness. They do not have only one word for water. They have words for water that beads, water that threads, water that drips, water that gathers, water that runs, water that seeps, water that stands where it should not stand. They have different verbs for moss that is healthy, moss that is drying, moss that is smothering, moss that has begun to slip from a roof. They have words for the sound a channel makes when it is clear and for the sound it makes when leaves are beginning to block it. Outsiders sometimes think they speak quickly; in truth, they speak practically. In a place where a small obstruction can become a rotted floor if ignored, precision is a kind of kindness.

They are attentive above all else. That is the deepest thing to say about them. They live by noticing. They notice if a drip changes rhythm. They notice if a railing has grown soft on the underside. They notice if frogs stop calling near a particular basin. They notice if birds abandon a nest too suddenly, because that may mean a predator, a rot-hole, or coming weather. They notice if the moss along a walkway has been pressed flat in a pattern no foot should make. They notice if a root has shifted after a gust. They notice if the market tarp smells more sour than it did yesterday. They live in a world where small signs are not small. A day’s neglect can turn into a season’s repair.

Because of this, their social life is organized around maintenance. The hollow is not ruled by wealth alone, and certainly not by the idea of owning the most space. Space is too shared, too vertical, too alive for that. What matters is responsibility. Who watches which channel? Who tends which root terrace? Who knows the history of this buttress? Who can climb to the upper canopy when the wind allows? Who has the patience to train a young stem into a rail over ten years? Who remembers where the last rot appeared? The most respected people are often those who keep things moving without drawing attention to themselves: the channel-keepers, the root-binders, the moss-tenders, the pruners, the nest-wardens, the rope-makers, the fire-watchers, the old listeners who can hear a blocked drain before anyone sees it.

There are roles, but they are not sealed. A child may carry messages one year and learn to weave the next. A strong climber may spend mornings in the canopy and afternoons helping repair a cistern. A grandmother with stiff knees may sit on a root shelf and teach knot-tying, fungus-drying, or the proper way to coax a vine across a doorway without strangling it. Everyone knows at least a little of everything, because the hollow is small enough that no one can afford to be useless in a single way. Still, certain people become known for particular skills. Some are trusted to prune the living walls without wounding them. Some can make a rope that will hold through ten storm seasons. Some can judge whether a wooden stair needs a strut, a peg, or nothing but time. Some can calm nesting birds. Some know exactly when mushroom logs should be misted, covered, or opened.

They do not think of themselves as masters of the place. That is important. They are tenants, stewards, negotiators, sometimes servants. The hollow has its own will: water wants to move, roots want to spread, birds want to nest, fungi want to enter softened wood, wind wants to find the weakest latch. The people have learned to work with those wants rather than deny them. They do not say, “Make this place dry.” They say, “Give the water roads.” They do not say, “Clear away the green.” They say, “Train the green where it can carry us.” They do not say, “Drive the animals out.” They say, “Make room for those that help us, and watch those that do not.”

Their homes reflect this. The dwellings are small, low-ceilinged, warm in a damp way, and full of hooks. Sleeping platforms are often raised above the dampest air, built into trunk hollows, along branch shelves, or inside carefully enlarged tree cavities. Walls are woven panels, bark sheets, living stems, or some combination of all three. Nothing is perfectly sealed, because a perfectly sealed wall would trap moisture and because the people do not live alone. Small gaps allow air to move. Doorways may have woven screens that can be lifted, tied back, or dropped against wind. Many thresholds have moss mats or shallow water channels to clean feet before entering. Inside, there is the smell of dried herbs, woodsmoke, oiled cloth, moss, stored berries, and the faint green breath of living walls.

Furniture is minimal and hung wherever possible. Baskets, cups, tools, clothing, bundles of herbs, nets, and spare rope all hang from pegs, hooks, and loops. Floor space is precious, and anything left on the ground may become damp or be crossed by something that should not be crossed. Beds are padded with layers of moss, fern, bark cloth, and woven mats, often covered with a canopy or tucked beneath a roof lip to catch drips. In homes where there is a hearth, it is small and carefully built: clay-lined, stone-edged, with a short chimney or smoke-shelf to draw fumes upward without setting the living roof alight. Fire is precious and dangerous here. It is kept under strict habits. Ash is stored in clay pots. Sparks are watched. Wet wood hisses and smokes, so fuel is dried on racks above warmth, and every household knows the weight of a properly seasoned stick.

They eat what the hollow can provide without being stripped bare. Their food is practical, seasonal, and often steamed, smoked, dried, or fermented, because wet cold makes preservation difficult. They cultivate mushrooms on shaded logs and harvest them carefully with knives, leaving enough for regrowth and for the small creatures that depend on them. They grow or gather water-loving greens in channels and marsh-edge beds: tender shoots, crisp leaves, things with a sharp, clean taste that can be eaten raw or wilted into broth. They pick berries, fern curls, buds, seeds, and mountain herbs. Where small fish, crabs, or other water-life exist in the basins, they are managed as much as fished. They may keep certain pools clear of larvae, feed them with kitchen scraps, or encourage the creatures that eat mosquitoes but not the fish. Bird eggs are taken rarely and with care, usually only from nests that have too many or have been damaged, because birds are too useful to rob casually. Insects are not merely endured; some are harvested, especially larvae, beetles, and pollinators that can be gathered without breaking the balance. Insect paste, dried grubs, or crushed seasonings are ordinary foods, though the taste is acquired by outsiders and native to children.

The market is one of the fullest expressions of their life. It is crowded, noisy, damp, and constantly adjusting. Stalls are not fixed in the same way city stalls might be; they lean against trunks, hang from branches, fold into root shelves, and shift according to what needs to be sold or protected from rain. Vendors stand under leaf canopies or bark awnings, their goods displayed on woven mats, wooden trays, and hanging nets. Mushrooms are laid out in wide baskets, some pale and fleshy, some dark as wet stone, some with caps still beaded from misting. Bundles of greens sit with their stems in shallow water. Berries are heaped in clay bowls or large leaves. Fern shoots are tied in neat coils. Dried herbs hang in small bunches that release scent when brushed. Bark cloth is folded in stacks, some dyed with moss-green, root-brown, berry-stain, or ash-grey. Rope is coiled in loops. Clay cups, bowls, lamps, and water jars are nested together. Woven fish traps lean against supports. Tiny cages hold beetles for trade, or sometimes just for children to watch.

People bargain in short, familiar phrases, because everyone knows the prices of common things and everyone knows when the weather is about to change. A seller will glance up at the canopy. A buyer will listen to the wind in the upper leaves. If a gust is coming, hands move faster. Tarps are tightened. Baskets are pulled under cover. Feather-light goods are weighted down. Then the shower falls, everyone swears or laughs depending on temperament, and the market continues under drumming roofs. Children dart between legs with messages, scraps, or stolen berries. Porters move through with loads balanced on their backs, saying little but given way by habit. Someone is always selling something newly made, repaired, found, or salvaged: a hook re-sharpened, a basket rewoven, a fallen branch carved into ladles, a dead tree’s bark peeled and softened into cloth.

The social feeling is intimate to the point of pressure. Privacy exists, but it is partial and negotiated. Walls are thin enough for voices. Floors carry footsteps. If you argue, someone may hear. If you are ill, someone is likely to notice before you admit it. If a bird nests over your doorway, you may have to take another way out until the young fledge. If a frog chooses your water jar, you may have to pour carefully until it leaves. If a marten appears on a high branch, people warn each other and watch their hanging food. If a neighbor’s gutter overflows toward your walkway, the problem is not abstract; it must be solved before the path softens. This kind of closeness can be hard for outsiders. For the people of the hollow, it is simply the texture of being alive there. They have developed manners for it: asking before touching another person’s hanging goods, stepping around a sleeping animal, giving room to a porter with a heavy load, lowering a voice when someone is sick, tying back a branch that has grown too far into a shared path.

Their relations with animals are especially revealing. They do not live beside animals; they live among them as fellow occupants of a crowded house. Birds are not ornaments. They are weather-readers, insect-eaters, alarm-criers, and neighbors. Swifts and swallows are watched with affection because their flight tells what the wind is doing above the rim. Wrens are tolerated even when they are scolding, because their sharp voices often reveal something moving. Finches and other seed-eaters are encouraged where useful plants grow. Nest sites are protected if they do not weaken a structure. People leave out bits of cord, moss, and shed hair for nest-building. Fallen feathers are collected for use in insulation, fletching, decoration, or trade. If a nest becomes dangerous—too heavy, too close to smoke, or built into a load-bearing joint—it may be moved carefully, not destroyed. If eggs are lost, there is often a brief quietness, because everyone understands that the hollow is not theirs alone.

Frogs and toads are among the most respected small lives, though not in a sentimental way. They are useful, and their usefulness is loved. Frogs in leaf cups and troughs show that water is still living. Their throats working, their sudden leaps, their clear or croaking calls all say that the wetness is not stagnant. If frogs leave a basin, someone checks the basin. If toads gather under a board, the board may be left alone until they move. Children are taught to step over amphibian crossings, not to chase them for sport. Some households keep shallow stone dishes near doorways, more for frogs than for themselves, because a frog near a doorway means fewer insects inside. Toads are considered slow tenants, somewhat grim, somewhat lucky. A large old toad under a root shelf may become a local landmark, known to generations.

Insects are a condition of existence, and the people have made peace with that. There is no final victory over insects in a place like this; there is only management, respect, and occasional surrender. Midges rise in threads over wet moss and are accepted as part of the air. Beetles are watched: some clean waste, some harm wood, some are food, some are simply beautiful. Ants have roads that the people try not to break. If an ant road crosses a food shelf, it is redirected with scent barriers, water gaps, or moved stores, not with reckless destruction, because a war on ants can become more exhausting than an ant. Moths are numerous at dusk and night, and they can damage stored goods if left unchecked, but they also pollinate and are food for other lives. Sleeping spaces often have fine nets or smoked repellents to keep the worst of them away. Flies are managed through careful waste handling, covered compost, and the encouragement of creatures that eat them. Bees and highland pollinators are honored in the practical way: flowering vines are maintained, nests are avoided when possible, and if honey or wax is obtained, it is done with patience and smoke.

Small mammals live in the spaces between human intention and animal persistence. Mice and shrews are everywhere in the thatch, root floors, and stored bundles. They are not loved when they enter food stores, but they are understood. Food is kept in sealed baskets, raised bins, or hung bags. Traps are set where necessary, but poison is avoided because it would move through the web too cruelly. Instead, the people encourage predators: owls in the upper clefts, martens in the branches, sharp-eyed birds on the rim. A marten may be cursed when it steals an egg, but it is not hunted without reason, because it also kills rodents. Sometimes a half-wild marten becomes known by name, the way a difficult neighbor might be: “The red one again,” someone will say, and the phrase contains annoyance, familiarity, and a kind of respect. Bats may roost in higher hollows, emerging at dusk to work the insect haze, and their presence is considered good for sleep.

Even the plants are more inhabitants than scenery. The trained trees, buttress roots, living walls, moss roofs, and epiphyte baskets are not passive materials. They grow, resist, sag, thicken, die, and recover. The people speak of them almost as slow persons, not in a mystical sense but in a practical one. A root that has been guided for forty years has a history. A wall-stem that has thickened around a peg has made a decision about its own strength. A living roof needs feeding, trimming, and occasional replacement. If a section of moss begins to yellow, it is treated like an illness. If a vine is strangling a rail, it is moved, not hated. If a tree dies, it is not immediately removed if it can become a nurse log, a mushroom bed, or a frog shelter, though its structural role is reassessed. The hollow’s architecture is therefore partly carpentry and partly cultivation. The builders are also gardeners, and the gardeners are also civic engineers.

This gives the people a particular patience. They do not expect results in a day. A root may take years to become a railing. A trained arch must be tied, teased, pruned, and trusted. A roof becomes stronger as it grows older, but only if it has been properly cared for. A cistern improves as mineral deposits and moss seal its seams. A path becomes safe only after many feet have taught it shape. Children are given tasks whose completion they may not see. A girl may tie a young stem into a ladder rail knowing that her younger brother will use it when it is strong. An old man may repair a channel that his grandmother first cut. This long attention is not romanticized; it is necessary. The hollow punishes haste in small, wet ways: a softened plank, a slipped terrace, a collapsed basket, a flooded market corner.

Their unwritten rules are simple but severe.

Water must move.  
No surface should be left bare if it can be covered.  
A living support is not cut unless it can be replaced or retrained.  
Animals that help are given room.  
Animals that threaten food or children are watched and, if necessary, removed, but not out of panic.  
A path is shared before it is owned.  
A blocked channel is everyone’s emergency.  
A nest in the way is a delay, not an insult.  
Fire is carried, not left loose.  
What falls must be caught or cleared.  
What rots must be watched, because rot is both death and soil.

They enforce these rules less by punishment than by shame, habit, and assigned labor. If someone blocks a watercourse through carelessness, that person clears it and then tends it for a season. If someone cuts too much green, they must plant, train, or carry moss until the wound is healed. If someone harms a useful animal without cause, they may be required to work with the nest-wardens or frog-tenders until they remember the balance. Serious crimes exist, of course—violence, theft, cruelty, sabotage—but even these are judged in light of the hollow’s fragility. To harm a water channel deliberately is worse than breaking a cup. To set fire to a living wall is almost unthinkable.

Their beauty is likewise practical and saturated. They find loveliness in wet shine, in moss thickness, in the curve of a root that has accepted a shape over decades, in the sudden pale flip of leaves when wind enters, in a flock scattering out of the canopy, in the blue-black wing of a swallow, in the glass eyes of a frog, in the spiral of a fern unfurling, in the silver trails of snails after a shower. Their songs often imitate layered sound: dripping, tapping, calling, wingbeats, market voices. Their stories are full of trickster frogs, patient roots, careless droughts, hungry winds, and children who saved a settlement by noticing a single wrong sound. They tell tales where the hero is not the one who kills the monster but the one who clears the blockage before the flood comes. That says much about them.

They also have tenderness. They are not merely tough. In a crowded wet place, small kindnesses keep the mind from souring. Someone will hang a dry cloth near a worker’s sleeping shelf. A vendor will put aside a sweet berry for a child who carried messages all morning. An old climber too stiff for the heights may still sit and mend nets, telling the young ones where to place their feet. When a person dies, the hollow becomes quieter for a time in the way that many small sounds make silence more noticeable. The dead are not laid carelessly into bare ground. In many such places, bodies are returned to compost or placed in hollow logs where roots can take them slowly, moss can cover them, and the living settlement can continue without creating unstable graves. The exact customs vary, but the feeling behind them is often the same: the hollow fed its people; the people give themselves back without breaking the floor that carries the living.

Children grow up soaked, noisy, and highly competent. They are not shielded from the place; they are introduced to it. Babies are kept in slings or suspended cradles with moss padding and small canopies, close to warmth and breath. Toddlers learn to grip. Young children run messages along branch walks because they are light and quick, but they are also taught to stop when a rail feels wrong. They learn to carry cups without spilling, to duck under low nests, to step over toads, to recognize the sound of a loosening shutter. They play games that are secretly training: balancing on wet wood, catching drips in the mouth, imitating bird calls, tying knots behind their backs, racing through market crowds without touching baskets. They are smudged, damp, insect-bitten, and usually busy. They are also watched by many eyes, because in the hollow every adult is at least partly responsible for every child.

Outsiders who enter often notice the noise first. Then the wetness. Then the crowding. Then the strange intimacy of so many lives moving within arm’s reach. A visitor from a drier, wider country may feel enclosed, touched too often, watched by too many birds, deafened by frogs, irritated by insects, unnerved by the way the walkways bend and spring. The people of the hollow are usually patient with such visitors, but not endlessly. They dislike heavy boots that tear moss. They dislike careless hands pulling at living supports. They dislike open flames. They dislike anyone who calls the frogs vermin, kicks a beetle, or tries to straighten a trained branch by force. They will offer dry mats, hot broth, and advice if a stranger is respectful, but they expect a guest to learn the first law quickly: move with the place, not against it.

At dusk, the hollow changes rather than empties. The bird noise shifts from day business to evening argument. Swifts may still cut the upper light, but lower levels grow shadowed. Lanterns appear: horn panes, oiled cloth, small clay lamps shielded from drips. Their glow turns wet surfaces into amber and green. Moths gather near light and are shooed or netted. Frogs become louder, taking over the sound-world from birds. Water seems louder too, because human voices settle. The market slows. Baskets are covered. Food is hung higher. Nets are checked. Doors are tied but not fully closed, because the night still needs to breathe. People eat in small groups, sitting on root shelves or inside trunk chambers, their faces lit from below, their clothes steaming faintly if they have been near warmth. The smell of smoke, wet leaves, and cooked mushrooms becomes the smell of home.

At night, there are watchers. Not soldiers in any grand sense, but people who listen. Someone checks the upper channels after wind. Someone makes sure a hearth has cooled. Someone notes whether a storm is building above the rim by the behavior of leaves and the pressure in the air. Sometimes they carry a small horn or rattle to warn of falling water, loose branches, or an animal in the wrong place. Mostly, the night belongs to the hollow’s older sounds: drip, creak, frog, moth-wing, the soft settling of wood, the breathing of many small lives packed into a green room. The people sleep inside that sound. They are not afraid of it. It is the silence that would wake them.

If you had to say what kind of people live there, you might say: they are the people of the drip, the channel, and the trained root. They are not conquerors. They are not hermits. They are not wilderness savages, and they are not city-builders in the usual sense. They are urban in their density, trade, gossip, and shared labor, but rural in their dependence on living systems. They are mountain people in their tolerance for cold, height, and sudden weather, but hollow people in their closeness, dampness, and green shadow. They are practical, loud, observant, and unsentimental, yet capable of deep reverence for small lives. They know that a frog may save a food store, that a bird may reveal a storm, that a root may hold a house, that moss may keep a floor from sliding away. They have built a world in which almost nothing is separate.

And if you had to say what else lives there, you would have to list them all: the swifts stitching the upper air, the wrens raging in the eaves, the finches plucking seeds from damp heads, the hawks whose shadows make the hollow hold its breath, the mice in the thatch, the shrews in the root floors, the martens moving like quick rust through the branches, the frogs blinking in leaf cups, the toads under boards, the salamanders beneath loose bark, the snails writing silver on railings, the beetles carrying their business across logs, the ants keeping roads finer than any human road, the moths sleeping like folded wet leaves, the bees working flowering vines in short bursts, the larvae in compost, the fish or crabs in the basins if the mountain water ever gave them, the fungi breaking death into food, the moss holding everything in soft green fists, the roots climbing and gripping and thickening around human intentions.

The truest answer is that the hollow lives in them as much as they live in it. Their hands have shaped it, but it has shaped their hands. Their voices carry because the air is thick and noisy; their feet are careful because the ground is alive; their laws are water-laws; their kindness is maintenance; their beauty is saturation. They are not numerous compared to the insects, not larger than the trees, not stronger than weather. But they are the part of the hollow that can tie a knot, clear a channel, tell a child to step lightly, and laugh when a gust drops a whole roof of rain onto an already wet crowd.

Who lives there?

The wet-adapted, the root-trained, the bird-watching, frog-tolerating, moss-keeping, rope-twisting, channel-listening people of the green hollow live there. Their children live there. Their birds live there. Their frogs, beetles, snails, moths, martens, mushrooms, and trained trees live there. Water lives there, and has the first claim. The place is crowded with lives, but none of them is merely decoration. Every resident, human or not, is part of the working body of the hollow. They do not live inside a landscape so much as inside a long negotiation. And the negotiation is still going on, drip by drip, root by root, voice by voice, wingbeat by wingbeat, in the cool green wetness of the mountain room.
