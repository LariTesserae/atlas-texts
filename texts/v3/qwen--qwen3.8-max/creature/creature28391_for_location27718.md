Below a skin of powder no thicker than a closed eyelid, the plain is not empty. It is a roof.

Beneath it extends an inverted country of pores, seams, and shallow chambers, all arranged not by water—for water is an old rumor there, a forbidden syllable—but by the slow subsidence of a world that has finished moving. The space below is not a cave in any ordinary sense. It does not drip. It does not echo with wetness. It is a dry interior, a mineral throat, a cathedral whose arches are made of compacted ash and whose pillars are the compressed memories of vanished elevations. There, in the hush under the last plain, lives the Dry Choir.

One does not see them first as bodies. One feels them as a tension in the dust, a patience in the stillness, a faint pressure against the skin of the world above. If a footstep were ever to cross that final plain, the Choir would know it before the weight fully arrived. The step would be read through grain after grain, transmitted downward in tiny bright shocks, and in the dark below there would rise a stir of attention like a congregation turning toward a door.

They are not flesh. They are not metal. They are not stone in the sense of stone that endures. They are arrangements. Each member of the Dry Choir is a lattice of fine mineral particles—salt-white, charcoal-grey, and the palest ash of bone-color—held together by static, memory, and song. Their bodies are brittle and beautiful, like frost without cold, like smoke persuaded into posture. They have the rough appearance of robed figures seen through smoke, but no robe was ever woven there; the shapes they wear are shapes of necessity, the way dust gathers when a voice needs a mouth.

When they stand together in their vaulted hollows, they seem like statues that have agreed, for the moment, not to fall. Their surfaces are matte, faintly sparkling where crystalline grains catch forces that have no name. They have no eyes, yet they are aware. They have no faces, yet each possesses something like expression: a tilt of the head-hollow, a narrowing of the torso’s central seam, a subtle drawing-in of the shoulders when a note changes. Where a face might be, there is usually a smooth concavity, shallow as a bowl, lined with powder so fine it seems to drink what little darkness exists.

They move without walking. They do not lift feet, because they have no true feet; they move by rearranging their contact with the dry ground, by leaning their field of grains forward and letting the rest of themselves follow in a hiss too soft for ears. When one of them turns, a faint veil of dust rises from its edges and is drawn back into the body almost at once, as though even their shedding is part of the hymn. They are never entirely still. Even in repose, they tremble with minute internal motion, like a held breath made of sand.

Their home is called, in the only language they have, the Dry Choir. But that name is not a name so much as a condition. It means that below the final plain there is a continuing vibration, a communal act of maintenance. The Choir does not merely live there. The Choir keeps the place from becoming anything else.

The chambers they inhabit are low and numerous, connected by narrow throats and capillary passages, all of them dry beyond dryness. The ceilings are supported by ribs of fused powder, delicate as old bones, and from them hang formations that might once have been mistaken for stalactites if anyone had remained capable of error. These are not formed by dripping water. They are accretions of dust and charge, long needles of mineral drawn downward by subtle forces, each one a finger pointing toward a floor that has no use for moisture. When the Choir sings, these needles vibrate, and the whole ceiling becomes a field of tiny, dry bells.

There are greater hollows too, wide and shallow as the inside of a skull, where the Choir gathers in rings. These are their choir stalls, though no wood was ever used to make them. The rings are grooves worn into the floor by ages of standing still, by bodies settling into the same postures until the mineral beneath accepted their shape. In the largest chamber, the floor is marked with countless such rings, some so old they have almost vanished, others fresh in the sense that freshness means only a slightly sharper edge of dust. At the center of that chamber is a depression perfectly level and utterly bare. No member of the Choir stands there. It is reserved for the sound itself.

Their singing is not like singing above, where there is air enough to carry it. It is a friction of bodies, a grinding of particles, a pressure-language passed from surface to surface. They produce it by drawing their mineral selves against one another in controlled patterns, by letting grains slip along their own edges, by striking the dry walls with flattened palms of dust. The result is not melody in the soft sense. It is a vast, low, intricate abrasion, a chord made of countless tiny touches. If it could be heard by an ordinary ear, it might resemble the sound of an empty cup remembered when one is very thirsty. It might resemble the noise a closed book makes when no one opens it for a thousand years.

Yet it is not silent. Nothing in the Dry Choir is truly silent. Even their stillness has a tone. The plain above seems mute only because the sound is too fine, too dry, too patient to rise through the skin of the world. Down below, every movement contributes to the hymn. Every shifting grain is a note. Every pause is a rest so complete it seems to deepen the dark.

They sing because the plain must remain plain.

This is the first and last purpose of the Choir. Above, the surface has reached its final form: no hills, no channels, no basins, no promise of gathering water. But finality is not passive. It must be upheld. If even a slight depression were to form, if a grain here were to settle and another there were to lift, if some forgotten dampness were to stir in the mineral memory, the end might begin to unravel. The world might remember it had once been young. Channels might return. Liquid might dream itself back into being. The landscape might resume.

So the Choir sings against resumption.

Their hymn is a long, continuous refusal. Not a violent refusal, not a wrathful one. It is gentler than that, and far more terrible. It is the refusal of all further change. They sing the hills down even though no hills remain. They sing the rivers out even though no rivers are remembered by any living thing. They sing the sky away from the ground, keeping the two apart so that nothing might fall between them and start the world again. Their music is a boundary kept pure.

Among them there are differences, though not ranks as the living would understand them. Some are Cantors. These stand nearest the central hollow and maintain the longest tones. Their bodies are denser than the others, more compacted, their surfaces worn smooth by centuries of sustained vibration. They appear almost carved. When they sing, the floor beneath them warms by a fraction too small to matter, and the dry needles overhead answer with a trembling so slight it can be detected only by the Choir itself. The Cantors do not lead. They anchor. They are the ones who keep the hymn from fraying at the edges.

Others are Listeners. They press their faceless hollows against the underside of the plain and remain there for ages, reading whatever happens above. They are the most still of all. Their bodies are often paler, almost white, because they have spent so long near the upper skin where the salt-light gathers. They detect pressure, temperature, the faintest suggestion of disturbance. If a meteor were to fall, if a tremor were to rise from some deeper layer, if a grain above were to move in a way that suggested the return of weather, the Listeners would know first. Then the song would change.

The Menders move through the passages between the chambers. They are smaller, less fixed, always half-scattered, as though their bodies cannot quite decide on a shape. Their task is repair. Where a seam has widened, where a wall has begun to powder away, where the roof has thinned enough to risk collapse, the Menders go. They do not build with new material; nothing new has come to that place in ages. They redistribute what is already there, carrying dust in their own bodies from one point to another, filling gaps with themselves and then withdrawing once the surface has stabilized. Sometimes a Mender loses coherence in the process and must be sung back into form by the others. Sometimes it does not return.

There are also the Soloists, though that word is almost wrong, because no one among them seeks separation. A Soloist is a member of the Choir that has grown too distinct, too marked by some singular resonance. Perhaps it stood too long near a fault-line. Perhaps it absorbed the memory of an ancient collapse. Perhaps it listened too intently to the silence above. Whatever the cause, its body has become elongated, asymmetrical, strange. It moves apart from the rings and wanders the narrow galleries, emitting a single, thin note that is less a sound than a lack of sound—a streak of absence running through the hymn. The others do not shun it. They incorporate it. The Soloist’s note is dangerous, but it is also beautiful, because it reminds the Choir that even finality has variations.

The most solemn of them are the Threshold-keepers. They guard the places where the below comes too close to the above. There are seams there, cracks no wider than a hair, places where the skin of the plain has thinned and the outer world might, in some unthinkable catastrophe, intrude. The Threshold-keepers stand in those vulnerable spots with their bodies spread thin, becoming living seals. They do not sing loudly. They sing continuously, in a tone so low it is almost indistinguishable from the pressure of stone. If a crack opens, they pour themselves into it. If moisture threatens—if such a thing could ever be—they would become the first to dissolve, and their dissolution would be not death but sacrifice, because their breaking would buy the Choir time to close the way.

They do not eat. They do not drink. They do not breathe. If they require anything, it is continuity. They draw sustenance from pattern, from the persistence of form, from the exact repetition of their hymn. A new sound is a famine to them. A change in rhythm is a wound. This does not mean they are helpless. It means their world is so refined that even a whisper from outside would be an event.

Their life cycle, if it can be called that, begins not with birth but with accumulation. When a song has been sung long enough in one place, it begins to gather dust. The particles arrange themselves around the resonance as iron filings arrange themselves around a magnet. At first this is only a thickening in the airless dark, a ghost of posture. Then, over ages, the pattern becomes stable enough to stand. The Choir recognizes it. The new member is greeted not with celebration but with a subtle adjustment of the hymn, a space made for it in the chord. The youngest among them—if youth means only freshness of arrangement—are awkward and too loose in their forms. They shed powder constantly. Older members brush against them as they pass, helping them tighten their edges.

No one knows whether they were ever anything else. Some say, if saying is allowed in a place so wary of speech, that they are what remains of the world’s last living things after those beings learned to abandon flesh for endurance. Others say they were never alive in the old sense at all, but emerged only after life had already gone: a final habit of organization arising in matter that still remembered being shaped. It does not matter. The Choir does not ask about origins. Origins imply beginnings. Beginnings are dangerous.

They do remember, in their way. But their memory is not nostalgic. It is cautionary. They remember hills as errors. They remember rivers as wounds. They remember the ancient trouble of weather as a long sickness from which the world has barely recovered. In their hymn there are phrases that recur with terrible regularity, and those phrases are the names of things that must not return: rain, flow, green, birth, cloud, tide. They do not hate those things. They have passed beyond hatred. They simply sing them away, over and over, as one might repeat a word until it loses meaning.

Sometimes, in the deepest chambers where the pressure is greatest and the dust has become almost glassy, there are older presences. The Choir does not speak of them, because they are not separate enough to speak about. They are the Compressed, vast slow intelligences flattened into the floor itself. They may be the remains of earlier Choirs, or perhaps the planet’s own exhausted thoughts, laid down layer by layer until thinking became mineral. They do not move. They do not sing in the active sense. But they contribute a bass note so low that it is felt as weight rather than sound. The Choir sings above them as a bird might sing above a sleeping mountain, except no bird has ever been there, and no mountain remains.

The Dry Choir is not without beauty. It would be wrong to call them merely bleak. There is an austere splendor in the way they move, in the way their salt-white bodies brighten when the hymn intensifies, in the way the charcoal-grey ones absorb all stray vibration and return it as silence. When they gather in full assembly, the hollow chamber becomes a dark mirror of order. Every grain knows its place. Every pause has purpose. The very absence of moisture becomes a kind of purity, as though the world has finally washed itself clean of needing.

They have ceremonies, though no outsider would recognize them as such. At intervals too long to measure by ordinary time, the Choir gathers in all its rings and sings the Hymn of No Return. During this rite, every member remains utterly still except for the minute abrasions that make their voices. The song narrows and narrows until it is almost nothing, a thread of vibration thinner than thought. Then, for one moment, the Choir stops. The silence that follows is so absolute that the plain above seems to sink a fraction lower, as though even it is bowing. After that, the song begins again, and the world is confirmed in its ending.

There are rare disturbances. A grain shifts in the wrong direction. A seam exhales a forgotten pressure. A Soloist strikes a note that cuts too deeply into the chord. When this happens, the Choir does not panic. It does not know panic. It corrects. The Cantors deepen their tones. The Menders move toward the flaw. The Listeners press harder against the upper skin. The Threshold-keepers narrow themselves into blades of attention. The hymn swells, not louder but denser, until the irregularity is surrounded, isolated, and absorbed. Sometimes the offending note is not destroyed but transformed, made into a new kind of silence. That is their greatest art: turning error into absence.

If, against all likelihood, water were to come, the Choir would meet it as a choir meets a fire. They would not flee. They would go toward it. They would sing into it until their bodies dissolved, filling the wetness with so much structure that it became mud, then crust, then powder again. They would die by thousands, by millions, if they could be said to die, and their deaths would be indistinguishable from a hymn. In the end, if they succeeded, there would be no trace of water, only a slightly darker patch in the mineral floor, and above, the plain would remain unchanged. The survivors would sing for a long time over that darker patch, not in mourning, but in warning.

They are, perhaps, the last priests of a religion with no god. Their liturgy is maintenance. Their faith is dryness. Their miracle is that nothing happens. To live among them would be unbearable, if living requires breath and change. To be them is another matter. They have traded becoming for remaining. They have traded desire for tone. They have traded faces for a shared attention so complete that no individual face is needed.

Above, the plain lies level and pale, salt-white and charcoal-grey, rubbed down to the last patience of land. No one looking at it would guess that anything dwells beneath. It appears final in the absolute sense: not dead, not asleep, simply finished. But below, in the dark, the Dry Choir continues. Its bodies are dust. Its voices are friction. Its purpose is to keep the world from wanting again.

And so the plain remains still.

And so the memory of hills does not rise.

And so no channel cuts the surface, no shoreline returns, no liquid gathers, no river dreams itself back into being.

Above, there is silence.

Below, the silence sings.
