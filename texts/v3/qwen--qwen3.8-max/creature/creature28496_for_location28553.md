What lives there is not one kind of life, but a pale assembly of lives arranged around attention.

The first inhabitants are not the people, nor even the animals, but the living skins of the stone itself. They are the microbial films that breathe over wet limestone, the chalky crusts that thicken where seepage is slow, the slime-layers that make smooth floors look as though they have been varnished with milk, and the fungal felts that spread in damp cracks like wool left too long in cold water. These surfaces are quiet, patient, and essential. They are gray-white, bone-pale, ash-blue, sometimes faintly luminous in the places where moisture lies longest. They do not wave, bloom, or rustle as green things do. They adhere. They thicken. They peel. They return.

To the people of the basin, these living skins are not scenery. They are pasture, garden, storehouse, and sacred surface all at once. The inhabitants do not speak of them as “wild” or “tame,” because neither word fits well. They speak instead of surfaces that are awake, surfaces that are resting, surfaces that are hungry, surfaces that are ready. They move across the basin with the care of people walking over a sleeping body.

Where the living skins thicken, the fauna comes.

In the shallow pans, blind crustaceans move like thoughts made visible. They are broad-backed and pale, their shells the color of wet chalk, their legs slow and deliberate. They rake the microbial crust with fine comb-like mouthparts, leaving faint parallel grooves that vanish almost as soon as water smooths them again. When a seep is about to open, they appear from hidden crevices before the people do, and their arrival is one of the signs the damp-readers watch for. Their molted shells are gathered with reverence; they become cups, scoops, light reflectors, small ceremonial rattles, and sometimes the curved ribs of infant pens. Nothing is taken from them carelessly. Even their cast-off armor is considered a kind of gift.

Translucent worms rise in the water when pressure changes. They are nearly invisible until the light catches the line of fluid inside their bodies. They hang in vertical stillness, then sink, then rise again, as if testing the mood of the air. Children learn to read them before they learn many hand-signs. A pool full of rising worms means weather in the ceiling: mist gathering, condensation thickening, a thin rain soon to fall from the high stone throats.

Flat, eyeless grazers move over the wet rock in slow processions. They resemble beetles only in the sense that they are armored and low, but their bodies are wider, softer-edged, and more flexible, adapted to sliding over mineral crust without breaking it. They graze on the palest films and are themselves grazed upon by larger creatures when the basin fills with temporary water. Their shells, when they die, become small plates for sorting salt, dust, and bone beads. In some districts, people encourage them by leaving damp corridors undisturbed for long intervals. They are not herded so much as permitted.

In the deeper cisterns live the fish without pigment. They are among the most beautiful things in the basin, though beauty there is not bright or flamboyant. Their bodies are glassy, their spines visible as fine lines, their movements quick but soundless. When reflected light passes through the water, they flash not with color but with clarity. They turn in schools as though sharing one mind, spreading, contracting, vanishing into the pale depths, then reappearing along the walls. The people do not chase them. They wait for times when the fish gather near the edges after a change in mineral taste, and then they harvest with nets of fungal fiber and sinew, moving slowly so as not to startle the school into scattering for days.

Wingless insects cluster on damp walls like living ash. They are small, gray-white, and numerous, forming patches that seem to breathe. They feed on films and dust and are food for larger things in turn. When disturbed, they do not buzz; they scatter in a soft hissing whisper of tiny feet. Their clusters are left alone unless needed, because they are one of the first signs that a wall has become moist enough to support larger life.

Then come the scavengers.

They descend from higher galleries when moisture returns, drawn by the smell of drowned dust, dead insects, fish waste, and the rich decay that gathers in newly filled hollows. They are soft-footed, low-slung, cautious, and mostly mute. Some are long-bodied and pale; others are broader, with padded limbs and blunt faces. They do not roar, snap, or snarl. They work with a quiet intensity, cleaning the basin of what has died so that what lives may continue. The people tolerate them, even welcome them, though they keep children and drying racks away when the scavengers are numerous. In the basin’s logic, the scavengers are not unclean. They are finishers. They are the ones who close a circle.

There are predators too, but fewer, and they are harder to see.

They live in the margins: long-limbed, pale, patient things that wait near seeps and mist-thickened hollows. Some have bodies so thin they seem made of stretched skin and silence. Others are flat and broad, lying against mineral crust until a grazer passes too near. They do not often threaten people, but they can take young crustaceans, small fish, and unwary insects. The basin folk know their shapes and habits. They do not try to eliminate them, because predators keep the grazers moving, and moving grazers prevent any one patch of living crust from being stripped bare. Instead, people learn the signs of their presence: a certain stillness in a pool, a faint drag-mark in wet dust, the abrupt absence of smaller fauna from a hollow that was full a day before.

All this life appears in pulses.

At times the basin seems almost mineral alone: bright stone, still water, empty plazas, light moving across walls without being watched by many eyes. Then a seep opens. Mist thickens. A pool fills in a pan that had been dry for longer than memory. Channels appear. The crustaceans emerge. The worms rise. The fish turn toward fresh mineral taste. The grazers spread over newly wet stone. The scavengers arrive. The predators follow. For a short time, the basin is intensely alive, full of movement in every hollow and along every damp terrace. Then the water retreats, the mist thins, and life draws back into cracks, cisterns, and hidden galleries, leaving behind white residue, tracks, molted shells, and silence.

Into this pulse-world live the people.

They are pale, but not in the sickly or ghostly sense. Their skin carries the colors of the basin: bone-white, chalk-gray, faint ash-blue, sometimes a shell-pink around the knuckles and cheeks where blood lies close to the surface. Their bodies are marked by mineral dust and water. Fine powder settles into the creases of their hands, the hollows of their collarbones, the lines around their eyes. They wash often, but the basin leaves its trace on them. They smell of wet chalk, cold salt, hide, fungal felt, and the faint clean sharpness of chert freshly flaked.

They are not tall in the way surface people might imagine tallness. Their bodies are compact, balanced, and careful. They have learned to move across smooth stone without slipping, to crouch low when working over pools, to step with the outer edge of the foot first when testing a damp floor. Their legs and feet are strong; their toes are long and sensitive, able to read the difference between dry marl, wet crust, and a surface about to seep. Many go barefoot. Those who wear foot-coverings use soft hide or layered fungal felt, but even then they prefer to feel the ground.

Their hands are their most evident inheritance. The fingers are not delicate, but extraordinarily precise. Palms are calloused in patterns specific to their trades: mirror-keepers have smooth patches from polishing shell-bright panels; net-makers have grooved fingertips from drawing cord; bone-shapers have hardened thumbs from pressing antler and chert; damp-readers have sensitive hands that seem almost blind in their attentiveness, always hovering near stone to feel coolness, moisture, and subtle changes in texture. The people touch the world constantly, but not graspingly. They touch to listen.

Their eyes are adapted to the basin’s cool brightness. They are not great black pits made for darkness, because the basin is not dark. Instead, their eyes are clear and pale, often gray, whitish-blue, or the color of wet shell. They are accustomed to diffused light, reflected glare from mineral crust, and sudden shifts when water films brighten or dim a district. They blink slowly. They are good at noticing small movement at a distance: a crack in a mirror panel, a bead of moisture forming on a ceiling rib, a school of fish turning in a cistern, a child’s hand-sign across a plaza. They do not stare hard. They watch widely.

Their voices are low.

They can speak, but they rarely raise their voices, and loudness is considered more than rude: it is clumsy, even dangerous. In a place where small signals carry, where sudden noise can scatter animals, startle workers on high mirror paths, or break the concentration of those reading damp, a shout is a kind of violence. The people use speech mostly for close conversation, for intimate matters, for naming things that require nuance. Public life is carried by hand-sign, body posture, tapped rhythm, and the arrangement of objects.

A tilted shell on a causeway means water has changed course. A line of pale dust across a ramp means the path is temporarily closed. A bone token laid point-up near a platform means a seep is expected beneath it. A hand moved slowly downward with fingers spread means “wait.” A palm pressed to the throat and then extended means “I heard you and will answer later.” There are hundreds of such signs, and children learn them before they can walk steadily over wet stone.

They have names, but names are not usually shouted across plazas. A name is often a rhythm tapped on bone, accompanied by a gesture unique to the person. Some names are inherited, passed from an elder to a child who shows a similar temperament. Others are earned. A child who first notices the rising of pressure-worms in a particular pool may receive a name that means “early watcher.” A mirror-keeper who restores a broken light-path after a ceiling drip may be called, in later life, by a tapped phrase meaning “returned brightness.” Names are not fixed forever. They can settle, change, or be layered as a person becomes more fully themselves.

The people call themselves by a phrase that is difficult to translate into surface language. The closest meaning is “those who answer the damp.” In hand speech, the sign is made by lowering one palm toward the other, then lifting the lower hand slowly as if moisture were rising to meet it. They do not think of themselves as owners of the basin. They think of themselves as participants in its moods.

Their bodies are adorned simply. They wear wraps of softened hide, vests of layered fungal felt, belts of twisted sinew, and pouches made from shell or cured skin. Fasteners are carved from bone, antler, chitin, or polished shell. Where surface peoples might value gold or bright metal, the basin people value polish, fit, and age. A smooth shell button worn by many hands is more precious than a newly made one. A cloak patched with pieces of old hide is not shameful; it shows continuity. Ornaments include fish vertebrae strung on cord, chips of translucent chert, pale calcite beads, small crustacean carapaces, and shells that have been rubbed until they glow with a soft, milky luster. No one wears green. The idea itself would feel alien.

Their status is shown less by richness than by responsibility. The most respected among them are not the loudest or the most decorated, but those whose attention is most trusted. Mirror-keepers are especially honored, because light is common inheritance. They tend the shell-bright panels, the polished limestone faces, the angled slabs that pass daylight deeper into the basin. They climb carefully along worn paths, carrying pads of soft felt, fine abrasive dust, and cords for adjusting angles. They know the seasonal drift of the shafts, the way a film of moisture can scatter light, the way a single misplaced shadow can dim a drying court. They are calm, exacting people. To block a light-path without cause is an offense among them, not because it steals property, but because it diminishes the shared world.

Damp-readers are another revered kind. They are the ones who know the moods of water. They press their palms to stone, watch the behavior of worms, listen to faint drips in upper galleries, smell the mineral sharpness of the air, and read the softening of dust in hollows. They do not command water. They anticipate it. They can say, with quiet certainty, that a seep will open beneath a certain terrace before most others can feel any change.
