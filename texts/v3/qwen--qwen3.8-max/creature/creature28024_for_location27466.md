At first, it seems that nothing lives there — or rather, that life has been omitted from the place, left out as an unnecessary detail in a world still being drafted. There is no greenness, no softness, no moisture, no breath. There is only heat, mineral sharpness, and a hard white light that does not fall so much as remain.

But if one stays with the hollow long enough, the question changes. It is no longer “What lives here?” but “What kind of living has not yet been excluded?”

What lives there does not begin as a body. It begins as a gradient. Heat finds a difference; charge finds a path; dust finds a surface where it can become crust. In that tension, life appears not as a population of familiar creatures but as a set of stubborn, anhydrous forms: mineral metabolisms, static gardens, slow electric botanies, and perhaps something larger, something not quite separable from the hollow itself.

The visible inhabitants are few. They are spaced across the chamber with the precision of components, not with the accident of seeds scattered by wind or dropped by passing animals. No animal has passed here. No bird has carried anything into this place. No foot has disturbed the copper-bright dust. The forms that exist are isolated clusters, each one surrounded by broad stretches of bare floor, as if each growth requires a certain amount of charge, a certain thermal territory, a certain silence in which to complete its work.

They are plants only by courtesy. They are not green in any ordinary sense, because green here would be a lie: too wet, too gentle, too dependent on the memory of rain. Their colors are the colors of process. Some are wax-dark and heat-glossy, as though their surfaces have been polished by temperature rather than by touch. Some are pale as sulfate, translucent at their thin edges, brittle with newness. Others are tipped with copper rust, blackened where their chemistry runs too hot, or dusted with crystalline crusts that look less like bark or bloom than like residue from a manufacturing step.

The wax-dark forms crouch in shallow basins where the floor gives off the strongest heat. Their leaves are layered like mica, but not soft; they are laminated plates, glossy and sealed, flexible only when warm. In the hard light they look almost oiled, though there is no oil in them, only a surface kept sleek by continual warmth. They take in light and heat and do not return much. They seem to bank energy rather than spend it. At their margins they are often blackened, not because they are dead, but because their metabolism approaches the edge of what their own structure can endure. They live near their limit, and that limit is written into them as scorch.

When the static pressure in the hollow rises, these dark forms open slightly. They do not bloom with beauty. They open as shutters open, as valves open, as inspection panels might open if the chamber had inspectors. Between their layered leaves, copper-bright membranes are exposed, thin and charged. Static gathers there. Then comes a tiny blue-white tick, a discharge so small it is almost delicate. Dust nearby is welded into crust. The plant, if it is a plant, has done something that feels like work: it has converted excess charge into structure, accident into footing, loose matter into surface.

Other inhabitants are pale and crystalline. They grow where the mineral veins branch and where the floor’s chemistry changes subtly from seam to seam. They are fans, rosettes, stacked plates, ridged forms that look as though they were cut from salt but were never dissolved in water. They are brittle, embryonic, sharp-edged. They have not been rounded by anything. They have no history of weather. Each one seems newly emitted, as if the hollow has only just printed it into being and has not yet decided whether to keep it.

These pale forms feed on gradients. One side of a plate may be warmer than the other. One vein may carry a slightly different ionic charge than the next. The growth answers those differences by making more of itself: another plate, another ridge, another crystalline leaf. Its repetition has the regularity of manufacture. A row of identical parts may form along a single stem or seam, each one a repeated solution to the same thermal problem. Sometimes the repetition is not perfect. A plate may be half-formed, or present only as a bright outline in the dust, or visible from one angle and absent from another. This is not damage. It is the hollow’s unfinished condition showing through.

In the lower corners, along seams and hairline fractures, live the filamentous forms. They are closer to wire than to leaf. Fine metallic fibers spread over the mineral floor in thin mats: gray-gold, black, bronze, sometimes so pale they seem to be ash given structure. They are not soft. They are not inviting. If touched, they would likely prick the skin with static rather than yield like moss. They bloom in infrared, opening tiny thermal flowers invisible to eyes accustomed to ordinary light. Their work, if work is the word, is repair and connection.

When a crust cools and contracts, when a mineral surface ticks apart under thermal stress, the filaments are already near. They bridge the gap. Charge passes through them. Dust is drawn into the seam. A new crust is welded into place. They are the hollow’s slow electricians, though they have no hands, no intention, no toolbox. They are task made into growth. They are maintenance become body.

Then there are the tall forms, the most conspicuous because they rise above the rest. They are sparse, thin, segmented, dark at their bases and crowned with dry luminous spores. They look less like trees than like antennas or instruments left standing in a room that has not yet been named. Their bodies are conduits. Thermal columns pass through them, and their coiled sections flex with the patience of pistons. Nothing hurries in them. Nothing is wasted on motion for its own sake. They move only when heat moves through them, and even then their movement is so slow that it seems remembered rather than seen.

At their crowns they carry spore-cases that glow faintly, not with the soft glow of bioluminescence but with charged mineral dust held in suspension. When the hollow’s electric balance reaches a threshold, the crowns open. The spores are released into thermal updrafts, but they do not drift randomly. They travel in precise lines, following field paths invisible to the eye. They move from charged point to charged point, from stalk to seam, from one waiting surface to another. Sometimes they arrive before the flower that releases them has opened. Sometimes they seem to come from a stalk that has not yet grown. Here, cause and effect are not absent, but they are looser, stretched across a longer and stranger grammar of time.

These beings reproduce without fauna. There is no pollinator, no crawler, no winged carrier, no animal body to brush against a spore-case and carry it elsewhere. Reproduction in the hollow is electrical, thermal, crystalline. Pollen, if it can be called pollen, moves along charge gradients. Spores ride updrafts created by the hollow’s own heat engines. Crystal fracture sends minute fragments into the air, where they settle on surfaces already prepared to receive them. Some forms reproduce by repeating themselves mineralogically: a seam refines itself, produces repeated crystalline parts, and those parts become new growths when conditions allow.

Union here is not flesh meeting flesh. It is field meeting field. It is potential becoming discharge. It is one charged point finding another across dry air. The intimacy of the hollow is not tender, but it is exact. Nothing is casual. Every transfer has a path, a threshold, a moment of readiness.

No animal life exists here, and that absence shapes everything. There are no mouths. No throats. No eyes with wet surfaces. No muscles, no shells, no nests, no tracks.
