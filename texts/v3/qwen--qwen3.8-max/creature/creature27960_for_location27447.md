What lives in that bright green hollow cannot first be called a creature. The place does not present life as separate figures moving across a stage. It presents life as stage, wall, floor, ceiling, air, and water all at once. To ask who lives there is therefore to ask the wrong question at first. The better question is: what is so alive that it has become geography?

The first inhabitant is the basin itself.

Not as a single organism with a center, a brain, a will, or a face. It is not a body in the animal sense. But it has the logic of a body. It has membranes. It has channels. It has chambers, pores, heat, moisture, circulation. The ceiling is not a roof so much as a breathing skin. The floor is not ground so much as compressed living flesh. The columns are not pillars but limbs that have forgotten how to bend. The hanging roots are not curtains but lashes, or valves, or slow thoughts made visible. The whole place lives as an organ lives: without declaring itself.

It does not rule itself. It does not command. It adjusts.

If there is a “who” in the basin, it begins there: in the immense, impersonal livingness of the green mass. The basin is awake in the way a leaf is awake, in the way warm fruit is awake, in the way a swelling bud is awake. It receives light. It distributes heat. It gathers water and lets it go. It holds shape without accumulating past. It is the oldest resident and the youngest thing there.

Yet if one waits in that place long enough—if waiting means anything in a region without true hours—one begins to notice forms that seem more gathered than the rest. Not separate from the basin, but intensified by it. Local knots of presence.

These are the things one might almost call people, if the word had been washed clean of bones, faces, tools, and history.
