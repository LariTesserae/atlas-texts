What lives in the basin has the character of the basin itself: clear, cool, sparse, exact, and almost unbearably present without being numerous. Life there has not conquered the stone. It has entered into the stone’s long agreement. Nothing tries to thicken into forests, herds, colonies, or settlements. Everything exists as a small, deliberate disturbance in the mineral stillness—a flash, a rasp, a wingbeat, a bead of water lifted and gone.

The first living things one becomes aware of are the slow ones. The lichens are the basin’s oldest visible inhabitants: black-green crusts like old varnish, orange patches like rust breathed onto pale quartz, gray-white rosettes so tight to the rock that they seem less like organisms than discolorations of the stone itself. They do not wave, do not bloom, do not lean. They absorb the passing dampness through their entire surfaces, waking briefly when mist beads on them, then returning to a dry, patient dormancy. They grow so slowly that the idea of growth becomes almost meaningless. A handspan of lichen may be older than a human memory. In places their presence dulls the glare of the rock; in others, their dark crusts make the light seem to deepen rather than vanish. They are not scenery. They are the basin’s living skin.

The cushion plants are fewer and harder to find. They crouch in fissures and along the shaded lips of hollows where condensation lingers a little longer. They are not soft, despite their name. Their leaves are tiny, stiff, and dry-looking, packed close against wind and cold. They do not form carpets. Each plant is an island, a small knot of endurance separated from the next by meters or tens of meters of bare stone. When they flower, they do so sparingly: pale, unscented blossoms no larger than a grain of rice, opening only during the brightest hours when the air is still enough to permit brief insect visits. Even their greenness feels restrained, as if the basin permits color but discourages excess.

The rock pockets are the basin’s smallest living centers. These shallow wells, no wider than a hand, are lined with microbial films and thin algal skins that turn the water faintly green, brown, or iridescent under the light. When a pocket fills, the film wakes. When it dries, the life within it retreats into dust, cyst, spore, or crust. In these little reservoirs live some of the basin’s most delicate creatures: transparent mites, minute crustaceans, threadlike worms, and rotifer-like animals that endure drying by becoming almost entirely inert. They are not visible from any distance. They are discovered only when one bends close and sees, in a pool no deeper than a thumbnail, a faint movement like a speck deciding to become an animal. These pocket-dwellers feed on microbial layers, on one another, and on the mineral richness that condensation brings from the air. They are the basin’s hidden aquarium, repeated thousands of times across the stone, each one temporary, each one sufficient.

Insects are numerous, but they do not crowd. The most widespread are the midges: tiny, translucent, cold-adapted flies whose wings make a high, glassy sizzle when they move together in loose, sparse ribbons through the air. Individually they are nearly nothing. Collectively, they are an essential thinness. They feed on microbial films, on algal skins, on one another, and on the organic dust the wind carries across the basin. They are not clouds in the lowland sense; they do not darken the air or swarm thickly into the face. They appear as glittering interruptions, brief veils of motion that catch the light and then disperse. Their presence is felt less by sight than by the faint electric quality they add to the already loud air.

The beetles are darker, slower, and more deliberate. Many are black or soot-colored with narrow seams of orange or copper along their wing cases. They move across lichen crusts with a patient, exploring gait, scraping, tasting, turning. Some feed on lichen. Some prey on smaller insects. Some work the edges of water pockets, lifting moisture on hydrophobic bodies or drinking from capillary grooves. Their rasping against the dry crusts is one of the small sounds that joins the basin’s greater sound. They do not burrow deeply. They use cracks, seams, and the undersides of flakes. Their larvae live in the same narrow places, flattened and still, waiting through intervals of dryness. Nothing about them is abundant, but everything about them is precise.

Birds are the most visible of the basin’s larger inhabitants, and in many ways the basin belongs to them more than to any walking creature. The open volume, the bright air, and the constant acoustic pressure favor flight over residence, movement over settlement. The birds here are not dense in number, but they are widely distributed, and their forms have been shaped by the basin’s peculiar demands. Calls are short, hard, and often percussive.
