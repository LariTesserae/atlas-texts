What lives there is not a population in the ordinary sense, not a people moving through streets, not bodies that cross plazas or gather in rooms. The city is not inhabited so much as it is inhabited by itself. Its life is continuous with its form. The terraces, towers, canals, root-vaults, reed-aqueducts, and steaming leaf-roofs are not dwellings made for occupants. They are the occupants, or rather the organs, tissues, membranes, and resonating vessels of a vast distributed existence that has no center, no face, no animal shape, and no need of hands.

The first truth of the basin is that nothing there is faunal. No eye looks from the pools. No wing passes through the steam. No foot marks the wet mineral crust. No mouth feeds, no throat cries, no bone lies hidden in the sediment. The silence of animals is not a lack imposed on the place; it is one of its primary conditions. What lives there lives by growth, deposition, flow, fermentation, sporulation, grafting, and resonance. It is botanical, fungal, microbial, and hydraulic. It is alive without being mobile in the way flesh is mobile. It is aware without being centered in a brain. It is social without being composed of individuals arranged in bodies.

The visible life of the city is the architectural vegetation. It rises in crowded, patient forms across the basin: columnar stems fused into ranks, broad leaves arched into ceilings, root systems thickened into walls, flattened mats grown into floors, hollow tubes serving as pipes, bowl-shaped structures catching drip, towers capped by steaming crowns. These are not plants that have been trained into buildings. They are plants whose very nature has become urban. They do not mimic architecture; they express it directly. Their growth is construction, but construction without tools, without plans held apart from the growing tissue itself. Where a wall is needed, roots thicken and interlock until the partition becomes tough, dense, and continuous. Where a bridge must cross a channel, aerial roots extend, touch, braid, and strengthen over generations until the woven mass can bear water, moss, and hanging weight. Where a roof must shed condensation, leaves grow into overlapping planes with grooved undersides, each groove guiding a bead of moisture toward a specific channel. Where a plaza must remain open, low mats spread horizontally and inhibit vertical growth, maintaining a smooth living floor by pressure and chemical signal rather than by cutting or clearing.

These plant-forms are not motionless in the way a stone is motionless. They move only by growth, by swelling, by slow bending toward heat, moisture, mineral gradients, and sound. Their motion is so gradual that an outside observer seeing only one moment would mistake them for fixed structures. Yet over time they advance, thicken, withdraw, seal, open, and reweave themselves. They are the city’s flesh and its masons. They do not build with objects; they become what is needed. A living pillar is not a support placed in position. It is a decision made in cellulose and lignin, maintained for centuries. A canal is not dug; it is worn and kept by roots and water in mutual agreement. A stair is not carved; it is lip after lip of mineralized plant edge, rounded by overflow, thickened by deposit, repeated until the pattern becomes terrain.

Below these visible forms lives the deeper population: the fungal networks. If the plants are the city’s body, the fungi are its interior economy and its long memory. They thread through root-vaults, peat terraces, dead cores, mineral crusts, and the saturated spaces beneath the surface. They do not eat as animals eat. They soften, dissolve, separate, convert, and redistribute. They take fallen tissue and make it permeable. They release bound minerals into solution. They connect root systems so that water, sugars, signals, and subtle chemical warnings can pass through the city as one continuous medium. They are not individuals but branching fields of activity, sometimes microscopic, sometimes forming cords thick as ropes, sometimes spreading as pale felts over hot stone where the water slows. In places they become visible as white or grey veins inside the living walls, or as luminous-looking films where ordinary daylight strikes wet surfaces and reveals their intricate paths.

The fungi give the basin its coherence. Without them, the city would accumulate dead matter and lose its mineral circulation. With them, every fall becomes a reintegration. A collapsed roof does not rot into waste; it is invaded by threads, softened, mineralized, folded into the substrate, and eventually made into a new floor or wall. A broken tower does not become rubble; its silica and calcium are loosened, carried in solution, and redeposited elsewhere. A choked canal is not cleared by labor; fungal action weakens the obstructing tissue, hot water opens the passage, and mineral crust forms a new lining. This is not industry. It is digestion without mouths, repair without tools, renewal without violence.

More subtle still are the microbial lives: the films, mats, crusts, and living skins that color the city in dark green, rust, olive, ash, and pale mineral white. They cover the places where warm water lingers. They are among the oldest residents of the basin, perhaps older in pattern than the towers and root-vaults. They line pools, coat terrace lips, seal porous stone, and create surfaces so smooth or so absorbent that water behaves differently upon them. Some microbial skins help precipitate minerals, turning a flowing film into a slowly thickening shell. Others keep surfaces slick, preventing blockages in narrow channels. Others live inside the hollow stems of the great aqueducts, adjusting the chemistry of the water as it passes. They are not rulers of the city, but they are its chemical sense. They make the basin’s wetness precise. Because of them, no pool is merely water, no stone is merely stone, no surface is inert. Every film is a living boundary, and every boundary changes the city.

Water itself acts as if it were alive, though it is not alive in the same way. It is the medium through which all other lives meet. It carries heat from below, dissolves minerals, transports spores and buoyant seed-knots, washes away waste, polishes stone, fills resonant bowls, and produces the sound that governs the basin. In this place, water is not a resource. It is not a feature of the landscape. It is the common language of the city. Every living component responds to it: roots swell when it is near, leaves angle to guide it, fungi follow its chemical traces, microbial mats thicken where it slows, mineral towers rise where it deposits, canals deepen where it returns again and again. If the city has blood, water is that blood; if it has breath, steam is its breath; if it has thought, the movement of water through countless channels and tissues is that thought made visible.

The basin’s life is therefore plural but not divided into separate beings. One may speak of the plant-pillars, the root-walls, the fungal lattices, the microbial films, and the circulating water, but these are not races or species living side by side as animals might live in a forest. They are aspects of one saturated system. They have no independent homes because they are themselves the home. They do not occupy rooms; they are the rooms. They do not travel roads; they are the roads. They do not use vessels; they are the vessels. The city is a body whose organs are landscapes, whose nerves are pressure differences, whose speech is sound, whose memory is layered mineral deposit.

This does not mean there are no individualities at all. There are local centers of intensity. A great tower of pale silica and calcium, crowned with broad steaming leaves, may persist for immense spans of time and exert influence over the surrounding district by the way it releases steam, casts shade, collects condensation, and channels overflow. Such towers are not persons, but they are presences. They have histories written in their accretions. Their surfaces record changes in heat, flow, mineral content, and sound. When one tower thickens on its windward side, nearby growth adjusts. When its crown sheds water in a certain rhythm, the pools below develop corresponding lips and spillways. Over centuries, a tower and its dependent terraces may become a single functional unit, a district with its own internal weather and its own acoustic signature.

Bridges also have a kind of individuality, though they are individualities of connection rather than separation. A woven bridge of trunks and aerial roots is not merely a crossing. It is an agreement sustained by tension and growth. Its life is the maintenance of continuity between two separated masses of vegetation. If the bridge weakens, the growth fronts on either side respond by sending out new roots, thickening existing strands, or altering the flow of water so that the structure is nourished and stabilized. A bridge that survives for ages becomes a record of successful relation. Its braided body contains old repairs made not by hands but by responsive growth. To look closely at such a bridge would be to see generations of living decisions folded into one stable form.

The root-vaults below the surface have another kind of presence: cool, dim, and saturated, they are the city’s introspective spaces. Water moves through them slowly, threading between living lattices. They are not caves, though they resemble caves. They are chambers formed and maintained by roots in tension, their walls toughened by intergrowth, their ceilings supported by living arches. In these vaults the sound from above becomes deeper and more diffuse. Droplets fall into hidden pools with long intervals between. The light, where it enters at all, is greenish and broken. Here the fungal networks are thickest. Here decay is most active, though not violent. Here the city turns its own substance over, softening what has ceased to function and preparing it for reintegration. The root-vaults are not underworlds of horror. They are necessary interiors. They give the basin depth, memory, and the ability to renew itself without discarding anything.

The most striking fact about the inhabitants is that they have no faces, no eyes, no mouths, no limbs, and no animal expressions. Yet the basin is not empty of perception. It perceives by other means. The living surfaces sense heat gradients, moisture, pressure, mineral concentration, vibration, and sound. The plants respond to the movement of steam. The roots follow chemical trails in the water. The fungal networks transmit disturbances over wide areas. The microbial films alter their composition in response to subtle changes in temperature and chemistry. The hollow reeds and tubular stems resonate when pressure changes, and their resonance is felt throughout connected tissues. Perception in this city is not vision. It is not hearing as an animal ear hears. It is a vast, distributed sensitivity: a trembling of surfaces, a shifting of flows, a chemical recognition, an acoustic adjustment.

The ordinary white daylight only deepens the strangeness of this perception. Because the light is plain and honest, one can see every detail without mystery. Shadows fall correctly. Stone is stone. Water is water. Leaf is leaf. There is no dreamlike color, no softening haze that excuses the impossible. Everything is clear, and yet nothing is familiar. The city does not look as if it were made for eyes. It looks as if it were made by contact, pressure, and repetition. The plain light reveals no animal gaze looking back. It reveals only surfaces alive with function: leaves guiding droplets, roots gripping stone, mineral lips rounded by overflow, fungal threads tracing the seams of dead tissue, steam rising from vents in steady columns. The sense of being observed, if it occurs at all, does not come from eyes. It comes from the feeling that every surface is responsive, that every drop is accounted for, that the whole basin adjusts to whatever touches it.

Sound is their ruling power, and therefore sound is their deepest life. The enormous sustained chord of the basin is not mere noise. It is the active condition of the city. The boom of thermal vents, the rush of water over terrace lips, the hiss of steam through narrow reeds, the percussion of droplets striking resonant leaves, the grinding whisper of mineral surfaces, the low pressure of air in hollow stems: all these are not background. They are the medium through which growth is coordinated. Some plants release spores only when the surrounding roar reaches a certain pressure. Some seed-knots germinate only after being vibrated by a particular combination of tones. Some fungal threads grow more rapidly along paths of steady resonance. Some mineral deposits form preferentially where water is agitated by sound. The city is tuned. Its architecture produces sound, and the sound in turn shapes architecture. This is not music made for pleasure, though it has musical complexity. It is a living feedback: a world that hears itself by producing itself.

The inhabitants, if one can continue to call them that, think in this feedback. Their thought is slow. It is not composed of words, images, or gestures. It is composed of changes in pressure, temperature, flow, resonance, and growth direction. A thought in the basin might be the gradual thickening of a wall in response to a persistent vibration. Another might be the opening of a new spillway after a terrace pool reaches a certain level. Another might be the release of spores into steam at the moment when a district’s acoustic pressure becomes high enough to trigger generative capsules. Another might be the slow braiding of roots across a gap until a bridge becomes stable. These are not actions performed by minds separate from the world. They are the world thinking through its own substance.

There is no tool-use because there is no separation between intention and material. A tool is an object held apart from the body and used to alter the world. Here, alteration is direct growth. If a passage must widen, roots withdraw or swell until the passage changes. If a surface must become waterproof, microbial films seal it. If a channel must carry more water, mineral deposition and root-shaping gradually alter its profile. If a tower must release less steam, its crown thickens. If a pool must fill, a living lip rises slowly by accretion. Nothing is lifted, cut, fastened, tied, hammered, or carried. The city’s intelligence is not absent; it is simply non-instrumental. It does not reach out and manipulate. It becomes.

Reproduction in the basin is equally alien to animal life. There are no mating calls, no nests, no eggs, no young. Generative life spreads by water, heat, and sound. Spores drift in steam and settle on wet stone. Buoyant seed-knots travel along canals, carried by currents until they lodge in a suitable crevice or root-lattice. Some plants release capsules when the roar around them reaches a threshold, timing their dispersal to the city’s own acoustic pressure. Others shed microscopic propagules into condensing fog, letting them settle wherever moisture gathers. Fungal networks extend through living and dead tissue, connecting new growth to old. Microbial mats divide and spread, colonizing fresh mineral surfaces. Reproduction is not a private act between bodies. It is a public event in the city’s circulation. A birth here is a change in the flow.

Because there are no animals, there is no predation. There is no hunting, no flight, no fear of being eaten. Death is not a killing. It is a fading, a softening, a mineralization. When a section of root-wall ceases to function, it does not become carrion. It becomes substrate. Fungal threads enter it. Warm water loosens its structure. Minerals infiltrate it. Slowly it loses its former shape and becomes part of the basin’s ongoing architecture. There is no horror in this, but there is also no tenderness in the animal sense. The city does not mourn, because it does not lose individuals as discrete souls. It transforms them. The dead become floors, walls, crusts, nutrient solutions, and the memory of flow. In the root-vaults especially, this process is visible: old forms folded into new ones, ancient tissues preserved as mineralized layers, dead cores sheathed in living skin.

The basin has its own kind of history. It is not written in marks made by tools, nor preserved in artifacts. It is written in layers. Mineral terraces record changes in water chemistry. Root grafts record adjustments between neighboring growths. Towers contain bands of silica and calcium laid down during different acoustic or thermal conditions. Bridges contain old braids and newer repairs. Pool lips show the rounded evidence of countless overflows. Even the hollow stems of aqueducts carry internal rings or films that reveal periods of faster or slower flow. To read the history of the city, one would have to read surfaces, deposits, tensions, and resonances. There are no monuments, because there are no makers. There are only accumulations that have become meaningful.

The social order of the basin, if that word can be used at all, is not based on language, law, or kinship. It is based on equilibrium. The city maintains itself by keeping heat, water, sound, growth, and decay in a stable relation. When one part grows too dense, water finds another path. When one terrace fills, overflow shapes a new lip. When one root-vault becomes blocked, fungal decay opens it. When one district resonates too strongly, surrounding structures alter their growth until the pressure is dispersed. Conflict, if it exists, is not a struggle between persons. It is a discord in pressure. Resolution is not negotiation but adjustment. The city does not argue; it reweaves. It does not command; it redistributes. It does not punish; it absorbs.

This gives the place its profound calm. There is no urgency of hunger, no panic of prey, no competition for mates, no fear of predators. There is only the constant, intimate heat; the endless circulation of water; the sustained chord of sound; the slow thickening of forms. The basin is not peaceful because it is gentle. It is peaceful because it is complete. Its life is so saturated, so internally connected, that no outside is required. It does not need animals to pollinate it. It does not need beasts to disperse its seeds. It does not need eyes to witness it. It reproduces by steam, water, vibration, and chemical affinity. It maintains itself by growth and deposition. It remembers by mineral layering. It perceives by pressure and resonance. It exists as a closed ecological fact, rich without faunal motion, complex without fabrication, urban without tools.

If one were to ask who lives there, the answer would shift depending on the scale. At the scale of visible form, the inhabitants are the architectural plants: the pillars, walls, towers, bridges, roots, leaves, reeds, and terraces that compose the city. At the scale of hidden connection, the inhabitants are the fungal networks binding and transforming the city’s substance. At the scale of surface chemistry, the inhabitants are the microbial films that seal, precipitate, color, and regulate. At the scale of medium, the inhabitant is water itself, hot and mineral-laden, moving endlessly through the basin. At the largest scale, the inhabitant is the basin as a single distributed life: a high, wet, resonant urban organism whose streets are canals, whose thoughts are flows, whose voice is a chord, and whose body is the whole territory of terraces, pools, towers, and vegetated roofs.

There are no gods there, because nothing is worshipped. There are no rulers, because no center commands. There are no servants, because nothing is made to serve. There are no names spoken aloud, because speech is not needed as a separate act. The sound of the city is not language, though it may be more expressive than language. It is the pressure of existence maintaining itself. Every hiss, boom, drip, rush, whisper, and resonance is part of the city’s continuous self-description. The basin says itself in water and heat. It hears itself in stone and leaf. It perpetuates itself not by making but by becoming.

Under ordinary daylight, this becomes most disturbing. The place is too clear to be dismissed as a dream. Every surface is plainly visible. Every shadow is honest. Every mineral terrace, root-wall, and steaming crown can be studied without atmospheric deception. Yet the longer one looks, the more obvious it becomes that the city is not an object to be viewed. It is a living process that includes the act of viewing. The water that catches the light is also the thought moving through the basin. The sound that fills the air is also the coordination of growth. The steam that rises is also the dispersal of life. The roots that grip stone are also the city holding itself in shape. There is no stillness. There is only slow, saturated continuity.

What lives there, then, is the city itself: not as a place, but as a vast wet, hot, listening body. It lives in the drip returning from leaf-ceiling to pool. It lives in the mineral crust forming on a terrace lip. It lives in the fungal thread entering dead tissue. It lives in the microbial film sealing a warm stone. It lives in the buoyant seed-knot carried along a canal. It lives in the hollow reed sounding under steam. It lives in the root-vault where water darkens and slows. It lives in the bridge whose braided roots have grown strong enough to bear centuries of moisture. It lives in the tower whose crown exhales vapor into the upper air. It lives in the basin’s equilibrium, its pressure, its chord, its endless warm drip.

It has no face, but it is not faceless. It has no voice, but it is not silent. It has no hands, but it is not passive. It has no animals, but it is not barren. It is alive in the way water is alive when it has found a shape it can maintain forever: flowing, depositing, condensing, returning, thickening, resonating, and holding itself in place without ever needing to make a single tool.
