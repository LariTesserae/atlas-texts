What lives there does not first appear as a body. It appears as a stillness that is too attentive to be stone.

If you look across the hollow from the rim, especially when mist is thinning and the wet surfaces are all moving with reflected light, you may see nothing but the bowl itself: the black rock, the pools, the moss, the slow threads of water. Then one of the rounded shapes near the lowest part of the hollow seems to change in some small way. A surface lifts. A shadow shifts. A place that looked like a wet boulder breathes.

That is the dweller of the hollow.

It is low and broad, no larger than a crouched child, though its size is hard to judge because it seems to settle into the rock as if it had grown there. Its back is rounded, smooth, and dark, the color of basalt after long rain, greened here and there by a fine living film of algae. In old seasons, moss has taken hold along one shoulder where water slows before passing on. The moss is not decoration. It is part of the creature’s stillness. When the dweller remains motionless long enough, the hollow begins to claim it. Mineral dust settles in the creases of its skin. Tiny bright grains lodge along its sides. A thin crust may form on its back, fragile as sugar, until the next long soaking softens it and lets it dissolve away.

Its body is made for wetness. The skin is slick, close, and warm to the eye, not shiny in the way of fish or wet insects but in the way of stones that have been under water for centuries. It does not repel moisture. It receives it. Water spreads over the dweller rather than running from it in separate beads. When a drip strikes its back, the impact does not splash sharply; it blooms outward in a soft crown and then sinks into the moving film that covers the creature. One could almost say it drinks through its surface, though that is not quite true. It takes in water as a mood is taken in: gradually, evenly, through the whole of itself.

Its head is blunt and rounded, set low between shoulders that rise like shelves. There is no nose, no ears, no obvious mouth until it moves. The face, if it can be called a face, is dominated by two wide, dark eyes. They are not bright like an animal’s eyes. They are deep and still, like small pools seen from above. They do not flash. They answer. When light touches them, it seems to arrive a moment late, as if the eyes are thinking about the light before returning it. There is a strange gentleness in that delay. It gives the creature the look of one who is not slow, but unhurried; not dull, but saturated with patience.

When the dweller breathes, its whole body rises almost imperceptibly. There is no chest heaving, no sharp intake of air. The wet skin tightens and relaxes. The water film upon it shivers. Along its sides, faint folds open and close: thin, dark membranes like the gills of some old water-creature, or like leaves pressed flat against stone. These membranes tremble with every sound in the hollow. They are how it listens.

It has limbs, but they are difficult to describe as legs or arms. They are broad, soft-ended, and strong in a slow way, more like roots that have learned to press against rock. Beneath each limb are wide pads, wet and clinging, which allow it to rest on slick stone without slipping. When it moves, it does not step so much as pour itself from one position to another. There is no scrape of claw, no scratch, no displaced pebble. It leaves no prints. The water closes behind it. The moss springs back. The hollow accepts the motion and forgets it at once.

The dweller lives near the black central stone because that is where the sound is fullest and the water most continuous. It does not build a nest there. It does not make a lair. It simply chooses the place again and again, as water chooses the lowest path. Sometimes it rests directly over the stone, flattened so closely that its body becomes a dark mirror. Sometimes it sits at the stone’s edge where the water passes in a thin sheet, letting the current divide around it. In that position, it seems less like a creature sitting in the hollow than like the hollow’s own center given a body.

It feeds in small, unhurried ways. It grazes the fine green skin that grows on wet rock, not tearing it but drawing it away with a soft, sucking motion of the mouth, which opens as a pale, damp line beneath the face. It takes mineral salts from the water. It feeds on the filmy life that gathers where mist settles and warmth lingers. Occasionally, when an insect falls into a pool and struggles there, the dweller may turn its head slowly toward the disturbance. It does not hunt. It does not chase. It simply allows the small creature to come within reach, or not. If it eats, it does so without violence. If it does not, it returns to stillness. It seems to require very little. It seems to have learned the hollow’s lesson: that enough is not abundance, but persistence.

Its relation to sound is intimate. Because the hollow amplifies water and wind, the dweller lives inside a constant architecture of vibration. Each drip is a touch. Each sliding sheet of water is a long pressure against its membranes. When the wind enters over the rim and divides among the ferns and hanging roots, the dweller’s body answers in tiny ways. The membranes along its sides tremble. The water on its back ripples in patterns that do not belong to the falling drops alone. Sometimes, when the hollow hums most deeply, the creature’s throat, if it has one, gives back a vibration so low it is felt more than heard. This is not a call. It is not speech. It is resonance. It is the sound of a body agreeing with its place.

The dweller does not speak, yet it is not mindless. Its attention is unmistakable. If you stand long enough at the edge of the hollow, you may feel it become aware of you. Not suddenly. Not with alarm. The change is subtle: the angle of its head shifts a little. Its eyes take you in without moving. The water on its skin seems to calm. It does not flee, because there is nowhere in the hollow that is not itself. It does not threaten, because it has no need to defend what it does not own. It only watches, in the way that still water watches the sky.

If you move too quickly, it may become stone again. That is not camouflage in the ordinary sense. It does not imitate rock so much as return to rock’s manner. Its breathing slows. Its membranes fold close. The eyes darken and lose their apparent depth. It becomes a rounded, wet shape among other rounded, wet shapes. Only when you have been quiet for a long time does it begin to re-emerge: a bead of water sliding down its back, a tremor in the moss on its shoulder, the slow blink of those pool-like eyes.

It is not solitary in the way of creatures that avoid one another. There may be only one of it in the hollow, or there may be more than one, but they do not gather. They do not need to. The place is small enough that any other dweller would be felt through the water film long before it was seen. If another moved, the pools would change. If another breathed, the sound would alter. Yet the hollow seems tuned to a single presence. Perhaps the dweller is the last of its kind. Perhaps it is not a kind at all, but a condition that occasionally becomes flesh when a place is old enough, wet enough, and quiet enough to hold it.

In the brightest part of the day, when sunlight comes through the mist in columns and the hanging water glows, the dweller seeks the softer shadows. It does not fear the light, but it prefers the places where moisture is most stable. It may slide into one of the broad, shallow pools until only its eyes and the ridge of its back remain above the surface. There it can rest while the sun draws warmth into the stone. The water around it darkens with its body. Small ripples move outward from its sides at intervals so regular they seem like thought made visible.

When clouds thicken and mist rolls over the rim, the dweller becomes more present. It lifts its head. The membranes along its sides open wider. It may raise itself slowly onto the central stone, letting the mist condense on its skin. In such moments, it looks almost like something receiving a gift. Droplets gather on its back and run down into the water below. The creature may remain this way for hours, motionless but intensely alive, as though the hollow’s breathing has become its own.

It is in mist that the dweller seems strangest and most itself. The air thickens. The sound softens. The walls disappear into pale vapor. The creature’s outline blurs, and one can begin to imagine that it is not a body at all but a knot of wetness held briefly in shape by the hollow’s curvature. Yet then it blinks, and the illusion breaks. Or rather, the illusion becomes truer: it is a body, but a body made of the same forces that make the hollow.

When the air clears and the mist withdraws, the dweller sinks lower. It follows the wetness. If a pool shrinks, it moves to a deeper one. If a shelf dries to a mere film, it leaves that shelf before the film is gone. It does not struggle against the hollow’s changes. It adjusts to them as a hand adjusts to the shape of a cup. In drier seasons, it may retreat into a cleft near the lowest terrace where water always beads in darkness. There it can become almost invisible: a folded, patient mass, its skin duller, its breathing so slow that a watcher might mistake it for mineral deposit. It does not sleep exactly as animals sleep. It enters a long quiet. It waits.

When rain comes again, or when the mist returns thick enough to soak the stone, the dweller revives. Its skin darkens. The membranes unfold. Its eyes clear. Water slides over it and it seems to expand, not in size so much in presence. The hollow feels fuller with it awake. The sounds seem to find their listener again.

It does not make tools. It does not carry anything. It does not cut, bind, heap, or mark. Nothing about it suggests craft. Even its movements leave no trace that could be called intention. Yet there is a kind of intelligence in the way it places itself, in the way it chooses the exact point where a drip will fall, or rests where the water’s warmth is deepest. This is not the intelligence of hands. It is the intelligence of fitting. It knows the hollow the way moss knows stone: by long contact, by pressure, by continuing.

The other rare life of the hollow seems to accept it. The translucent snail may rest on a leaf near where the dweller sits, unmoved by the creature’s slow breathing. The pale insects that drift through the upper light do not alter their uncertain flight when they pass above it. If there are other soft-bodied things in the pools, they do not scatter at its presence. They may not even perceive it as separate from the wet dark they inhabit. The dweller, for its part, does not pursue them. It is not a ruler of the hollow. It is not a predator. It is a resident so complete that it has become part of the conditions under which the other lives persist.

There are moments when the hollow’s strangeness gathers around it most strongly. In the pauses between sounds, when even the drips seem to hold back, the dweller may raise its head and open its mouth in a wide, silent curve. No cry comes from it. Instead, the water in the nearest pools trembles very slightly, as if something has passed through it. Then the hollow resumes its noise: drip, slide, hum, echo. The dweller lowers its head. The moment closes. Nothing has been revealed, and yet something has been felt.
