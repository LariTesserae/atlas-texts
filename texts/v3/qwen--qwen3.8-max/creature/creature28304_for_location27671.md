What lives in the Hollow Nursery does not gather so much as condense. It appears first as maintenance, then as weather, then as the faint sense of being attended to by something without a face. The place is inhabited, but not in the way a house is inhabited. It is inhabited the way a seed is inhabited: by a future that has already begun to organize matter around itself.

The first residents are small, patient, and countless.

In the root-felt underfoot, pale rootlice move in long, deliberate processions. They are flat-bodied and translucent, more like moving flakes of pith than animals, and they travel in lines that follow the faint warmth of sap-ducts. Their mandibles work constantly, not with greed but with care. They clean the walking surfaces, removing fungal film, mineral dust, and the shed fibers of the city’s own growth. Where they pass, the floor becomes slightly smoother, slightly darker, as if they are not merely eating but polishing a law into existence. When a drop falls from above, the entire procession pauses. Each rootlouse lifts its foreparts just enough to taste the disturbance. Then they drink, tremble, and resume their route. Their excrement is a clear, hard lacquer, so fine that it accumulates over time into the shine of paths and curbs. Much of what looks like wear in the Hollow Nursery is actually worship of this kind: tiny mouths making surfaces fit for feet that may not yet exist.

Beetles the color of wet bark patrol the pipe joints and valve stems. They are heavier than the rootlice, with elytra that seem carved from soaked wood and antennae tipped in mineral glitter. Their work is inspection. They tap the pipes with their heads, listen through the cellulose, and detect the difference between a healthy flow and a coming blockage. Some carry droplets of condensed water in hollows along their backs, transporting them to dry places where new rootlets must be coaxed open. Others carry spores, adhesive pollens, or chemical instructions sealed in wax. When two beetles meet at a junction, they touch antennae and remain still for a long moment, exchanging not news but calibration. Then one turns left, the other right, and the city adjusts itself by a fraction of a degree.

Worms live inside the walls.

They are not decorative, and they are rarely seen. They thread through living panels of bark and culm, their bodies aligned with the structural grain. When vibration passes—a footfall, a piston’s breath, a drop striking a basin—they contract, tightening the weave of the wall. In this way they are both inhabitants and reinforcement. They do not build the city so much as hold it in readiness. If a panel weakens, worms gather there in silence, their bodies becoming temporary stitches until the plant tissue can secrete a harder layer. They dream, if they dream, in pressure. They know the town by compression and by the taste of strain. When the Hollow Nursery settles into a new shape, it is their hidden bodies that teach it not to crack.

Slugs move through the cooler alleys, leaving silver trails that vanish almost as soon as they are made. Their passage is brief and instructional. They graze on bacterial films, excess spores, and the first mistakes of growth. Where they feed, they leave chemical traces that tell surrounding tissue what should not become a wall, what should remain a channel, what may soften into a doorway later. Their maps evaporate, but they are not lost. The city reads them while they last. Some slugs are small as eyelashes. Others, in the deeper ducts, are broad and dark, their backs ridged like folded leaves. They do not hurry. They have the unhurried dignity of creatures whose labor is erased daily and must be repeated without resentment.

In the air, life is sparse but exact.

Moths drift between the hanging fibers, their wing-edges pale with fungal dust. They are not drawn to light in the ordinary sense, because there is almost none. They are drawn to moisture gradients, to the faint warmth of sap-valves, to the chemical opening of flower-organs that are less flowers than architectural apertures. When a moth enters one of these valves, it becomes part of the building’s reproduction. It emerges dusted with adhesive spores and carries them to joints, seams, and waiting surfaces. Some of those spores will become lamps. Some will become filters. Some will become skin for future rooms. The moth does not know this. It only follows the scent of cool sweetness into darkness.

Gnats gather in thin clouds around newly formed thresholds. They do not swarm so much as deliberate. Their bodies are nearly transparent, their wings quick enough to blur, and their collective movement has the appearance of thought made visible. They taste the geometry of openings. They detect where an arch is too tense, where a doorway has grown asymmetrically, where a passage has been shaped by accident rather than intention. When they disperse, the threshold is often subtly altered: a little wider, a little smoother, a little more prepared for passage. No one sees them decide. One only notices later that the space feels more like a door than it did before.

Lacewings hunt among the suspended conduits. Their wings look like wet glass, and they fly without sound, except when they choose to make a dry clicking warning. They feed on excess spores and on the smaller insects that mistake immature towers for food. Mantid-like hunters wait in the angles between railings and walls, their bodies narrow as bent cane, their forelimbs folded with a patience indistinguishable from prayer. They do not kill often. They cull. When one strikes, the event is over before the air has time to complain. The prey is consumed quietly, and the hunter remains motionless again, becoming part of the structure until need returns.

There are flies whose wings make no sound. They move through the lower dark with a precision that suggests memory rather than sight. Some feed on decay before decay can become disorder. Others carry minute seeds in their bristles, planting them in cracks where the city wishes to thicken itself. A few are scavengers of failed growth, those soft, aborted shapes that sometimes appear in the nurseries and must be taken apart without ceremony. They are not unclean. They are necessary. They keep the Hollow Nursery from becoming sentimental about life.

In the upper conduits, where the ceiling is a woven darkness of fronds and tension roots, bat-like creatures roost in small numbers. They are not truly bats, perhaps. They may be closer to folded leaves that learned to breathe warmth. Their wings open and close slowly as they hang, as if they are still deciding whether to remain objects or become animals. They make sounds below ordinary hearing, pulses that travel through the city’s upper lattice and encourage certain vines to contract, certain vents to open, certain immature towers to harden. They eat little: a moth here, a gnat there, never enough to empty the air. Their droppings fall into hidden troughs and seed phosphorescent moss. In this way, even their waste becomes illumination.

Water is where life thickens.

In the black leaf-basins and root-knot pools, creatures live with the intensity of beings confined to very small worlds. The fish there are almost without pigment. They are needle-thin, their bodies clear enough to reveal a line of darker organs moving like a thought through glass. They do not school from fear. They school from attention. When a drop strikes the surface, they turn as one, not to flee but to interpret. The ripple tells them whether the moisture came from a healthy condensation vein, from a factory vent, from a nursery, or from a place where the city is growing too fast and weeping. They feed on spores and microorganisms, but they also feed on information. Their lateral lines are the reservoir’s memory.

Blind tadpoles graze the bacterial films along the sides of basins. Their mouths work slowly, erasing excess life before it can bloom into imbalance. They do not need eyes. They know the basin by taste, by pressure, by the subtle curvature of the water against their skins. Sometimes they rest motionless in the centers of pools, suspended like dark commas. If a keeper passes near, they rise slightly, as if acknowledging not a person but a change in chemistry.

Eels, or eel-like things, occupy the conduits and larger pipes. They are the city’s deep movers. Some are black and muscular, suited to the root-channels where water flows in slow pulses. Others are pale and almost liquid, able to flatten themselves until they become little more than a ribbon of movement inside a duct. They know the plumbing of the Hollow Nursery better than any other living thing. They pass through valves, around turbines, between sealed root-knots, carrying mineral deposits, chemical messages, and occasionally the living fragments of other creatures that need to be relocated. When an eel flashes blue inside a dark pipe, it is not showing itself. It is discharging some excess potential, as if the city has briefly thought a thought too bright to keep.

In one courtyard pool, a pale frog sits on a floating leaf-platform. It is small, no larger than a thumbprint, and so still that it seems carved from pith. Its skin has the damp, semi-translucent quality of young stem tissue. It does not croak. Instead, its throat pulses at intervals too slow to be heard, sending pressure changes through the water that open and close nearby valves. When a gnat lands near it, the frog does not strike in any visible sequence. It simply becomes the state in which the gnat is already gone. It is a predator folded into the rhythm of the place. It eats mistakes: misplaced insects, overproduced spores, the tiny accidents that every living city makes. Afterward, it returns to stillness, and the pool continues its quiet administration.

Beneath all these lives, and perhaps older than any of them, is the mycelium.

It is not a single animal, nor exactly a plant, though it behaves with the patience of both. It runs through the ground, the walls, the ducts, the machines. It is white lace under everything. It connects root-columns to sap-pipes, factory beds to reservoirs, nursery chambers to the cold troughs where absence is made. It does not rule. It negotiates. When one part of the Hollow Nursery needs moisture, the mycelium helps redirect it. When a wall is overgrown, it signals for pruning. When an engine grows too warm, it guides water toward the fermenting tissue. Its decisions appear as faint green glows at junctions, or as the sudden appearance of slime along a seam where two systems meet. It thinks in gradients. It remembers in chemical trails. It is the closest thing the place has to a common mind, though it would be wrong to call it a ruler. It is more like the silence between notes that makes the music possible.

But the Hollow Nursery is not only fauna and fungus. It is also the place where beings are kept who have not yet become what they will be.

In the lower courtyards and the sealed inner chambers stand the Hollow Mothers.

They are not trees, though they have the patience of trees. They are not animals, though they have warmth, membranes, and slow internal pulses. They are massive, sessile organisms, their bodies made of fused bark, folded leaf-sheaths, and living gaskets. Their trunks, if trunks they are, contain hollow chambers lined with black, waterproof bark and filled with sap-water. These chambers are the nurseries. Inside them, suspended in cool fluid, are the unborn shapes of the place: seed-children, larval citizens, future tenders, future keepers, future bodies not yet named.

The Hollow Mothers do not move. They stand in dim rows like chapels made of living wood. Their surfaces are covered in valves, listening pores, and fine root-hairs that tremble when the air changes. They breathe out humidity. Their breath forms the localized mists that gather under the lattice. When they are well, their bark is slick and dark, and the fungal veins around them glow a steady green. When they are strained, their valves click irregularly, and the water in their chambers grows cloudy. They do not speak. They release scents: readiness, alarm, rest, hunger, completion. These scents move through the Nursery like liturgy.

They are tended constantly.

The tenders are the most visible of the Hollow Nursery’s larger inhabitants, though even they are few and often mistaken for parts of the architecture.

They are tall, slender beings, more jointed than any animal born in the open. Their limbs look like bundled cane wrapped in root-cord. Their bodies are layered with thin sheets of living bark, mossy filaments, and membranes that tremble with respiration. They move slowly, not from weakness but from precision. Each step is placed as if the floor might become something else if they are careless. Their faces, if faces they have, are smooth masks of polished leaf-stalk, broken by dark bead-lenses where eyes might be and by narrow slits through which they taste the air. They do not speak with mouths. They communicate by tapping on hollow stems, by releasing chemical traces, by altering the pattern of condensation on nearby surfaces.
