What lives there is not one creature, but a pressure of lives.

If you try to picture a single inhabitant of that sunken green city, the image keeps breaking apart into many bodies: a whiskered muzzle pushing through wet roots, a salamander’s slow footfall in the shallows, a caddis larva adding one more grain to its portable wall, a bat folding itself into a dark seam overhead, a moss stem spreading a new green filament across stone. The place resists the singular. It is occupied in the plural. Whoever lives there is also whatever lives there, and the distinction softens almost at once, because the lives are so intertwined that they seem to belong to one another as much as to themselves.

The first residents are the green ones.

They are not merely decoration; they are the foundation-citizens of the hollow. Moss lives there in dense, patient nations. It fills the shallow pans of rock, climbs the freshly broken faces, softens the sharp edges left by collapse, and turns bare stone into a surface where other lives can anchor. It is the oldest-looking inhabitant, though the hollow is young, because moss carries the memory of damp places everywhere. In the brighter shafts of daylight it glows in acid threads; in the dimmer corners it becomes black-green and close, like wet velvet laid over the bones of the world. It holds water in its leaves, slows drip, traps spores, shelters springtails, and gives a floor to things that would otherwise slide into the pools.

Liverworts live there too, sleek and dark on vertical faces, their broad wet leaves pressed against rock as if listening. Ferns live in the crevices where seepage never stops, their croziers uncoiling slowly into the cold air, each new frond a pale green question opening in the dark. Algae lives in the water as drifting smoke and as slick films on submerged stones. Fine rooted strands rise from the shallows, making underwater meadows where tiny animals can hide, feed, and vanish. Roots from the plants above live downward through the broken ceiling and walls: torn, exposed, dangling, yet still alive, still reaching, still holding the loose world together. In places they form hanging ropes, braided ladders, living rafters. They are not dead timber. They are active limbs of the upper world reaching into the lower one.

Even the wet stone might seem alive here, because every stone is clothed. But the true green lives are those that grow visibly, crowdedly, insistently, as if growth were the only answer to the cold.

Among the most constant workers of the hollow are the small mammals.

Water voles live there in the marsh edges and along the submerged banks. They are soft-furred, blunt-faced, deliberate animals, their eyes dark and alert, their bodies made for narrow passages and sudden dives. They do not merely occupy the hollow; they furnish it. Their runways are hidden streets through the sedges, low tunnels pressed into the mud and root-mat, just wide enough for a body to pass without fully swimming. Where an entrance needs strengthening, they pack it with mud. Where a chamber needs lining, they chew stems and pull fibers until the wall becomes a woven thing. Their nests are not crude heaps but careful rooms, insulated with shredded reed and grass, tucked into root cavities where the drip cannot reach.

A vole here is always in relationship with water. It knows which channels are safe, which roots bear weight, which mud is firm enough to carry it, which pools have fish-shadows that mean danger, and which bends in the stem-tunnels provide escape. It lives by touch and scent as much as sight, its whiskers reading the dark like fingers. Its life is quick, wary, and industrious. It repairs, enlarges, clears, and hides. It is one of the hollow’s closest equivalents to a builder, though it builds with nothing but teeth, paws, and inherited knowledge.

Water shrews live there as well, smaller and fiercer, moving with a darting urgency that makes even the cold seem startled. They hunt along the edges and in the wet crevices, diving into the shallow pools, probing beneath stones, chasing larvae and insect nymphs with sharp, rapid bites. Their metabolisms are urgent; they cannot afford long stillness. In a place so saturated and slow, they are sparks of nervous heat. Their breath fogs visibly in the cool air. Their fur darkens where it wets. They are among the few inhabitants whose presence feels like a quickening, a sudden acceleration of the hollow’s pulse.

There are other small bodies, too, less conspicuous: mice or field voles that use the upper dry ledges when the floor floods, animals that arrive through cracks and root-gaps and leave behind tracks of disturbed moss, chewed seeds, and faint musk. They do not dominate the place. They are threaded through it, part of its pressure, filling the spaces that open between wet and dry, dark and light.

The insects and their kin are not incidental. They are co-architects.

Caddis larvae live in the slower channels, building cases from sand grains, plant fragments, and tiny shells, binding them with silk from their own bodies. In numbers, their cases become more than individual shelters. They form miniature reefs, low retaining walls, pebble terraces that slow the current and trap silt. These structures alter the flow just enough to create new edges, new still pockets, new nursery spaces. A caddis larva is small enough to be missed, but its kindred work is part of what makes the water behave like streets and canals rather than a single chaotic seep.

Beetles live under bark and in drowned wood, moving through the dark with hard, deliberate bodies. Some skim the surface films; some burrow in softened stems; some carry the smell of tannin and decay. Springtails live in the moss by thousands, tiny spring-loaded lives that leap when disturbed, making the green surface seem briefly to breathe. Flies and midges live in the shafts of light, their wings catching the grey glow, their bodies forming brief clouds where the air enters. They are food as much as inhabitants: they pass through the hollow’s illuminated intervals and are taken by spiders, shrews, young fish, and nesting birds.

Wasps live beneath the dry overhangs, where they hang paper nests of chewed fiber, each cell a perfect hexagonal room. Their architecture is lighter than the stone around it, almost fragile, but it is placed with exactness: always where drip will not dissolve it, always where the thin ceiling shelters it, always where flight paths can enter and leave without striking wet roots. They bring insects to their young, and in doing so connect the upper world to the lower. Their presence adds a faint dry sweetness to the air near the ledges, a papery smell beneath the mineral damp.

Spiders live everywhere there is structure enough to string. They are not merely hunters; they are tension-workers. Their silk braces loose root fibers, catches falling droplets before they strike vulnerable nests, stabilizes frayed edges, and turns empty gaps into inhabited surfaces. Some webs are flat and close to the stone, almost invisible; others are suspended between hanging roots like faint rigging. Dew and condensation bead on them, making them appear as lines of light. They wait with stillness that is not emptiness but attention. When an insect strikes, the whole web speaks in a tiny tremor, and the spider moves with sudden competence.

Mites, worms, larvae, and countless smaller beings live in the mud, the leaf litter, the submerged roots, and the accumulated softness of the hollow. They are the unnoticed citizens whose labor is endless: breaking, binding, aerating, digesting, moving a grain here, tunneling there. Without them, the place would clog. They keep the city’s floor alive and porous.

Birds live in the hollow because light and air enter through the broken surface.

They come and go through cracks and sinkhole mouths, flashing into the grey columns of daylight with expert wings. Mud-nesters build cupped homes on the sheltered ledges where the overhang protects them from drip but the open space allows flight. Their nests are pressed close to one another, sometimes in long terraces, each one a small architecture of mud pellets, grass, and saliva. When the hollow is quiet, you can hear the faint dry rustle of a wing adjusting in a nest, the small open-mouthed whisper of nestlings, the soft return of a parent bringing insects.

These birds do not stay long in the deeper parts. They are visitors who have made rooms in the walls. They tie the hollow to the sky. Their calls, when they occur, are brief and clear, then swallowed by moss. They carry seeds in feathers, drop food fragments, leave feathers and whitewash on ledges, and add another layer of living traffic to the vertical city. In the colder parts of the year, their presence may thin, but the nests remain, mud cups holding the memory of wings.

Wrens or similar small secretive birds may work the lower margins, slipping through root-tangles with flicking tails, hunting among the wet stems. They are less visible than the aerial nesters, more like breaths moving through the green. Their lives are quick, private, and intensely territorial, though their territories are measured not in wide fields but in bundles of root, shelf, and dripping crevice.

Bats live in the upper dark pockets.

They hang in tight clusters where the ceiling is dry enough and the air still enough for sleep. Their bodies are folded, wing membranes wrapped close, fur soft and brownish-grey in the dim light. They breathe slowly. Sometimes one shifts, and a tiny sound passes through the cluster, a rustle of leather and bone. They are not noisy citizens, but they are vital ones. At dusk or before dawn, they leave through cracks to hunt elsewhere; at return they bring back insects in their bodies and nutrients in their droppings. Guano accumulates in small dark mounds below their roosts, feeding beetles, fungi, and the slow chemical life of the floor. In this way, the upper darkness is connected to the lower pools.

A bat in the hollow is a creature of precise orientation. It reads the space with sounds too fine for human hearing, turning walls and roots into a map of texture and distance. Where humans would see only shadow, bats find ceilings, perches, gaps, and hazards. They do not build much; their architecture is choice: the right crevice, the right temperature, the right nearness to flight paths. Yet their presence changes the hollow. It adds a warm-blooded secrecy to the higher dark.

Below the surface of the water live the patient ones.

Small cold-water fish hold in the narrow channels, their dark backs nearly the color of wet stone. They face the current with minimal effort, fins adjusting, tails barely moving, until something draws them into a dart. They feed on larvae, drifting insects, and small crustaceans. Their shadows move over the clay like stains. In the shallower pools they gather near root rims, where they can slip into cover if a shrew or bird disturbs them. They are quick, but their quickness is contained; they do not race through open distances because there are no open distances here. Every run is bordered, every channel furnished with hiding places and hazards.

Eels or eel-like forms live in the deeper root tunnels and submerged passages. They are muscle and patience, moving with slow serpentine grace through spaces too tight for most fish. Their eyes are small but competent in the dark. They hunt by scent and touch, sliding through the woven underworld with a calm that can seem deliberate, even thoughtful. When they rest, they may be almost invisible, only a thickened shadow inside a root-hole. When they move, they reveal how much of the hollow’s architecture is tubular, winding, and body-shaped.

Salamanders live in the shallows and under stones. They are among the most emblematic inhabitants of the place because their tempo suits it. They stand motionless for long periods, their limbs planted, their eyes like dark beads, their skins mottled with cold-water colors. When they move, it is with a slow ripple, one foot placed before another as if considering each step. They eat worms, larvae, and small invertebrates. Their eggs are laid in damp hidden places or attached to submerged roots, soft clusters guarded by moisture and obscurity. A salamander here feels less like a visitor than like a spirit of the hollow’s own patience: cold, amphibious, unhurried, alert.

Mussels and other shellfish live where the water is stable enough for them. They pave certain beds with their bodies and shells, creating firm underwater floors where mud would otherwise shift. Their lives are filtration and stillness. They draw water in, take what they need, release what remains, and in doing so clarify the channels. Old shells become substrate; new shells become part of the pavement. They are quiet engineers of the lowest tier, making the city’s foundations less mutable.

Crustaceans, insect nymphs, worms, and snails occupy the mud and stones in between. They scrape algae, shred leaves, burrow in soft sediment, and serve as food for nearly every larger predator. The water is not empty anywhere. Even when it looks still, it is full of small appetites and small motions.

Fungi live there as the hidden digesters.

They thread through dead stems, fallen root-mat, shed feathers, drowned insects, and old nests. Their mycelium is a pale lace under surfaces, breaking what is finished into what can be used again. In damp corners, fruiting bodies rise: small caps, thin stalks, brackets on softened wood, pale cups on mud. They are not decorative; they are metabolism made visible. Without them, the hollow would become its own waste. They give death back to the city in a form roots and larvae can use. In that sense, they are among the most necessary inhabitants, though they do not move and do not call.

Even the pools have populations that seem almost like weather: drifting algae, floating leaflets, microbial films, larvae rising and sinking, the fine dust of organic matter suspended in cold water. These are not merely things in the water; they are lives, or the immediate remains of lives, continuing to act.

What lives there, then, is a set of relationships as much as a set of creatures.

A moss bed lives because it is grazed, grown over, wetted, and sheltered. A root tunnel lives because animals keep it open, mud reinforces it, and water has not yet collapsed it. A nest lives because it is repaired, defended, warmed, and fed. A pool lives because fish move through it, insects hatch from it, roots drink from it, and droplets keep entering from above. Nothing here can be understood alone. Each inhabitant is a node in a dense web of use, shelter, food, and repair.

The lives are arranged by layers.

In the lowest layer, under and within the water, live fish, eels, salamanders, mussels, larvae, snails, worms, and the fine rooted plants. This is the slowest layer, the one where movement is cushioned by fluid and where sound becomes thick. It is also a layer of nurseries. Many lives begin there as eggs, nymphs, or fry, hidden among roots and stones.

Above that is the marsh layer: water voles, shrews, beetles, spiders, frogs if the climate allows, sedges, mosses, and the woven causeways of small bodies. This is the busiest layer, the one most shaped by constant repair. Here the floor is neither fully land nor fully water, but a negotiated surface.

Above that are the ledges and overhangs: bird nests, wasp colonies, roosting bats, dry moss, shells, feathers, food caches, and the accumulated traces of animals that prefer not to sleep in the wet. This layer is more aerial, more scent-marked, more fragile. It is also where the hollow’s warmth gathers, such as warmth exists in that cold place: in fur, feathers, crowded bodies, and insulated nests.

Higher still, near the cracks and sinkhole mouths, live the passing lives: winged insects, birds in transit, seeds, spores, and the occasional falling leaf or dead moth from the world above. This is the threshold layer, where the hollow breathes.

Each layer has its own specialists, but many lives cross between them. A shrew hunts from marsh edge into shallow water. A bird takes mud from the pool rim and carries it to a ledge. A bat leaves the upper dark and returns with insects from outside. An eel moves from submerged channel into root-tunnel. A spider’s web catches insects that rose from the water. The city is vertical, folded, and connected.

Because the hollow is crowded, its inhabitants have developed subtle ways of living near one another.

Territories are close but not always violent. Boundaries are often marked by scent, by worn paths, by the particular shape of an entrance, by repeated use. A certain root-shelf may belong to one animal in the morning and another at dusk. A pool may be a feeding place for fish at noon and a hunting place for shrews at dawn. A perch may be used by a bird, then abandoned, then occupied by a bat as night deepens. Time-sharing reduces collision. The hollow is too full for every life to demand exclusive possession.

Predation is intimate. There is no vast open chase here. A hunt is a sudden disturbance in one corner: a splash, a rustle, a shrew’s bite, a fish taking a larva, a spider tightening around a midge. Then the disturbance is absorbed. The moss springs back. The water settles. The silence returns, not because nothing happened, but because the place is built from many small deaths and births and has room for them within its pattern.

The inhabitants are shaped by the cold.

Their movements are often slower than they would be in warmer places. Growth is patient. Eggs develop gradually. Young remain hidden longer. Warmth is conserved by closeness: bodies press together in roosts, nests are lined, fur and feathers trap air. In the upper dark, bats cluster; in the marsh, voles line their chambers; in the water, fish hold where the current is least exhausting. The cold does not stop life; it refines it. It makes every expenditure of energy meaningful.

Water is the law they all obey.

Every inhabitant must account for drip, seep, rise, and flow. Nests are placed under lips of stone where moisture cannot reach. Burrow entrances are angled to avoid flooding. Dams and case-walls are built not to stop water entirely but to slow it, guide it, or create stillness beside it. When rain above raises the water level, the city shifts upward. Lower streets flood and are abandoned temporarily; animals move along higher roots, ledges, and woven bridges. When water falls again, new mud appears and is immediately explored. The inhabitants do not resist change as if it were an enemy. They adjust to it as if it were weather made personal.

This makes them seem both ancient and immediate. They are ancient in habit: the same kinds of weaving, burrowing, case-building, and roosting have occurred in wet places long before this hollow opened. They are immediate in circumstance: this particular landscape is young, sharp, and still settling. The lives here are not softened by long centuries of smoothness. They are adapting to fresh stone, new channels, unsettled ceilings, and raw edges. The city is persistent in pattern, but temporary in form.

Whoever lives there is also very small.

If you imagine an inhabitant standing upright, speaking, wearing clothes, you have imagined wrongly. The true citizens are low, wet, furred, feathered, shelled, segmented, rooted, or scaled. Their languages are scent, touch, vibration, wingbeat, and instinct. Their tools are bodies. Their buildings are extensions of need. Their memory is carried not in words but in repeated paths, inherited behaviors, and the physical traces left by previous generations.

And yet there is something person-like in the intensity of their presence.

A water vole chewing fibers into a wall seems intent. A spider repairing a broken line seems careful. A salamander watching from the shallows seems contemplative. A bat shifting in its roost seems private. These are not human qualities, but they are qualities that invite recognition. The hollow is so saturated with life that it feels watched back. Not by one mind, but by many small attentions.

In the brightest places, life is fastest and greenest. There, flowering herbs press upward, small and hardy, their leaves thick with moisture. Insects hover in the light columns. Birds flash through. Mosses glow. The air seems briefly warmer, though it is not. These zones are nurseries and meeting places, where winged lives cross with rooted ones.

In the dimmer places, life is slower and darker. Fungi spread through decaying matter. Beetles move under bark. Salamanders wait. Bats sleep. Eels rest in root-hollows. The dim zones are not empty; they are where the hollow keeps its stillness. They smell of cold stone, wet fur, and old leaves. Sound there is even more muffled, as if the darkness itself were lined with moss.

Between light and dark are the transition places, and they are the most crowded of all. Edges attract. A crack admitting grey daylight becomes a highway for insects and birds. A drip-line becomes a garden. A root hanging into water becomes a ladder for larvae, algae, and climbing beetles. A shelf just above flood level becomes a perch, a nest site, a food cache, a lookout. The inhabitants of the hollow are edge-dwellers, even when they also occupy deeper zones, because the richest opportunities appear where conditions meet.

The young terrain shapes them too.

Because the collapse is recent, the animals and plants here are pioneers as well as residents. Some have arrived through cracks. Some have drifted in as spores or seeds. Some have crawled down from the surrounding land. Some were already below, in root systems or water tables, and found new space opened to them. They are testing the hollow, filling it, making it usable. There is a rawness to their settlement. The place has not yet settled into the ease of old caves or ancient wetlands. Every life here is partly an experiment in occupancy.

This gives the inhabitants a quality of alertness. They seem to listen to the place as it changes. A crack may widen. A clod may fall. A channel may shift. A root-ceiling may sag. The animals respond quickly: rerouting a runway, reinforcing a wall, moving a nest, abandoning a flooded chamber. Their lives are flexible because the terrain is not yet stable. They are citizens of a city still being born.

If one were to ask, “Who is the most important inhabitant?” the answer would be no single one.

The moss is important because it creates surface. The caddis larvae are important because they shape flow. The voles are important because they make streets and rooms. The birds are important because they link the hollow to the sky. The bats are important because they bring nutrients from outside. The fungi are important because they return dead matter to use. The fish and salamanders are important because they regulate smaller lives and keep the water from becoming only insects and larvae. The insects are important because they are countless, varied, and foundational to food. The roots are important because they hold the broken ceiling together. Even the water itself, though not alive in the same way, acts like a citizen with preferences, moving where it will and forcing all others to accommodate.

Together they form a body of inhabitants.

This body has no single face. It has many eyes: the black bead-eyes of voles, the still eyes of salamanders, the clustered darkness of bat faces, the tiny facetted eyes of insects. It has many mouths: chewing, filtering, sucking, biting, weaving, singing in brief calls, opening nestlings’ throats. It has many hands, though none are human hands: paws, claws, fins, tails, root-tips, larval silk-spinners, mandibles, sucker-mouths, feathered wings that serve as hands of flight. It has many homes, but they are all made of the same wet world.

At times the hollow seems to breathe as one creature.

Droplets fall in rhythm. Condensation trembles and releases. Moss swells with moisture. Roots draw water upward. Fish move in currents created by seepage. Bats exhale in the dark. Birds return with insects. Spiders tighten lines. Shrews hunt. Fungi digest. The whole place inhales damp and exhales life. Its breath is not loud. It is a texture: cool, green, mineral, alive.

No one owns this place.

That is part of what lives there: an absence of ownership in the human sense. There is no ruler, no planner, no keeper of records. There are territories, yes, but they are temporary and bodily. There is labor, but no wage. There is architecture, but no blueprint. There is density, but no crowd made of strangers. Every inhabitant is bound to the place by need and competence. They live there because they can fit, because they can endure the cold, because they can find food, because they can build or hide or hunt in the wet intervals.

If anything can be said to “live” there as a single being, it is the hollow’s ecology itself: the wet, cold, green, many-bodied pattern that persists even as its parts change. That pattern is the true long-term resident. It survives floods by re-forming. It survives death by feeding new life. It survives collapse by being woven again. It is not immortal, but it is continuous. It is not conscious, but it is full of awareness. It is not made, but it is built.

So the answer to the question is this:

What lives there are the packed, patient, industrious lives of a wet underground world. Mosses and roots make the ground and ceiling. Insects make walls, webs, cases, and paper rooms. Small mammals make tunnels and nests. Birds make mud cups and carry the sky in through cracks. Bats make the upper darkness warm with sleeping bodies. Fish, eels, salamanders, and shellfish fill the water with slow motion and stillness. Fungi and microbes digest the past into the present. Every drop, ledge, pool, crack, and root is occupied, used, repaired, or waited beside.

Whoever lives there is small, plural, wet, and close. They are the citizens of interval. They live in the spaces between stone and water, root and air, darkness and grey light, collapse and repair. They do not speak, yet the place is full of their meanings. They do not build with tools, yet the hollow is a city. They do not gather in one figure, yet together they become the one being that belongs there: a cold, green, breathing settlement of lives beneath the ordinary surface.
