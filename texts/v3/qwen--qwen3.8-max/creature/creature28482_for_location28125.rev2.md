What lives there is not one thing, but a compact commonwealth of dry lives, all fitted to the hollow as the shards are fitted into the floor. Nothing there seems to live alone. Even the stillness has tenants. The inhabitants are mostly small, because the place itself is small and because smallness is the scale at which its details can be answered. They are quiet, because sound is rare and therefore expensive. They are slow, because haste would be a kind of thirst, and thirst has no meaning in a world that has already refused water.

The first lives you notice are the ones that move among the tiles.

There are beetles with shells like fired pottery, their backs glazed in dull browns, blacks, and smoke-grays. They move with the grave confidence of little laborers. Their legs tap almost silently on the mosaic ground, and where they walk they leave no trail of moisture, only the faintest rearrangement of dust. Some carry grains of powdered brick larger than their heads. Others press their bodies into cracks and remain there for long intervals, as if they are not resting but being fitted into the architecture. When they fly, which is seldom, their wings make a dry, papery sound, like pages being separated in a closed room.

There are moths that cluster beneath leaves in pale, trembling groups. Their wings are not soft in the ordinary way; they are thin and fibrous, like horn scraped to translucence or paper made from husks. When at rest, they open and close their wings by tiny degrees, tasting the warmth. The patterns on them are often too regular: a repeated chevron, a carved line, a mark like a maker’s stamp. Sometimes three moths on three separate walls bear the same mark so exactly that it is easier to believe they are one moth repeating itself than three creatures born apart.

There are lizards, or things with the posture of lizards, that keep watch from seams in the clay. They are matte and dry, their scales not glossy but faintly chalky, as if painted and then left in a warm kiln too long. They do not dart. They turn their heads with great deliberation. Their eyes are small dark beads of resin, and when they blink, the motion seems to happen slightly after the need for it. They eat the smaller insects, but even predation in the hollow has a courteous quality: a stillness, a tilt, a tiny disappearance, and then the stillness again.

There are ants, or ant-like creatures, that harvest the resin beads as they swell from the stems. Their bodies are narrow and warm-colored, their abdomens sometimes translucent with stored oil. They do not march in urgent lines. They move in patient chains, each one touching the one before, not to command but to confirm. When a resin bead is ready, they stand around it in a loose circle until it loosens, then carry it away between them like a lantern. Some tend the seed capsules, chewing tiny openings at exactly the right moment so that the oils inside can be distributed without waste.

There are spiders that do not make sticky webs. Their silk is more like fine cord, more like thread pulled from plant stems, and they use it to stitch gaps rather than trap prey. You will find their work in corners where a tile has separated slightly from its neighbor, or where a bundle of reeds has begun to loosen. The seam-spiders repair as much as they hunt. Their webs are not traps but sutures. They move along them with the care of seamstresses, tightening, testing, adding one filament at a time.

There are small fur-covered or feather-covered things that live in the upper tangle, where the braided plants grow thickest. They are not birds and not mice, but something between the two in habit. Their bodies are soft and dry, their eyes bright and careful. They eat seeds, nest in hollow stems, and carry shed fibers to line their chambers. Some have faces like masked shrews; others have crests of stiff plumes that fold flat when they are still. They do not sing. At most they make a low breathy note, a sound like air passing over a warm vessel. That note is not music. It is information: a change in the light, a shift in the dust, a seedcase about to open.

There are creatures that swim through the dryness itself.

They are the hardest to name, because they invite the wrong names. They move through fine dust, floating pollen, and motes of resin-light as though the air there were thick enough to bear them. Their bodies are slender, almost eel-like, but not wet, not sleek. They are fringed with dry filaments that stir the particulate glow around them. When they pass, they leave faint wakes in the dust, and the wakes close behind them without sound. They do not fly so much as lean against the medium of the hollow. Sometimes they rise in slow spirals around a beam of filtered light. Sometimes they rest inside a translucent pod, curled like a question mark, motionless for so long that they seem to have become a pattern inside the plant. They are not fish, except in the way a dream might call them fish. They are swimmers in absence, animals of a medium made from warmth, dust, and patience.

And then there are the plants, because in this place they are not scenery. They are residents too.

They are crowded and serious. They do not sprawl with the abandon of wet leaves; they hoard themselves. Their bodies are thick, sealed, furred, or plated. They grow into the constructed ground as though the ground had been made for them, though perhaps it is more accurate to say that they have made the ground by growing into it. The fat rosettes open only a fraction in the warmth, revealing inner leaves polished with wax. The hanging ropes of segmented stems sway when there is no wind, because their own weight is enough. The mineral-threaded cushions cling to copper pins and clay cracks, drawing nourishment from reactions too slow to see. The broad fan-leaves give off resinous scent when brushed, and the odor is not unpleasant: warm, faintly bitter, like heated wood and old incense.

Some plants seem to exist chiefly to feed the others. Their seedpods split with dry little reports, offering hard grains to the husk-mice and plume-voles. Their resin beads feed the ants and moths. Their hollow stems provide nurseries. Their leaves form shade that is not cool so much as softened, a shade that keeps the heat from becoming too direct. Some plants grow over one another in slow rivalry, not strangling but incorporating, turning a neighboring stem into a trellis, a wall into a shoulder, a doorway into an arch of green-brown patience.

The plants also obey the hollow’s strangeness. A leaf may begin as a single shape and continue as a repeated pattern across three surfaces. A vine may climb toward a corner that is farther away than it appears, and still arrive without seeming to have stretched. Some rosettes do not grow outward so much as they grow into recurrence: the same leaf appearing again and again with only slight variation, as if the plant has learned a word and is practicing it.

But if you stay long enough, and if you let your eyes stop demanding ordinary movement, you become aware of other inhabitants. These are not the first lives you see. They are the ones the hollow seems to produce out of its own need for hands.

They might be called the inlaid ones, because they belong to the place the way a tile belongs to a floor. They are small, no larger than a child’s forearm when they choose to be measured at all, though their size seems negotiable. They are not human, though they have hands, and their hands are what you notice first. Their fingers are long, precise, and tireless. They are made for pressing dust into cracks, for tying cords, for smoothing wax, for lifting things too small for ordinary concern. Their bodies are matte, dry, and warm, with the texture of fired clay softened by fine fiber. Some seem to have skin like smoothed brick dust; others like woven matting; others like old bone polished by handling. They are not fragile. They are fitted.

Their faces are difficult to remember because they are not always faces in the expected sense. Some have two dark resin eyes and a seam where a mouth might be. Others have masks of thin clay, fitted so closely that the seam is nearly invisible. Others present no face at all, only a smooth surface, and yet you feel regarded. When one turns toward you, it does not startle. It considers. It may offer a small, courteous blankness, as if it has decided that your need to see a face is not its immediate problem.

They move like artisans who have no deadline and no desire to finish quickly. One will crouch beside a loosened tile and press powder into the gap with its thumb. Another will carry a cord of twisted fiber over its shoulder, not with effort but with familiarity. Three may stand together at a cracked wall, passing a wooden peg back and forth in a rhythm that seems more like conversation than labor. They do not speak in words. They communicate by taps, by the rubbing of dry leaves, by the scent of resin released when a joint is warmed, by the angle of a hand held still. Sometimes their speech is a pause. Sometimes it is the decision to repair one thing instead of another.

They are the menders, or at least they perform mending as a form of life.

They do not appear to have been born in any ordinary way. Sometimes, when a wall is repaired, one of them remains afterward, as though the act of fixing had left behind a residue with hands. Sometimes a seedcase opens and a small clay face looks out. Sometimes a knot of fibers tightens in a corner and becomes a body over the course of a long afternoon. They are not young or old in the usual sense. They have the newness of recent construction and the familiarity of repeated gesture. They may be descendants of builders, or tools that learned to walk, or memories of making that the hollow has kept alive by giving them bodies. It is possible that all three explanations are true and that the hollow does not distinguish among them.

They do not drink. They do not seek water, do not mourn it, do not remember it as a loss. They take nourishment from warmth, from oils, from resin, from the contact between materials. One may press its palm to a bead of amber resin and remain there until its fingers shine faintly. Another may sit in a pooled patch of light with its hands open, absorbing heat as though heat were a food. Some eat seeds by holding them until the oils loosen. Some feed by touching their fingers to the dust around the dry fountain and then to their faces, if they have faces. They are sustained by participation. To be needed by the hollow is, for them, a kind of eating.

They tend the plants without owning them. They bend stems aside when a wall needs repair. They clear dead leaves before the leaves can crumble too finely into the substrate. They place powdered shell near roots that require it. They leave gaps for new shoots. They do not garden in the sense of imposing order; they negotiate. If a vine has grown over a doorway, they may decide that the doorway has become a trellis. If a tile has been lifted by a root, they may not put it back. They may instead fill the space with crushed ceramic and let the root become part of the floor. Their work is not restoration in the old sense. It is continuation.

The inlaid ones are not the only people-shaped things in the hollow, but they are the most present. There are also others that seem less like bodies and more like repetitions.

Some residents exist as patterns. A carved mark appearing on three tiles may not be a mark at all but a creature holding itself in three places because three places are easier than one. A beetle seen at the top of one terrace, then at the edge of a basin, then beneath a leaf may be one beetle moving through the hollow’s negotiable distances. A small figure bent over a repair may be visible only at the corner of the eye, not because it is shy but because it is partly an intention. When you look directly, it becomes a wall, a stem, a shadow. When you forget it, it resumes its work.

There are beings that seem to be composed of resemblance. They are the same leaf shape occurring in three plants. They are the same ripple of dust happening twice in the same beam of light. They are the sense that someone has just placed a thing where it belongs, though no one is there. The hollow tolerates individuality, but it prefers recurrence. It likes things to echo. It does not make copies so much as it allows one thing to be several.

Near the center, or one of the centers, where the dry fountain rises from stacked bowls and broken columns, the inhabitants gather more thickly.

The dry fountain is not wet, has never been wet, does not remember wetness as a possibility. It holds seeds, shed skins, copper filings, folded leaves, and fine warm dust that moves in slow spirals when anything passes near. Around it, the beetles pause. The moths settle in pale rows. The dust-fish circle without urgency. The inlaid ones come to deposit things: a grain of salt, a fiber cord too short to use elsewhere, a shard with a useful edge, a resin bead not yet ripe. They do not worship. There is no priesthood, no altar fire, no liquid offering. They attend. They maintain. They treat the fountain as a place where the hollow’s many small acts briefly admit that they belong together.

Sometimes one of the inlaid ones stands in the basin and becomes still for so long that it seems to have become part of the structure. Then, after a long interval, it lifts a hand and places a single grain of dust into a crack. The gesture is tiny. It is also complete. Around it, the other lives continue. A seedcase opens. A moth lifts and settles. A dust-fish passes through the spiral and leaves a trail of light. Nothing is dramatic. Everything is sufficient.

There are larger creatures, but few.

They hide in the thickest growth, where the architecture has been most thoroughly overgrown. One may be something like a tortoise, or something like a movable terrace: broad-backed, slow, plated with fitted fragments of tile and living cushions of mineral moss. It moves so rarely that it is easy to mistake for a wall. When it does move, the plants on its back shift with it, and small creatures scatter into the seams. It eats dry matter: shed leaves, old fiber, the papery skins left behind by molting things. It does not digest wetly. It reduces what it takes into fine powder, which it leaves behind in neat heaps. The inlaid ones gather this powder and use it. In this way, even the largest resident of the hollow serves the same law as the smallest.

Another large creature may be nothing more than a presence in the green tangle, a warmth that is not warmth, a pressure that suggests a body too patient to be seen whole. It may have limbs like lashed poles, or it may only have the memory of limbs. It does not threaten. It shelters. It is the reason some paths feel safer than others, and the reason certain corners seem to be kept.

Sound among the inhabitants is rare, so each sound has weight.

The click of a beetle’s claw is a small statement. The rasp of leaf against leaf is a conversation conducted over long intervals. The creak of a cord under tension is a warning that something has shifted, though perhaps only by the width of a hair. When a seedpod splits, the sound is too loud for the stillness, and every nearby life pauses, not in fear but in attention. Then the pause ends. The pod opens. The ants arrive. The dust settles. The hollow continues.

If you enter the place, the inhabitants do not flee in alarm. They adjust around you.

Your heat is noticed. Your breathing is noticed, perhaps with mild surprise, because your breath carries moisture and the hollow has no use for it. Your shadow is examined by the moths. The beetles note the pressure of your steps. The inlaid ones watch you with their resin-dark eyes, or with their blank clay faces, or with whatever faculty they use when they are not looking. They are not hostile. They are not eager. They are courteous in the way of beings who have made room for one another under pressure.

They may come closer if you become still. One may place a warm pod near your hand, not as an offering in the sentimental sense but as an explanation: this is what liquid is here. Another may lead your attention to a crack where a resin bead is swelling, showing you that the hollow provides. If you ask for water, they will not understand the ask as a need. They may understand it as longing, and longing makes distance stubborn. The entrance may become smaller. The corner you want may move farther away. The path behind you may fold itself into a screen of stems. Not to punish you. Only because desire, in this place, is a clumsy architecture.

If you accept the law of the hollow, the hollow becomes more legible.

You begin to see that the residents are not separate from the construction. The animals are also tools. The plants are also walls. The menders are also habits. The dust is also memory. The silence is also pressure. Every life is a way of maintaining the place, and the place is a way of maintaining the lives. There is no clear edge between inhabitant and dwelling. A beetle crossing a tile is part of the floor’s thought. A vine climbing a wall is part of the wall’s continuation. An inlaid one pressing powder into a crack is both a creature and a joint in the structure.

What lives there, in the end, is not a species or a single keeper. It is a distributed body of small attentions.

It is the beetle carrying grit.  
It is the moth with a maker’s mark on its wing.  
It is the ant bearing resin like a small sun.  
It is the spider stitching a wall back into itself.  
It is the dust-fish moving through dry light as though absence were a sea.  
It is the fat-leaved plants hoarding warmth instead of water.  
It is the inlaid ones with their patient hands, repairing what was never ruined, only unfinished.  
It is the dry fountain where shed skins and seeds gather into significance.  
It is the silence that is not empty but crowded.  
It is the hollow itself, breathing slowly through many small lives.

None of them are natural, if natural means untouched. All of them are alive, if alive means responsive, continuing, and capable of care. They have learned to be lush without wetness, abundant without excess, strange without violence. They live in a room that has become a world by refusing to be less than one. And if you leave, the hollow will not seem empty behind you. It will go on as it always does: mending, growing, settling, repeating, keeping its warmth, keeping its dust, keeping its quiet law, utterly without water and utterly full.
