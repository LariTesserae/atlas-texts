What lives there cannot be pointed to all at once. The chamber distributes being as it distributes light: first a brightness, then a waiting, then small presences emerging from the pause.

At first, you would say the birds live there.

They are pale, narrow-winged things, more like swifts than swallows, though no surface naturalist would claim them without hesitation. Their bodies are the color of air that has touched stone for too long: a soft gray-white with a faint mineral bloom along the breast and throat. Their eyes are dark and wet-looking, adapted to the bright column and the sudden dim beyond it. They enter through the light-shaft in quick descents, folding themselves through the bright air as if passing through a membrane, then wheel upward again with a soft rushing of wings. The sound is brief and textile, like cloth shaken once and then folded away.

They do not truly nest, or not in any way that admits of accumulation. They use the small hollows high along the upper wall, cups of stone no larger than a hand, where condensation gathers and insects occasionally stray. They rest there for moments, sometimes for hours, pressed against the rock with their wings crossed, becoming another pale lump in the mineral sheen. When they leave, they leave almost nothing. A dropping, perhaps, but the damp takes it. A feather, perhaps, but the water films accept it, darken it, and let it dissolve into the chamber’s slow economy. They are not tenants so much as visits. They are the chamber’s quick breaths.

In the pool, there are other lives, slower and more withdrawn.

The fish are blind and pale, waxen in color, their bodies long enough to suggest eels but too gentle to be predatory. Each keeps its own distance from the others, moving near the bottom with a deliberation that seems almost ceremonial. Their fins tremble like thin sheets of old ice. Their mouths open and close without urgency. They feed on minute things, perhaps on bacteria, perhaps on films of algae, perhaps on some unseen drift of life too small for the chamber to name. When the light strikes the water at midday, their bodies become faintly translucent, and one can almost believe they are made of the pool itself given temporary shape. They do not school. They do not scatter. They simply occupy separate spheres of water, as if solitude is their element.

Along the pool’s edge move small crustaceans, translucent and unhurried. They are nearly invisible until light passes through them, revealing tiny dark eyes and the faint architecture of limbs. Water beetles rise now and then, carrying silver bubbles on their backs like stolen pieces of the bright air. They break the surface without sound, hover for a moment, then sink again. The pool accepts them as it accepts everything: without surprise, without emphasis.

On the walls live the crickets, the moths, and the damp-cornered salamander-things.

The crickets stand motionless for long periods, their antennae lifted in faint question marks. They do not seem to chirp so much as remember chirping; one click may escape, hang in the air, and then be swallowed by the room’s patience. The moths rest with folded wings, patterned like mineral stains. They are difficult to see until they move, and even then their movement seems less like flight than a brief disagreement between one patch of stone and another. The salamander-like creatures stay where condensation is thickest. Their skin is wet and faintly luminous, not brightly, but with the dull sheen of water holding light after the light has gone. They move only when a droplet falls near them, as if each drop were a word in a language they no longer speak but still understand.

The mosses live there too, if one counts restraint as life. They form silver-green patches where moisture is reliable, thin as breath on glass. The algae are less plants than stains: dull gold where the sunlit side touches the pool, blue-green where shadow lingers. The root threads from the ceiling above live there in their slow probing, blind fingers descending into the dark, tips swollen with water. Even the droplets seem alive in the way they gather, tremble, fall, and vanish.

But none of these, alone, is the one who truly lives there.

They are inhabitants as notes are in a chord. They are presences arranged around a center that is not empty but withheld. The chamber has a way of suggesting a dweller without displaying one. You feel it first in the small disturbances that are not quite disturbances: a chert cobble near the pool turned so that its edge catches the light, then returned to its former place; a root thread severed cleanly but not carried away; a hand-sized print in the clay already smoothing itself under a film of moisture; a flat stone that seems warmer than it should be, as if something has recently rested there and then risen without needing to leave a mark.

If a name is needed, call it the Unfinished.
