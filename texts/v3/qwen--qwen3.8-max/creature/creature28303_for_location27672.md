What lives there is not one tenant but a congregation, and not merely a congregation but a single slow body made of many small lives. It is a green population without streets, doors, or voices, yet it inhabits the bowl as completely as any people could inhabit a city. The residents are the plants themselves, the fungi threading beneath them, the microscopic lives moving in films of water, and the few hidden animals that pass through the wet architecture like quiet strangers. To live there is to be pressed close, to be damp, to be patient, and to be part of a structure that is always being made by the act of continuing to exist.

The most visible inhabitants are the dwarf conifers. They are the elders of the settlement, though not tall, not commanding in the way trees of lower forests are. They are dark, resinous, and bent by cold. Their trunks lean into one another as if sharing weight is the oldest custom of the place. Their bark is rough and brown-black where wet, silver-gray where dry, and their needles are short, stiff, and packed tight along the branches. They do not rise freely toward the sky; they thicken sideways, downward, inward. They make low halls with their limbs. They make doorways where branches have grown together. They make dark little rooms where no wind enters easily and where the air smells sharply of sap and cold leaf.

These conifers are not individualists. They live as a fused community. Where one trunk leans, another props it. Where one branch dies, another grows through it. Where one tree falls, it may remain held aloft by the living arms around it, becoming a beam in the city rather than vanishing into decay. Their roots do not merely anchor them; they climb over rock, lace through peat, and bind the loose mountain material into something stable enough for others to grow upon. In this sense, the conifers are both citizens and architecture. They are inhabitants, but they are also walls, roofs, pillars, and bridges.

Among them live the cushion plants, the most quietly numerous of the bowl’s residents. They grow in rounded, tight mounds, so dense that they seem less like separate organisms than like green organs of the earth itself. They are the upholsterers of the place. They soften the angles of fresh rock without erasing them. They build small raised borders around hollows. They hold moisture in their centers. They catch spores, dead needles, and windblown grains, turning each tiny accumulation into a future foothold for another plant. They are not soft in the indulgent sense; their surfaces are firm, compact, and resilient. But they make the ground inhabitable. They make it possible for roots to enter where bare stone would have refused them.

Mosses are everywhere, and in their quiet way they are the most civic of all. They are the masons, roofers, plumbers, and binders of the small mountain city. They line channels that carry water. They seal cracks where wind would otherwise search too deeply. They cover fallen wood so that it becomes a nursery rather than debris. They climb over stones with patient green fingers, not by force but by persistence. They drink from fog as much as from rain. They hold water in their leaves, in their stems, in the spaces between themselves, and in the layers of dead growth beneath their living tips. When the bowl weeps after a cloud has passed, it is the moss that turns that weeping into storage.

The lichens are slower still. They are the oldest-looking residents even when they are not truly old. They crust the exposed angles of rock in pale gray, greenish white, and ash-blue. They are the first interpreters of the newly exposed stone, reading the raw glacial surfaces and beginning the long work of turning mineral hardness into future soil. They do not rush. They do not crowd. They spread by fractions, by weatherings, by tiny chemical persuasions. Where a lichen lives, the rock begins to lose its absolute refusal. It becomes slightly porous, slightly crumbly, slightly capable of holding moisture. In a place so newly uncovered by ice, the lichens are the first quiet witnesses. They are the archivists of the bowl, writing their slow pale script over fracture scars and glacial polish.

Sedges, dwarf willows, and cold-hardy grasses live at the wet margins. They are the edge-dwellers, the terrace-makers, the ones who prefer the places where water lingers. Their roots bind the softer ground. Their leaves catch droplets. Their dead stems from previous seasons remain among the living, adding structure to the surface. The dwarf willows are low and sinuous, more like cords than trees, threading through the undergrowth and making hidden pathways. The sedges stand in small clumps near dark pools, their pale tips visible even in dim light. The grasses are thin and tough, not lush, not waving in any cheerful breeze, but enduring. They live where the bowl is most saturated, and they help turn wetness into form.

Beneath all of this, though unseen, lives another population: the fungi. They are the hidden clerks of the place, the workers of transition, the ones who negotiate between death and growth. Their threads pass through dead wood, through peat, through fallen needles, through old root channels. They break down what cannot be broken down quickly and turn it into something that can be used. In colder places, decay is slow, and here it is slower still, but the fungi make it possible for death to become infrastructure. A fallen branch is not simply a corpse; it is a future beam, a future bridge, a future surface for moss. A dead needle is not simply waste; it is insulation, floor material, future humus. The fungi help the city absorb its own losses. They make loss useful.

The roots are the living roads, muscles, and nerves of the bowl. They are so dense beneath the surface that the ground itself feels made of intention. They thread through scree and peat. They stitch together loose stones. They enter the bodies of fallen trees and hold them in place. They form bridges over meltwater cuts. They grip, release, regrip. They follow water. They avoid the harshest cold by staying wrapped in the living mass. In a place with little true soil, roots are the city’s engineers. They do not excavate. They do not import materials. They simply grow into the spaces available, and by doing so they make those spaces permanent.

If one looks closer, into the wettest seams of this world, there are lives even smaller still. In the thin films of water sliding over roots and leaves, there are microscopic beings for whom a droplet is a landscape and a bead of condensation is a temporary sea. Rotifers turn in the moisture among mosses. Nematodes move through the water-filled spaces between peat fibers. Tardigrades endure in the damp, their bodies almost unimaginably small, waiting out cold or dryness in forms of suspended patience. Bacteria and yeasts work at the edges of leaf surfaces, in bark crevices, in the cold tea-dark water pooled among roots. They are not visible as individuals, but their presence is part of the smell: mineral, green, faintly fermented, ancient and fresh at once. They are the chemists of the bowl, making nutrients available, making decay productive, making the whole system metabolically alive.

Animal life, in the larger sense, is rare. The city is not built for herds, flocks, or loud bodies. It is too cold, too wet, too slow, too densely vegetal. Yet it is not entirely without animal residents. A springtail may move through the moss, pale and minute, navigating a forest of leaf tips that to it must seem like towers. It lives in the interstices, in the damp hidden places where fungal threads and decaying matter offer enough for survival. A mite may cross a wet leaf, dark and deliberate, traveling across a surface that is to it both road and territory. It is not passing through in the casual way of larger creatures; it is living there, entirely within the smallness of that leaf, that branch, that patch of moss.

In one sheltered hollow, where a root arches over a dark depression, a spider may live. It is not a large or flamboyant creature. It is patient, angular, and adapted to scarcity. Its web, if it has one, is likely modest, placed where moisture and structure allow it to intercept a stray gnat or a wind-blown insect. It waits because waiting is the economy of the place. It does not need many chances; it needs one. It belongs to the bowl not as a dominant presence but as a quiet specialist, a small predator in a world mostly governed by growth, water, and cold.

A moth may appear from time to time, but it will seem out of place, as if the weather has carried it there by mistake. Its wings are folded close. Its body is still. It rests against bark or under a branch, not because it has chosen the place as home, but because the place has temporarily accepted it. It may survive the night if it finds a crevice where the air is less sharp. It may not. Its presence does not alter the bowl. The bowl continues its slow respiration of fog and drip. Still, for a moment, the moth adds one small animal silence to the green silence.

A bird may cross overhead now and then, but it does not stay. It is too exposed in the bowl, too visible against the dim interior. It may circle, glance down, and move on. There is little here for a creature that requires open flight, abundant prey, or easy nesting. The city is too crowded, too low, too damp, too inward. If a bird were to remain, it would have to become small, secretive, and patient, almost a mammal of the undergrowth, almost a rumor. So birds are mostly movement above the rim, brief interruptions in the mist, not residents.

Yet even the rareness of animals is part of the place’s character. The bowl does not need them to be complete. Its life is not dependent on conspicuous motion. It is complete in the way a saturated sponge is complete: by holding, by absorbing, by continuing. Its drama is not pursuit or flight. Its drama is condensation, freezing, thawing, rooting, stitching, and the slow conquest of bare stone by green persistence.

If one asks who lives there in the deepest sense, the answer is not a list of species. The answer is a collective being. The bowl itself lives as a composite entity. It is a body made of many bodies. Its surface is skin. Its mosses are felt. Its roots are sinew. Its pools are dark eyes. Its channels are veins. Its dead wood is memory held in place by living tissue. Its fog is breath drawn in from above the rim and exhaled as drip. It does not think, but it responds. It does not plan, but it arranges. It does not build with tools, but it builds all the same.

This collective resident has no single face. It has no voice that could be heard except as the tiny percussion of water and the whisper of needles. It does not move from place to place. Its movement is internal: water moving through moss, roots moving through cracks, stems thickening, cushions expanding, fungi spreading, leaves falling and becoming floor. Its life is measured in inches, in layers, in the slow filling of gaps. It is newborn because the rock is new, but ancient in habit because the processes at work there are among the oldest forms of life: clinging, binding, holding moisture, making shelter from smallness.

There is something almost monastic about the inhabitants. They live under strict conditions: cold, thin air, little light, constant damp, short growing seasons, frost at the edges of every day. They do not complain. They adapt by becoming compact. They do not spread extravagantly; they deepen. They do not waste. Every dead needle is retained. Every fallen branch is incorporated. Every drop of water is caught, slowed, passed along, stored, or released with care. Their existence is a kind of discipline, not imposed from outside but grown from within. Closeness is their success. Patience is their method. Saturation is their form of abundance.

The city’s residents are also conservators of one another. A seedling does not begin on bare stone. It begins on the body of another life: on moss, on peat, on bark, on a cushion plant’s edge, on the decayed surface of a fallen limb. The young are raised by the old, not intentionally, but materially. The old provide texture, moisture, and shelter. The dead provide structure. The living provide warmth, if warmth can be said to exist there in any but the faintest chemical sense. Even in cold, there are microclimates: a shaded hollow may be colder, a sheltered crotch of branches may be slightly less cold, a sunlit droplet may hold a momentary bead of weak gold. The residents inhabit these tiny differences with exquisite precision.

Because the place is young, its inhabitants are also pioneers, though they do not know the word. They have entered a space recently released from ice. They have found not comfort but opportunity. They have accepted instability. The ground may heave with frost. A meltwater channel may cut through a root platform overnight. A storm may tear a gap in the canopy. A cushion plant may die and leave a hollow. Each disturbance becomes a new task. Moss enters. Fungus enters. A creeping stem tests the opening. A seed catches in the wet surface. The wound is not repaired from outside; it is grown over from within. The resident is always becoming more itself by absorbing interruption.

There are no owners in the bowl. There is no hierarchy in the human sense, though there are roles. The conifers make height where height is possible. The cushion plants make floors. The mosses make seal and sponge. The lichens make beginnings. The fungi make transitions. The roots make cohesion. The water makes movement. The cold makes restraint. The fog makes supply. Each resident depends on the others not through agreement but through arrangement. They have grown into one another so thoroughly that to remove one would be to alter all.

If one imagines the bowl from above, it seems like a green bowl of breath held between stone. If one imagines it from within, it seems like a crowded, dim, wet chapel where every surface is alive and no space is empty. The inhabitants do not worship, yet they create a feeling of reverence. They do not speak, yet they make a language of texture, moisture, and pressure. They do not possess tools, yet they have built a city of seams, corridors, cisterns, roofs, and bridges. They are not gentle because they intend gentleness; they are gentle because harshness would break them. Their softness is survival. Their density is tenderness made physical.

What lives there, then, is a multitude and a unity. It is the dwarf conifer leaning against its neighbor. It is the cushion plant swelling by millimeters. It is the moss drinking fog. It is the lichen weathering stone. It is the fungus threading dead wood. It is the root stitching rock. It is the springtail in the moss. It is the mite on the leaf. It is the spider in the root arch. It is the moth resting against bark. It is the bird crossing and not staying. It is the microbial life trembling in a film of water. It is the bowl itself, breathing cloud through green lungs, making a world where there was recently only newly exposed rock.

To live there is to be part of a slow green city that repairs itself by growing, remembers itself by accumulating, and endures by staying close. The inhabitants are not separate from their home. They are its substance. They are its walls, its floors, its roofs, its drains, its gardens, its citizens, and its silent keepers. The place lives because they live, and they live because they live together, pressed into one another by cold, wetness, and the patient necessity of growth.
