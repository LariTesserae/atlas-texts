What lives there is not easy to separate from what is being made.

The first life one notices is not a body but a process: green light moving through water, mineral snow falling without urgency, warmth rising from hidden cracks, silt binding itself into something that almost remembers being stone. The basin feels inhabited before any inhabitant is seen. It feels attended to. It feels kept.

Then, if you watch the terraces long enough, you begin to distinguish the keepers.

They are people, after a fashion, though not in the way of people who live under open skies and walk on finished ground. They are shaped for the drowned valley: compact and supple, dense without being heavy, their bodies made to endure pressure, dimness, warmth, and the slow resistance of water. They do not move quickly. Nothing there does. Their motion is a kind of deliberate drifting, aided by ropes, handholds, footholds, poles, and the long familiarity of bodies that know every change in current. They climb through the town more than they swim, passing from terrace to walkway to hanging garden with the patience of creatures who understand that haste belongs to places where the ground is already certain.

Their skin is smooth and faintly glossy, as if always newly washed by mineral water. It may be greenish, gray, amber, or the pale buff of wet chalk, depending on district, lineage, and the particular living films that dwell upon it. Many of them carry a delicate bloom over shoulders, wrists, and cheekbones: a thin symbiotic coat of algae and mineral-secreting moss, cultivated from birth. This living skin helps them breathe in the water, taste the chemical moods of the basin, and sense where lime is gathering or failing. It also makes them look slightly unfinished, as though their outlines have been softened by the same patient processes that build the town.

Their hands are their most striking feature. They are broad, long-fingered, extraordinarily sensitive, with palms that can read a wall the way surface dwellers might read a face. A hand placed on a new terrace can tell whether the current is carrying enough mineral matter, whether the crust is hungry, whether a frame is tired, whether a root has taken hold. Their fingertips are often pale from lime, their knuckles polished by rope, their nails short and strong. They gesture constantly: small motions, precise, unhurried. A raised palm can mean wait. A closed hand resting against stone can mean this place is stable. Two fingers drawn along a cord can mean follow. A touch to another’s wrist and then to the nearest wall can mean we are still here; the ground is still becoming.

Their eyes are large and low-light, adapted to the green gloom. They blink slowly, and a second translucent membrane can close across the eye when they work in bright mineral dust or near vent-water too sharp for ordinary sight. Their faces are calm, often quiet, shaped by a culture that does not waste expression. They do not shout. They do not need to. Voices, when used, are low and close, softened by water, reserved for names, warnings, blessings, and certain old work-songs that are more vibration than melody. Much of their speech is tactile: tugs on ropes, taps on frames, knots tied in shared cords, patterns pressed into the arms of others. To listen among them is to feel meaning arrive through touch before sound.

They are amphibious in a broad and patient sense. They have lungs, and they remember air, but their bodies have learned the water’s habits. They can remain submerged for long periods, slowing their hearts, letting the living film on their skin exchange gases with the warm mineral medium. Even so, they do not abandon air entirely. They keep it, cultivate it, house it. Inverted bells of woven reed and sealed bladder-skin hang beneath platforms like strange fruits, each one holding a pocket of breathable air. These bells are tended by special keepers who massage their skins, patch their seams, and refresh their contents by guiding bubbles upward from vents or from carefully controlled surface exchanges. Children learn to rest inside them, curled in the dim warmth like seeds inside pods. Elders sometimes sleep there for days, breathing slowly, their bodies suspended between water and air.

They wear little that is rigid. Their garments are made from the living and the buoyant: woven fronds, bladder-leather, fine nets, sashes of root-fiber, tool loops of braided reed. Clothing is often half alive, patched with moss or seeded with tiny crust-forming organisms that harden certain edges over time. A worker’s shoulder-wrap may begin as flexible fiber and, after years of use and mineral contact, become a stiff pale collar marked with the shape of the body that wore it. Ornament is modest and meaningful: a milk-shell bead, a knot of blue fiber, a sliver of obsidian polished to a dark mirror, a tiny bone clasp carved with a pattern that records a family’s first terrace. Metal, when encountered, is not admired. It is considered sharp in the wrong way. It tastes badly in the water. It may sour the forming stone. They keep it away from nurseries and growing beds, and if a piece must be handled, it is wrapped in cloth, resin, or leaf.

The people are often called tenders, though they do not use the word as a title so much as a daily condition. They tend the roots. They tend the mineral flows. They tend the young stone. They tend one another. Their whole civilization is a practice of maintenance. They do not believe that a thing, once made, remains made. In that basin, permanence is not a fact but a relationship. A wall becomes real because hands return to it. A path firms because feet repeat it. A platform survives because someone feeds its edges with lime-water and presses new fiber into its joints. A house is not an object; it is a slow creature needing care.

Because of this, their settlements are crowded with presence. Not noisy presence—never noisy—but the pressure of many small attentions. At any hour, if hour has meaning in such light, there are figures moving among the terraces. A woman steadies herself with one foot against a basalt knob while she guides a current of warm mineral water into a forming bed. A child ties a practice knot into a communal cord, then unties it, then ties it again until the pattern is right. An elder taps a wall with a rounded stone, listening to the answer, deciding whether it needs another season of feeding. Two workers part a curtain of root-filaments and pass through, leaving the strands trembling. Someone scrapes excess crust from a hinge of woven wood. Someone else leaves a small offering of fiber at the base of a cut root, smoothing the gift into place with a thumb.

Their labor is organized, but not harshly. They have roles, and roles may change over a lifetime. There are root-speakers who know how to encourage plants to bind loose ground. There are air-keepers who maintain the breathing bells. There are rain-readers who watch the pale columns where surface water enters and predict how the upper temperature will shift. There are crust-singers who test young stone by sound and touch. There are frame-makers who build the first lattices that will later become walls. There are silt-guiders who use low barriers to direct settling particles into useful beds. There are bladder-tenders who cultivate the floating plants that give buoyancy to platforms. There are knot-readers who keep records in cord-patterns, because writing with marks on flat surfaces is less suited to a world where surfaces may soften. Nothing there is purely administrative; even record-keeping is bodily. A history is a rope one can touch. A law is a habit repeated until it becomes balance.

They do not own land in the way of surface peoples. They keep it. A terrace belongs to those who maintain it. A walkway belongs to the hands that prevent its dissolution. A house belongs to the circle of people who feed its walls, prune its living crust, and rest beneath its membrane roof. If a place is abandoned, it does not simply stand empty. It begins to forget itself. Moss may overtake it. Currents may unravel it. Mineral flows may pass elsewhere. The edges blur. The shape weakens. The town knows this, and so abandonment is feared more than poverty. To leave something untended is not merely to neglect it; it is to loosen its hold on being.

Children are born into warmth. Nurseries are placed near gentle vents, where the water is rich with lime and the current is steady but not strong. Infants there are soft in every sense: soft-bodied, soft-skinned, soft in outline, still uncertain of their edges. They are held often. They are touched constantly. They are rubbed with mineral pastes and living moss so their bodies learn the basin’s chemistry. Their cradles are woven baskets suspended in slow currents, lined with bladder-skin and fine filament plants. To be a child there is to be confirmed by many hands. A baby is not only fed and kept warm; it is reminded, again and again, that it has a shape. Its limbs are stroked along their length. Its palms are pressed against stone. Its name is tapped into its skin before it is ever spoken aloud.

Naming among them is layered. The first name is a touch-pattern, known to parents and nurses. The second name comes when a child can complete a simple act of care without being guided. The third, if one is earned, comes much later, often in adulthood, when a person has kept something real through a difficult season: a wall that wanted to dissolve, a root-line that nearly failed, an air-bell that almost tore, a district that sank a little and had to be raised again. Some people never take a third name. Some take several. Names are not shouted. They are pressed into the palm, tapped on the wrist, or tied in cord. To know someone’s true name is to know how to remind them of themselves.

Adolescence is marked less by rebellion than by responsibility. A young person may be given a small unfinished thing: a new platform, a weak corner, a stretch of loose shoal, a seeded frame. They must keep it through a rain-season, a vent-shift, or a period of cold upper-water. If the thing becomes more real under their care, they are considered to have entered full work-life. If it fails, they are not shamed. Failure is studied. The young person is asked what the water wanted, where the current was wrong, what the roots refused. In that place, error is not stupidity; it is information from a world still deciding.

Elders grow heavy with mineral beauty. Their skin becomes mapped with pale crusts, delicate ridges, tiny sparkling deposits that gather where the living film has worked longest. Some of these crusts are kept polished; others are left rough, as records of seasons survived. Elders move more slowly, but their slowness is not weakness. It is alignment. They have become more like the basin itself. Their hands are often in demand for judging young stone, calming frightened children, or reading the mood of a vent by the taste of the water. Some elders choose, near the end of their independent life, to become part of a structure. This is not considered death. It is considered deepening.

They are seated in a chosen place—often a threshold, a courtyard corner, a wall-base, or a place where two currents meet—and then the community begins the long work of integrating them. Living moss is encouraged over their shoulders. Mineral water is guided across their hands. Roots are trained around their limbs. They are fed, touched, spoken to by tap and pressure. Over time, their bodies become part of the architecture. They may no longer move as separate beings, but they are not forgotten or treated as stone alone. They are tended. They are visited. Their surfaces are cleaned. Their crust-patterns are read by younger tenders. Sometimes a wall built around an elder’s body will develop a warmth or a resonance that workers recognize. They will lay palms against it before making important decisions. They will say, in their tactile way, that the wall is still listening.

The people are not the only life there, though they are the life that most intentionally shapes the place. The plants are everywhere, and they are not merely scenery.
