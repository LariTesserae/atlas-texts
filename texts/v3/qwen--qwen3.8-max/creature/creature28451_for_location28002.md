What lives there has learned to live inside a finished body.

The mountains no longer offer the drama of becoming, so life has turned inward: toward maintenance, capture, repair, and the careful distribution of damp. The first inhabitants one notices are human, but they are not one image. They are rim-walkers and roof-farmers, engine-tenders and reservoir clerks, moss-shepherds and cable-singers, glasshouse growers and quiet artisans of paper and wire. They do not live upon the land so much as within its settled folds, in the hollows, along the rims, inside the thickened skin of buildings that have grown over the ancient stone like bark over a healed wound.

They are a dense people, not necessarily in number alone but in nearness. Their lives overlap. Their walls touch. Their roofs touch. Their water touches the water of others through pipes, troughs, cisterns, and hidden channels. To live there is to be aware of neighbors as one is aware of weather: by sound, by moisture, by warmth moving through stone, by a lamp appearing in fog where a doorway should be.

The people themselves vary by district, because the tableland, though worn into softness, is not uniform. Those born on the exposed rims are often lean and wind-marked, their cheeks chapped by drifting mist, their eyes accustomed to distance that is never quite visible. They learn to walk with tether-lines, to listen for a torn fog-comb, to read the pitch of mesh under condensation. Their voices tend to be thin but clear, as if shaped for calling across grey air without wasting breath. They are proud of their balance and of the first water they gather from the combs, which is considered pure and cold and slightly wild.

Those born in the sunken basins are often broader, paler, and more soft-spoken. They live beneath the weight of roofs and cloud, in courtyards where light arrives as a general brightening rather than a beam. They know the exact behavior of every eave. They can tell by sound whether a cistern is nearly full, whether a drain is choked with moss, whether the stone under a doorway has begun to sweat. Basin children learn to be quiet early. They play games of listening: guessing which roof is dripping, which pipe is filling, which household has opened its vents for the day.

In the industrial terraces, where engines gather fog and heat leaks upward through floors, the people have another look. Their skin is often warmer, faintly shining, their clothes lighter and stained with oil, verdigris, and mineral dust. They are the engine-keepers, pump-tenders, metal-menders, valve-listeners, and furnace-watchers. They move with a slow, economical confidence, because their work is dangerous not through violence but through pressure, steam, and hidden heat. Many of them develop a language of hand signs and finger taps, useful in rooms where sound is muffled or where a spoken word might be mistaken for a warning signal. Their hands are almost never cold.

In the lowest reservoir courts live the water clerks, sounding-rod bearers, sluice-openers, and counters of drops. They are often pale and deliberate, with a formality that comes from handling something precious. Their districts are dim, cool, and extremely quiet. Even children there speak as if inside a library. The clerks keep ledgers in waterproof paper, record the rise and fall of stored water, and settle disputes between districts when one basin claims another has drawn too heavily from a shared conduit. They are respected and slightly feared, because they know how near the city is to thirst.

Yet for all their differences, the inhabitants share certain traits. They have deep chests and patient lungs, adapted to the thin high air. Their hearts tend to beat slowly. Their hands are their most eloquent feature: fingers practiced at knots, valves, seed trays, moss bundles, fine wires, waxed thread, and the small daily work of keeping damp in its proper place. Their eyes are made for weak light, and many have a grey-green cast, or amber, or a pale smoky brown that seems to gather illumination rather than reflect it. They squint not from brightness but from habit, searching in mist for shapes that are almost present and then gone.

They move with what outsiders sometimes call the damp gait: short, sure steps, knees slightly bent, shoulders rounded forward just enough to keep the hood from slipping. They do not rush unless there is a true emergency, because wet stone punishes haste. Their bodies have learned the city’s surfaces: mossy steps, slick basalt, iron gratings, rubber beddings, timber walkways softened with age. A person from the tableland can often tell, without looking down, whether a path has been recently fogged, washed, or warmed by pipes below.

Their clothing is layered and practical. Inner garments are usually soft, close-fitting, and absorbent: wool, moss-felt, or woven fiber from reclaimed cloth. Outer coats are darker, waxed or oiled, cut with high collars and deep hoods. The hoods often have narrow brims to keep condensation off the eyes. Sleeves end in cuffs that can be loosened for valve work or tightened against wind. Many coats have hidden pockets for small tools, wax matches, oil vials, and cups. Everyone carries some vessel, however small: a tin, a glass phial, a collapsible leather pouch. To be without a container is considered careless, almost improper, because water may appear anywhere and must not be ignored.

Fastenings are copper, brass, bone, or dark horn. Iron is used but must be protected from damp, so it is often blackened, painted, or wrapped. Children wear little capes with toggles they can manage alone, and their boots are soft-soled until they are old enough to work on harsher surfaces. In the colder districts, people wear scarves across the mouth, not because the air is poisonous but because breathing visible vapor for hours can chill the throat. During spore season, when roof mosses release their green smoke, many wear masks of oiled mesh and cloth, sometimes embroidered with small family marks.

They are a quiet people. This is not because they lack feeling, but because sound behaves differently there. Noise spreads and disappears; a shout can seem to fall into the fog and be smothered before it reaches another person. Voices therefore stay low, intimate, precise. Conversations are often conducted close together, with pauses. A person may stop speaking to listen for a change in the city: a pipe filling, a comb beading too heavily, a cable creaking under new load. Silence is not awkward. It is a medium through which the settlement speaks.

Their language is rich in words for damp. They distinguish between fog that clings, fog that passes, fog that beads, fog that hangs in sheets, fog that arrives with warmth, fog that smells of stone, fog that carries insects from far basins. They have separate verbs for water that drips, water that runs, water that seeps, water that gathers invisibly before falling, and water that is almost vapor but leaves the skin wet. They also have many terms for heat: engine heat, lamp heat, body heat, stone heat, glasshouse heat, the temporary warmth of a crowded room, the lingering warmth of a wall after a boiler has been shut down. To them, these are not abstractions. They are daily facts as important as seasons.

Their greetings reflect this. A common greeting is not a loud word but an offer: a dry side of the cloak, a place near a lamp, a cup of hot water. Among workers, one may touch knuckles lightly, then touch the thumb to the wrist where the pulse is, as if acknowledging life in the cold. Among close family, the greeting can be simply pressing a warm palm to the back of another’s neck. Children are taught not to slam doors, not to leave vents open, not to let warm air escape without need. Politeness is ecological.

They eat warm food whenever possible. Kitchens are often placed near shared flues or engine returns, so heat is not wasted. Meals are thick and slow: porridges of highland grain, fungal broths, root vegetables traded from lower lands, preserved berries, eggs from domesticated birds kept in sheltered courts, insects cultivated in warm moss beds, and dense cakes made from moss fiber and sedge flour. Nothing is served scalding, because steam is too valuable to lose to the air. Instead food is kept just hot enough to comfort. Bowls are deep and lidded. Spoons are broad. Tea is made from lichen, dwarf mint, dried flowers, or roasted moss grain, and it is drunk slowly, often while watching the fog change.

Water is never casual. Even when it is plentiful in the cisterns, it is treated with the respect of something that can vanish. Children learn to pour without splashing. They learn the taste of different waters: roof water, comb water, reservoir water, water touched by basalt, water touched by gneiss, water that has passed through copper, water that has stood in a mossy tank long enough to become soft and green. A household may boast not of gold or fine furniture but of a cistern with a particularly sweet drip. To waste water is one of the few acts that can turn neighbors openly cold. To gift clean water to a sick person, a traveler, or a new parent is a serious kindness.

The inhabitants are ruled by maintenance. Their days are not filled with conquest or expansion but with the endless, delicate work of keeping the city alive. Fog-combs must be inspected. Mesh must be cleaned of dust and insect silk. Troughs must be cleared. Cisterns must be measured. Roofs must be walked to remove moss from drainage seams, but not too much moss, because moss holds water and insulates. Engines must be oiled, their fins cooled, their vanes balanced. Cables must be tested. Funiculars must be greased. Bridges must be checked for damp rot. Glass panes must be wiped so that whatever weak light exists can enter. Moss meadows must be patched. Fungal beds must be turned. Heat must be moved from one place to another before it dies.

Because of this, their professions are also identities. A fog-comb tender is not merely employed at the rim; she belongs to the rim. Her family may have tended the same lattice for generations. Her hands know its missing wires by touch. She can tell by sound whether condensation is forming evenly or if one section is shedding too soon. In hard weather, she may be tied to a rail with a harness of layered webbing, her hood beaded with mist, her breath brief and white. She does not think of herself as heroic. She thinks of the comb as an animal that needs care.

Roof-farmers are another people. They live upward, among ladders, hatches, walkways, and low railings. Their roofs are not mere covers but fields. Moss is cultivated there like grain. Sedge is trimmed. Cushion plants are encouraged in cracks where they hold moisture. Small flowers are left to open when fog thins. Children learn to walk on roof planks before they learn to walk in streets. They carry bundles of moss on their backs, their fingers green to the knuckles. In dry spells they shade the most delicate patches; in wet spells they open channels. They are cheerful, often bare-headed despite the cold, and they have a habit of looking at the sky as if expecting a personal message from it.

Engine-keepers live closer to warmth and metal. Their terraces are loud only in a contained way, a hum felt through boots and teeth. They wear shorter coats, rolled sleeves, and aprons of treated leather. Their tools hang in orderly rows, each one wrapped in cloth to prevent chill-snap. They speak to their machines in a matter-of-fact way, not from madness but because each engine has a temperament. One may bead too quickly and flood its trough. Another may draw air unevenly and develop a shudder. Another may sing faintly through a cracked pipe at dawn. The keepers know these habits. They adjust, coax, repair, and record. Their greatest skill is listening through noise to find the one sound that does not belong.

The water clerks of the reservoir courts are less visible but essential. They descend into covered basins with lanterns and sounding rods. Their boots are soft. Their ledgers are bound in waxed covers. They speak of water levels as others speak of moods: rising, restless, settled, thin, heavy, tired. They must know not only how much water exists but how it moves through the city, which district will draw first, which factory needs cooling, which glasshouse requires humidity, which cistern must be left full for emergency. When they open a sluice, they do so slowly. Sudden water can disturb pipes, crack old seals, or flood a lower court. Their patience is civic.

Moss-shepherds and lichen-tenders occupy a quieter trade still. They move slowly, with trays, small knives, brushes, and spore catches. They transplant moss from shaded seams to roofs that have gone thin. They feed lichen with mineral washes. They map growths on walls, because a lichen pattern can indicate dampness, age, air quality, and even structural movement. Some are so stained by their work that their palms and fingertips remain green or gold for years. During dry periods, when mosses release spores in faint smoke, they wear masks and speak little, moving through greenish air like doctors in a gentle plague.
