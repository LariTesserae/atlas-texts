What lives there lives in the faults.

Not in the broad empty decks, not in the open centers of the flooded pans, not in the long loud avenues of grating where water runs too fast and the cold sits most heavily. Life keeps to the margins: to the seams, the cracked liners, the leaking joints, the warm stains left by failing pipes, the places where stray current beads on metal like dew. Every inhabitant of the basin is a consequence of some failure — a seal that no longer holds, a valve that weeps, a terminal that hums with unwanted voltage, a heat exchanger that bleeds warmth into the mist.

The first lives are thin enough to be mistaken for staining.

On damp terminals and along the shoulders of conduit bundles, galvanic moss spreads in dark blue-green continents. It is not soft in the way moss elsewhere might be soft. It is fibrous, faintly metallic, rough with mineral bloom, and it feeds on leakage: microvolts, iron salts, the sour chemistry of condensation. Where it grows thickly, it gives off a smell like cold pennies and wet paper. Under its edges live filament mites, almost invisible until they move. They are clear-bodied and quick, no larger than grains of dust, and when they have fed their interiors show as tiny dark smears. They swarm only in the smallest sense: a hand’s breadth of moss may hold a dozen generations. When a lamp flickers, the mites stir, because the change in current changes the taste of the moss.

In cracks through the trough linings grow the pale reedlike stems. They are not plants in any gentle sense. Their roots are white threads that grip rubber seals, wire mesh, and the crushed edges of old gaskets. Their stems are hollow, ribbed, and tough as hose. They do not flower. Instead, in their joints, larvae shelter: blunt white grubs with copper-colored mandibles that rasp at the inner walls of the stem. When water levels rise and the hollow joints flood, the larvae seal themselves in oily cocoons made from their own saliva and machine grease. When the water drops again, they emerge — not as butterflies, not as anything beautiful in the old sense, but as brittle, narrow-winged things with cloudy wingspans and too-long antennae. Most die within a few cycles. A few find another wet crack in which to lay their eggs.

In the warmer effluent channels, where cracked pipes leak low-grade heat into otherwise black water, the glassworms gather. They are translucent annelids, thin as fuse wire, and their bodies reveal their contents: dark grit, bright flecks of metal, the green stain of coolant. They move through biofilm with slow muscular waves, eating the precipitates left by industrial water. They are not numerous, but where a warm leak persists they can be abundant enough to make the sludge seem faintly alive. When vibration changes — when the pumps shift from one rhythm to another — they contract into knots and drift downward into crevices. They do not flee so much as become unavailable.

On the mineral crusts near outflows live the blind white grazers. They are crustacean in shape but not in any clean ancestral sense: flat, broad, slow, their backs armored with accretions of lime and rust until they resemble chips of ceramic more than animals. They have no eyes. Their antennae are feathered with fine sensory hairs that read vibration in the metal beneath them. They emerge only when the subsonic pulse of the pumps falls into a pattern they seem to recognize as safe. Then they scrape at the crusts with tiny rasping mouths, making a sound too small to hear except perhaps as a faint roughness beneath the drip and knock of the basin. They are patient. If a shadow passes over them, they freeze. If the vibration becomes wrong, they retreat into seams and hollows, sealing themselves with their own bodies.

Above them, when the basin permits, are the hunters.

The crawler is the most evident of these, though even it is rare. It moves along damp metal with too many legs, each leg ending in a precise grip that can hold on wet iron, slick polymer, or vertical cable sheathing. Its carapace appears made from fused cable jackets, split loom, and strips of old insulation, but this is not merely camouflage; the material grows with it, or is incorporated into it, so that each individual carries the debris of the basin as armor. Its head, if it has a head, is a cluster of sensing organs: heat pits, vibration bristles, chemical slits, and a narrow mouthparts assembly that can pry open a crustacean or strip parts from a stalled drone. It hunts by patience more than speed. It will stand for hours above a warm joint, waiting for a grazer to emerge. When it strikes, it is sudden and mechanical, almost indistinguishable from the movement of a falling cable or a snapping clamp.

It may be biological. It may be a feral maintenance drone whose original programming collapsed into appetite. It may be something engineered so long ago that the distinction between grown and built has worn away. In any case, it is alive enough to hunger. It avoids the strongest electrical fields, because those fields make its limbs seize or its sensors blind. When one dies, or stalls, smaller things come for it: rivet-sized scavengers with hard backs and deliberate legs, some insectile, some not, all of them interested in loose metal, dead insulation, and the tiny reserves of stored current. They disassemble the carcass with a sound like fine teeth working a watch. Nothing remains for long. Even death here is processed.

The flying things are few, and all the more noticeable for that.

The sodium moths are the most common of them, though “common” is too generous a word. One appears alone, usually near a failing lamp or a heat vent, moving in jerks and pauses as if unsure whether light is food, danger, or direction. Their wings are pale, powdered with mineral dust, and when they rest on a warm lens they leave a faint print of lime and rust. They do not swarm. They seem to be born singly from fungal shelves or filter pools, emerging after some unnoticed condition is met: a rise in humidity, a change in current, a particular pulse of heat from the engines below. They live briefly. They feed, if they feed at all, on airborne spores and charged droplets. They mate rarely, if they mate at all; sometimes one will find another near a lamp and circle it in silence before parting. Their presence feels less like a population and more like a series of improbable events.

The fungal shelves belong to the warmer leaks. They grow in overlapping black tiers from cracked pipes and split housings, their surfaces wet, their undersides furred with spore-bearing tissue. They are tough, rubbery, and faintly warm to the touch. They metabolize what most life cannot: oil films, degraded insulation, synthetic binders, the sweet chemical residue of coolant. When disturbed, they release clouds of spores that hang in the mist and catch lamplight like smoke. These spores settle in filter pools and become food for microscopic organisms, which in turn become food for worms, which in turn may become prey. The fungal shelves are not gentle. Some curl slowly inward when touched, trapping small creatures against their surfaces. Others exude beads of black liquid that smell of burnt sugar and metal. They are among the closest things the basin has to hearths.

And then there are the larger presences.

They are rare enough that one could spend a long time in the basin and know them only by signs: a drain cleared of debris, a bolt recently tightened, a lamp lens wiped free of film, a chalked mark beside a hatchway, a patch of frost disturbed by something that had paused there and moved on. The few who live there in any sustained way are not numerous, and they do not gather in crowds. They are scattered across the terraces and reservoirs like faults themselves.

They might be called the Attendants, though they attend to nothing that has ever explained itself to them.

They move upright, but with a stoop, as if always expecting low overheads and swinging chains. Their bodies are wrapped against the cold in layers that have become part of them: cured gasket rubber, foil blankets gone gray with age, ceramic plates laced with wire, sleeves of braided hose, patches of translucent membrane taken from old control housings. Their gloves are thick, stiff, and repaired many times. Their faces are usually hidden behind fogged visors, respirator plates, or hoods of layered filter cloth. When one is uncovered, the face beneath is pale, drawn, and watchful, with lips darkened by cold and eyes adjusted to the orange poverty of failing lamps. Some among them look almost wholly like people who have survived too long in a hostile place. Others look less simple. Their joints may click with mechanisms not born with them. Their forearms may end in tools. Their chests may carry small heat exchangers or moisture traps that rise and fall with breath.

They live where they can: in lockers, junction cabinets, hollow tank legs, empty control housings, service niches behind weeping panels. They prefer spaces that can be closed, warmed, and made less wet. Their nests are made from shredded insulation, fiber clumps, fungal leather, and whatever soft debris can be gathered without exposing oneself to the open decks. They hang mesh hammocks near heat sources but carefully away from drip fall, because falling water can chill a sleeper faster than the air itself. Their shelters are marked with chalked serials, tally lines, and symbols that may mean ownership, safety, or memory. Sometimes a hatch bears a crude drawing of a lamp. Sometimes a lock has been replaced with a mechanism so old and patient it seems grown rather than installed.

They do not eat well. They eat carefully.

They farm leaks the way others might farm fields. They encourage galvanic moss to spread across live terminals, using insulated tools and slow, practiced movements. They transplant reedlike stems into fresh cracks where water seeps, pressing the roots into rubber seals and binding them with wire until they take hold.
