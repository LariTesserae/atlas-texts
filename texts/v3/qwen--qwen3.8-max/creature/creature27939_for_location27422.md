At first, the basin seems to answer the question with water alone.

Then the green life becomes visible: reeds standing in the shallows, moss darkening the shaded joints of stone, algae spreading in smooth films over warm pools, cushion plants holding condensation on open rock. Only after that, and not always at once, do the people appear.

They are easy to mistake for parts of the place. A figure standing at a sluice gate may look like a dark post of wet wood. Someone bent beside a channel may seem to be only a shadow thrown by a low terrace. A person crossing a causeway moves so evenly that the eye does not register urgency, and the mind dismisses the shape as something placed there by the landscape itself.

But they live there. They are the ones who keep the water moving.

Outsiders, if any ever climb the young ridges and descend into the warm wet air, might call them the Drip-Folk, the Sluice-Folk, the Basin-Tenders, or the Quiet Ones. Among themselves they may not need a grand name. They may simply be those who know where the water wants to go and have learned not to oppose it. Their presence is sparse and deliberate, like the built forms of the city. They do not crowd. They do not fill every platform or terrace with bodies. They are distributed according to the needs of the basin: one at a weir, two on a stairway, a small household near a cistern, a lone punter moving across a broad sheet of dark water.

They are people, if one must use that word, but people so changed by the place that they no longer resemble the usual hurried animal life of lower lands. Their bodies have obeyed the basin for generations. They are not large, because largeness would be wasteful where space is left open for water. They are slight, patient, and strong in the way of those who work slowly for long hours. Their limbs are unhurried. Their movements are economical. They do not waste breath, do not waste heat, do not waste motion.

Their skin is smooth and mostly hairless, as if fur or dense body hair would be a burden in perpetual damp. It may be grey-green, basalt-dark, pale clay-colored, or stained with the green-black gloss of wet mineral ground. In the ordinary light of the basin, their flesh often carries a faint shine, not oily but moist, as though the air has settled on them and become part of them. They do not smell of sweat, musk, or animal life. They smell of wet stone, cut reeds, moss, faint clay, and warm water.

Their hands are their truest instruments. The fingers are long, steady, and sensitive, able to feel a splintered weakness in a wooden sluice board, a loosened pin in a rope binding, or grit gathering where a channel begins to choke. Their palms are calloused in the right places but not coarse. They are hands made for tying knots, pressing clay, lifting small stones, clearing runnels, and testing the tension of fibers. Their feet are broad and sure, accustomed to wet ledges, slick causeways, algae films, and the soft give of saturated ground. They often go barefoot, because bare feet read the ground better than woven sandals, though they also make footwear from braided rush, bark strips, and layered reed soles when working among sharp gravel or fresh-cut stone.

Their eyes are not sharp with the alertness of hunters. They are steady, patient eyes, accustomed to silver light and the small differences between one damp surface and another. They blink slowly. They look long. They can watch a pool for a minute and know whether its level has risen by the width of a fingernail. They can listen to a spring and hear whether it has become looser, thinner, or blocked.

They dress in plant fiber because the basin gives them no animal materials and their world contains no herds, no skins, no wool, no bone, no horn. Their clothes are woven from reeds, bast fiber, bark, leaves, and perhaps cultivated flax or cotton where the warmth allows. They wear layered tunics, wrap-skirts, loose trousers tied at the ankle, and long capes made from felted bark or woven mats. Outer garments are treated with plant oils or resins so that water beads and slides from them. The colors are muted: black mud, green algae, pale tuff, rusted mineral stain, the grey-brown of wet wood. Brightness is rare, and when it appears it is usually the accidental brightness of fresh copper, pale seed-beads, or a polished stone pin catching the light.

Fastenings are simple. They use wooden toggles, knotted cords, woven belts, and occasional pins of soft metal or bone-white wood. Where metal exists, it is used sparingly: a copper pin, a bronze fitting on a gate, a thin sheet hammered over a vulnerable wooden edge. Nothing is extravagant. Nothing is made for display. Ornament, when worn, tends to be quiet: a knot of fine cord, a bead of fired clay, a smooth pebble drilled and hung at the throat, a circlet of woven sedge left green until it darkens with age.
