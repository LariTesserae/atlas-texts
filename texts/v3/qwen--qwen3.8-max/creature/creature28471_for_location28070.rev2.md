What lives there is not a single kind of being, and not even a clean separation between kinds. Life in that warm, dry hollow is numerous, crowded, unfinished, and constantly being folded into the place that made it. The first impression is not of individual animals or individual plants, but of a living pressure: something always chewing, swelling, opening, sealing, carrying, falling, hatching, dying, and being built over.

The smallest lives are the most numerous. They are the weather of the underplace.

There are beetles in vast, shifting populations, though they are not beetles in any simple surface-world sense. Their shells are dry and hard, some dull as seed-husks, others glossed with resin dust or pale mineral powder. They move over the bark-like floor in broad, clicking tides, their legs finding the grooves of compressed leaf-plates and root-felt. When they cross hardened resin seams, the sound is like rain on shell, except there is no rain here, only the dry percussion of countless bodies. Some of these beetles feed on fallen husks, breaking down dead plant matter into fine powder that later becomes substrate for new growth. Others are seed-breakers, their heads broad and wedge-shaped, their mandibles made to crack tough casings that would defeat softer mouths. Still others are resin-carriers, their backs sticky with amber gum, dragging fragments of plant wound-sealings to places where the living architecture has split open.

Where the beetles pass, smaller creatures follow. There are mites and mite-analogues, thread-legged and pale, living in the crevices between bark plates and inside old eggshells. There are tiny winged things no larger than dust motes, coppery or black, rising in sudden clouds when a seed-tower bursts. They do not drink. They absorb, sip, smear, or metabolize. Some carry pollen that is not pollen but a dry, volatile powder that smells sharp and bitter. Some are so small that they live on larger insects, riding the backs of beetles like passengers on moving terrain, feeding on shed skin, resin flakes, or fungal-resistant secretions.

The borers are another nation entirely.

They are long-bodied, segmented, patient, and relentless. Their heads are hardened caps of dark chitin or mineralized tissue, their mouths made for rasping rather than biting. They enter dying stems, swollen root joints, and fallen plant columns, then tunnel inward, turning tough fibrous mass into tunnels, chambers, and powder. Their sound is continuous: a dry, persistent scraping that becomes part of the underplace’s roar. Where they work, they create hollows. Those hollows later become pipes, nests, passages, egg-chambers, or reservoirs for resin. The borers are not builders in the sense of intending anything, but their eating shapes the architecture as much as growth does. A gallery opened by borers may later be widened by root-pressure, colonized by climbing vines, occupied by nesting creatures, and eventually used by the tool-making inhabitants as a ready-made corridor.

Some borers are large enough to be seen as individual animals. Others exist as swarms of smaller larvae, pale and blind, moving through the inner flesh of dead stems in communal chewing masses. When a stem finally collapses under their work, the sound is a sudden crack followed by a rush of bodies, then a new silence that is quickly filled by other sounds. Nothing remains empty for long.

Then there are the grazers.

They crop the living walls with tough, grinding mouths. Some are blind, or nearly so, because sight is less important here than touch, smell, and vibration. Their eyes are reduced to dark beads or sealed beneath thick skin; their faces are dominated by broad sensory plates, bristles, and blunt probing snouts. They move slowly along terraces and buttresses, eating the outer layer of plant growth in careful strips. They do not destroy the walls outright. Instead, their grazing opens passages, prevents certain thickets from closing completely, and stimulates new growth. In this way, they are like living tools. The underplace is partly maintained by their hunger.

Some grazers are small and numerous, like seed-shearing rodents made of dry muscle and fibrous fur. Others are broader, lower, and slower, with backs plated by shed plant material and mineral crust. These larger grazers resemble moving shelves or walking scabs of bark. They may stand still for a long time, their bodies becoming part of the scenery, until a vibration or chemical signal prompts them to move forward again, cropping a fresh edge of living tissue. Their dung is dry and fibrous, almost like compressed dust, and it is immediately collected by smaller creatures, broken apart, or used as nesting material.

The predators are smaller than one might expect, because in such a crowded world, ambush and speed matter more than size.

There are bark-runners: flattened, quick-limbed hunters that slip between root-cables and beneath loose plates of plant-stone. They have short, sharp limbs, sensitive antennae or whisker-like filaments, and mouths designed to crack open larvae, eggs, or soft-bodied nest-dwellers. They do not chase prey across open ground. There is little open ground. They dart through the weave, pause, listen with their whole bodies, then vanish into a crevice. Their bodies are often dusted with resin, pollen, or shed skin, making them look like fragments of the environment rather than separate animals.

There are also trap-makers. Some predators do not pursue at all. They build or occupy valves, folded leaves, or hollow seed-cases that close when touched. A creature may wander into what seems to be a sheltering tube or a curled leaf and find itself suddenly enclosed. The trap may be an animal disguised as plant tissue, or a plant organ used by an animal, or something in between. The distinction is not stable. Some of these traps are cultivated by their users. A predator may clean a hollow stem, smear it with scent, adjust its entrance with bits of fiber, and wait inside while the architecture itself does most of the work.

The resin-wasps, or whatever they truly are, move through the air in disciplined, humming lines. They are not wet-bodied insects. Their forms are lean, polished, and solvent-scented. Their wings make a dry metallic sound, like shaken seed-cases. They harvest resin, wax, and volatile gums from wounded plant organs. They use these substances to seal nests, preserve eggs, paralyze or intoxicate prey, and mark safe routes. Some of their nests hang from the ceiling like drops of hardened amber, translucent in the light, with dark larvae visible inside. Others are built into cracks in the walls, their entrances narrowed by carefully applied beads of resin.

They are dangerous not because they are cruel, but because they are precise. A resin-wasp may ignore a passing grazer one moment and, the next, seal a crack in response to the wrong vibration. If a traveler wounds a root-wall carelessly, resin-wasps may arrive within moments, not to attack but to close the injury. To be near such a wound is to be treated as part of the wound. A hand, limb, or tool caught in the sealing flow may become trapped until the resin hardens.

There are egg-guarders everywhere.

Some are animals. Some are modified plant organs that behave like animals. Some are old husks reused as nurseries. In the underplace, reproduction is not a soft or watery affair. Eggs are dry-shelled, resin-coated, or enclosed in fibrous cases. They are laid in warm crevices, inside hollow stems, against mineral ribs where geothermal heat rises, or within the bodies of dead creatures whose dried tissues provide first food. Many parents do not feed their young with liquid; instead, they provide chewed seed paste, powdered insect flesh, wax, oil-rich larvae, or regurgitated resin. Some mothers are little more than living pouches, their bodies reduced to protective cases. Others are active guards, hovering or crawling near egg clusters, stridulating warnings when vibrations come too close.

There are creatures so saturated with plant material that they seem less like animals than walking buds. Their limbs are coated in pollen, their joints sealed with dried sap, their backs sprouting fibrous growths that may be living tissue or carried debris. They move slowly through thickets, almost indistinguishable from the stems around them. Some feed by inserting their mouths into plant valves and drawing out thick internal fluids. Others are carried along by the slow movement of the vegetation itself, riding expanding root-mats or leaf-folds as if they were terrain. When they are disturbed, they may not flee; they may simply close, becoming seed-case, knot, or thorn.

Some seed-cases move.

At first they appear to be ordinary plant structures: hard, dry, oval or angular, hanging from stalks or lodged in crevices. Then one shifts. Another unfolds. A line of them crawls slowly across a root mat, or drops from a stem and scurries into cover. These may be animals disguised as seeds, or seeds that have acquired animal behavior, or symbiotic unions so old that neither part remains separate. They are important because they carry genetic material, food, or dormant life from one basin to another. They may be eaten, carried, broken, or planted. In the underplace, to be planted is not passive. A seed-case may choose where to lodge itself, what vibrations to answer, and when to open.

There are also the tube-dwellers.

They live in the hollow stems and pipe-like organs that grow through the walls and ceiling. Some are worm-like, some insect-like, some more like flexible wooden fish swimming through air instead of water. Their bodies are adapted to narrow cylinders: long, flattened, segmented, able to grip inner walls with hooks, pads, or rhythmic contractions. They feed on inner sap, fungal growths, trapped dust, or each other. Their presence turns hollow stems into sounding bodies. When air moves through the tubes, or when the creatures shift inside them, the architecture produces low notes, clicks, and resonances. The tube-dwellers do not make music intentionally, but they are part of the acoustic life of the place.

In the higher levels, where light falls in pale gold shafts through mineral plates and root-webs, there are winged forms. They do not fill the air with the careless abundance of surface insects, because the ceiling is crowded and the passages are narrow. Their flight is short, sudden, and precise. Some have translucent wings that catch the light like scraps of membrane. Others are dark, heavy-bodied, and loud, rising in clouds when a seed-column ruptures. Their flight is often triggered by vibration rather than hunger. A burst of sound may release them; a different rhythm may call them down. They pollinate, scatter spores, carry resin, or feed on airborne powder. Some die soon after emerging, their bodies falling like dry leaves into the layers below, where they are consumed before they settle.

There are larger animals too, though they are rarer and often mistaken for landscape.

Some are slow, mineral-backed grazers that move through the lower basins where bare stone still shows beneath the living carpet. Their bodies are low and broad, their limbs thick as buttress roots. They chew mineral scabs and brittle salt seams, either to digest trapped nutrients or to wear down their teeth. As they move, they leave shallow channels in the stone. Later, plant roots follow those channels, cracking them wider. In this way, these creatures begin the process of turning geology into biology. They are not intelligent in any obvious sense, but their bodies perform a kind of long, blind engineering.

Others are climbing forms, adapted to the vertical thickets and hanging root-curtains. They have long limbs, gripping pads, and tails or rear appendages that anchor them to stems. They feed on seed-flags, hanging nests, resin beads, or smaller climbers. They are quiet despite their size, because in a place governed by vibration, clumsiness is fatal. A large creature that moves badly can trigger seed-bursts, defensive spines, or collapses. So these climbers move with a strange, deliberate grace, testing each hold, waiting through the noise, then shifting when the surrounding rhythm covers their motion.

There are old remains in the walls.

Not all of them are dead in the usual sense. Some bones, carapaces, and broken tools have been incorporated into new growth. They appear inside translucent resin seams or wrapped in root tissue, dark shapes preserved in amber-like layers. Occasionally, one sees what might be an animal skull, a cracked insect thorax, or a crude blade sealed within a plant organ. These are not displayed. They are simply absorbed. The underplace does not memorialize. It continues. But the preserved remains give the impression that the place remembers by overgrowth rather than by thought.

Then there are the inhabitants who make things.

They are not numerous, and they do not dominate. They live in the place the way a few careful birds might live inside a vast, noisy machine. Their presence is marked by small signs: a platform lashed into the fork of a stem, a ladder of thorn and knotted root, a woven pouch hanging from a leaf-fan, a drum stretched over a hollow stump, a line of resin-blackened markers along a route that would otherwise close.

They are dry-bodied, warm, and watchful.

Their forms are not entirely unlike the other creatures of the underplace, which may mean they evolved there, or that the place has shaped all who remain long enough. They are narrow and tough, built to move through crowded layers rather than open ground. Their limbs are long enough to reach across gaps, but not so long that they snag in thickets. Their hands are precise, with sensitive fingertips and palms toughened by resin, fiber, and sharp seed-shell. Their feet grip. Their chests are compressed, allowing them to slip between root-cables and folded stems. Their skin is dry, fibrous, and dust-colored, sometimes patterned with resin stains or pollen marks that make them difficult to see against the living walls.

They do not sweat freely. In a world without free water, fluid loss is too costly. Instead, they vent heat through throat flaps, underarm folds, or thin vascular plates that flush with warm internal fluid and then cool in the air. Their eyes are adapted to scattered gold light: large enough to gather illumination, but protected by membranes or brows against dust, pollen, and sudden resin sprays. Their ears or vibration-sensing organs are more important than their voices. They may have ears, but those ears listen less for spoken words than for rhythm, structural strain, insect movement, and the subtle change in tone that means a passage is about to close.

Their voices are rare.

When they do use sound, it is usually not speech as a surface people would understand it. It is rhythm. A tap. A scrape. A patterned knock against a hollow stem. A sequence of clicks made by striking bone against seed-shell. A low pipe-note blown through a reed or tube. They communicate through vibration first, gesture second, scent third, and vocalization last. A complex message might be sent as a drummed pattern through the plant architecture, confirmed by a hand sign, then reinforced by a smear of resin or crushed leaf whose smell indicates intention: safe passage, danger, food, growth, collapse, waiting, retreat.

Because the underplace is deafening, their language has become physical. It travels through surfaces. It is felt in the feet and fingers as much as heard. Their children learn to listen with their whole bodies before they learn to make signs. A child who cannot distinguish between the vibration of a feeding swarm and the vibration of a collapsing terrace will not live long.

They live in small groups, not because they are antisocial, but because large settlements would be too loud, too hungry, and too vulnerable to sudden ecological change. A cluster of shelters may house a few families or working bands. Another cluster may lie a short distance away, connected by a maintained route. These groups are not isolated, but they are not dense. They move when the place moves. If a district fills with aggressive feeders after a seed-burst, they withdraw. If a new cavity opens and stabilizes, they may explore it, mark it, and eventually build in it. If a route closes despite their efforts, they abandon it and seek another.

Their shelters are humble and ingenious.

They do not build houses as permanent boxes. They build within existing growth. A shelter may be a hollow bulb lined with fiber. It may be a platform suspended between two stems, roofed with overlapping leaf-scales. It may be a woven pod hanging from a root-cable, swinging slightly with the movement of the surrounding architecture. It may be a chamber inside a dead stem, its entrance narrowed by resin and thorn. They avoid cutting into large living organs unless necessary, because wounds attract swarms. Instead, they bind, fold, lash, wedge, and persuade.

Their tools are made from what the place provides.

Bone awls. Chipped salt blades. Seed-shell cups. Polished insect carapaces. Thorn needles. Root-fiber cord hardened with resin. Levers made from stiff stems. Hooks carved from animal ribs or thorn bases. Small drums. Flutes. Pipes. Scrapers. Carrying pouches. Nets woven from fibrous bark, though the nets are not for fishing, because there is no water; they are for catching falling seeds, restraining struggling larvae, carrying resin, or blocking narrow passages.

Their most important tools are sound-tools.

A bone flute may be tuned to a frequency that causes certain leaf-folds to open. A drum stretched over a hollow stump may send a rhythm that calms a reactive thicket or persuades a valve-stem to widen. A pair of struck seed-shells may imitate the vibration of grazing herds, signaling to the architecture that the passage is still being used. A pipe blown softly into a hollow root may imitate the presence of tube-dwellers, discouraging new growth from sealing a corridor. These tools are not magical. They are practical. They arise from long observation of how the living architecture responds to vibration. But because the responses are complex, the tools become almost ritual in their careful use.

The inhabitants know that the place hears.

Not in the sense that it has a single mind, but in the sense that its tissues react to rhythm. A certain tremor can open a seed-case. Another can trigger defensive spines. A sustained low hum may encourage root-felt to thicken. A sharp crack may attract borers. The wrong beat near a swollen tuber wall can cause it to split. The right beat can guide a growth away from a camp. The inhabitants do not command these processes. They test them. They adjust. They fail sometimes. When they fail, the result may be a flood of pollen, a swarm of feeders, a collapse, or a sudden sealing of a route.

Their work is maintenance more than conquest.

They spend much of their time keeping paths open. This is not done by clearing alone. The place grows too quickly for simple clearing. Instead, they imitate the fauna. They graze edges with tools. They apply scent-markers that discourage certain buds. They drum rhythms that mimic constant movement. They install small grazer nests or larval colonies at route edges, allowing the animals to eat back new growth. They repair bridges, replace lashed platforms, and remove dangerous dead stems before they fall. They are less like builders of a city and more like caretakers of a living maze.

They eat carefully.

Nothing is wasted, but nothing is taken carelessly. They harvest seeds, larvae, beetles, resin, wax, oil-rich grubs, dried fruit-husks, and certain mineral salts. They chew tough materials into paste. They press oils from seeds using levers and shell-cups. They dry insect flesh in warm air currents. They ferment or cure food in resin-lined pouches, not with water but with volatile plant juices and powdered preservatives. Their meals are dense, dry, and strong-smelling. They do not drink from streams or pools. They sip sap, chew wax, extract oils from larvae, or consume carefully prepared plant organs that store internal fluids. Some fluids are intoxicating or dangerous. They know which ones to avoid, which to dilute with resin, and which to use only in small amounts.

Their bodies are adapted to scarcity and danger. They can go without fluid longer than a surface creature might imagine possible, because their internal chemistry is efficient and their food is chosen for both nourishment and moisture-equivalent metabolism. But they are not careless. A damaged sap-organ is a gift. A ruptured oil-seam is a hazard. A sweet-smelling vapor may be food; a sharp-smelling one may be poison. They teach children to approach new growth with the whole body alert: nose first, then fingertip, then tool, then taste only if the earlier signs are safe.

They have few ceremonies, but they have rhythms of passage.

Because the underplace is always becoming, their rituals are not about permanence. They are about transitions. A child is not named with a fixed word alone, but with a rhythm pattern tapped into the shelter wall. That rhythm is repeated at intervals until it becomes recognized. If the child survives, the rhythm may be added to a route-marker or a family drum. If the child dies, the rhythm is tapped once into resin, then sealed.

Adulthood may be marked by an act of maintenance: opening a passage without triggering collapse, harvesting a dangerous organ without wounding it, or learning to imitate the vibration of a useful swarm. Death is not left exposed in open ground, because open ground invites scavengers and uncontrolled growth. The dead are often placed inside hollow stems, wrapped in fiber, or sealed into resin seams. Over time, their bodies become part of the architecture. A bone may appear in a wall years later, dark and still, incorporated into new tissue. This is not considered horror. It is continuity.

They are cautious about the larger swarms.

The beetle-tides, winged clouds, and borer masses are not enemies, but they can overwhelm. The inhabitants watch for changes in insect movement. A sudden stillness is as alarming as a sudden rush. If beetles begin moving in one direction, the people may move opposite, or climb, or seal their shelters. If winged swarms gather around a seed-column, they withdraw and wait for the rupture to exhaust itself. If borers concentrate in a support stem, they may abandon the area before the structure fails. They do not try to kill the swarms. They avoid becoming part of the swarm’s food, obstacle, or trigger.

Some among them specialize in listening.

These are not chiefs in the ordinary sense. They are openers, tenders, path-keepers, rhythm-readers. They press hands and cheeks to stems. They place ears against hollow tubes. They watch leaf-fans turn. They read the smell of resin and the movement of dust in the light. They decide when a route is safe, when a wall is swelling, when a plant organ can be harvested, when a shelter must be abandoned. Their authority is practical. If they are wrong, the place corrects them quickly.

There are also binders and wound-tenders.

Because exposed living tissue draws scavengers, the inhabitants cannot simply hack and cut. If a stem must be pruned, the wound must be sealed. If a root must be redirected, it must be encouraged rather than sliced. Binders use resin, wax, fiber, and living grafts to close injuries. They may apply a paste of chewed bark and insect wax over a cut, then wrap it with root-fiber until the plant accepts the patch. They may guide a tendril across a gap until it grows into place. They understand that every tool-mark is a scent signal, a vibration, and a potential feeding site. To make a mark is to invite response.

Their art is small and functional, but not without beauty.

They polish seed-shell until it shines. They arrange thorns in patterns that also serve as ladder-rungs. They weave fibers into cords that are both strong and rhythmically knotted, each knot perhaps a reminder of a route, a person, or a danger. They carve simple images into dried bark plates, but the images are often worn away by growth or resin. They do not build monuments because the place would eat them. Instead, they make temporary refinements: a beautifully balanced lever, a well-tuned pipe, a pouch woven so tightly that it can carry volatile resin without leaking, a drum with a membrane stretched to exactly the right tension.

They fear and respect blooming events.

A blooming event is not always beautiful. It may be a sudden release of pollen, seed, volatile oil, or egg-laying insects. It can fill a chamber with choking powder. It can draw vast numbers of feeders. It can cause the air to shimmer with solvent fumes. It can make the architecture swell, open, or contract. The inhabitants know the signs: a change in hum, a tightening of leaf-folds, a sweet or sharp smell spreading too quickly, an unusual warmth in a stem. When a bloom threatens, they retreat, seal their faces with resin-treated cloth, or climb to higher, better-ventilated passages. Sometimes they use sound to delay it. Sometimes they can only wait.

Their relationship to the other animals is complicated.

They eat some. They avoid others. They cultivate a few. They imitate many. They may encourage certain grazers to keep passages open. They may protect egg-guarding husks if those husks deter more dangerous feeders. They may place carcasses or shed skins near a route to confuse predators. They may hang old insect shells from a shelter to make it smell like an established nest, discouraging intruders. They do not domesticate in the surface sense, because the underplace is too wild and too responsive. Instead, they enter into temporary agreements with animal behavior. A swarm may be useful if it moves where you want it to move. A grazer may be dangerous if it begins eating your shelter. Everything is negotiation.

There are places they avoid.

Some hollows are too loud, their sound amplified by pipe-like growth until the pressure becomes painful. Some corridors are solvent-rich, the air shimmering and deceptive. Some terraces are occupied by large, territorial grazers whose bodies block the way and whose stampedes can crush platforms. Some old seed-columns are unstable, their walls thin and ready to burst. Some resin seams are so thick and transparent that preserved remains inside them seem to watch. The inhabitants mark these places with scent, knotted cord, or a particular rhythm struck on a hollow stem. They do not always forbid them; sometimes they only say: not alone, not now, not without listening.

They are not innocent, and they are not gentle in every way.

Survival in such a place requires hard choices. They may deliberately divert a borer swarm toward a dying wall to create a new chamber, knowing the swarm will later threaten other routes. They may harvest larvae from egg clusters, leaving enough to maintain the population but not enough to avoid hunger. They may cut a small wound in a plant organ to release scent that draws predators away from a camp. They may abandon a shelter when the architecture begins to heal around it, rather than risk being sealed inside. Their ethics are local, immediate, and ecological. They do not ask whether the underplace is kind. They ask what will grow from the next action.

There are beings in the deeper parts that the inhabitants do not hunt, do not build near, and do not fully understand.

These are not gods. They are too material for that. They may be very old animals, very large symbiotic growths, or something between. In one basin, there is a wall of tubers that swells and contracts in a slow rhythm, as if breathing. The inhabitants do not approach it during its swelling phase. In another gallery, a cluster of hollow tubes produces tones even when no creature is near; the people call it a listening place, because sound made there travels strangely and seems to return altered. In a lower stepped depression, something broad and mineral-crusted moves so rarely that generations may pass without seeing it shift. Its back is ridged with salt and old root-scars. It may be grazing stone. It may be sleeping. It may be part of the geology becoming alive, or life becoming geology.

The inhabitants do not explain these beings with large words. They mark them. They avoid them. They adjust their routes around them. If one of these presences moves, the people move too.

What lives there, then, is not a population in the simple sense. It is a layered congress of lives.

There are the tiny lives that form weather: beetles, mites, borers, winged swarms, resin-wasps, pollen-daubs, egg-guarders.

There are the grazing lives that shape the walls: blind croppers, plated shelf-backs, seed-shearers, root-nibblers.

There are the predatory lives that move through the weave: bark-runners, trap-makers, valve-lurkers, tube-hunters.

There are the ambiguous lives that blur the boundary between plant and animal: moving seed-cases, walking buds, responsive leaf-fans, tendrils that hunt, valves that breathe.

There are the large, slow lives that alter stone and ceiling: mineral-backed grazers, root-climbers, deep basin shapes, old tube-dwelling masses.

And there are the tool-making inhabitants, small in number, careful in motion, who live by listening, repairing, rhythm, and restraint.

None of them owns the place.

The underplace owns itself through growth, hunger, sound, and collapse. Every creature inside it is both resident and material. A beetle is an eater, but its shell may become terrain for mites. A grazer opens a path, but its body may later feed borers. A predator controls a nest population, but its scent may guide plant growth. The people build a platform, but the platform is made of living stems that will eventually grow around the lashings. A drum is made from a seed-shell, but the seed-shell came from a tower that burst because of vibration, and the vibration came from a swarm, and the swarm came because of growth, and the growth came because of heat, dust, mineral oil, and pressure.

If one had to say who lives there, the truest answer might be: the unfinished.

Not a species, but a condition. Everything there is in the middle of becoming. The inhabitants are not finished people in a finished land. They are dry-handed listeners learning the rhythms of a place that is still being made. Their children are taught not to be still, not to be loud without purpose, not to wound without sealing, not to take without watching what comes to feed on the wound. They are shaped by the same logic as the bark-runners and resin-wasps: move with the density, use the sound, preserve the fluids, respect the swarm, and never mistake silence for safety.

To live there is to be constantly negotiated with.

The ground shifts underfoot because root-mats adjust. The walls lean in because growth answers pressure. The air thickens because a seed-column is about to open. A path closes because it has been too quiet. A shelter must be moved because the stem that supported it is now swelling with new tissue. A tool is not merely used; it is introduced into a living system that will respond to its presence. A person is not merely present; they are a vibration, a scent, a potential wound, a carrier of resin, a maker of rhythm.

And yet there is a strange tenderness in the way life persists there.

Not softness. Not wetness. Not gentle green fields. But the tenderness of careful fit. A larva sealed safely inside an amber egg. A mother’s body wrapped around a dry husk. A woven pouch holding precious oil. A rhythm tapped to calm a wall. A bridge repaired with thorn and fiber. A dead tool preserved in resin. A route kept open by generations of small, repeated sounds. A child learning to press a palm to a stem and wait until the architecture tells her whether it is safe to pass.

The underplace is loud, crowded, dry, and perilous. But it is not empty. It is full to the point of pressure. It is alive in every layer, every crack, every tube, every bead of resin, every falling husk, every listening leaf, every moving seed, every hidden shelter, every drumbeat sent through the living walls.

What lives there is everything that can learn to live without water, without silence, without final form.

What lives there is whatever can become part of the construction while still being built itself.
