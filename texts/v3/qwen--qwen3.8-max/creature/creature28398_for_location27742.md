Some grow rapidly where a new fracture admits light.

They are the first comers: pale suturing creepers with threads instead of roots, membranous sheets no thicker than frost, hollow filaments that rise toward the broken roof as though trying to drink the cold daylight before it is lost. They do not hide the wound. They make the wound usable. Where a slab of frozen soil has fallen away, they fringe the edge with white fibers. Where a root-mass has been torn, they bridge the gap with translucent strands. Where sap weeps from a severed vein, they gather it, thicken it, and draw it into new tissue. In time, the raw place becomes a joint. The break becomes architecture.

So the first answer to what lives there is not a creature at all.

The hollow lives.

Not as a single body, and not as a simple colony, but as a vast vegetable consensus. It lives in tension and repair, in moisture carried bead by bead, in light borrowed from a sky most of its tissues will never touch. It lives in the slow adjustment of roots to weight, in the opening and closing of pores, in the drifting of spores, in the quiet self-mending of fibers. The hollow is not merely inhabited. It is, in many ways, the inhabitant.

The truest residents are the plants themselves, if “plants” is still the right word for things that have become so structural, so architectural, so nearly deliberate.

Above, the light-catchers live half in the frozen world and half in the hollow below. Their leaves are the shallow lenses pressed flat against the surface, cold and slightly convex, angled toward the sky. They are the outermost organs of the vault, the thin, exposed skin that feeds the depths with radiance. They do not merely endure the pale crust of frost and low wind; they harvest from it. Each glossy face gathers what little brightness the sky allows. Each stem carries that brightness downward like a held breath. When the surface weather changes, they change with it. In clear cold, they tighten, brighten, and pour light below in hard blue threads. Under cloud, they soften their output, becoming diffuse, almost mournful. In storm or night, they close down, store what they can, and leave the deeper hollow to its own phosphorescence.

They are not conscious in any ordinary sense, but they attend. They turn toward light. They angle themselves to advantage. They replace broken lenses with new growth. They send signals downward as chemical tension, as pulses of sap, as alterations in the color of transmitted brightness. If one leaf shatters under frost or is torn by some surface accident, the change is felt below: a dimming, a flicker, a sudden coldness in one of the light-columns. Then growth answers. A replacement bud swells. A fiber-path reroutes. The hollow notices in its own slow way.

Below, the root-columns are older and more massive inhabitants. They are the load-bearing presences, the pale vertical forests that are not quite trees, the frozen lightning forms that hold the vault open. They live as compression and grace. Some are thick enough to be rooms in themselves; others are slender as wire and vanish into brightness. They are not individuals so much as long-lived intentions: stand here, carry this weight, keep this passage open, draw moisture there, let light pass. Their surfaces are ridged or smooth, frosted or faintly mossed, sometimes translucent enough to show internal filaments glowing like veins of cold fire. They creak under changes in air pressure. They shed frost-dust from their upper reaches. They knit themselves to one another where a span has become unstable, and they let go where a structure must be allowed to fall.

The ground itself is alive. That is easy to forget because it can be walked upon. The woven skin of roots, compressed fibers, bark-pale plates, and interlocked stems is not dead flooring. It responds. It gives slightly under pressure, then decides whether to hold. In places it thickens in response to repeated weight; in places it becomes brittle and warns a traveler by a faint, papery sound. It is not malicious, but it is aware of interruption. A footfall is registered as a small compression, a disturbance in capillary moisture, a tremor passed along root-netting. If something remains too long in one place, the ground may begin to grow around it, not aggressively, but with the blank persistence of tissue seeking stability. A dropped tool left untouched may, after enough time, be embraced by pale fibers and made part of the surface.

There are also the membranes, the curtains, the suspended nets, and the high leaf-platforms where mist collects. These are the hollow’s softer inhabitants, the flexible ones. They live by movement. They turn in the large, silent currents. They rise and fall with changes in humidity. They are the weather-tenders, though they do not control the weather so much as participate in it. When spore-clouds gather, they catch and filter them. When frost-smoke drifts, they condense it into beads. When a storm-cell of pale green dust passes through the vault, they sway, absorb, and release it slowly afterward, so that the hollow continues to exhale the storm long after the visible squall has gone.

The water-pocket lives are smaller and stranger.

In curled leaves and swollen root-joints, in sealed seed-shells and bladder-cups, tiny reservoirs persist. Some are sweet with dissolved sugars and remain liquid despite the cold. Others freeze at the rim and keep a dark, still center. Inside these pockets live translucent worms, almost glassy, their bodies visible as moving threads when light passes through them. They graze on microbial films that are themselves borderline things, neither fully plant nor fully fungus, but something old and unresolved. The worms do not hurry. They feed, pause, freeze into stillness, then begin again when the water warms by some fraction of a degree. They are not numerous enough to be called a population. They are incidents of life, small clear sentences in a much longer text.

On frost-rimed bark ridges, the pale beetles move with extreme caution, as though aware that every surface may be alive and listening. Their shells are dull, cold, and faintly pitted, often dusted with spore-dust. They feed on dead fiber, on shed bark, on the slow residues of sap that have hardened into bright crusts. They do not swarm. One may go days without seeing any sign of them, then find a single beetle traversing a suspended root like a traveler on a high bridge, its legs moving with deliberate care. When it stops, it becomes nearly indistinguishable from the plant matter around it. It seems less an animal than a temporary knot of animal presence.

The root-moths are even more elusive. Their wings are almost colorless, folded like pressed paper when at rest, and so thin that the glow of nearby stems shows through them. They spend most of their lives as larvae inside hollow tubes, feeding sparingly on inner pith and microbial film, growing slowly in the dark-light. When they emerge, they do so quietly, often during phosphorescent hours when the vault is lit by its own cold green shimmer. The adults do not feed much, if at all. They fly short, soundless distances, guided by air currents and light gradients. Their purpose is dispersal: carrying microscopic spores, pollen-analogues, or living films from one isolated structure to another. When they die, their bodies dry into translucent husks that may remain clinging to a stem for seasons, looking like shed thoughts.

In the mossy condensation zones, where dampness gathers on fine root-hairs and frost forms in delicate crusts, something like springtails lives. They are rare, quick, and startling. For long stretches there is no sign of them. Then a disturbance in the air, a falling droplet, or a shift in temperature may provoke a sudden leap—a tiny flash of motion, almost too small to identify, gone before the eye can follow. They are among the few inhabitants capable of abruptness. Their existence is otherwise hidden in the minute wet places of the hollow, where they graze on films, spores, and cold living dust.

Animal life here does not form an economy in the usual sense. There are no herds, no nesting colonies, no predatory chains, no loud competition. Animals are punctuation marks in a vegetable sentence. They appear, alter something almost imperceptibly, and disappear. A beetle carries spores from one bark ridge to another. A worm cleans a water pocket. A moth releases a payload of spores into an updraft and dies. A springtail leaps, and for one moment the silence becomes event.

But there are other presences in the hollow that are harder to classify.

Some of the drifting seed-bladders are not merely reproductive structures. They are mobile nurseries, floating vessels that carry living tissue across the empty distances of the vault. They glow faintly from within, pale as lanterns, and their surfaces are marked with translucent veins. They rise on warm drafts from the light-columns, drift through high spore-haze, and settle where moisture and tension permit them to anchor. Some never germinate.
