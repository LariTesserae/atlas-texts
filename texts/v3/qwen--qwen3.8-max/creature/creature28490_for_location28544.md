What lives there does not announce itself with eyes.

There is no face in the hollow, no track, no feather, no breath visible in the cold air. If you ask for a single inhabitant, the place refuses the question. It gives you instead a multitude, a low and crowded congregation of things that live by clinging, soaking, etching, spreading, and enduring. What lives in the Unbuilt Engine is not a creature so much as a condition of life pressed tight into stone and mist.

At first, the living seem to be only green.

They resolve slowly out of the darkness: mosses packed into the seams of the cliffs, liverworts plastered over wet ledges, stiff clumps of sedge-like plants rooted in the newest fractures. They are not large. None of them are. Size is not their strategy. Their strategy is density, patience, and the ability to make a world from a crack.

The mosses are the first visible citizens of the bowl. They grow in cushions and mats so thick they look less like separate plants than like upholstery stitched onto rock. Each tiny stem holds water against its body; each leaf is a small reservoir. Together they become sponges, catching the mist as it drifts through the hollow and holding it long after the air has moved on. They soften the stone without softening it. They do not smooth the cliffs, but they take the violence out of the surface at a scale too small to see. Where water beads along a ledge, moss drinks it. Where spray strikes a shelf, moss accepts it. Where a fissure leaks a thin thread of meltwater, moss gathers the thread and spreads it into a damp frontier.

Their color is not the cheerful green of sunlit fields. It is a darker, wetter green: blackened in shadow, silvered when dry, almost luminous when a beadline of moisture catches the faint light. They live in dimness. They make do with what little illumination falls over the rim. Their leaves are small enough to be ignored, but their mass is not. A single cushion may contain thousands of tiny lives layered over one another, the living surface resting on the compressed bodies of those that came before. In this way, the mosses are already making ground. Their dead do not vanish quickly. The cold slows rot. The damp preserves it. Layer by microscopic layer, they become the first dark substance that could one day be called soil.

Beside them live the liverworts.

They are flatter, sleeker, more skin-like. They lie against the rock in wet sheets and lobed mats, glossy as old leather after rain. Where the mosses build upward in cushions, the liverworts spread sideways, claiming surfaces, sealing them with a living membrane. They grow where water films are persistent but not forceful: under overhangs, along the shaded sides of fallen blocks, in the inner corners of crevices where the wind cannot scour them dry. Their bodies seem less interested in height than in contact. They press themselves to the stone as if trying to become part of it.

In the wettest places, black algae and dark microbial films take over. These are not green at all. They are slick, shadow-colored varnishes that coat the rock faces where water seeps or drips. They make the stone look as though it has been smoked, oiled, or polished by some invisible hand. Under the mist, they glisten faintly. They are among the hardiest inhabitants of the hollow, able to live where even mosses cannot gain a hold: on bare, glassy surfaces, on vertical faces, on the lips of fissures where only capillary moisture remains. They darken the rock. By darkening it, they change how it takes in what little warmth and light exist. They make the mineral world slightly more receptive to life.

Then there are the lichens.

If the mosses are the first gatherers of water, the lichens are the first miners of stone. They live in grey, rust, bone-white, and dull green rosettes, some as small as a thumbnail, others spreading in slow constellations across the face of a fallen block. They are composite beings, not single plants but alliances: fungal structure holding algal or cyanobacterial partners. They do not root in soil because there is almost no soil. They root in stone itself, or rather they adhere to it, enter it chemically, and begin the long work of taking it apart.

The lichens eat rock in the slowest sense. Their acids loosen mineral grains. Their bodies weather surfaces that would otherwise remain inert for centuries. They leave behind rust-colored stains, chalky patches, granular dust, and microscopic pits. Where they grow, the stone is no longer merely stone. It becomes substrate. It becomes a surface that can be inhabited. They are among the oldest kinds of life for such places: the first colonists of fresh fracture, the beings that turn a new cliff into a future home for other lives. In the Unbuilt Engine, they are workers in the deepest department of manufacture. They do not build with tools. They build by decay, adhesion, chemistry, and time.

In the sheltered seams where enough dust and organic matter have gathered, the higher plants push upward.

They are dwarfed, stiff, and stubborn. Sedge-like leaves rise in dark clumps from between stones. Grass-like threads stand rigid against the rock. Some have roots that enter fresh cracks with astonishing persistence, threading into fractures no wider than a hair. These roots do more than anchor the plants. They hold unstable scree together. They bind small fragments into place. They create the first resistance against gravity’s constant sorting of the rubble. Where a clump survives, the stones around it become slightly less likely to slide. Where several clumps grow near one another, a tiny platform begins to form.

The cushion plants are especially important. They grow into dense rounded masses, tight as fists, their leaves packed so closely that the interior of each cushion becomes a separate climate. Inside, moisture lingers. Temperature changes more slowly. Wind is blunted. Spores may lodge there. Fragments of dead plant matter collect there. In such a place, a cushion plant is not merely a plant; it is a shelter, a nursery, a micro-landscape. Other lives may begin inside it. Moss may take hold at its edge. Fungal threads may move through its lower layers. A fallen spore may find in its center the first place in the hollow where conditions are not purely hostile.

These small rooted lives are engineers without intention. They do not know they are stabilizing the slope. They do not know they are making the first pockets of ground. They simply grow toward moisture and light, and in doing so they alter the world around them.

Beneath and among them live the fungi.

They appear in pale clusters from buried fragments of wood, from old moss, from accumulated plant matter compressed into thin dark layers. Their fruiting bodies are brief and strange: small caps, cups, fingers, or brackets, often ghostly in the low light. They seem less like flowers of the place than like organs exposed by it. When a stone falls nearby, the vibration releases clouds of spores from them. The spores puff upward like smoke, like exhaust, like the place exhaling.

But the visible fungi are only the outward signs. Their real life is hidden. Mycelium threads through the moss beds, through the leaf litter, through the damp gaps between stones. It moves where roots cannot. It enters dead matter and dismantles it. In a warmer country, decay would be swift and hungry. Here it is slow, cold, almost reverent. The fungi do not rush. They convert dead moss into dark crumb, dead sedge into fiber, buried fragments into something closer to earth. They are the recyclers of the hollow, the beings that prevent life from becoming only a heap of preserved corpses. Without them, the green would accumulate without transformation. With them, the first true making of soil becomes possible.

And then there are the lives too small to see.

They are perhaps the most numerous inhabitants of all. Bacteria, cyanobacteria, microscopic algae, invisible films of living chemistry spread across wet mineral surfaces. They exist in the water threads, in the beadlines along rock edges, in the pores of scree, in the boundary where stone becomes biological matter. They dissolve minerals and precipitate new ones. They fix carbon. Some may fix nitrogen. They form crusts, stains, and slicks. They make the water taste faintly metallic, the air smell clean and sharp, the rock feel less inert. They are the smallest laborers in the Unbuilt Engine, working at the scale of molecules.

These microbial lives are not separate from the larger ones. They are stitched into them. A lichen contains them, or their kin. A wet moss bed hosts them. A dark algal film is their city. The first soil is their work as much as the work of moss and fungus. If the hollow has a metabolism, it begins here: in the thin living skin that forms where water, mineral, and light meet.

The air itself is inhabited too.

Not by creatures with wings, but by potential. Spores drift in the mist. Fungal dust rises when stones fall. Moss fragments and microscopic green particles move with the gusts. The fog is not empty; it is a medium carrying life from one ledge to another, one crevice to another, one newly broken surface to the next. Some of these airborne lives will die. Many will dry, fail, or land where no purchase exists. But some will settle in a seam, a basin, a crack, a damp shadow, and begin again.

This is one reason the hollow feels occupied even though no animal moves through it. The air is full of traveling life. It is full of reproductive weather. The mist does not merely obscure; it disperses. The wind does not merely blow; it sows. The occasional blue flicker of charge, the smell of ozone, the lifting of fine hairs on the skin — all of these make the atmosphere feel alert, as though the air itself were part of the living system.

There are no animals here.

That absence matters. It gives the place its strangeness. The eye expects motion: a bird, a fly, a spider, a beetle, a lizard, a mouse, something with eyes and legs and hunger. But the hollow withholds those lives. It may be too cold, too high, too dark, too loud, too unstable. It may be too young. It may simply not yet have become a place for them. Whatever the reason, the ecology is not organized around predators, prey, tracks, calls, or burrows. It is organized around attachment, moisture, spore, decay, and slow mineral change.

This does not make the hollow empty. It makes it differently full.

The lives that belong there do not move across the landscape so much as they inhabit surfaces. They do not flee danger. They absorb it. They do not hunt. They secrete, adhere, photosynthesize, decompose. They do not cry out. They release spores. They do not build nests. They build by accumulating their own bodies, layer over layer, until what was once bare stone becomes capable of holding something else.

If one must ask who lives there, the answer may be: no one.

But if one asks what lives there, the answer widens.

What lives there is a collective. A congregation. A low, damp, many-bodied beginning. It lives in mats and films, in cushions and crusts, in threads and spores. It lives in the capillary water between stones. It lives in the dark under the moss. It lives on the faces of new cliffs where nothing else could yet survive. It lives in the slow acid work of lichens, in the cold preservation of dead plant matter, in the pale fruiting of fungi, in the invisible films coating quartz and feldspar.

And perhaps, if you stay long enough in that loud, mist-filled bowl, another answer begins to emerge.

The hollow itself seems alive.

Not as an animal is alive. Not as a person is alive. But alive in the way a process can become alive when it has enough parts, enough motion, enough exchange, enough internal consequence. The Unbuilt Engine is not merely inhabited by small organisms. It behaves as though it were a single working body made from weather, stone, water, and low life.

Its circulation is water. Meltwater seeps through cracks, disappears into rubble, returns as thin trembling threads. Mist condenses into beadlines. Beadlines become drips. Drips strike pools. Pools overflow into runnels. Runnels vanish into scree. The hollow has no rivers, but it has capillaries. It has no heart, but it has pressure. Water moves through it in pulses, in hidden channels, in intermittent hammering sounds behind the visible wall. The place does not merely contain water; it routes it, slows it, accelerates it, hides it, releases it.

Its breath is mist and vapor. The bowl fills with grey wool, thins, boils upward, collapses into drizzle, fills again. Rain arrives in hard squalls and stops. The air tastes of ozone and crushed green plants. Gusts push into fissures and return as moans or metallic shrieks. The hollow exchanges air with the sky, with the cliff faces, with the spaces between stones. It inhales fog. It exhales spore-dust and wet mineral breath.

Its voice is sound.

Sound is not decoration there. It is part of the place’s life. Water forced through narrow slots thrums. Hidden channels hammer irregularly. Rain hisses on the rim. Droplets strike pools in thousands of small impacts. Loose stones click and grind. A rock breaks free and falls, producing a crack and a cascade of collisions that echo around the bowl. The hollow listens to itself in the only way a landscape can: by responding. A loud sound can shake loose what is already unstable. A vibration can release spores. A gust can move dust. The place is full of feedback. Its noise is not separate from its action. Its noise is action.

Its nerves are electrical tension and pressure.

There is no manufactured current, but there is charge. Falling spray separates it. Grinding rock produces it. Dry gusts carrying spores and stone dust make tiny sparks snap against stone. The mist itself seems wired. The air lifts hair on the skin. The taste of ozone gives the hollow a tense, industrial alertness. It is not conscious, but it is sensitive. Change one thing — a gust, a drop, a fall of rock — and another thing answers.

Its flesh is the living skin of moss, lichen, algae, and plant.

Its bones are the raw cliffs and fallen blocks.

Its blood is water.

Its waste and product are the same: scree, powder, spores, mist, ozone, and the first black grains of soil.

If the hollow has a life of its own, it is this: it is making itself into a landscape. It is converting rupture into habitat. It is taking a fresh wound in the mountain and beginning the long process of turning that wound into ground. Every life inside it contributes to that work. The mosses catch water. The liverworts seal surfaces. The lichens etch stone. The algae darken rock. The sedges bind rubble. The cushion plants create shelter. The fungi dismantle dead matter. The bacteria alter mineral surfaces. The spores drift through the mist looking for new seams. The water carries everything. The gravity sorts everything. The sound marks every rearrangement. The electricity makes the air feel awake.

So the true inhabitant of the Unbuilt Engine is not a single being.

It is the engine’s operation.

It is the fact that life has entered a place of broken stone and begun to make it into something else. It is the fact that no hands built this, no minds designed it, no workers tend it, and yet it runs. It manufactures weather. It manufactures rubble. It manufactures spores. It manufactures the earliest soil. It produces noise, charge, dampness, and darkness. It produces continuation.

What lives there is beginnings.

Not gentle beginnings, not pastoral beginnings, but beginnings with sharp edges. Beginnings that smell of ozone and wet stone. Beginnings crowded into cracks. Beginnings loud with water and wind. Beginnings without eyes, without tracks, without voices. Beginnings that do not need witnesses.

If you were to stand in that hollow, you would not meet its inhabitants as individuals. You would feel them as presence: the packed resistance underfoot, the slickness of wet rock, the green smell rising from disturbed moss, the faint prickling of charged air, the sound of hidden water striking stone. You would be surrounded by life that does not look at you. Life that does not flee you. Life that simply continues its work around your body as if you were another temporary obstruction, another warm object soon to be misted over.

The Unbuilt Engine is inhabited by the first makers of the world.

They are small, wet, dark, patient, and countless.

They live where the mountain has just broken open, and they begin, without tools, without plans, without rest, to make the broken place into a place that can someday hold more.
