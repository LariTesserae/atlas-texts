What lives there is not, at first glance, a single tenant. It is a population so finely distributed that it seems less like a community of beings than a condition of the room itself: a way the chamber remains alive without becoming crowded.

If you look for large bodies, you will find almost none. If you look for movement, you will find it everywhere: a flutter in the upper air, a slow crawl along a wet seam, a trembling of leaves where no wind has passed, a brief ripple in a basin that was still a moment before. The hollow is inhabited the way an old instrument is inhabited by music—by many small agencies that only become visible once you learn where the silence is not silence.

The most visible inhabitants are the birds.

They are swifts, or something that has become swiftlike over long generations of living inside vertical spaces. Their bodies are narrow and hard-feathered, dark above and paler beneath, with wings like curved blades and tails cut for sudden turns. They do not perch in the ordinary manner; their toes are hooked and strong, made for gripping louvres, rivet lines, cable brackets, and the rough edges of acoustic felt. They ride the warm updrafts that rise from the central engine housing, spiraling upward without effort, then vanish into the dim upper volume where the louvres breathe with grey light.

Their calls are not delicate. They are sharp, dry cries, half-lost in the chamber’s greater sound. Because the drone of the room is so immense, the birds have become creatures of pressure more than hearing. They feel changes in air movement through the hollows of their bones. They know when the fans are about to shift pitch, when a vent will open, when condensation is about to fall from a ceiling trough, because the room tells them through vibration before it tells them through sight.

They nest in the high places where the noise is slightly softened: behind old acoustic panels, inside sealed alcoves whose covers have warped just enough to admit a body, in the inner lips of louvres where the wind moans but does not strike directly. Their nests are not made of twigs, because there are no twigs. They are made of fibers pulled from insulation, shed moss, moth wings, seed cases, and fine strands of vegetable matter caught in the plant beds. The birds bind these materials with saliva and condensation until they form shallow, felted cups.

Their droppings do not accumulate as waste. The floor and walls are built to receive them. The droppings fall into collection seams, dry into pale flakes, or are washed downward by scheduled drips, becoming nutrient slurry for the mosses and vine beds. The birds are not visitors here. They are part of the room’s metabolism.

Below the birds live the moths.

They are pale, mostly, the color of dust, old porcelain, and the undersides of dried leaves. Their wings carry a fine scale that seems almost like powder, but it is not merely ornament. The scales help them shed moisture, muffle the touch of wet surfaces, and perhaps dampen the overwhelming vibration of the chamber. They move in slow, uncertain paths near the lamps and over the moss beds, not because they are confused, but because their lives are tuned to small gradients: warmth, dampness, spore-scent, mineral taste.

The moths are pollinators, cleaners, and food. Their larvae feed on dead fibers, fungal films, and mineral deposits that would otherwise choke the plant mats. The adults drink condensed water from beads on copper, from the cuplike joints of epiphytes, and from the sweating surfaces of glass bulbs. When they die, their bodies become part of the nutrient cycle. Sometimes a bird takes one; sometimes a beetle drags a fallen body into a seam; sometimes a moth simply rests against a warm pipe and becomes dust.

Beetles live in the deeper vegetation, where the plant beds are thickest. They are small, hard, and polished, some with shells the color of tarnished copper, others black and wet-looking, as if they have been lacquered by the room’s own condensation. They move through moss-felt and woven insulation with the seriousness of workers. They eat spores, dead roots, mineral crusts, and the eggs of other insects when those eggs become too numerous. They are the chamber’s careful check against excess.

In the brightest shallow channels, where algae grows bright and slick, there are snails.

Their shells are thin, pale, and faintly translucent, like chips of glazed ceramic. Some are mottled with green; others are nearly white, with brown repair lines where a crack once threatened them and was sealed by slow growth. They graze the wet tiles and the sides of basins, leaving clean trails through the algal film. They do not hurry. Their movement is one of the room’s slower clocks. When the chamber is in a dry phase, they withdraw into crevices and seal themselves with thin membranes. When moisture returns, they emerge again, patient and exact.

The snails are important because they keep the wet surfaces from becoming clogged. If algae thickened too much, water would sheet instead of bead. If water sheeted, it would run into seams not meant to receive it. The snails prevent that by eating the living film back to a manageable thinness. They are gardeners without intention, but their work is gardening nonetheless.

In the water pockets themselves live the eelish things.

They are not true eels, or perhaps they once were and have since been altered beyond any original classification. They are small, translucent, finger-length or less, with bodies like strips of moving glass. You see them best when they pass over a dark surface: a faint ripple, a bend, a glint of something that is not quite visible until it moves. They live in the basins, the drip pans, the ceramic cups, and the shallow channels where collected condensation is held briefly before being drawn away.

They eat larvae, microbial films, and the smallest organic debris that might otherwise sour the water. Their movements keep the basins from stagnation. They are sensitive to temperature and mineral content; when a basin becomes unhealthy, they rise to the surface in restless coils. The chamber’s other inhabitants read this as a sign. A basin that holds still, clear eel-shapes is a basin doing its proper work.

There are also midges, gnats, and vapor-flies, though “swarm” is almost the wrong word for them. They hang in thin, shifting veils where the light enters, so sparse that they seem less like insects than a texture in the air. They are not pests. They are a moving nutrient, a small living bridge between moisture, plant, bird, and lamp. When their numbers rise too high, the birds take them. When the air grows too dry, they diminish. They do not disappear entirely. The room requires their thinness.

The vegetation is also life here, not merely scenery.

The mosses are the oldest visible inhabitants. They grow in thick felts over pipes, shoulders, brackets, and wall panels, wherever moisture lingers long enough. Their color ranges from deep green to olive brown, with rust-red tips where mineral-rich water touches them. They soften the chamber without hiding it. They blur hard edges, hold droplets, filter condensate, and provide nesting material, food, and shelter. They are not wild mosses in the sense of having arrived by chance. They are descendants of installed mats, but they have grown into their places so completely that they seem to have always been there.

The vines climb along cable runs and conduit straps. Their leaves are dark, waxed, and tough. Their roots do not seek soil; they seek texture. They grip rivet heads, sealant beads, rough patches of old adhesive, and the tiny grooves left by previous repairs. They drink from the air and from the thin films of condensation that move down the walls. Where they grow thickly, they create hanging gardens that catch droplets and channel them into cups, seams, and basins. In this way, the plants themselves help enforce the room’s first law: water must be caught where it falls.

The broad-leaved epiphytes hang from gantries and high rails. Their leaves form cuplike joints where water collects. Small creatures drink there. Snails graze the inner surfaces. Moths rest beneath them. The epiphytes are among the chamber’s most deliberate inhabitants, because they turn accidental droplets into held reservoirs. They are living architecture.

The algae in the lighted channels is brighter, more immediate. It grows where daylight reaches the wet walls, a vivid green or yellow-green skin that looks almost painted. It feeds snails, shades the water from excessive warmth, and indicates where the light has been constant. If a section of algae dies, it means something has changed: a lamp has failed, a drip has shifted, a vent has altered the temperature. The algae are not beautiful in a decorative way. They are beautiful the way a gauge is beautiful.

All of these small lives are held together by the people of the hollow.

There are very few of them.

If they have a name, it is not one that can be spoken in the ordinary sense, because ordinary speech cannot exist in that sound. They might be called the Drip-born, but that is a name given from outside, a name for their condition rather than a word they would use for themselves. They do not need a word for “people” as a separate category. To them, there is the room, the water, the green, the birds, the sound, and the work. They are the part of the room that has hands.

They are small. Not child-small in a fragile way, but compact, light-boned, and long-limbed, as if their bodies were shaped for reaching into narrow spaces and moving without adding weight to the floor. Their hands are wide, their fingers strong and precise. Their palms are callused from gripping wet metal and soft from working moss. Their feet are broad and sensitive, with thickened soles that can read vibration through the perforated bronze plates. They wear little that is ornamental: patched felt, oilcloth, aprons of thin treated canvas, harnesses with loops for tools, soft boots whose soles can be replaced again and again.

Their faces are calm and watchful. Their eyes are adapted to diffuse light and sudden shadow.
