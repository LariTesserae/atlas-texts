**What Lives in the Unbuilt Works**

What lives there is not a set of inhabitants placed inside a landscape. It is a saturation of agencies. Nothing in the Unbuilt Works is merely resident; everything alive is also a working surface, a valve, a scraper, a conductor, a brood-chamber, a residue, or a moving pressure in the basin’s endless low metabolism. The creatures do not occupy the place the way animals occupy a forest or a shore. They occupy it the way parts occupy an engine that has never been assembled and yet has always run.

There are no peoples there in the ordinary sense. There are no makers, no tool-users, no hearth-keepers, no road-walkers, no sign-carvers. The basin has no need of hands. What it has instead is a dense population of processes that have become bodily, and bodies that have become processual. If one asks *who* lives there, the question slowly changes into *what is living there*, and then into *what is occurring there*, until the distinction between life and event wears thin.

The first and greatest resident is the living skin.

It is not one creature but a layered population: pale mineral crusts, thread-fungi, gelatinous mats, microbial films, cold brine-saturated colonies, and trembling membranes too thin to name. This skin covers the wet stone, seals cracks, binds sediment, and makes the ground into something that can be grazed, crossed, broken, and repaired. It is food, foundation, and industry at once. It grows in sheets over iron-dark rock, in milk-colored scabs over weeping fissures, in gray cables stitching collapsed channels, in glossy films that look like skin laid over cold machinery. Where it is thick, it dulls sound. Where it is thin, it shines. Where it is torn, it does not bleed; it beads, stiffens, and begins to close.

This living surface is the basin’s oldest citizen. It has no brain, no center, no face, but it has preference. It moves toward certain gradients of salt, charge, and cold. It withdraws from zones where water has gone still and inert. It thickens where electricity flickers. It thins where brine runs too fast to be held. It produces faint phosphorescence under pressure, weak blue-green glimmers where no green thing exists, and dull reflective sheens that make the ground seem lit from beneath. In many places, the light one sees is not sky-light or fire-light but the exhausted glow of this skin continuing to live.

Upon that skin move the grazers.

They are numerous beyond counting and patient beyond hunger
