What lives there is plural before it is personal.

If you ask *who* lives in the drowned basin, the question breaks open. The basin does not answer with a single face, a single voice, a single kind of body. It answers with pressure, warmth, chemical traces, and the slow movement of many lives arranged around one another. What lives there is a city of tenants, symbionts, growers, filterers, hunters, cleaners, and living walls. Some residents are small enough to be mistaken for sediment. Some are so large and slow that they are mistaken for geography. Some are persons in the sense that they remember, choose, and communicate. Some are only hunger, patience, and appetite. Together they make the place inhabited.

The oldest inhabitant is the city itself.

Not the stone, not the mineral crust, not the dead calcium left behind by vanished generations, but the living accretion that still grows, contracts, feeds, repairs, and rearranges itself. The walls are not empty supports for other creatures. They are made of colonial beings: polyp-like organisms fused into lattices, shell-layering animals cemented to old shells, sponge-flesh threaded through pores, microbial skins breathing over surfaces, and slow mineral-secreting bodies that add new substance wherever warmth and food allow. They do not think as a single mind thinks, but they have preferences. They grow toward certain currents. They close passages that irritate them. They open feeding mouths when the water carries the right chemical signs. Over centuries, their appetites have made corridors, vaults, towers, and basins.

In a way, the city is the largest creature there.

Its thoughts are growth. Its memory is layered shell. Its moods are changes in flow. When a district becomes crowded, it is often because the living wall there has grown more mouths, more pores, more feeding channels. When a street narrows, it may be because a colony has expanded into the current, tasting the nutrients that pass through. When a doorway disappears, it may not have collapsed; it may have been closed by slow tissue, sealed by mucus, or overgrown because the organisms around it found another route richer in food. The inhabitants do not merely live inside this structure. They live inside the body of something older and more distributed than any one of them.

Yet the city is filled with other lives.

The most obvious residents near the warm seeps are the heat-clans: pale shrimplike creatures with translucent bodies, long antennae, and armored backs ridged with mineral crusts. They are not large, but they are everywhere in the heated districts, crowding around fissures where mineral-rich water rises in slow pulses. Their bodies are almost ghostly in the dark, some nearly clear, some milky white, some faintly amber where symbiotic bacteria live inside them. They move with delicate urgency, tapping their claws against stone, fanning their swimming limbs, tasting the water constantly.

They are farmers, though they have no tools and no fields in the surface sense. They tend the chemosynthetic threads and bacterial lawns that fringe the warm vents. They graze selectively, removing older filaments so newer ones can spread. They stir the water with their bodies and limb-beats, keeping heat and nutrients moving across the mats. They secrete sticky organic threads that help bind sediment into low ridges, encouraging microbial growth in favored places. They do not own the vents in the way a surface creature might own land, but they defend them through presence, scent, and number. To be born near heat is to inherit advantage. The hottest ledges are dense with them, and their bodies form living carpets over the richest flows.

Their social life is intimate and crowded. They brood eggs beneath their bodies, carrying them in warm pockets where the water pulses gently. When the young hatch, they are almost invisible: tiny drifting sparks in the darkness, easily eaten, easily lost. Many die. Those that survive settle into the same crowded neighborhoods, often onto the same shelves where they were born, because the chemical signature of home is already in them. A heat-clan is not a family so much as a neighborhood that has learned to reproduce itself. They recognize one another by scent, by the bacterial film on their shells, by the particular mineral taste of their home vent. Strangers are not always attacked, but they are tasted repeatedly, tested, nudged, and pressed aside unless they can find an unclaimed crevice.

They communicate in small ways: a tap of a claw, a flick of an antenna, a change in the faint glow carried by some of their symbionts. They are not brightly luminous, but when a warm pulse rises, their bodies can shimmer with dim amber points, as if the vent itself has remembered light. In those moments the crowded shelves look like streets filled with slow-moving embers.

Above and around them move the ribbon readers.

These are the eel-like creatures that slide through the wider channels and vertical shafts, long-bodied, nearly blind, with skin as sensitive as a tongue. They are pale, sometimes bone-white, sometimes smoke-gray, with fins that ripple like torn cloth. Their heads are not sharp or expressive in a surface-animal way; they are blunt, soft-lipped, crowded with sensory pits. They taste the water with their whole bodies. They feel pressure changes along their flanks. They can follow a chemical trail through the dark for long distances, returning to the same routes again and again.

They are the pathfinders and historians of the basin.

Not because they record things as scholars might, but because they remember. They remember the scent of a warm pulse, the signature of a district after a collapse, the chemical trace left by a predator, the living smell of a wall that has recently grown over a passage. They learn the city by moving through it, and they carry that knowledge in their bodies. Other creatures seem to recognize them. When a ribbon reader passes through a channel, small animals often pause, not in fear exactly, but in attention. The eel’s movement carries information. Its wake tells something about where it has been.

Some of them are solitary. Others move in pairs, their bodies winding close without touching, sharing a current for hours or days. They do not build homes. They rest in hollows, inside old towers, under bridges of fused shell, sometimes within the open mouths of inactive growths. They are tolerated by most other residents because they do not compete for the same spaces and because their movements help distribute scent and disturbance in ways that warn others of change. When a warm current is about to strengthen, ribbon readers often become active before many other creatures respond, rising through shafts with slow, searching motions. When oxygen-poor water settles into a low courtyard, they leave before the smaller animals realize there is danger.

If the heat-clans are the city’s crowded laborers, the ribbon readers are its wandering memory.

The terraces and upper shelves belong to the crabs.

They are broad, flattened creatures, pale or mottled with chalky growths, their limbs fringed with stiff hairs that trap silt and living film. They move with sideways patience over the overlapping shelves, their claws folded close unless they are feeding or threatening. They are not fast. Nothing here needs to be fast unless it is dying. Their lives are spent maintaining surfaces: scraping bacterial lawns, clearing dead sponge from living stone, moving small accumulations of sediment, pressing their bodies into crevices to dislodge debris. They are gardeners of stone, though their gardening is mostly eating, rearranging, and inhabiting.

They have territories, but the territories are defined less by walls than by flow. A crab may control a ledge where warm water drifts in a particular rhythm, or a stretch of shell where edible microbial film grows thick. It marks itself with scent, leaving traces in the water and on the surface. Rivals approach cautiously, testing these traces. Most conflicts are not violent. They are displays: raised claws, slow tapping against stone, chemical posturing. A fight here is risky. A cracked shell can become infection, weakness, death. So most crabs prefer to threaten until one yields.

Some crabs carry life on their bodies. Sponges cling to their carapaces. Small worms hide in the joints of their legs. Bacterial films grow over their shells, giving them the look of moving stone. This is not decoration. It is camouflage, shelter, and sometimes food.
