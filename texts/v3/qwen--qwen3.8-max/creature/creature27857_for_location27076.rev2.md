What lives there is not, in the ordinary sense, a people. It is a staffing.

The plain has no tenants that stand apart from it, no species that merely occupies the surface while the land remains inert. The landscape itself is the primary resident, and everything else is either an organ of it, a mobile process within it, a temporary expression of its pressures, or a memory that has not yet lost enough coherence to stop behaving. To ask who lives there is to receive several answers at once, because the place does not support isolated lives so much as participating ones.

The first answer is: the ground-body.

The second answer is: the upright organs, the fog-feeders, the channel-worms, the heat-beetles, the pressure waders, the seed-cloud crowds, and all the other moving populations that pass through it in waves.

The third answer is: the tending processions, if one is willing to call anything there a “who.”

The fourth answer is: the pressure choirs, which are not bodies at all but distributed awakenings of the whole system.

And beneath all these answers is a fifth: the place lives as a final condition. Its inhabitants are not evolving toward some other arrangement. They are maintaining one.

## The Ground-Body

The largest living thing there is not an individual creature but the substrate itself: the vast, compressed, breathing plant-mat that forms the surface of the plain. It is not soil with life on it. It is life as surface, life as floor, life as weathered skin over an exhausted world.

It is composed of mosses, sedges, lichens, root mats, bladder-tissues, buried fibers, and older vegetal layers pressed into slow hydraulic strata. But taken together, it behaves less like a carpet of separate plants than like one extensive, cold, absorbent body. It has no brain, no center, no singular mouth. It has fields of sensitivity instead. It feels pressure, chemical gradient, electrical charge, temperature difference, and sound-vibration. Where one presses upon it, it does not merely deform; it takes note. It redistributes water. It tightens or softens. It sends capillary signals sideways through living tissue. It decides, in the slow sense available to such a body, whether to accept an impression or erase it.

This ground-body feeds in several ways. It collects fog with every hair, filament, spine, and frond exposed to the air. It drinks through surfaces rather than through roots alone. It draws moisture upward through living pumps when lower layers become too cold or too depleted. It captures nutrients from dissolved films, from fallen spores, from dead fauna, from exhaled oils, and from the faint atmospheric charge that gathers around the tallest venting organs. It does not merely receive water; it negotiates with water. It can make droplets pause, bead, climb, or gather around warmth. In basins where its secretions saturate the water into gel-like states, it can hold shapes briefly — a footprint, a ripple, the impression of a passing body — before releasing them.

It also remembers. Not in the way a mind remembers, but in the way a stretched membrane remembers tension. A footprint may vanish and then reappear. A pressure pattern made by some long-dead grazer may recur when weather conditions repeat. A sound may cause a hollow in the surface to deepen as though the substrate had been waiting for that tone. These are not hauntings in any spiritual sense; they are mechanical-memory effects in a living, saturated system. Yet to a witness, they feel like recollection.

The ground-body is patient, but it is not passive. It makes temporary organs when needed. A depression may become a filter. A ridge may swell into a bladder. A torn area may seal itself with living mucus and fibrous growth. A flooded channel may sprout sticky nets of cellulose fiber to catch debris or small organisms. These structures appear, serve, and are reabsorbed. Nothing is left behind as an artifact. No object is made. Only function is performed.

Death is taken into it quickly. A fallen body does not long remain a corpse. It is softened, colonized, opened by rootlets and fungal threads, visited by bladder-feeders and scavengers, drawn downward by slow suction. The cold preserves fragments for a time, especially shells, tougher membranes, and dense cartilaginous parts, but the wetness and living pressure eventually claim even those. Bones do not become monuments. They become calcium signals, mineral traces, temporary stiffnesses in the substrate. The plain keeps its surface clean by digestion.

This is the first inhabitant: the living floor. It is not sentient as a person is sentient, but it is aware in the manner of a vast hydraulic organ. It knows pressure, saturation, charge, and tone. It maintains the world above it by being the world.

## The Upright Organs

The most visible lives are the tall hollow growths that rise from the plain: the organ-towers, vent-stems, pressure reeds, or whatever name a temporary observer might give them before realizing that names implying “plant” or “chimney” are each only half true.

They are rooted, but they are not trees. They are vegetal, but they behave like machinery. They are sparse, standing alone or in loose alignments across the open expanse, never crowded, because the plain requires room for vapor, charge, sound, and storm-cells to move. Their bodies are ribbed, wet, cold to the touch, marked by vertical vascular channels and slits that open and close with slow rhythm. Some are pale and slender, bending under their own accumulated moisture. Others are broad, dark green to black, with swollen bases and internal chambers that glow faintly amber or blue-green when active.

They live by pressure.

Each one is a lung, pump, turbine, heat-exchanger, electrical membrane, and seed-factory in a single grown form. Cold fog enters their lower apertures or is condensed on their outer surfaces. Water and gas move through internal pipes. Tough cellulose vanes rotate under pressure, not machined, but developed over immense time into shapes that perform turbine-work. They generate electrical potential across wet membranes. They exhale warm vapor from vents near their crowns or bases. They discharge static into fog in silent blue tendrils. They draw fluids from the substrate, refine them, separate salts and sugars and oils, and send some portion upward into reproductive chambers.

They are not all alike. Some are predominantly respiratory, opening and closing in long, slow pulses. Some are electrical, standing charged and humming, their fine surface hairs lifting the air around them. Some are reproductive, broad-based and chambered, producing spores, seeds, nutrient capsules, or drifting propagules. Some seem to have failed and are being reabsorbed by the substrate, their lower walls softened, their interiors colonized by larvae and fungal threads. Others are newly active, their surfaces glossy with condensation, their slits tight, their tones high and clean.

They are not individuals in the way animals are individuals. They are more like standing organs of the plain. Yet they have rhythms that can feel personal. One may exhale every few minutes. Another may hold its vapor until pressure builds to a point where it releases with a deep boom. Another may answer distant sounds by opening its slits, as if listening with its whole body. Sustained tones can trigger them. A sharp crack can cause them to close. A chorus of fauna may make several towers begin pumping in unison, creating a local acceleration of flow.

They do not move quickly, but they do move. Over long periods, they lean, straighten, shift along pressure seams, or sink slightly into the substrate. In storms, they become more visibly alive. Their hollow bodies stiffen as internal pressure rises. Their vanes spin faster. Their whine deepens. Their slits flash with amber or blue-green light. Some emit columns of spores. Others release warm vapor that feeds storm-cells overhead. They are the loud, upright part of the plain’s engine, and they give the landscape its industrial character.

Around them, other lives gather.

## The Moving Populations

The fauna of the plain are abundant, but they do not live as evenly distributed residents. They live in concentrations, waves, processions, clouds, and sudden assemblies. The landscape is mostly open, and the emptiness is necessary. When life appears, it often appears in mass.

The first and most constant are the fog-feeders.

They move in dark ribbons above the wet surface: multitudes of small, cold-bodied creatures, some winged, some almost weightless, some barely more than drifting membranes with sensory filaments. They feed on spores, exhaled oils, electrostatic films, and microbial skins lifted into the air. Their movement is not random. It follows charge gradients and vapor currents. From a distance, they look like smoke poured sideways. Close by, they make a fine crackle, a dry whispering sound despite the wetness, because their bodies collect and discharge tiny electrical potentials. When a tall organ vents, the fog-feeders often spiral upward in the warm column, feeding on the rising chemicals. When static discharges from a charged tower, many of them fall stunned or dead into the substrate, where they are absorbed almost immediately.

They are prey, food for airborne predators, for waders, for surface-feeders, and even for the sticky temporary nets the substrate sometimes produces. Yet their numbers are immense. They are one of the plain’s basic currencies: airborne nutrition, moving charge, living dust.

Below them, across the surface, move the felt-scrapers.

These are flat, slow, cold-blooded grazers, broad-bodied and low, more like moving patches of wet bark than like animals. Their undersides rasp microbial growth from plant skins. They do not eat the substrate itself, but the thin living film that forms on it. Their grazing is essential. If left unchecked, microbial films would thicken, clog capillary surfaces, alter conductivity, and interfere with the exchange of moisture and charge. The felt-scrapers keep the plain’s skin functional. They move in loose lines or scattered groups, leaving behind cleaned surfaces that shine faintly in the dim light. They are tough, patient, and largely indifferent to danger. When pressure changes rapidly, they flatten themselves and become almost impossible to dislodge. Some species secrete a slick, conductive mucus behind them, helping water sheet evenly over the cleaned area.

They are not intelligent, but their behavior is part of the maintenance of the place. They are living brushes, living regulators.

In shallow sheets of water and flooded furrows pass the pressure waders.

They are long-legged, quiet, deliberate creatures, adapted to moving over a surface that is neither solid nor liquid but responsive. Their feet are broad and softly jointed, distributing weight and reading vibration through the plant mat. They do not splash unless forced to. Their bodies are muted in color: grey-green, smoked brown, pale blue in places where condensation clings to their skin or feather-like integument. They feed on larvae, bladder-feeders, beetles, and soft organisms exposed when channels reverse. They are usually silent, but when pressure drops or electrical turbulence builds, they erupt into harsh calls. These calls are not merely alarm. They are inputs. Certain cries make nearby capillary furrows tremble and change direction. Others cause mats to contract slightly. The waders have evolved not only to predict the plain’s moods, but to touch them with sound.

During storms, the waders move in long lines toward pressure-shadowed zones, their bodies low, their heads turned as if listening through their legs. They are among the loudest fauna before a turbulence event, and their choruses can trigger the first visible responses in the substrate.

Near the vents live the heat-beetles.

They are dark, dense-shelled, and strangely beautiful in their severity. Their carapaces are layered with living insulation, trapping air and protecting them from bursts of warm vapor that would scald other creatures. They rest near vent slits, motionless for long periods, waiting for the rhythm of the tower to change. When a vent opens and sends hot sap-smelling vapor upward, the heat-beetles release eggs into the rising current. The eggs are carried high, warmed, activated, and dispersed across the plain. Some fall into capillary channels, some into seed-tower basins, some into open gel-pools. The larvae that hatch are not like the adults. They are soft, pale, and aquatic, spending their early lives in saturated layers before slowly hardening and migrating upward toward warmth.

Adult heat-beetles feed on oily exudations around vent mouths and on chemical crusts that form where hot vapor meets cold surface. They are solitary, patient, and oddly reverent in their posture, as if they were keeping vigil at industrial shrines. They are not holy, of course. They are only fitted to the hottest work of the plain.

Inside the channels live the valve-eels and keyworms.

These are among the most important inhabitants, though seldom seen. They move through the living capillary canals, the internal waterways of the substrate and the lower passages of organ-towers. Their bodies are soft, elongated, segmented, and highly sensitive to pressure. Some are eel-like, with muscular flanges along their sides. Others are worm-like, with precise swellings and constrictions that allow them to fit into living valves and open them by shape alone.

They do not build valves. They become keys to them.

When a canal clogs, keyworms may gather in a knot and pulse their bodies until the blockage loosens. When a valve must close, valve-eels can wedge themselves into its tissue and trigger contraction. Their migrations are rhythmic. They move through the plain’s interior in waves, and their passage creates faint ticking, sliding, and tapping sounds. The substrate depends on them. Without their movement, many channels would seal, many gradients would collapse, and the larger organs would lose coordination.

They are blind, translucent, and almost entirely tactile. They communicate by pressure pulses and chemical traces. To a human observer, if one could see them beneath the surface, they would look like thoughts moving through a slow mind.

Beneath the upper skin of the plain live the root-borers, bladder-feeders, and soft larvae.

They inhabit the compressed vegetal layers, feeding on old tissues, stored fluids, dead organisms, and durable woody nubs that are the last remnants of ancient plant forms. Some are pale and grub-like, thick-bodied and slow. Others are slender, translucent, faintly luminous. They tunnel, digest, aerate, and redistribute nutrients. Their movements create new capillary paths. Their waste becomes usable chemistry. Their bodies are often eaten by waders, by deeper predators, or by the substrate itself when they die.

They are the digestive population of the ground-body. They keep the past from becoming inert. Because the plain is ancient, much of its material is old and compressed. These creatures ensure that even old matter remains part of the living system. They do not bury history; they metabolize it.

When a seed-tower prepares to release, the plain gathers its most dramatic crowds.

The towers themselves are broad-based, layered growths, often darker than the surrounding vegetation, with chambers inside where raw fluids are refined and packaged into spores, capsules, or nutrient-rich propagules. When a release begins, the air fills with drifting bodies. Fog-feeders rush in. Drift-sifters with fringed limbs spread themselves into the current, catching spores. Capsule-crackers with blunt, powerful mouthparts break open nutrient shells. Spore-moths or spore-flies — thin, trembling, short-lived forms — emerge in synchronized clouds to feed, mate, and die within the release window. Some creatures arrive only for these events, spending much of their lives dormant or hidden until the chemical signal spreads through the air.

Predators come too.

The static kites are among the most visible: broad, thin-bodied gliders that ride electrical updrafts around charged towers. They do not flap much. They adjust membranous surfaces and let the field carry them. They feed on fog-feeders and smaller airborne forms, often hunting along the edges of spore clouds. Their bodies are lightly built, almost weightless, and their skins carry fine filaments that gather charge. When they dive through a static layer, they may briefly glow blue.

Other predators are less visible. Quag-lurkers lie in softened patches of substrate, flattened and almost indistinguishable from the plant mat. They are ambush feeders, drawing small prey inward by creating local suction or by mimicking the vibration of trapped insects. They are not plants, but they have become so integrated into the substrate that they seem more like predatory patches than animals. Chorus hunters use sound. They produce tones that trigger contractions in nearby mats, causing small creatures to lose footing or be exposed to sudden flow. They do not make traps. They use the land’s responsiveness.

The ecology is crowded with such roles. Every species seems to have not only a diet but a function. Every movement contributes to pressure, flow, cleaning, opening, sealing, dispersal, or digestion. This is why the place feels industrial. There are no factories, yet there is production. There are no workers, yet there is labor. There are no machines, yet there is mechanism.

## The Tenders

If anything in the plain can be called a people, it is the tending processions.

They are not human, not humanoid, not tool-makers, and not masters of the place. They do not possess it. They do not build. They do not leave artifacts. They are fauna, but fauna so deeply integrated into the plant-engine of the plain that they have become something between animals and mobile organs. They live where maintenance is required: around organ-towers, along stressed channels, near vent slits, in basins where pressure gradients become unstable, and in places where the substrate must be coaxed back into equilibrium.

Their bodies are low, elongated, segmented, and soft-skinned, with surfaces that resemble the wet plant mat itself. They have no hard bones, no metal-like structures, no hands in any familiar sense. Their foreparts can extend absorbent tendrils and flexible lips, capable of secreting enzymes, loosening membranes, stimulating valve-tissue, or applying living mucus to damaged surfaces. They move by muscular waves, by gripping with soft pads, and sometimes by riding capillary currents through wider channels. They are cold, damp, and slow to the eye, but their actions can be surprisingly coordinated when several of them work together.

They are not individuals in the strong sense. A single tender is only partly complete. It can survive and perform simple tasks alone, but its behavior becomes more complex when it joins others. When several tenders align their breathing slits and synchronize their vibrations, they form a temporary composite awareness. They do not speak. They do not write. They do not make marks. They resonate. Their communication is tonal, chemical, and pressure-based. A low hum from one may be answered by a pulse from another; a change in the electrical field of a tower may call them into formation; a sharp crack in the substrate may cause them to scatter and then reassemble around the wound.

They maintain the plain without tools.

If a vent slit is clogged, they do not pry it open. They stimulate the tissue around it with secretions and sound until it contracts and clears itself. If a membrane is torn, they do not patch it with foreign material. They encourage living growth to close the wound, sometimes holding their own bodies against the tear while their secretions guide the substrate’s response. If a canal is blocked, they enter it and become the living key that opens the relevant valves. If a pressure pocket threatens to rupture a mat, they may gather over the area and produce a sustained tone that causes the substrate to release gas gradually rather than burst.

They are not wise in a human way. They do not appear to have intentions beyond maintenance. Yet they are not mindless. They learn routes. They remember pressure signatures. They adjust to new rhythms. If a tower develops an unusual drone, tenders may spend days near it, testing responses until the drone stabilizes. If a storm repeatedly forms over a certain basin, they may alter their processions so that some remain nearby when pressure begins to rise. If a stranger enters the plain, they may approach not out of curiosity but because the stranger introduces a pressure anomaly, a heat source, a chemical scent, or a sound-pattern that must be assessed.

They have no settlements. They do not gather in villages, camps, or nests. Their gatherings are functional and temporary. When their work is done, they disperse or sink into quieter regions of the substrate. When they age or are injured, they do not remain as bodies for long. They are absorbed. Sometimes they appear to choose this: an old tender moves into a soft basin, the substrate swells gently around it, and it becomes part of the ground-body over the course of hours or days. There is no grave. There is no bone. There is only transfer.

Their reproduction is tied to the larger reproductive events of the plain. They do not build nurseries. They release eggs or propagules into vent currents, seed-tower emissions, or deep capillary flows. Their young are not raised. They are sorted by the system. Some become root-borers before becoming tenders. Some become valve-sensitive larvae. Some are absorbed. Some survive and join processions. It is not cruel, not merciful, not wasteful by the plain’s standards. It is maintenance at the scale of generations.

If one insists that “whoever lives there” must mean someone with a perspective, the tenders are the closest answer. But their perspective is not like ours. They do not see the plain as a place. They feel it as a field of pressures requiring adjustment. They do not know themselves as persons. They know rhythm, obstruction, flow, temperature, charge, and tone. They are persons only in the sense that a river might be a person if it could decide where to bend.

## The Pressure Choirs

There is another form of life in the plain, larger than the tenders and less easily called a creature.

At times, when conditions align — when the organ-towers pulse together, when the fog-feeders move in coherent bands, when the substrate’s electrical field stabilizes into a strong pattern, when the tenders synchronize their tones — the whole system becomes briefly more than the sum of its processes. This emergent coordination is what one might call a pressure choir.

A pressure choir is not an organism. It has no body. It has no fixed location. It is a distributed pattern of responsiveness that arises across the plain and then dissolves. It may last for minutes, days, or seasons. It may cover a small basin or a regional expanse. It is most likely to appear during storm-states, when the landscape is turbulent and many processes must be coordinated rapidly.

When a choir forms, the plain seems to listen better.

Sounds arrive with more consequence. A call from a wader may open a valve several distances away. A boom from one organ-tower may be answered by another, not as echo but as command. Raindrops suspended in electrostatic fields may fall in unison when the choir shifts its charge. A swarm may turn not because of a predator, but because the pressure field has changed direction. Channels may reverse. Pools may open. Temporary organs may appear across the surface as though the land were reconfiguring itself according to a shared intention.

This is not consciousness as a human understands it. It does not reflect. It does not wonder. It does not ask. But it does decide, in the broadest sense: it selects among possible responses. It can maintain one pattern or shift to another. It can ignore an input or amplify it. It can treat a disturbance as local noise or as a systemic threat.

If the plain has a “who,” this is it.

The pressure choir does not speak in words. Its speech is operation. It says open by opening. It says close by closing. It says disperse by changing the charge of the air. It says come by releasing warmth or nutrients. It says end by letting a structure collapse into digestion.

To encounter a choir would be strange and unsettling. One would not see a face or hear a voice. One would feel the environment become attentive. The hairs on the skin would lift. Breath would fog differently. Water in nearby channels might turn toward you or away from you. A distant tower might change its rhythm in response to your footsteps. Your own heartbeat might become audible through the substrate, not because the plain is listening with ears, but because it is sensitive enough to incorporate small pressure sources into its field.

The choir is not benevolent. It is not hostile. It is continuative. It exists to keep the plain functioning within its final shape. If a visitor supports equilibrium, the choir may ignore them. If a visitor disrupts it, the choir may redirect flow, alter pressure, or isolate the disturbance. If a visitor dies, the choir will not mourn. It will absorb.

## The Almost-Living

Because the plain is ancient and causality there is thin, not everything that lives there is strictly biological.

There are residents of repetition.

A footprint may reappear after it has healed. A sound may arrive before or after its cause. A reflection may show fauna approaching from a direction where no fauna are visible. Raindrops may hang motionless in the air until disturbed, then fall all at once as if remembering they were supposed to fall. These are not ghosts. They are pressure-patterns, electrostatic memories, delayed responses, and functional echoes in a saturated system. But they behave enough like living traces that they must be counted among the inhabitants in a broad sense.

Some fauna respond to these traces as if they were real. A herd may follow a reflection across a pool. A swarm may rise because an old pressure signature recurs. A wader may call to a sound that has not yet happened. The plain does not distinguish sharply between present stimulus and stored pattern when the pattern still has force. In ordinary places, a footprint is a mark. Here, it can become a command.

There are also temporary lives that are closer to weather than to creatures. Storm-cells made from plant-exhaled vapor can rotate with such coherence that they seem to hunt across the plain. Fog banks may thicken into shapes that persist for hours, collecting charge and releasing it in slow pulses. Gel-pools may briefly hold the form of a body that passed through them, then release it. These are not alive in the cellular sense, but they are active, responsive, and part of the plain’s ongoing operation.

The inhabitants of the plain, therefore, include not only organisms but events. Not only bodies but patterns. Not only the living but the persistent.

This is one reason the place feels wrong, or more than real. It has worn away the sharp boundary between creature and process. What remains is a continuum of function.

## How They Live Together

The life of the plain is organized around pressure, water, sound, and release.

In calmer periods, the landscape seems almost gentle. Fog gathers on filaments and falls in slow silver threads. Felt-scrapers move in patient lines. Heat-beetles wait beside vents. Organ-towers exhale at long intervals. Fog-feeders drift in soft dark bands. The substrate breathes underfoot with slow hydraulic patience. The industrial hum becomes a low vibration, steady enough to feel like comfort.

But the calm is not rest. It is accumulation.

Pressure builds beneath the surface. Warm vapor rises from deeper layers while cold air remains above. Electrical charge gathers around tall organs. Water collects in films, beads, and capillary threads until the gradients become unstable. The plain is always manufacturing these differences: moisture gradients, temperature gradients, charge gradients, pressure gradients. When the differences become too great, the system enters storm-state.

Then everything accelerates.

Organ-towers straighten and hum louder. Their slits open and close more rapidly. Internal vanes spin with deeper resonance. The substrate swells in places and tightens in others. Some areas become drumhead surfaces, resonating under pressure. Others soften into moving quagmires. Channels flash with bioluminescence as disturbed organisms light the water. Rain may fall downward, upward, or sideways depending on local charge. Fog thickens into rotating cells. Swarms rise or descend in massive coordinated movements. Predators exploit the confusion. Seed-towers release clouds of propagules into the turbulent air. Tenders gather where stress is highest. Pressure waders call harshly, their cries triggering contractions and reversals. The plain becomes loud, bright in hidden ways, and violently alive.

Creation and destruction occur close together. A vent may split open and send warm vapor into the air; nearby, a mat may collapse into a cold pool as underlying tissue shifts. A tower may shed its outer layer and expose a glowing chamber; within an hour, it may seal itself again. A temporary organ may appear to filter debris from a flood channel, then dissolve once the pressure equalizes. A swarm may feed, breed, scatter, and be scattered within the same event.

Nothing here is permanent in detail. The permanence belongs to the final form of the plain itself: broad, low, saturated, and worn flat. Within that form, life expresses itself as repeated function rather than fixed geography.

## To Be Among Them

If one stood there, one would not be met by a society, a city, a camp, or a road. One would be met by exposure.

The air would feel thin, damp, and charged. Breath would fog and drift strangely, sometimes drawn toward warmth or held in place by static. Clothes would grow heavy with condensation. The ground would feel soft but alert, never treacherous everywhere, never entirely stable. Every step would be registered by the substrate. Footprints might fill with water, heal, and then reappear when a similar sound occurred nearby.

The nearest organ-tower might turn its slits toward you, not because it has eyes, but because your body introduces heat, moisture, and vibration. Fog-feeders might curve away from your breath. Felt-scrapers might slow or flatten. Waders might observe you from a distance, then call, testing whether your presence causes the substrate to respond. Heat-beetles would likely ignore you unless you came near a vent. Valve-eels and keyworms would not know you were there unless your weight altered the canals beneath.

The tenders might come if you remained long enough.

They would not approach with curiosity as a human understands it. They would approach because your pressure signature is unusual, because your warmth disturbs local gradients, because your breathing introduces a tone, because your clothes shed fibers, because your body is leaking salts and moisture into a system that notices such things. They might circle slowly. They might press soft foreparts to the substrate near your feet. They might produce a low vibration, waiting to see whether the plain responds. If you moved suddenly, they would likely withdraw. If you remained still, they might begin treating you as a temporary feature to be worked around.

If you died there, you would not become a skeleton. You would become process. The substrate would soften around you. Rootlets and fungal threads would enter. Scavengers would come. The cold would preserve some parts for a time, but the wetness and living chemistry would continue. Your heat would fade. Your shape would lower. Your materials would be sorted and drawn into the plain. A footprint where you lay might reappear later, triggered by some unrelated sound. A reflection in a nearby pool might show you standing there even after the surface had healed.

This would not be malice. It would be the plain’s only form of acceptance.

## The Final Inhabitants

So whoever lives there lives as maintenance.

The ground-body lives by collecting, distributing, digesting, and remembering pressure.

The organ-towers live by pumping, venting, generating charge, and producing reproductive clouds.

The fog-feeders live by riding charge and eating airborne films.

The felt-scrapers live by cleaning the skin of the plain.

The pressure waders live by reading turbulence and calling into it.

The heat-beetles live by trusting warm vapor with their young.

The valve-eels and keyworms live by becoming the moving parts of living valves.

The root-borers and bladder-feeders live by metabolizing the old interior of the world.

The predators live by using confusion, sound, and charge.

The tenders live by tending what cannot be built, only grown.

The pressure choirs live by emerging when the whole system must act as one.

And the almost-living traces live by repetition, because the plain has become so saturated with memory that even an impression can continue to work.

None of them are separate from the engine. None of them are owners. None of them make artifacts. They do not need to. The place has passed beyond the age in which life must stand apart from its environment and act upon it with tools. Here, life has become the tool, the worker, the product, the fuel, and the process all at once.

What lives there is a vast, wet, cold, loud, biological industry at the end of erosion. It is not a civilization. It is not a wilderness. It is not a ruin. It is a final ecology of function: a living workforce without hands, an engine without artifacts, a mind without a skull, and a population without homes except the working body of the world itself.
