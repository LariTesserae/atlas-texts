It feels like an empty room that has been filled with listening.

What lives there is not easy to count, because some of it is creature, some of it is weather, and some of it is only the shape made by long absence. The Dry Organ does not shelter life so much as tune it. Whatever has come to stay there has been thinned, clarified, and taught to exist by resonance rather than flesh.

The first thing one becomes aware of is the storm.

That is the oldest inhabitant, if inhabitant is the right word. It is the storm that has learned to stay in one place. It does not rage constantly; it has outgrown rage. It rests in the basin like a sleeping dog made of pressure and cold. On still days it is almost invisible: a faint bending of the clear air, a place where the light becomes too sharp, a trembling along the rim as though the stone were remembering thunder. On other days it moves in slow circles without ever leaving, lifting dust in small spirals, turning the dry blossoms inward, making the few standing stones hum.

It has no eyes, yet it notices. When something enters the Dry Organ, the storm turns its attention toward it the way a blind person turns a face. The air becomes heavier. The ears fill with a high, bright silence. That is the storm breathing.

Beneath that attention live the ones who might be called the Resonants.

They stand among the solid things of the basin—among broken columns, pale stumps of wall, cracked basins within the basin—and at first they seem only part of the ruin. They are tall, but not always the same height. Some are no larger than a child; others rise almost to the rim, as though they had grown in hopes of escaping. Their bodies have the look of chalk, pumice, bleached bone, and old porcelain, though none of those things exactly. They are porous. Their surfaces are worn through with tiny openings, slots, fissures, and narrow tubes, as if wind had spent centuries carving them into instruments.

Where a face might be, there is often only a smooth hollow or a vertical slit. Some have eyesockets that contain no eyes, only a deeper stillness. Others have no faces at all, only a crown of thin bores through which the air enters and leaves. When the wind crosses the rim, they sound. Not all at once. First one gives a low note, dry and distant, like a finger drawn around the edge of a cracked cup. Then another answers with a whisper of sifting sand. Then a third produces a chord so pure it seems to come from the sky rather than from the figure itself.

They are the pipes of the Dry Organ.

No one knows whether they were made for the place or whether the place made them out of whatever came to it. Some seem to have once been human, or at least to have belonged to beings who walked upright and had hands. In certain lights, one can still see the suggestion of fingers fused to sides, ribs opened into flutes, spines lengthened into hollow channels. But there is no grief in them. Whatever they were before has been refined into usefulness. They do not hunger in the ordinary sense. They do not thirst. They wait for wind, and when wind comes they become more alive than many creatures with blood.

They communicate by interval. A long tone from one means attention. A short dry click from another means change. When several sound together in perfect accord, the garden quickens. When they fall into dissonance, the storm stirs.

For there is a garden here, though no green thing in the common sense grows in it.

It spreads in low, pale forms across the floor of the basin: lichen like frost, threads of salt, brittle grasses with hollow stems, tiny crystalline flowers that open only when the air is so cold it hurts. There are bushes of gray wire, or what look like bushes; they are actually colonies of mineral filaments, each strand finer than hair, ringing faintly when touched. There are patches of dust that bloom after the storm moves over them, raising temporary blossoms the color of old moonlight. There are stones that flower once a year by splitting open along seams of quartz. There are roots that grow upward instead of down, feeding on clarity instead of soil.

This garden is tended by smaller lives.

They are quick and almost impossible to see directly. One notices them only in motion: a flicker of pale wings, a scuttle of jointed legs, a ripple in the dust that is not caused by wind. Some are like moths made of mica, their bodies dry and glittering, their wings cracked and translucent. They do not drink nectar; they feed on sound. They gather around the Resonants when the music is thin and precise, opening fragile mouthparts to catch the overtones.

Others resemble beetles carved from horn or ice. They carry fragments of ruin—grains of stone, splinters of glass, dried seeds of lightning—across the basin floor. They are the sextons and gardeners of small things. Where a note falls too heavily and cracks the ground, they gather to fill the fissure with fine dust. Where a crystal bloom grows too loud, they prune it with delicate bites until its tone becomes pure again.

There are also creatures like lizards, but without moisture. They are made, it seems, of heat-shimmer and cold air, and they move with sudden starts and stops. They bask not in warmth but in vibration. When the Dry Organ is silent, they lie flattened against the stones like clear stains. When the storm begins to turn, they rise and race along the rim in silent excitement, their bodies bending with the pressure changes.

And there are others, larger but less certain.

At dusk, when the light becomes knife-thin and the basin seems to deepen, shapes may be seen moving just beyond full perception. They have the suggestion of deer, or goats, or tall birds, but their limbs are too slender and their bodies too faint. They step between stones without making footprints. They lower their heads as if grazing on the garden, but what they eat is silence: the small pauses between notes, the gaps left after a tone dies. These are the Strays. They were perhaps once animals from lower places, or perhaps they are only the memory of animals, drawn upward by the strange purity of the Dry Organ. They do not remain always. They appear when the music becomes too dense, as though needed to eat away enough quiet for the storm to breathe.

At the center of the basin, where the hollow dips into a shallow depression like the mouth of a buried bell, lives what may be the keeper of all this.

It is harder to describe than the others because it does not consent to one form. Sometimes it seems a figure seated in the dust, robed in gray stillness. Sometimes it seems a woman made of high-air pressure, her hair a thin veil of cirrus, her hands long and cool, her face a smooth blankness turned upward. Sometimes it seems a man with an open chest full of wind, each rib a bar of pale stone. Sometimes it is only a column of dry turbulence, rotating slowly, holding within itself the memory of lightning without ever releasing it.

If the Resonants are pipes and the small creatures are gardeners, then this being is the organist.

It does not play with hands. It plays by intention. It listens until a note is needed, then inclines itself toward one of the standing figures, and the wind finds the proper angle. It moves a little dust from one part of the basin to another, and a tone changes key. It stills the moths for a moment, and a silence becomes so sharp it can cut through the drone of the lower pipes. It is ancient, patient, and utterly absorbed. It does not seem lonely, but there is something in its stillness that suggests it knows what loneliness is and has decided to make music from it.

The keeper tends the garden not by watering but by withholding. It removes whatever is too wet, too soft, too green, too mortal. It preserves the place in its high dryness. Yet it is not cruel. If a bird falls into the basin, stunned by the thin air, the keeper does not devour it. It bends the storm around the bird so that its last breath becomes a note. The small creatures gather afterward, not to consume the body but to harvest the faint tremors still clinging to its feathers. From those tremors, a new flower may grow: a brittle, pale bloom that opens only when the wind imitates the bird’s cry.

Sometimes a person reaches the Dry Organ.

That is rare. The height is difficult, the air punishing, and the clarity itself discourages most travelers. It is one thing to climb toward a place; it is another to be seen by it. Those who arrive feel first a strange relief. The pain of ascent becomes distant. The body seems lighter. The mind, usually loud with fear and desire, becomes very still. Then the visitor hears the basin playing softly, and the music seems to include them.

At first it is beautiful.

Then it becomes dangerous, not because the Dry Organ attacks, but because it accepts.

A person who stands at the rim too long begins to dry. Not painfully, not quickly. Their thirst lessens. Their memories grow thin. Their name becomes a sound they can almost forget. They find it easier to stand than to walk, easier to listen than to speak. The wind passes through them and takes a little of their shape. If they are wise, they descend before the first hollow feeling settles behind their ribs. If they stay, they may become one of the Resonants.

This is not death exactly. It is translation.

Their body loses color and weight. Their skin becomes pale and porous. Their thoughts simplify into tones. Whatever they loved becomes a recurring chord; whatever they feared becomes a low undertone; whatever they never said becomes a rest between notes. They no longer move freely, but they are not imprisoned. They are placed. They are given a position in the music. In time, the small creatures tend them as they tend the crystal blooms, brushing dust from their flanks, listening for the purity of their sound.

The Dry Organ also has visitors that are not flesh at all.

Ideas sometimes live there, especially ideas that have nowhere else to go. A prayer abandoned halfway up a mountain may curl beneath a fallen lintel and become a faint whistling when the wind changes. A promise never kept may settle in a crack and grow cold there, giving off a tone like glass. A grief too high to carry may condense into one of the Strays, wandering the rim with its translucent head lowered. These do not last as long as the Resonants, but while they remain they add strange colors to the music. The keeper listens to them carefully. Sometimes it protects them until they fade; sometimes it plays them until they are ready to become silence.

That is the true nature of life in the Dry Organ: everything there lives by becoming audible.

It is not a place of abundance, but it is not barren. Its fertility is mineral and acoustic. What grows there grows from absence. What moves there moves by permission of the storm. What watches there watches with the painful clarity of high air. The ruin is not dead; it is being used. The bowl is not empty; it is full of shaped air. The garden is not green, yet it flowers in cold light and fine vibration. The storm is not chaos; it is a heart with weather instead of blood.

At noon, the Dry Organ is almost still. The light stands straight down into the basin, and the few solid things cast short black shadows. The Resonants fall quiet. The moths hide in crevices. The keeper becomes invisible. The whole place seems merely a pale, broken bowl under a merciless sky.

But at dusk it begins.

The cooling air slips over the rim. A single high note rises from one of the standing figures. The storm turns inside itself, tightening. The hollow grasses lift. The mineral garden glimmers as if touched by frost. The small creatures pause, antennae raised. Then the keeper inclines toward the center, and the basin answers with a chord so vast and dry it seems to come from the bones of the world.

Then everything lives together.

The pipes sing. The moths rise in glittering clouds. The Strays step along the rim, eating the silences between tones. The storm breathes through the ruin, and the ruin becomes an instrument, and the instrument becomes a body, and the body becomes a single sustained note of cold, clear being.

Whoever or whatever lives there is therefore many and one.

It is the storm that has learned to stay.  
It is the figures carved into song.  
It is the small dry gardeners tending flowers of salt and silence.  
It is the keeper at the center, patient as pressure, old as a held breath.  
It is the arrivals that were not destroyed but translated.  
It is the prayers, promises, and griefs that became acoustics.  
It is the emptiness that has become attentive.

And if one leaves the Dry Organ and descends again into the lower world, one does not return whole. There is no wound, no mark, no fever. But afterward, in very quiet rooms, one may hear a thin note in the chest when the air grows cool and clear. It is not unpleasant. It is only a small, dry resonance, as though somewhere high above the world the Dry Organ remembered you, and for a moment, played you again.
