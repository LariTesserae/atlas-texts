What lives there has learned the discipline of the brim. It is not numerous, not because the basin is harsh, but because fullness there is measured by space rather than crowding. A single figure on a terrace can belong to the whole view. Two figures in a plaza are enough to make a civic event. Three, if they stand with intention, begin to resemble a council.

At first, a traveler might mistake the inhabitants for statues set out to remember a procession. They stand very still on flat roofs, beside the pale canals, along low parapets where moss thickens into blue-green seams. They are human in outline, but not in tempo. Their bodies are tall and narrow without being severe, as if shaped less by hunger or labor than by long exposure to cool damp air. Their skin has the matte sheen of wet slate: grey-green in shadow, faintly silver where light rests on it. Along their shoulders, forearms, and the crowns of their heads grow fine fibers not quite like hair, more like moss or lichen, and in those fibers tiny beads of water cling without falling.

When they move, they move with extraordinary care, as though each one carries an invisible bowl filled to the lip. Their feet are broad, soft-soled, and silent on stone. Their fingers are long and patient, slightly cupped at the tips, as if every hand is naturally offering a small hollow. Their eyes are dark, reflective, and unhurried. In bright intervals their pupils show as horizontal bars, like the eyes of creatures accustomed to ledges and distances. When they look at someone, they do not seem to judge; they seem to weigh balance.

They wear garments that do not flutter. There is no wind here to justify loose cloth, so their clothing lies close and heavy: layered mantles of woven sedge, pressed leaf, and fine root-thread, weighted at the hems with smooth stones. The fabric remains cool and faintly damp against the body. Their ornaments are not made of metal or cut gems, but of water arranged into preciousness. A drop may be held inside a ring of pale stone. A line of beads may be caught in a net of fiber and worn across the chest. A tiny basin, no larger than a palm, may be carried with such reverence that it seems to contain something holy. In fact, it does. It contains stillness.

To spill is not considered a crime among them, but it is a grief. They speak of spilling the way other peoples might speak of breaking a vow.

If they must offer strangers a name, they call themselves the Arrangers, though among themselves they often use a phrase that means something closer to “those who keep the water from deciding to leave.” Their city is divided not by ownership but by districts of care. Each person tends a portion of the basin’s completed shape. One watches a stairway where moss meets built stone. Another keeps a row of channels clear of misplaced leaves. Another attends a shallow pool that mirrors a particular ridge at a particular hour. Another listens to the rock. No one rules all of them, and no one is wholly solitary. Decisions are made by standing together at the water’s edge until agreement forms as quietly as condensation.

The Brimkeepers are the most visible of their orders. They visit the canals and hollow pools at long intervals, carrying leaf-shaped scoops no larger than a hand. If a pool has sunk by the width of a hair, they add water from a covered vessel. If a bead on a fern edge trembles too heavily, they support it with a touch so light it seems to be listening rather than touching. They do not add water to make the pools overflow. They add it to preserve the miracle of the brim: fullness without spilling.

The Edgetenders care for boundaries. They walk the margins where stone becomes green, where green becomes built ground, where built ground becomes mirror-still water. They do not sweep with urgency. They place. They adjust. They move a leaf from the center of a channel to a point where its reflection completes a pattern. They are not offended by leaves resting on water; they are concerned only that each leaf rest in its proper stillness.
