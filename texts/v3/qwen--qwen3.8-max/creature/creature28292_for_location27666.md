What lives there has learned to be heavy.

At first the basin seems more mineral than alive: water, cold rock, bright sky pressing down like a lid. But life has already entered by the small, stubborn ways life enters isolated places — on the wind, in the guts of birds that never stay long, in dormant eggs sealed into dust, in old salt memory. The first inhabitants are not the largest or the cleverest, but the patient ones. Black and rust-colored microbial crusts bloom along the seams where mineral-rich water weeps from the rock. They are not plants, not quite animals either; they are living stains, rough to the touch, tasting of iron and brine. They feed on what the mountain gives: cold, salt, trace metals, the thin chemistry of new stone. Where they thicken, the water smells faintly metallic, like a coin held too long in the mouth.

On those crusts graze little limpets no larger than a thumb. Their shells are not beautiful in any ordinary sense: scabbed, uneven, patched with orange and charcoal, each one fitted to its own favorite crack the way a hand fits a tool. Their teeth are hardened with magnetite, so when they rasp the rock there is sometimes the faintest metallic whisper, almost too small to hear. They leave bright tracks behind them, little grazed roads across the rust. The basin-folk do not eat many of them, except in lean times, but they tend them carefully, the way a gardener tends beetles that keep the right balance between moss and stone.

There are shrimp, too — pale red things with long antennae and a habit of standing on their heads in the crevices. They gather where the cracks breathe out colder, denser water, waving their feathery forelegs through the seep. In motion they look like scraps of rust-colored moss torn loose. They are important more for what they do than for what they are: they clean, they sift, they carry tiny burdens of sediment from one shelf to another. Their bodies make the water feel inhabited even when nothing larger is moving.

Then come the fish, if fish is the word for them. They are small, narrow, almost translucent, with spines dark enough to be seen through their sides like the ribs of a little boat. They do not school in the bright, flashing way of lowland fish. They hang in the columns of sunlight as if suspended by threads, turning slowly together when the light shifts. They feed on the minute living motes that drift down from the surface and up from the microbial gardens. When they move, they move with a minimum of effort, because nothing in that cold salt lens can afford waste. They are not abundant enough to darken the water, but there are enough of them to make the basin feel watched by many small, patient minds.

Deeper down, where the hollow narrows into a shadowed pocket beneath the largest fallen slab, live two or three pale eel-like creatures. They are nearly blind and very old, or seem old because they move so slowly. Their heads are blunt, their mouths soft, their bodies the color of wet chalk. They do not hunt so much as collect: dead shrimp, fish bones, the rare bird feather that has sunk after being trapped in the surface film, whatever the basin gives back after it has finished being alive. The larger inhabitants do not molest them. There is a sense in which the eels are treated as a kind of lowest clergy, keepers of endings, necessary and slightly solemn.

But the beings who make the basin what it is are not the small creatures. They are the ones who have begun, without quite intending to, to turn the hollow into a cathedral.

They are not human, though at a distance a human eye might mistake them for one. They are shorter than most lowland people, but not dwarfed; they are condensed. Their bodies seem built according to the same agreement that holds the water in the hollow: altitude and depth have compromised, and the compromise is density. Their bones are heavy. Their muscles are compact and slow-twitched. Their skin is smooth, slate-gray to greenish black, mottled with patches of salt-white and iron-brown. Along their shoulders and ribs are gill slits, and their chests have a broad, barrel-like fullness because breathing that cold salt water takes more work than breathing air. They can rise to the surface and take air into a secondary chamber, but they dislike it. The sky feels too large to them. The air feels thin and sharp and insufficient.

Their eyes are large, dark, and deeply set, made for gathering the white daylight that falls through the water without depending on color. They have a second lid, clear and tough, that closes when the surface above becomes too bright or when grit loosens from the walls. Salt glands near their eyes give them a perpetual look of having wept, though they do not weep from sorrow. They wipe the brine away with the backs of their hands as absentmindedly as a person might brush off rain.

Their hands are their finest feature. Four long fingers, a strong thumb, and a palm padded with tough, ridged skin allow them to grip the sharp rock without injuring themselves. They can hold a blade of obsidian with the delicacy of a surgeon and the certainty of a butcher. Their feet are nearly as articulate as their hands, and when they rest they often cling to the underside of a ledge, bodies hanging in the water like dark bells.

They wear little, because clothing that flows freely is dangerous among blades of fresh rock. What they do wear is close-fitting and practical: bands and wraps made from woven microbial leather, cured shrimp chitin, and the shed inner casings of tube-like creatures that live in the cracks. Their ornaments are not bright. They favor chips of magnetite, flakes of obsidian, and small angular stones tied with sinew to belts or shoulder straps. They do not polish these things to shine. They value edges, weight, and fit. If a stone has been carried long enough to become smooth from use, that is respected, but only because use itself is respected.

They call themselves by a name that is not truly a name in the way lowland peoples understand it. It is more of a pressure-pattern, a sequence of taps and low pulses that can be felt through the rock as much as heard. If translated roughly, it means something like “those who remain in the held place.” For convenience one might call them the basin-folk, but that makes them sound smaller and simpler than they are. They are not primitives hiding in a puddle. They are a complete people shaped around a complete world, and that world happens to be very small.

They live by careful husbandry. The basin cannot support waste. Every dead thing, every loose stone, every change in salinity or temperature is noticed. They cultivate the rust gardens by scraping away dead crust in patterns that encourage new growth, leaving enough untouched to keep the water chemistry stable. They harvest shrimp and limpets in rotation. They drive the little glass-bodied fish into nets woven from fine bacterial fiber and weighted with basalt chips, taking only what they need. They store food in the coldest crevices, where the water is nearly still, or cure it in salt and iron-rich paste packed into shells. They do not cook with fire; there is no fuel and no way to keep a flame in such a place. Instead they soften, ferment, and sun-warm their food in shallow black stone trays when the daylight is strong enough. They eat with great economy, cracking shells with their teeth, sucking mineral-rich fluids from joints, wasting almost nothing.

They are not gentle in the way lowland stories mean gentle. They are deliberate. Their world is full of edges, and they have no sentimentality about sharpness. But they are not cruel either. Cruelty requires surplus energy, and the basin does not produce enough for that. Their conflicts are usually resolved by separation, by one party withdrawing to another ledge until the water between them has cooled. When they must fight, they fight briefly and terribly, with obsidian points and sudden pressure in the throat or ribs. Then it is over, and the basin returns to its slow rhythm.

They have made the hollow into a nave without building walls. They do this by arranging fallen stones, not into grand structures but into low alignments that guide movement, sound, and current. They leave the central water open, because open water is both their road and their meeting place. Around it they set angular rocks like pews, though no one sits on them. They lean against them, wedge themselves beside them, use them as anchors for nets and storage lines. The effect is ceremonial without being intentional. The space has a forward direction, a gathering place, a sense of height and depth. If one looks down from the rim on a bright day, the arrangement below seems almost liturgical: dark shapes gathered around a clear central aisle of water, all held inside a stone bowl under a moving ceiling of sky.

Their relationship with sound is more important than their relationship with light. The basin carries sound strangely. A tap on basalt can travel through the water and into the rock at once, arriving as both a noise and a pressure against the chest. The basin-folk speak in clicks, thumps, grating whispers, and subsonic pulses. Much of their language would be felt rather than heard by an outsider: a warning in the bones, a question in the teeth, a name like a change in current. They are especially attentive to the magnetite seams. Those dark veins in the rock give back a faint tug, a sense of orientation, and also carry vibration with unusual clarity. Their listeners press their jaws or palms to the seams and attend to the mountain’s small statements: where a stone has shifted, where water is finding a new crack, where the surface above is troubled by wind.

This is why they seem priestly, though they do not worship as others might. They have no god of the sky and no god of the sea. They have practices of attention. Each day, at times determined by the angle of sunlight through the water, several of them gather at the seams and listen. They tap the stones in set rhythms, not to make music but to hear how the basin answers. If the echo returns clean, the rock is stable. If it comes back dulled or doubled, something has changed. They remember these sounds for generations. Their elders are not necessarily the oldest in body but those who can distinguish one echo from another and know what each variation foretells.

They read the sky too, but not as birds do. To them the surface is a ceiling, and the clouds passing over it are slow distortions: swelling shadows, brightening veils, gray tides that press down or lift away. They do not imagine the clouds as shapes of animals or heroes. They read them as pressures. A cloud that darkens the center of the basin means a coming change in temperature. A high thin brightness means the surface will grow colder without darkening. A storm is announced long before it breaks by the way the water tightens. When the ceiling begins to tremble with wind, the basin-folk move to the deeper pockets and secure their stores. They have learned to fear hail not because it is rare but because it is absurd: stones of ice falling from the sky into their stone bowl, drumming the surface until the whole water rings.

In winter, if the surface freezes, they live under a dim white shield. Then the basin becomes quieter and brighter in a strange way, though still without any uncanny glow. Ice changes the sound. It makes their taps sharper, more brittle. They keep openings where they can, chipping carefully with obsidian tools, not because they need air so urgently as because a sealed basin feels too much like a closed fist. Under ice they move less, eat less, and spend long hours wedged into crevices with their limbs drawn close. They tell stories then by pressure and touch, passing patterns from hand to hand in the dark water. Some of those patterns are very old, older than the basin itself, carried in the body-memory of the people from wherever they came before the mountains broke open.
