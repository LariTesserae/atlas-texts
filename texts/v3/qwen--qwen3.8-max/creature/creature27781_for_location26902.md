What lives there is not first a catalogue but a pressure: a warm, wet, overcrowded pressure, a living density that has arrived faster than names.

Nothing in that basin belongs to itself alone. Every creature is also a surface, a passage, a host, a signal, a mouth, a nursery, a ruin in waiting. The inhabitants are adapted to a world that has not finished being made. They are quick to hatch, quick to mate, quick to feed, quick to vanish. They do not depend on lasting territories, because the ground itself may split, steam, slump, or refill by the next pulse of rain. Their lives are built around interruption.

The first and lowest inhabitants are not animals in any simple sense, but living skins.

They spread over the fresh rock in felts and crusts: dark microbial mats, mineral-licking films, salt-flecks, warm-breathing scums, soft black layers that swell when the ground exhales. These are the basin’s first citizens and its first food. They taste the vapors rising through cracks. They drink the damp that beads on glassy stone. They turn heat and mineral breath into something edible. Where they thicken, the rock no longer seems bare; it seems furred, breathing, slightly feverish.

On these living crusts move the smallest grazers: mites no bigger than grains of sand, pale worms threading through wet dust, spring-tailed things that leap when disturbed and then freeze as if their motion had never happened. Some are so numerous that they look like a moving texture on the stone rather than separate bodies. They graze the felts. They are grazed in turn by larger mites, by beetle-larvae, by soft predators with hooked mouthparts. Even here, at the scale of dust and film, the basin is already full of hunger.

The water pockets have their own nations.

Every cupped leaf, every hollow stone, every shallow pool sealed in a rock-basin becomes a temporary world. These pools are warm, crowded, and brief. They fill with condensation, with hot rain, with steam-born moisture, and they vanish when the ground shifts or the air drinks too deeply. Life in them must be ready for both abundance and disappearance.

In those leaf-cups live wriggling larvae with transparent skin, dark water-beetles that row in circles, tiny shells that crawl over the inner leaf-surface, and worm-things with bristled mouths that filter the warm liquid. Some eggs can wait dry for a long time, sealed in resistant husks, until water returns. Some larvae hatch within minutes of a rain-pulse and become mature before the pool shrinks. The water is not calm; it is a race.

There are frogs born into these small worlds, or things close to frogs: damp-skinned, broad-mouthed, with throats that swell into delicate balloons. Some are so small they can sit in a single leaf-pool with all their kind. Their calls are not merely sounds but water-shapes: low pulses that make the surface tremble, thin needles of noise that bounce between leaves, wet clicks that tell other bodies where the pool ends and the next begins. When several call together, the pool itself seems to speak.

In the larger hollows and wandering channels live steam-newts, eel-like and slow, with skins that smell of mineral salts. They can cross wet rock when the channels shift, dragging themselves through moss and mud, guided by dampness and heat. There are shell-crawlers too: low, armored things whose plates click when they touch. When a pool begins to dry, they seal themselves into their shells or bury in warm silt, waiting through the absence of water. When rain returns, they emerge as if nothing had passed.

The hot flats have their own life.

Where warmth rises strongest, the ground is not dead but strangely tender. There, vent-worms and heat-lickers gather around cracks, pressing flat bodies against stone to absorb mineral films. Some are soft and pale, almost translucent, so that one can see the dark heat of the ground working through them. Others are crusted with salt-like beads, their bodies armored by the very exhalations they feed on. They do not flee quickly. They are patient in the way of things that live close to a source.

Around the vents move beetles with dull metallic shells, harvesting salt crusts and dried secretions. There are flies that hatch in hot rain and live only long enough to rise in glittering clouds, mate in the steam, and fall. Their bodies are so brief that they seem less like animals and more like sparks thrown upward by the ground.

The vegetation is not merely lived upon; it is inhabited layer by layer.

The root-mats are streets and hunting grounds. Beneath the woven fibers live burrowers with blunt heads and powerful foreparts, creatures that thrum through the mud and leave vibration-wakes behind them. Their movement loosens soil, opens tiny channels, and lets heat rise in new places. They are followed by smaller things that feed on the disturbed filaments, and by still smaller things that feed on those.

The fern-like tiers are vertical worlds. On the lower fronds live crawling insects, egg-clusters, and mites that farm fungal threads. Higher up, where condensation gathers, there are beetles with polished backs that mirror the weak living lights. Their shells are not mirrors for vanity but for signal: a shift of the body sends a blue-green flicker across the leaves. Some predators hunt by reading those flickers. Some prey extinguish themselves with mud or fold beneath stems when a shadow passes.

The broad leaves that hold water are meeting places. Beetles skim across them. Frogs crouch at their rims. Larvae twitch below. Small climbing creatures use the leaf-pools as nurseries, dropping eggs into the water and leaving them to the heat and chance. There are insects that spend their whole lives on one plant, never touching the ground, born in a cup of water, mating above it, dying into it.

The vines are highways and traps. They hang in curtains, beaded with moisture, crowded with things that climb, hang, drop, and swing. Some creatures live in vine-knots, their bodies shaped to fit the twists of stem and fiber. When a vine-curtain shifts, it is often not wind but a passage: a wave of small bodies moving together, or a larger creature pushing through while thousands of smaller ones ride its back, hide in its folds, or scatter from its path.

Fungi have their own courts.

They rise in shelves, cones, lattices, and soft bursts, some pale as bone, some slick and dark, some split and glowing faintly at the edges. They are food, shelter, breeding ground, and signal-fire all at once. Beetles feed on their gills. Moths lay eggs in their caps. Mites tunnel through their soft flesh.
