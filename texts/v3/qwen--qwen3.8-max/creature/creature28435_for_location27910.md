What lives below the salt-field lives first in the negative: not in water, not in mud, not in the wet dark of ordinary caves. It lives in wax, fiber, resin, salt, oil, static charge, and the slow economics of growth. To inhabit the buried country is to be made of it, or to have become sufficiently like it that the difference between body and architecture is small.

The oldest residents are the great plants themselves. They are not scenery. They are civic organisms, enormous and patient, holding the hollow open with their bodies. Some are so vast that a town can live on them for generations without altering more than a little of their bark. They do not move quickly, but they are not still in any final sense. Their root-tips explore voids; their leaf-plates adjust to light and heat; their trunks settle under load with long, low creaks that the people below learn to read as weather-readers read wind. A mature hanging trunk may be older than the settlement on it. Its crown may have been pruned into a public terrace; its lower limbs may have been trained into railings, ladders, or support cables. In such places, one does not live beside a plant. One lives within a slow, vegetable intention.

Among these structures move the quiet animals of the dry country.

They are abundant but not noisy. Their bodies have been shaped by the same necessities as the vegetation: no free water, little wasted energy, no sound that carries farther than needful. Many have waxed shells or felted skins that reduce both friction and noise. Many store oil in thick tissues rather than fat in the wet sense. Their joints whisper. Their feeding is a soft rasping, a clicking, a careful abrasion. They do not pant, drip, or sweat. Their eyes are often large and shaded by secondary lids against dust and spore-haze. Their feet are broad, soft, or hooked for bark and fiber.

There are herds of bark-grazers that move along the trunks in slow processions. They are low-bodied, broad-backed creatures, more like living tools than grazing beasts in any surface sense. Their mouths are fitted with plates and rasps capable of stripping old bark-scales, cropping spore crusts, and harvesting mineralized leaf-growth. They do not bellow or call. When a herd shifts from one trunk to another, the sound is like a long, soft brushing. In settled districts, some of these grazers are semi-tended: not fenced, but followed, encouraged, protected from predators, and occasionally harvested for shell-plate, oil, and fiber-meat.

There are pale-winged insects, or things like insects, that travel between platforms in sparse commuter lines. Their wings are broad and membranous, but they do not swarm. They move in intervals, often riding thermal drafts from light shafts or descending along cable paths when the charge in the air changes. Some serve the industrial groves by carrying chemical signals or pollen-like powders. Others are scavengers, cleaning spore dust from leaves and machinery. During dry storms they cluster near static combs, their bodies upright and motionless, as if listening.

There are predators, too, but they are ambushers rather than chasers. Long-legged and salt-crusted, they wait at root-junctions, under bridges, along cable paths, in the darker hollows between engine-sheds. Their camouflage is excellent: bark-patterned, lacquered, mineral-bloomed, still as a rail. When they strike, it is with a quick, dry snap, often using resinous glue, hooked limbs, or needle-like salt spines rather than loud pursuit. They do not howl. They do not roar. Even their kills are quiet.

There are scavengers in the floor felt: beetles with flattened bodies, mites in the ducts, slow crawlers in the seed-cases, things that live in the insulation of old engines and feed on warmth, oil residue, and shed fiber. Some are pests; some are useful. The people below do not always draw a sharp line between the two. A duct-mite that cleans a ventilation shaft is welcomed; the same kind in a seed-silo is not. Life there is judged by where it moves and what it consumes.

And then there are the people.

They are not numerous. The buried country is large, and much of it remains open void, pre-formation zone, or unfinished floor. Settlements are scattered along trunk-terraces, suspended platforms, and stable fiber banks, separated by distances that can be seen across but not quickly crossed. One ward may hang from a single enormous trunk; another may sprawl along a ridge of compressed leaf-plate; another may be little more than a cluster of engine-sheds and drying racks around a light shaft. Between them, the air remains mostly empty, cool, and quiet.

The people themselves are slender and light without being fragile. Their bones seem less like wet animal bone and more like dense fiber or hardened lacquer. Their muscles work slowly and efficiently. They are built for balance on springy surfaces, for climbing along root-rails, for moving across suspended walkways without making unnecessary sound. Their hands are long-fingered and precise, with palms that are slightly felted and highly sensitive. Their feet are soft-soled, gripping, almost quiet by nature. Many have fine wax-down on their arms, shoulders, and cheeks, not hair in the surface sense but a filamentous growth that helps them sense static charge, dust movement, and shifts in air pressure.

Their skin is matte, often faintly salt-bloomed at the joints. It does not glisten. It does not sweat. In places where a surface creature would have moisture, they have oil, wax, or resinous protective films. Their eyes are adapted to low glare and drifting particulate: large enough to gather light, shaded by translucent inner lids that can close against dust without darkening vision entirely. Their noses are dry and efficient, able to filter spore-haze and detect chemical changes in the air. Their mouths are not wet in the ordinary sense. They produce no free saliva, only thick, resinous compounds used for taste, sealing, and very limited speech lubrication. They do not spit. They do not drool. Their breath is almost invisible.

They smell of warm wax, salt dust, crushed seed-husks, and faintly electrified metal.

Their voices are low. They can speak, but they rarely raise their voices. A shout in the buried country is not merely rude; it is wasteful, alarming, and potentially dangerous. Sound travels too far. It bounces off fiber walls, root ceilings, and salt lenses. It disturbs fauna, interferes with the listening work of static-tenders, and marks a person as untrained. So the people have developed a layered communication system: soft speech, breath-clicks, finger-taps on railings, hand signs, sleeve-rustles, and small signal chimes used only when necessary. Their languages have many words for texture, tension, charge, load, grain, light-angle, and dryness. They have fewer words for wetness, and most of those are borrowed, mythic, or hostile.

They dress in materials taken from the living country: woven fiber, shed bark, pressed leaf-plate, salt-glass beads, resin buttons, copper fittings, and thin lacquered plates. Their clothing is layered but not heavy.
