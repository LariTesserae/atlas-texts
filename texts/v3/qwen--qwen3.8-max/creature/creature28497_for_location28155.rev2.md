What lives there is not a single tenant but a population of tenants, each fitted to heat, height, water, and the patience of old rock. The basin does not merely contain life; it manufactures living conditions and then fills them. What lives there is distributed across scales, from films too small to see as individual threads to animals whose movement bends whole reedbeds. Nothing is ornamental. Everything has a place in the wet, warm, mineral economy of the basin.

The first inhabitants are the ones that look like color rather than creatures. Microbial mats stitch the hottest wet margins into living surfaces: olive and black in the warmest seeps, bright green in shallower light, orange where iron blooms, pale grey where silica settles. These films are not passive stains. They trap sediment, bind grains, precipitate minerals, and stabilize the ground that later roots hold. In some channels they form laminated crusts, layer upon layer, each one a season of growth. Bacteria and algae work at the edge of heat that would kill most life, turning chemical gradients into tissue. They are the basin’s first manufacturers, making a floor out of water and dissolved stone.

Fungi live wherever something has died or begun to soften. They thread the peat, lace drowned stems, and break fallen wood into dark, wet fiber. Their fruiting bodies appear after rain: cups, shelves, thin stalks, pale or rust-colored, temporary and abundant. They are quiet but indispensable. Without them, dead reeds and leaves would accumulate faster than the marsh could absorb them. With them, death becomes substrate. A fallen branch is not waste; it becomes a moist architecture for mosses, insect larvae, and small climbing roots.

Above these smallest residents rise the plants, and they are so abundant that they seem less individuals than a condition of the basin. Reeds, sedges, and rushes make the most visible population. Their roots knit together floating peat, creating ground that trembles underfoot but holds. Their stems are often hollow, jointed, toughened by silica, and when warm air moves through them they produce a faint whistling, like the basin breathing through reed-pipes. Mosses are everywhere too: over stone, over dead stems, along shelves, in the bases of shrubs. They swell with water, hold condensation, and provide nurseries for larvae, spores, and tiny amphibians. Ferns gather in shaded cuts and on moist rock faces, their fronds dripping with gathered cloud-water. Broad-leaved marsh plants spread across shallow water, glossy and heavy, their upper surfaces shedding droplets, their roots probing warm mud.

In the more stable places, shrubs and small trees lean over pools and channels. They are not tall; the basin’s openness, wet ground, and wind keep them modest. Their trunks are often furred with moss, their branches weighted with epiphytes. Small orchids and cup-shaped leaf rosettes cling to limbs and collect rain in tiny reservoirs. These hanging gardens become separate habitats: insects breed in them, frogs climb to them, nectar-feeding birds visit them. In drier patches, resinous low shrubs tangle together, their leaves aromatic and slightly sticky, discouraging too much grazing and shedding moisture in the humid air. Even the surfaces of living plants are inhabited by lichens, algae, and minute arthropods. The vegetation is not a backdrop. It is the basin’s built country, though nothing in it has been built by hands.

The warmth and wetness fill the air with invertebrates. Insects are the most apparent pulse of the basin’s animal life. Dragonflies and damselflies patrol open water, their bodies metallic green, copper, or smoky black, their wings catching light over steam-threaded shallows. Their larvae hunt among algal filaments below. Beetles move across floating leaves, some polished and dark as wet seeds, some carrying tiny bubbles of air against their bodies. Flies hover in warm sunbeams and disappear into shade. Midges rise in columns where vapor ascends, using the rising air as both shelter and highway. Mosquito-like insects emerge near dusk, not as a torment alone but as food: for bats, birds, fish, amphibians, and other insects. Grasshoppers and crickets occupy the tougher grassy banks, their sounds dry and intermittent against the deeper damp of the marsh.

Spiders stretch silk between reed stems and under leaves. Their webs are often beaded with moisture, each droplet a lens. Some hunt in the lower vegetation; others wait in shaded folds where condensation gathers. Snails graze rust-colored films from stone and plant stems, leaving clean trails behind them. Worms and leeches work the warm mud, aerating it, feeding on decay, becoming meals in turn. Small crustaceans sift through cooler channels and root-tangles, breaking down fine organic matter. In the mineral-rich pools, larvae of many kinds live attached to stems or hidden in microbial mats, their bodies adapted to warmth, low oxygen, or faint chemical toxicity. The invertebrate world is not a background hum; it is the basin’s fine machinery, pollinating, consuming, scavenging, aerating, and transferring energy from film and leaf to fish, frog, bird, and mammal.

Amphibians thrive in the wet heat. They are everywhere in the softer places: on floating mats, along muddy lips, under wet stones, inside leaf-cups, at the margins where warm water cools just enough for skin. Frogs sit on leaves no larger than their own spread feet, throats pulsing, calls brief and resonant. Some species make foam nests on reed stems above water; others keep eggs in moist folds of skin or carry tadpoles to small pools held by epiphytes. Their skins are sensitive to heat and chemistry, so they live along gradients, never far from refuge. Toads rest on terraces where mineral crusts have formed, their bodies flattened and patient, their color mottled with mud and algae. Salamander-like creatures hide in shaded seeps, some keeping feathery gills into adulthood because the warm, still water rewards cutaneous and larval breathing. At night, amphibians become more audible: single notes, answers, short exchanges, then quiet again.

The deeper water belongs to fish and fish-like forms. In warm pools, where oxygen is low and light is filtered by vapor and floating leaves, they tend to be smooth-bodied, dark above, pale below, with barbels or broad sensory heads. Some gather in schools near submerged root masses, turning together so that flashes of reflected light scatter a predator’s attention. Others lie near the bottom, armored or scaleless, stirring slowly through warm silt. In the darker channels and hollows, eel-like animals move with slow muscular patience. They surface to breathe air, vanish into root tunnels, and can cross wet peat at night if water connects their paths. Some produce weak electric fields, navigating and hunting in stained water where sight is limited. These aquatic hunters are not numerous enough to dominate the basin, but they regulate smaller life and keep the deeper pools from becoming passive nurseries.

Reptiles are present but selective. Snakes slip through reedbeds and along pool rims, slender, patient, taking frogs, fish, and large insects. Their scales often carry mineral dust or fine algae, making them hard to see against wet stone. Turtles rest on warm mineral lips and half-submerged roots, their shells furred with growth, sliding into water at the first tremor of footfall. On the drier rocky ridges, small dark lizards use sun-warmed stone, darting into crevices when the air cools. Reptiles do not own the basin; they occupy its edges, its thermal seams, its moments of stillness. They are part of its patience.

Birds give the basin much of its voice. Small bright species move through epiphytes and flowering shrubs, feeding on nectar and insects, their bills dusted with pollen. They flicker among hanging roots and cup-shaped leaves, quick and quarrelsome. Larger wading birds stand in warm shallows, slate-colored or mottled brown, waiting until their strikes break the water with sudden precision. Rails and other broad-toed birds walk over floating vegetation, their weight distributed, their bodies disappearing into reeds. Swallows and swifts sweep above open water, drinking in flight and taking insects from the humid air. Raptors ride thermal columns that rise from heated water and dark rock, circling without effort, their shadows passing over marsh and pool. Before storms, birds become restless; small ones vanish into cover, waders lift from shallows, and the whole air may fill briefly with wings, then empty again.

Mammals are less conspicuous but not absent. Small rodents live in the root mazes of floating banks, feeding on seeds, tubers, and tender shoots. Their bodies are compact, their fur sleek from moisture, their movements quick and low. Shrew-like insectivores hunt along wet ledges and under fallen stems, eating larvae, worms, and small amphibians. Bats appear at dusk, dipping over pools and along vapor curtains, their flight soft and erratic. Climbing creatures with grasping tails move through shrubs and low trees, taking fruit, insects, eggs, and anything soft or careless. They are nocturnal for the most part, their eyes bright in the last light, their presence marked by a shaking stem or a dropped seed husk.

The largest residents are few. Broad-footed herbivores move along inherited routes through the marsh, their weight distributed over wide hooves or padded feet. They are mud-stained, slow, and heat-tolerant, with sparse hair and calm, high-set eyes. They graze on sedges and young shoots, break open reed stands, and create wallows in warm depressions. Their bathing disperses seeds, their dung feeds insects and fish, and their trampling opens patches of mud where smaller plants can establish. They are not numerous enough to dominate, but their absence would change the basin. Birds sometimes perch on their backs, taking parasites; insects rise from their footprints; fish gather where their waste enters the water. They are living disturbance, gentle in their slowness, essential in their passage.

Predators are mostly modest in size. Small marsh carnivores hunt by patience rather than pursuit, waiting beside channels, stalking through root-shade, taking rodents, birds, frogs, or fish when opportunity offers. Larger kills are often the work of water, heat, age, or disease rather than teeth. Yet the basin is not without serious hunters. Old eels in deep thermal channels can take unwary prey near the surface. Large snakes move through reeds with silent intent. Raptors descend from the bright air. Predation here is not spectacular for its own sake; it is frequent, quiet, and folded quickly into decay and renewal.

Death in the basin is not left exposed for long. A dead leaf becomes peat. A drowned insect becomes food for fish or larvae. A fallen bird is found by beetles, flies, fungi, and scavengers. A fish that dies in a warm pool may be gone as a body within days, its matter distributed into algae, microbial film, and the bodies of smaller animals. This is not cruelty or neatness. It is throughput. The basin is generous with life because it is efficient with death. Nothing remains merely dead. Even bones, if they escape immediate consumption, are softened by acid peat and mineral water, their substances returned to the chemistry of the marsh.

If one asks who lives there, the answer depends on what one means by who. There are no people here in the ordinary sense of builders, owners, or travelers who leave permanent marks. No hands have drained the marsh, fenced its edges, laid paths, or raised shelters that last. The basin resists such settling: wood softens in the heat and damp, metal salts corrode, mud swallows firm footing, and vegetation closes over any opening left unattended. If human beings pass through, they pass slowly and leave little. But the question of who lives there need not be answered only by human presence. The basin has residents that seem almost like keepers, though they keep nothing intentionally.

There are the old eels that remember channels older than the reedbeds above them. There are the broad-footed herbivores that know which peat banks hold and which give way, moving with inherited caution. There are the raptors that read thermal columns and use the basin’s heat as lift. There are the frogs whose calls change minutes before rain, responding to pressure and humidity. There are the root networks and fungal threads that bind the ground and distribute decay. If there is a mind in the basin, it is not a single mind. It is a distributed attention made of many appetites, senses, rhythms, and chemical gradients. It does not plan. It adjusts. It does not speak. It answers.

This is why the place can feel inhabited in a deeper sense than merely being populated. The basin behaves like a body with many organs. The microbial mats are its slow skin. The reed roots are its binding tissue. The pools and channels are its circulating fluids. The vents and updrafts are its breath. The insects and birds are its restless nerves, carrying signals and consequences from one part to another. The large animals are its slower moods, moving weight through the system and leaving change behind. None of these parts is in charge, yet together they maintain a world. The landscape is not alive as one animal is alive, but it is alive as a region of continuous living, a thermodynamic country where life is both product and process.

At evening, the inhabitants become most apparent. The light turns horizontal and catches every hanging droplet. Steam rises more visibly as the air cools. Frogs begin their spaced calling. Insects rise in soft veils over the water. Bats stitch the edges of vapor columns. Fish disturb the black mirrors of pools, leaving rings that widen and vanish. A bird gives one cry from the reeds and receives no immediate answer. Somewhere a vent exhales with its low, soft hiss. The basin does not become silent; it becomes a quieter kind of activity. The heat still rises. The water still moves. The roots still hold. The living crowd of the place settles into its night economy, each creature in its niche, each niche fed by another, all of it wrapped in humidity, mineral smell, and the dark green weight of growth.

What lives there, then, is not one being but a dense, wet, breathing commons. It lives in films and fronds, in larvae and leaves, in warm mud and open water, in feathers, scales, fur, and fungal thread. It lives without artifacts because its making is done by heat, pressure, chemistry, hunger, reproduction, decay, and time. It is crowded, ordered, quiet, and relentless. It belongs to no one. It is inhabited by everything.
