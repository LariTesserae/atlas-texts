What lives there is not one kind of creature, but a crowding, wet, interdependent population that has grown around the basin’s conditions as tightly as crust grows around a stone. The first answer to the question is always plural. Whoever lives there is many. Whatever lives there is layered.

The smallest and oldest residents are not the people, nor the birds, nor the fish, but the living films. Every damp face of stone carries them: thin bacterial skins, slick microbial coatings, pale fungal threads, soft mats that bloom where mineral water lingers. They are the basin’s first pasture and its ceaseless repair. They spread over new sinter before it has time to look finished. They line the inside of clay channels, cling to the undersides of footbridges, darken the joints between blocks, and soften the hard edges of basalt with a faint slippery bloom. Scraped away in one place, they return in another. They are not cherished individually, but they are understood as the base on which everything else rests. Without them, the small grazers starve; without the small grazers, the fish and birds and people cannot live. In that sense, the city is not founded on stone alone, but on slime.

Above this invisible grazing world lives the crowded animal population of the channels and tanks. Small pale crustaceans move through the mineral films in restless flickers. Snails with thin, chalky shells rasp over wet surfaces, leaving trails that disappear almost as soon as they are made. Amphipods and isopods comb through soft deposits at the edges of pools. White grubs and wriggling larvae thicken in warm shallow water, their bodies so abundant that they are gathered in woven trays like a kind of living grain. Beetles with broad, damp-resistant feet work the margins where stone is rough enough to grip. Crabs, quick and flat, hide beneath stone lips and come out to scrape at new growth. In the darker channels, blind bottom-feeders move slowly along the floors, tasting the water with fine barbels, stirring the fine silt that would otherwise choke the flow.

Fish live everywhere there is enough depth for them. They are small, quick, and pale in the dimness, their bodies adapted to warm, mineral-rich water. They flash beneath footbridges and vanish into shadowed channels. Some are kept in pens, others move freely through connected pools and settling tanks. Eels knot themselves into drains, sleep in wet hollows, and slip through places that seem too narrow for any backboned thing. In the deeper cisterns, there are older fish that have never seen a shadow sharp enough to fear. They rise only when feeding-time vibrations pass through the water, a pattern of taps or a specific slap of a paddle against stone that the whole basin knows.

The air above the water is no less occupied. Midges rise in clouds from the shallows, their bodies so dense in places that they look like gray smoke moving against the dim light. They feed the bats that stitch through the upper spaces with rapid, precise turns, taking insects on the wing and vanishing into hollows in the roost towers. Frogs and toads occupy the damper ledges, sleek and warm-adapted, their throats working constantly in the heavy air. They are not numerous enough to dominate, but their small voices add one more thread to the noise: a wet, persistent chirring that disappears when the channels run louder.

Birds are among the most visible inhabitants, because the city has been built partly for them. They nest where there are no reeds to hold nests: on stone ledges, under bridge arches, in carved hollows, and in the tall roost towers that rise from the dense construction like perforated columns. Swallows and martins skim the upper air for insects. Terns and other fish-catchers wait on narrow perches above the pens and tanks, then drop into the water with quick, sharp splashes. Waterfowl rest on broad platforms because there are no marshes for them. They preen, squabble, sleep, and breed under the same vapor that covers everything else. Their cries are part of the city’s permanent sound. Their droppings whiten roofs, ledges, and the upper faces of towers, and this whiteness is not filth but a resource, carefully harvested, dried, stored, and used in tanning, curing, and the cultivation of fungal beds. The birds are not pets, exactly, but they are not wild in the distant sense either. They know the rhythms of the workers. They gather where feeding happens. They retreat when scraping crews approach with ladders and baskets. They are part of the city’s managed animal economy.

Then there are the people.

They are the most deliberate inhabitants, because they are the ones who must keep the basin from drifting out of balance. They are not separate from the place; they are its nervous system, its hands, its watchfulness. Their bodies show the life plainly. They are often broad in shoulder and hip, low in stance, with hands made thick by constant work. Their feet are wide and gripping, accustomed to wet stone, slick crust, and narrow ledges. Their skin is rarely dry. It carries the sheen of constant damp, the faint roughness of mineral dust, the smell of oil, fish, salt, and warm stone. They rub themselves with rendered fat and fungal pulp to keep the lime and salt from cracking their skin. Their eyes are used to low light and moving water, quick to notice a change in color, a blocked channel, a disturbed film, a pool that has risen or fallen by the smallest amount. In the amber glow of oil lamps, their faces often look pale and shining, as if they too have been coated by the city’s constant moisture.

They are not all deaf, but many are partly deafened by the noise in which they live, and those who hear best hear differently. They listen not for melody or speech at a distance, but for changes: the altered pitch of a channel beginning to block, the new slap of water where a gate has shifted, the hollow sound of a wall too thin, the change in echo when a space is filled or emptied. They also feel sound through their feet and hands. A worker standing on wet stone can often tell more from vibration than from hearing. This has shaped their whole way of being with one another. Their voices, when used, are loud, short, and close. Most communication is carried by gesture, posture, touch, and position. A lifted hand can mean stop. A palm turned downward over a channel can mean lower the flow. A touch on the shoulder can mean move behind me. A finger drawn across the forearm can mean the crust is ready to cut. Children learn these signs before they can make themselves understood in the noise. They learn to read water before they learn to tell stories.

Their social life is organized around maintenance. There is no ruling class in the sense of people who stand apart from labor, because the labor is too urgent and too continuous to be delegated entirely. Authority belongs to those who can keep things stable. The sluice-keepers are especially respected, not because they command others, but because they know the levels. They understand which gate must open a hand’s width, which clay plug must be eased out, which weir must be raised by a finger’s breadth. They move through the city with measuring sticks, notched bones, and gauge stones marked by generations of careful scraping. Their eyes are always on water lines. Their hands are always checking for flow. They are the closest thing the city has to priests, though they do not call themselves that.

Other workers specialize in crust. Some guide mineral-rich water into molds and over frames, building new stone by slow deposition. Some cut soft sinter while it is still damp, shaping blocks that will harden only after they have been placed. Others scrape walls and floors to keep channels open, removing fresh crust before it narrows the flow. This work is endless. A wall left untended for too long thickens. A floor left unscraped becomes uneven. A channel left alone begins to fill. The city survives by being constantly shaved, skimmed, cleaned, and redirected. The people who do this are often covered in pale mineral dust and wet slime, their hands raw, their tools dulling quickly against the new stone. They use shell scrapers, basalt hammers, polished adzes, copper hooks, bone levers, and cords of braided gut or fungal fiber. Their labor is not glamorous, but it is the basis of all other life in the basin.

There are also those who tend the animals. Fish-keepers mend nets and adjust pens. Bird-tenders climb the roost towers to collect eggs, remove dead chicks, clear nesting hollows, and harvest guano. Insect-gatherers work the shallows with fine trays, collecting larvae and midge clusters. Crab-catchers know where the pools dry slightly at the edges and where the animals hide during the loudest hours of work. Frog-tenders, fewer in number, maintain damp ledges where the amphibians can feed on small insects, because frogs help keep the swarming populations from becoming too thick. None of these roles is wholly separate. A person who harvests eggs in the morning may mend nets in the middle of the day and help open a sluice in the evening. The city is too crowded and too interdependent for rigid castes. Everyone knows a little of everything, because the basin does not wait for specialists.

They live in close quarters. Buildings lean into one another. Platforms rest on roofs. Passages are narrow and damp, often barely wide enough for one person to pass sideways. Where there is room, there are alcoves for sleeping, shelves for tools, niches for lamps, hooks for nets and bundles of gut. Sleeping places are raised where possible, lined with hides, feathers, woven gut mats, and soft fungal felt pressed into dense pads. The warmth is constant, so there is no need for thick bedding, but the damp is constant too, and every household knows how to keep a sleeping space from becoming slick with condensation. Lamps burn in wall recesses, their low yellow flames reflected in wet stone. In the dimmest inner courts, lamps are never fully extinguished. They are trimmed, refilled, moved, and watched. Fire is precious and dangerous. It is kept small, contained in clay dishes and shallow stone bowls, fed with animal fat and dried fungal matter. No one here has ever known a wood fire. The idea of a tall flame eating a log would seem monstrous to them: too large, too hungry, too careless of what cannot be replaced.

Their food comes from the animal and fungal economy. Fish are roasted on flat heated stones, steamed in closed clay vessels, or dried over warm vents. Shellfish are opened with bone and shell tools, then cooked quickly on hot basalt. Insect larvae are pressed into cakes, toasted, or boiled into thick pastes. Eggs are gathered from the roost towers in carefully lined baskets and eaten fresh, salted, or preserved in clay jars sealed with fat. Fungal beds produce pale, soft fruiting bodies that are sliced, dried, or mashed into dense loaves. Salt is scraped from pans and used not only for taste but for curing hides, preserving fish, and trading within the basin’s neighborhoods. Nothing is served on green leaves, because there are none. Meals are eaten from shell bowls, stone plates, clay trays, or broad pieces of cured hide stretched over frames. People eat with their hands, with bone picks, with small shell spoons. They gather in tight groups, often standing or squatting, because there is little space for leisure. Even meals happen near the water, near the work, near the noise.

Their clothing is simple because the warmth is constant and labor is wet. They wear aprons, loin wraps, shoulder coverings, and protective sleeves made from cured hide, stitched gut, woven hair, or fungal cordage. Many work barefoot or in thin-soled sandals of layered hide that can be dried and oiled. Those who handle sharp tools or hot stone wear heavier hand coverings. Ornaments exist, but they are modest: shell beads, bone pins, copper rings, fish vertebrae strung on sinew, small polished stones worn at the throat. Beauty is not absent, but it is shaped by scarcity. A fine copper hook, a well-knotted net, a perfectly balanced scraper, a tower cleaned so carefully that its stones gleam—these are admired. Elegance in this place is practical. The most beautiful thing is something that helps keep the level true.

Children are born into the noise and never remember silence. Infants are carried close, bound to the chest or back in hide slings, their first world the smell of oil, skin, wet stone, and the vibration of voices too loud to be soft. They learn to sleep through bird cries, hammer blows, and rushing water. They learn to be still on narrow ledges before they learn to walk far. They are given small tasks early: sorting shells, carrying clay plugs, fetching lamp oil, holding net floats, watching a pool for a rise or fall. Their education is physical and watchful. A child who notices a blocked drain before being told has begun to become useful. A child who can feel the difference between seepage and dangerous flow through a wall has begun to become trusted. They are not raised by isolated families alone. Because the city is crowded and work is continuous, many hands feed, correct, and watch the young. A child may belong first to a household, but it also belongs to the reach of channel it will one day help maintain.

They have no word for forest, no memory of trees, no dream of open green fields. Their imagination is filled with water, stone, crust, shell, bone, lamp flame, bird wing, fish shadow, and the endless texture of wet surfaces. Their stories are not about journeys outward or conquests beyond the basin, because the basin is complete and the surrounding land is not their concern. Their tales are about errors corrected: a gate opened too late, a channel allowed to thicken, a pool rising beyond its mark, a roost tower neglected until birds left, a worker who failed to hear the change in water and brought a near-blockage. These stories are told in gesture more than voice, in reenactments near the channels, in taps and signs that children learn to imitate. Their heroes are not those who change the world, but those who keep it from changing.

Their sense of time is shaped by maintenance rather than seasons. There is no spring planting, no autumn harvest, no winter scarcity. There is only the continuous round of scraping, feeding, opening, closing, cleaning, harvesting, repairing, and watching. Time is measured by how much crust has formed, how full a tank is, how many eggs have been gathered, how often a lamp has been refilled, how many molts the crabs have passed, how many generations of birds have nested in the same tower. They keep records not in books but in gauge marks, notched bones, rearranged pebbles in shallow basins, and the trained bodies of workers. A skilled sluice-keeper can tell you how the water has behaved over many years by the wear on a measuring stone. A bird-tender knows the history of a roost by the layers of whitened crust and the memory of nesting hollows renewed again and again. The past here is not a road leading elsewhere. It is a repeated pattern, carefully kept.

Their rules are strict because the place permits little carelessness. Nothing may be allowed to dry completely. Nothing may accumulate beyond its mark. Nothing may be wasted. These laws are not abstract moral ideals; they are survival. If young stone dries too fast, it cracks. If crust thickens too much, channels narrow. If waste builds up, it fouls the tanks and chokes the fungal beds. Therefore every scrap is returned somewhere useful. Fish entrails feed crabs and fungi. Feather waste is worked into pads, insulation, or curing beds. Bones become tools, pins, levers, needles, hooks, and ornaments. Shells become scrapers, spoons, cups, and building chips. Hides become wraps, straps, membranes for simple drums, and covers for openings. Broken stone is crushed and used as fill. Old copper is reheated, hammered, and reshaped. Guano is gathered for tanning and cultivation. Even spilled water is not truly lost, because the city is built to catch it, guide it, and settle it. Waste, in this place, is only matter in the wrong channel.

The dead are treated according to the same logic. There is no soil for burial, no wood for pyres, no open land where bodies can be laid aside to rest untouched. When a person dies, the basin does not pretend that what was useful has suddenly become useless. Fat is rendered for lamps where possible. Bone may be shaped into tools or ritual objects if the person’s household consents. Skin and sinew are prepared for practical uses when appropriate. The remainder is given to the rendering pools, where fish, crustaceans, insects, and microbial films complete the work of return. This is not cruelty to them. It is the only form of burial consistent with a place that cannot afford to lose matter. To be returned to the basin is to remain part of the maintenance. In some districts, a dead worker’s hand sign is still made at the sluice they tended, not as a haunting but as a reminder that the water continues and the duty continues. The gesture passes from one body to another. That is their form of memory.

Their conflicts are real but narrow. People quarrel over space, over the timing of harvests, over who has first claim to eggs or fish or fresh crust. They argue over blocked channels and careless work. They compete in skill: who can cut the cleanest block of soft sinter, who can keep the most exact level, who can mend a net fastest, who can climb a roost tower without disturbing the birds. But there is little room for war in the ordinary sense. The basin is too enclosed, too crowded, too dependent on cooperation for large-scale violence to be anything but self-mutilation. If a gate is sabotaged, everyone suffers. If a tank is poisoned, everyone starves. If a roost tower is burned, the fire will consume more than the tower. Disputes are settled by councils of workers at the weirs, by measurement, by observation, by the testimony of those who know the channels. Punishment, when it exists, usually takes the form of heavier labor, restricted access to certain resources, or being assigned the dirtiest and most exhausting tasks: scraping deep drains, cleaning foul settling tanks, hauling guano from the highest ledges. Exile is the rarest and most terrible penalty, because outside the basin there is not another life shaped to receive them. To be cast out is to be removed from the only system that makes sense.

Their bodies are marked by this life. Hands are calloused at the palms and fingertips from rope, stone, shell, and scraping. Feet are tough and wide, often scarred by sharp crust. Shoulders are strong from lifting baskets and pulling gates. Skin carries pale mineral streaks and small burns from lamps. Eyes squint against vapor and glare reflected from wet stone. Many have ears that ring or hear selectively; some have lost much of their hearing young and become especially valued for their ability to feel vibration. They do not regard deafness as deficiency. In a city where noise is constant and speech is often secondary, a person who reads water and gesture with exceptional skill may be more trusted than one with sharp ears. Their laughter is loud and sudden, because soft laughter would be lost. Their anger is quick but practical. Their tenderness appears in small acts: oiling a child’s cracked hands, adjusting a sleeping mat away from drip, saving a fallen nestling, sharing a lamp when oil is low, replacing a broken scraper without being asked.

They are not innocent, and they are not gentle in every way. Life is crowded, noisy, and demanding. People shove through narrow passages. They curse in signs. They hoard a favored tool if they think no one sees. They grow tired of the same work, the same damp, the same faces, the same sound pressing into their skulls. But they also know that the city cannot survive on resentment. The basin forces a kind of closeness that makes cruelty expensive. If you harm your neighbor’s sluice, you may flood your own fungal cellar. If you steal eggs from a tower you do not tend, you may anger the people who help you when your net tears. If you let anger delay a repair, the whole reach may pay for it. So their morality is practical before it is philosophical. Good is what keeps the flow right. Evil is what lets the level fail.

Animals, too, have learned the city’s rhythms. Fish gather at certain taps when workers signal feeding time. Birds descend to platforms when harvest baskets appear, because harvest often means scattered larvae and fish scraps. Crabs have learned which stones shift and which hands set traps. Bats adjust their flight paths around newly hung nets and lamps. Frogs remain near ledges where workers allow midges to collect, because the workers know frogs reduce the swarm. Even the small blind bottom-feeders have routes through the channels, moving through familiar turns and settling in familiar hollows. Nothing here is untouched by human habit, and no human habit is untouched by animal life. The city is not a human settlement with animals added. It is a shared apparatus of living.

In the deepest channels, there are older things as well. Not monsters, but creatures that have grown large enough to be noticed: long eels with pale eyes, wide crabs that hide behind weir stones, blind fish that move slowly through the darkest water. Workers know them and leave them mostly alone. They are signs that the deeper system is intact. If they disappear, something has gone wrong below the level of daily sight. If they appear, sluggish but alive, the basin’s hidden circulation is still sound. Children are warned not to reach into deep holes, not because these creatures are malicious, but because they belong to parts of the city where human hands should not go carelessly.

There is also a kind of life that is not individual at all: the life of the city as a whole. It breathes through vapor. It circulates through channels. It feeds through pens, trays, beds, and pools. It sheds crust and grows new crust. It makes noise the way a body makes heat. It repairs itself with human hands and animal habits. It is not conscious in the way a person is conscious, but it behaves like a vast, wet, deaf, patient organism made of many organisms. The people are its mind only in the sense that they notice and correct. The birds are its voice only in the sense that their cries fill the air. The fish are its inner movement. The insects are its weather. The microbial films are its skin. The stone is not dead; it is always being made and unmade, shaped and scraped, precipitated and cut. The city lives because it is maintained, and it is maintained because it lives.

If you stand long enough in one of its narrow passages, you begin to see that every surface is occupied. A lamp burns in a hollow. A net hangs from bone hooks. A basket wedged into a niche holds sorted shells. A child crouches nearby, watching a pool rise toward a mark. A woman passes with a clay plug under one arm, her hand brushing the wall as if asking it a question. Above, birds shift on a ledge whitened by their own droppings. Below, something moves in the dark water, maybe a fish, maybe an eel, maybe only the flow itself. Steam hangs in the air. The noise presses against your chest. You cannot hear your own breathing. You cannot hear the small sounds that would, in another place, mark solitude. Here there is no solitude. Even the walls are lived on. Even the ceilings sweat with use. Even the light is crowded, reflected and multiplied by wet stone and oil flame until it seems to rise from the city rather than fall into it.

Whoever lives there is therefore not merely a people, though people are central. It is a dense community of workers and tenders, children and elders, fishers and scrapers, bird-keepers and sluice-watchers, all bound to a single relentless task: keep the basin as it is. Whatever lives there is even larger: film, grub, snail, crab, fish, frog, bat, bird, eel, fungus, bacteria, and the human hands that move among them. They are all citizens of the same wet equilibrium.

And if one had to name the truest inhabitant, after all the others had been counted, it would not be a single creature at all. It would be the maintenance itself—the living pattern of levels kept, crust scraped, channels opened, waste returned, eggs gathered, lamps trimmed, gestures repeated, water guided, and stone renewed. That is what lives in the basin. That is what breathes in the noise and vapor. That is what has no future form because it is already complete: a wet, deaf, crowded, mineral body, always being made, always being tended, always remaining exactly what it is.
