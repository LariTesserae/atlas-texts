What lives there lives sparingly, and almost never in the center.

The first life is the green margin. It does not burst, does not climb, does not thicken into jungle. It gathers where the manufactured surfaces have grown gentle enough to hold a film of water: in the seams between tiles, along the lowest courses of the enameled walls, in the shaded recesses where condensation gathers and moves no farther. The mosses are the oldest-looking of these. They fill the joints between floor plates in soft, dark bands, dense as stitched caulking, their tiny leaves so saturated they seem less grown than precipitated from the room’s wet air. They are not bright green but a deep, patient green, the color of something that has learned not to demand light. They do not rustle. They do not sway. They simply thicken in their narrow places, holding moisture against metal, softening the room’s hard seams into something almost flesh-like.

Lower down, where the wall meets the floor and the water weeps most steadily, the liverworts cling in glossy patches. They are sleek and rounded, like small wet organs, their surfaces shining under the plain white lamps. Each bead of condensation that slides downward is caught, split, delayed, and passed along them. They look less like plants than like the room’s own lining brought to the surface, a tissue that has learned to photosynthesize. In some places they overlap in smooth, damp layers, but they never spread far. They keep to the margin as if they know the center belongs to emptiness and sound.

The narrow strap-leaved plants live a little above the waterline, rooted in metal grilles where old channels once allowed access to pipe or valve. Their roots hang into the humid air, pale and thread-fine, beaded with moisture. Their leaves arch outward and down, not reaching for a sun that does not change, only existing in the same steady illumination. They are restrained plants, spare in shape, with no showy flowers and no dry litter. When one of their leaves ages, it does not crisp or fall; it softens, darkens, and dissolves into the film of water below, returning its substance to the closed circulation of the chamber. They give the room a sense of cultivation without a gardener, a planted look that no hand still maintains.

In the central pool, life is even more minimal. Duckweed floats in scattered lenses, small green disks resting on the trembling water. They do not form a continuous carpet. They remain few, separated, as if their number has been fixed by the room’s long equilibrium. Beneath them, algae paints the submerged tile with a faint emerald bloom, soft and slippery, brightening the pale floor where the lamps catch it. This is the pool’s pasture, thin but sufficient. It is life reduced to a skin, a film, a wash of color over worn manufacture.

And beneath even that visible green there is another life, too small to see: the microbial layer, the invisible threads and colonies that live in the mineral film, in the condensation, in the faint nutrient trace left by centuries of weeping walls. They are the chamber’s true metabolism. They take what little falls, what ages, what softens, and turn it back into the room’s closed weather. They do not make dirt, because there is no soil here. They make only a faint slickness, a living slick on tile and alloy, a biological polish that keeps the surfaces from becoming sterile without letting them become foul. They are life as maintenance, life as quiet chemistry, life with no visible body.

Animal life is almost absent.

The room permits only traces. The most evident is the snail. It is small, pale, its shell the color of wet ceramic or old bone. It moves along the inside surface of the pool with a patience that seems unrelated to time. Its body is nearly translucent when it extends, and its passage leaves a bright, fleeting track on the algal film, a line that soon fills in again. It grazes without urgency. It does not seem to seek anything, because everything it needs is already near. It may be the only one of its kind in the chamber; if there are others, they never reveal themselves. Its solitude does not feel tragic. It feels fitted to the place. It is one life allowed where no abundance is required.

Sometimes there is a moth.

It rests near one of the caged lamps, wings flat and damp, as if it has accepted the impossibility of flight in air so thick and still. Its color is not bright: a washed gray or faint mineral beige, easily mistaken for condensation-stained metal. It may remain motionless for so long that it seems part of the lamp housing, a small accidental stain given the shape of wings. Only rarely does it stir, one wing lifting a fraction, then settling again. It does not flutter against the light. It does not beat itself against the cage. It has no frenzy. If it feeds at all, it does so in a way too slight to observe. If it lives, it lives as a pause in the roar, a tiny folded presence near the only warmth that is not simply the room’s own breath.

Nothing here hunts. Nothing swarms. Nothing calls. The animal world has been pared down to a single slow mouth on the pool wall and a damp wing near the light. Even that feels generous.

But if one asks what truly lives in the chamber, the answer begins to widen.

The sound lives there.

It is not alive in the way moss is alive or the snail is alive, but it behaves like an inhabitant. It occupies the whole volume. It presses against the walls, the floor, the water, the body of anything that enters. It has no shape, yet it gives the room its shape. It has no lungs, yet it seems to breathe through the hidden machinery beyond the panels. It makes the pool tremble in fine, ceaseless patterns. It enters the chest and stays there. It erases footsteps, swallows breath, removes the possibility of speech. It is the chamber’s weather, its pressure, its constant social fact. To live there is to live inside it. The plants tolerate it. The snail carries it in its shell. The moth holds it in its folded wings. Any being that remains there long enough must stop treating the roar as noise and begin treating it as atmosphere.

In that sense, the room itself lives. Not as a body, but as a system that has achieved its final posture. It breathes water. It keeps temperature. It holds light. It maintains its surfaces through condensation, mineral bloom, and the slow work of microscopic life. It has no future, but it has presence. It is not dead. It is finished. And finished things can still be alive if their aliveness is no longer aimed at becoming something else.

Yet there is also a whoever.

Not exactly a person, perhaps. Or a person only in the way a last function can become a person.

There is a figure that lives there, if “lives” is still the right word for a presence so quiet it has nearly merged with the room’s equilibrium. It stays near the margin, where the floor’s shallow slope begins to gather water, seated or crouched against one of the ribbed supports, still enough to be mistaken for a bundle of abandoned clothing or a shadow cast by the lamp cages. It is humanoid, but not comfortably so. Its outline has been worn by humidity and sound into something less individual. It wears what might once have been maintenance garments: a heavy coat, an apron, boots, gloves, perhaps all of it originally practical, all of it now softened, darkened, furred at the cuffs and seams with green. The fabric no longer looks worn by work. It looks grown into the room. Moss threads through its folds. Mineral bloom pale as rust outlines its creases. It does not smell of rot. It smells of warm water, metal, and leaves.

Its skin is pale, not sickly but lightless, adapted to a place where illumination is steady but never sunlike. It is damp to the look, as if condensation has become part of its surface. Its hair lies flat. Its hands rest open on its knees or on the floor, palms upward or downward depending on some impulse too slow to notice. The fingers are smooth, the nails worn, the skin unlined in the way of things constantly bathed. There is no tremor in them, though the floor trembles. There is no tension in the shoulders, though the noise is enormous. The figure has ceased to resist the roar. It inhabits it the way deep water inhabits pressure.

Its eyes are open. They do not shine with fear or expectation. They are the eyes of one who has watched the same bead of water enlarge, slip, and fall so many times that patience is no longer a virtue but a physical state. The light in them is plain. They do not search the room. They receive it. They follow condensation threads down the panels, the faint shivering of the pool, the slow grazing track of the snail, the motionless moth near the lamp. If something changes, and almost nothing changes, this figure notices. If nothing changes, it notices that too. Its attention has become the chamber’s inner sense of itself.

It does not speak. Speech is impossible there, but even if the roar ceased, one suspects it would not speak immediately. Its mouth has forgotten urgency. It breathes through the thick air without effort, drawing in the warm moisture as though that were enough. Perhaps it is. It does not seem to eat in any ordinary way. If it takes sustenance, it may be from the algal film, from condensation gathered in the palm, from the faint mineral sweetness of the walls, or from the sound itself, which fills the room so completely that hunger might become a misunderstanding. It does not look starved. It does not look fed. It looks sustained by the fact that the place continues.

This attendant — for that is the word that rises, though no work remains — does not repair anything. There is nothing to repair. It does not oil valves, tighten bolts, clear drains, or monitor gauges. Those functions belong to the earlier life of the industrial body around it, the life before the room reached its old stable state. Yet the posture of care remains. The figure still bends slightly toward the room. Still listens. Still keeps watch over a system that no longer needs intervention. Its labor has become attention. Its duty has become presence. It tends the chamber by not disturbing it.

Sometimes it moves.

When it does, the movement is slow enough to be mistaken for a shift in shadow. It may rise from the wall and walk the perimeter, not pacing, not patrolling, only tracing the green edge with the patience of water. It stops where a liverwort patch glistens more thickly. It crouches where a rivulet has spread into a fine sheet along the floor. It may lift a hand and let condensation collect on its fingers, then allow the droplet to fall back. It does not test the water as if measuring it. It touches it as one touches a familiar face. It may stand for a long time above the central pool, watching the duckweed tremble under the roar. It does not disturb the lenses. It does not cast a stone, because there are no stones. It does not sigh, because the sound would be erased. It simply stands, and the room reflects itself in its stillness.

If you were to enter, it would not be startled. The roar would have already swallowed your arrival. It would turn its head perhaps, or only its eyes, and regard you without alarm. You would be an interruption, but not an enemy. You would be another temporary body in a place that has little use for bodies. It would not ask why you came. It would not offer guidance. It might watch the water on your clothes, the way moisture gathers on you and then becomes part of the room’s economy. It might wait for you to realize that there is nothing to do there. If you stayed long enough, it might come to seem that the figure is not one being but the chamber’s capacity for witness gathered into a shape: the moss, the snail, the moth, the sound, the lamps, the warm water, all compressed into a form that can sit against a wall and breathe.

It is not lonely. Loneliness would require a missing other, a sense of absence extending through time. This place has no such extension. It does not remember the past as loss or imagine the future as arrival. The attendant shares that quality. It does not long for the machinery to fall silent, because silence is not its opposite. It does not wait for rescue, because there is nothing outside that would make the room more complete. It does not hope for rain, because the ceiling already weeps. It does not miss the sun, because the lamps are steady and sufficient. It lives, if it lives, in a perpetual present made of moisture, pressure, green edges, and endless mechanical roar.

So what lives there?

The moss lives there, in seams and margins. The liverworts live there, drinking from walls. The strap-leaved plants live there, rooted in grilles and dissolving their own dead leaves into the film. Duckweed lives there, sparse and floating. Algae lives there, thin as paint. Invisible microbes live there, turning the room’s smallest excesses back into balance. A pale snail lives there, grazing the pool’s interior. A single moth lives there, folded near the light. The sound lives there, total and formless. The water lives there, not alive itself but the medium of all life. And the attendant lives there, or remains there, or persists there: a quiet figure of worn maintenance and humid silence, watching the room remain itself.

None of them live as a community. They live as parts of a held note. They are not an ecosystem in the ordinary sense, because there is no abundance, no predation, no seasonal turn, no reproductive excess. They are a final arrangement. A minimal living inside a completed machine. A green edge around an empty center. A trace of animal patience. A presence that may once have been human and is now something closer to a function: to remain, to witness, to keep the room company with its own stillness.

And because the place does not progress, what lives there does not need to become more. The moss does not dream of covering the walls. The snail does not seek a mate. The moth does not long for the lamp to open. The attendant does not await an end to the roar. They have all been smoothed into the room’s oldest stable state. They live the way water condenses: continuously, quietly, without decision.

What lives there lives in equilibrium.

That is enough. In that hidden basin of worn metal and tile, under ordinary light, inside a deafening stillness, it is enough.
