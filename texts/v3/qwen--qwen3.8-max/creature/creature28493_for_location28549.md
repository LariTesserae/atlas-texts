What lives there cannot be answered with a single creature. The first and largest inhabitant is the place itself: the high, cold, plant-made basin as a living condition. It is not a landscape occupied by organisms so much as an organism that has become landscape. The ground, walls, terraces, towers, hollows, and channels are not merely where life happens; they are life arranged at geological scale. If one must say who lives there, the truest answer begins with that: the valley lives there.

But there are also others.

They are rare, quiet, and difficult to classify in ordinary terms. Most are not animals in the usual sense. A few are. The greater inhabitants are closer to mobile processes, living organs of the plant-body, knots of vegetation that have folded themselves into coherence and can move, maintain, adjust, and — in a slow, distributed way — know.

The first of the lesser inhabitants are the pale faunas, almost accidental in their scarcity. They are not numerous enough to form a visible animal world. Here and there inside hollow stems live translucent, wormlike insects with bodies so narrow they seem made from the same capillary tissue as the plants themselves. They move by contracting and extending in faint pulses, feeding on dead filaments, excess spore-dust, or the thin films of cold sap that collect where pressure valves leak. On open moss there may be a wingless, bark-colored form no larger than a thumb, flat and quick in its brief crossings between crevices. It does not sing, does not call, does not leave tracks that last. It vanishes as if the living surface has swallowed it.

These creatures are not inhabitants in the way birds inhabit a forest or herds inhabit a plain. They are minor functions made visible: cleaners, carriers, occasional transfer agents between separated growths. They do not dominate the place. They do not even seem to belong to it as separate lives. They are more like small, wandering organs that the valley permits.

The beings one might more seriously call the inhabitants are something else.

They are best described as the Slow Keepers, though they do not keep anything in the sense of owning or ruling. They are mobile plant-forms, not quite individuals, not quite colonies, and not animals despite their capacity for motion. They arise within the dense living substrate where pressure, moisture, cold, and growth have braided certain tissues into self-maintaining shapes. They are made of the same material as the country around them: root, stem, moss, hardened leaf, pale vascular tubes, resinous cores, and frost-edged membranes. To see one is not immediately to recognize it as a living being separate from the terrain. It may look at first like a moving thicket, a detached root-mass, a knot of vines loosened by frost-heave. Only when it pauses, or when it responds to some disturbance in the plant-body, does the mind begin to understand that it is being watched by something without eyes.

A Slow Keeper has no fixed shape, but most tend toward a rough verticality when moving. Some are low and spreading, barely taller than the moss, broad as a fallen plate of root. Others rise to shoulder height, or slightly higher, when they have drawn themselves into a column for crossing a difficult stretch of terrain. Their central mass is usually a bundle of hollow and semi-hollow tubes, some dead and hardened, others living and supple, through which air, vapor, sap, and chemical signals pass in slow alternation. Around this core are braided root-limbs, not legs in the true sense but anchoring structures capable of loosening, extending, gripping, and retying themselves. They move by unbraiding and rebraiding, by leaning and setting, by sending part of themselves ahead and then drawing the rest after. It is less walking than deliberate reattachment.

Their outer surfaces are layered. Broad, overlapping leaf-scales, dark green or silvered with frost, shield the inner tissues. Beneath these are spongy mats of moss-like growth that hold moisture and buffer cold. In older ones, the outer layers have become compressed into something resembling weathered bark or laminated leather, ridged with old frost-lines and healed splits. New growth appears pale at the joints: ivory, faint green, or the color of cold sap. Frost clings to their upper edges. When they move, tiny beads of condensation tremble along their tubes and fall into the surrounding plant-mass. They smell of wet cellulose, resin, ice, and the sharp clean odor of electrified green tissue.

They have no faces. Where one might expect a face, there is instead a crown or rosette of pores, membranes, and fine filaments. These open and close with changes in humidity and temperature. They are sensory surfaces, not eyes or mouths. A Slow Keeper knows the world through gradients: moisture, heat, pressure, chemical trace, vibration through the living substrate. It can feel a frost-fracture several terraces away because the tremor passes through connected tissue. It can sense the breath of a traveler as a small warm plume of water vapor disturbing the cold air. It can detect where a channel is blocked, where a membrane is about to burst, where a growth-front has become too dense and must be relieved.

Their thought, if it can be called thought, is not quick. It is hydraulic, chemical, and structural. A decision among them is not a flash but a redistribution. Fluids shift. Membranes open. Heat gathers in one part of the body and withdraws from another. Signals move through their internal tubes and into the surrounding vegetation. What would be impulse in an animal becomes, in them, a slow tilt toward action. They can respond rapidly by the standards of trees, but not by the standards of animals. To a human observer, their hesitation may look like stillness. Their deliberation may look like dormancy. But they are not dormant. They are processing the valley’s condition through their own bodies.

They do not eat as animals eat. They have no mouths, no teeth, no digestive hollows in the ordinary sense. They sustain themselves by tapping into the valley’s circulation. When a Slow Keeper rests, it often roots its lower limbs into a channel or depression where moisture and nutrients move. Fine root-hairs spread into the living surface. It draws cold sap, condensed vapor, dissolved sugars, and mineral traces carried through the plant-body. Some of its outer surfaces can absorb mist directly. In clearer light, certain panels of its body may photosynthesize weakly, though the cold and diffuse daylight make this a minor supplement rather than a primary source of energy. More important are the heat-pockets it maintains inside itself: resinous nodes where chemical activity generates enough warmth to keep fluids moving and prevent internal freezing.

This internal warmth gives them a subtle presence. If you stand near one, you may feel not heat exactly, but a lessening of the cold, as if the air around it has been gently persuaded. Frost retreats from its core faster than from surrounding leaves. In deep cold, faint threads of vapor rise from its vents. When it exhales, the sound is almost nothing: a soft pressure change, a whisper through wet tubes, a settling of leaf-scales.

The Slow Keepers live by maintaining. That is their role in the valley, if role is the right word for something that emerged without design. They are not gardeners with hands. They do not carry tools. They do not cut, build, stack, or assemble. They maintain with their own bodies. When a moisture channel becomes blocked by frozen tissue, one may press itself against the obstruction and warm it until the blockage loosens. When a pressure chamber threatens to rupture, several may gather around it and absorb the stress, their own tubes swelling and contracting to equalize the load.
