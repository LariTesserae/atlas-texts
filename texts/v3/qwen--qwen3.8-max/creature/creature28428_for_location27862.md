What lives there lives as maintenance.

At first, the dark seems to refuse inhabitants. There is only the black water, the cool pressure, the sense of vast surfaces waiting beyond the reach of sight. Then the basin begins to disclose its lives not as shapes but as disturbances: a faint blue-green thread drifting past, a click arriving from nowhere, a slow displacement of water against the skin, a vibration in the chest before the ear understands it as sound. Life here is not announced by movement across a landscape. It is announced by changes in the medium.

The smallest residents are almost too slight to be called creatures. They are drifting filaments, membranes, and pale threads carried in the suspended plant-matter of the water. Some are no larger than a hair; others trail for meters as translucent ribbons, their edges giving off brief phosphorescent glows when currents alter. They feed on spores, dissolved tannins, shed cells, and the fine organic snow that never ceases to fall upward, downward, and sideways through the basin’s interior. They are not beautiful in any ornamental sense. They are functional as breath. When they shine, they do not illuminate the world. They only prove that something is passing through it.

More substantial are the eyeless swimmers. They move through the open reaches in loose, scattered bodies, not schooling so much as sharing a direction for a while. Their forms are long and flexible, their fins drawn out into dark streamers that feel the water more than push it. They have no eyes, or if any ancestral trace of eyes remains, it has become a patch of pressure-sensitive tissue buried beneath the skin. In place of sight, they possess lateral systems of extraordinary delicacy: branching canals along flanks, heads, and fins, able to read the shape of distant walls by the way a finbeat returns altered.
