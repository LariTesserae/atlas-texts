The mosses are the first inhabitants, or the first organ of whoever lives there. They do not grow toward any sun; they grow toward warmth, moisture, and the faint mineral exhalation of the stone. They form a continuous pelt over the basin floor, over benches, over the lower rims, so that the whole hollow seems lined with the inside of some dark, patient animal. Under the hand, the moss is warmer than the air, and when pressed it gives back a scent of slate, iron, and deep water. It is not silent. It muffles, but it also listens: every step sends a slow tremor through its wet threads, and the tremor is carried inward, toward something that waits.

Within this pelt move the lesser lives: blind mites round as seeds, pale worms that swim through the surface film as if through open air, fungal cords so fine they seem to be the moss’s own nerves. They are not numerous in the way of fertile places; they are exact, each one placed into the economy of damp.
