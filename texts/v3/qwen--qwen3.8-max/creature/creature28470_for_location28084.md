What lives in the High Sward is not one thing, nor even a handful of things, but a finished congregation.

At first the eye reports emptiness: light, distance, the long warm plain. Then the plain begins to resolve into inhabitants, the way a held note resolves into many voices. Some are small enough to be mistaken for texture. Some are so slow they seem like weathering. Some move only in the air. Some live almost entirely as sound. And among them, in their quiet way, move the people who have learned to belong there.

The first and greatest resident is the sward itself.

It is not merely ground covered with life. It is life become ground. The whole high tableland is a single ancient vegetation, folded and folded over itself until it has made a world out of root and fiber and seed. Its upper surface is a close nap of living blades, mats, and low fronds, tough and springy, warmed by the sun. Beneath that is the felt: a depth of interlocked rhizomes, shed skins of leaves, buried seed-cases, resin beads, and fine pale filaments that are neither wholly root nor wholly fungus. Deeper still the felt becomes darker, sweeter, and more compressed, holding the memory of every season that has ever fallen into it.

The sward is not conscious in the way a head is conscious, but it is aware in the way a very old body is aware. It feels pressure. It knows where it has been torn. It responds to rain with a slow, continent-wide relaxation. It keeps warmth long after sunset, and at night the hollows breathe out a green, fermented smell, like fruit and grass and wine held together in the dark.

Within this living ground live the small makers of the noise.

The constant high drone comes from millions of root-stridulators: tiny, blind, warm-bodied things no larger than a fingernail, armored in hardened seed-cases and living in galleries within the upper felt. They rub roughened limbs against lignified fibers, and the sound of them doing this rises together until it becomes the plain’s own note. They do not call to one another in any simple sense. Their sound is more like the sound of a vast metabolism, a sign that the sward is working.

Where the ground sinks into shallow basins and rain collects, the noise changes. These pools are not rocky ponds but soft green bowls, their bottoms made of living root-skin, their edges fringed with hollow stems. In them live the clickers—flat, translucent creatures with broad paddles and shells of polished plant-mineral. They feed on fine filaments and shed spores, and they make their clicking by striking hollow seed-cases against one another, either as courtship or as territorial warning. In the warm hours after rain, when the pools are newly full and thin-winged larvae rise from them, the clicking becomes a crackling fire.

The reedy whistle comes from the whistle-stems themselves. They look like pale green pipes rising from the margins of the pools, but each is a colony of small organisms fitted together into a living tube. From the mouth of each tube extend soft fringes that catch drifting spores, dust, and tiny winged things. When wind passes over them, the stems sing. The sound is empty and sweet, and from a distance it can seem like someone calling across a field.

Then there are the burstwings.

They rest flush with the sward, folded into the color and texture of seed-cases or dried leaf-sheaths, so that a traveler may walk for hours and see nothing but level ground. But when a cloud-shadow crosses, or when the pressure of the air changes before rain, they rise all at once. The plain seems to open. Thousands of them lift into the light: small, quick beings with broad, pale wings veined like grass, their bodies slender and warm-colored underneath. They feed on pollen, resin droplets, and the soft young knots of the sward. Their sudden flights are one of the plain’s most ordinary astonishments.

Larger life moves with more patience.

The most common of the great walkers are the corkbacks. From a distance they can be mistaken for low hummocks, because their broad backs are overgrown with living sward. Mossy fibers take hold in the crevices of their shells. Seedlings sprout there after rain. Their bodies are rounded, warm, and slow, supported on thick legs with wide, padded feet that spread their weight over the springy ground. They have no horns, no claws, no hard shining armor. Their protection is mass, quiet, and resemblance to the land itself.

A corkback grazes by pressing a wide, flexible mouth against the sward and lifting the upper growth with a rasping motion, cropping just enough to open the felt without tearing it. This is important. Without the corkbacks, the sward would thicken, smother its own lower layers, and grow sour in places. With them, the plain remains open, breathing, and varied. Their dung is not waste so much as a traveling nursery: warm pellets of chewed fiber, half-digested seed, and living filament, which break down quickly and become new felt.

They sleep standing. They gather in loose herds, not from fear but from preference, and their deep bodies add a lower note to the plain’s sound—a slow groaning and shifting, like wood settling in sunlight. When they move, the ground seems to rearrange itself gently around them. A traveler who has never seen one before may step onto what he thinks is a rise and feel it breathe.

In the hollows and shaded folds of the sward live the thatch-stalkers. They are hunters, but soft ones: low, many-legged, patient creatures with backs like compressed leaf-litter and bodies so flexible they can flatten into the felt. They do not chase. They wait beneath loose thatch or beside pool margins, spinning short threads of sticky resin from glands near their mouths. When burstwings or other small fliers come low, they lift themselves with a sudden fluid motion and cast the threads upward. Their prey is not killed with venom but smothered, then carried down into the sward where it is shared among the young.

The thatch-stalkers keep the smaller populations from becoming too thick, and they also perform a kind of gardening without knowing it. Their hidden paths through the felt remain open, making channels for air and water. The Softstep people watch for their movements, because a place where thatch-stalkers thrive is usually a place where the sward is healthy but beginning to close over itself.

Above all these lives, in the clean high air, move the bladderkites.

They are among the strangest and most beloved beings of the High Sward. A bladderkite is not a bird, though it flies. It is a light-bodied aerial creature, part animal, part living sail, with a translucent gas-bladder swollen by fermentation and heat. From this bladder hang trailing rootlets, fine as hair, and below them a soft central body with slow-moving mouths and wide sensory fringes. In the afternoon, when the plain warms and columns of rising air form under the cumulus, bladderkites ascend. Some are small as lanterns. Some are broad enough to cast a shadow over a walking band.

They feed on airborne spores, loose filaments, tiny winged things, and the fine organic dust that rises from the sward. They do not swoop. They drift with immense deliberation, adjusting their bladders and trailing filaments like nets. Their sound is a hollow, reedy sigh, very different from the ground-drone. When several pass overhead together, the plain seems to answer them with a lift of warmer air.

Sometimes, after high winds, a bladderkite will descend too far or become heavy with rain. It settles on the sward and lies there for a time, folded and breathing, while the ground slowly grows around its trailing filaments. If it recovers, it rises again at the next warm day. If it does not, the sward takes it in. Within a few seasons there is only a slightly raised oval of richer growth, and the Softstep people mark such places as sky-gifts.

Then there are the Softstep themselves.

They are the ones who have made the High Sward into a home without making it into something else.

To see them from far off, one might think they are tall, thin people moving in single file across the plain. To see them near is to understand that they belong to the place in the way the burstwings and corkbacks do. They are slender but not fragile, with deep chests for the thin air and long legs that move with an unhurried swing. Their feet are broad, their soles thick and extraordinarily sensitive, capable of reading the ground through pressure, temperature, and vibration. Their toes are long enough to part the sward without wounding it.

Their skin is warm, usually green-brown, umber, or faded gold, depending on band and season, and often marked with faint traceries of paler lines where the sun has healed small scratches into patterns. Their hair is fine and fibrous, more like a soft mane of dried grass than ordinary hair, and in elders it may be threaded with living filaments or tiny seed-buds that have taken root there. They wear little clothing in the warm daylight: woven capes of felt-fiber for night, belts of braided root, pouches of hardened seed-shell. Their ornaments are almost always sound-makers: small rattles, polished seed-beads, resin beads, whistles carved from hollow stems.

They are called the Softstep by outsiders because of their way of walking. Among themselves, their name is not exactly spoken. It is a rhythm: two light footfalls, a pause, and a low hum from the chest. A person’s true name is something you feel through the ground before you hear it in the air.

They live in small bands, usually no more than a few dozen, because the High Sward is generous but not endless beneath heavy use. They move often, but not as fugitives. They follow the health of the plain. They go where the pools need opening, where the corkbacks have left too much thatch, where seed-cases must be carried to warm hollows, where the felt has thinned and needs to be encouraged back together. Their paths are not roads. They leave almost no mark.

Their camps are grown more than built. A band arriving at a resting place will spend the first hours walking it, pressing the ground with bare feet, listening. Then they persuade the sward. They do not dig. They do not cut deep. They part the upper blades, encourage low walls of living fiber to rise a little, shape shallow sleeping hollows by weight and repetition. A camp is a series of soft depressions, wind-lips, and listening circles, all made of the plain itself. At dawn it looks like a place where the ground has briefly dreamed of people. By evening, after the band has moved on, it is almost ordinary again.

They use no fire except in tiny, contained ways, and even then rarely. Fire is the great danger of the High Sward, the one thing that could turn the living plain into ash and memory. Instead they use heat already given: sun-warmed resin, fermenting compost, the living warmth of the ground, and sealed seed-vessels in which food softens and sours into digestible forms. Their tools are made of hardened seed-cases, polished resin, bone from shed corkback plates, and fibers twisted to strengths that would surprise a lowland smith. They have no metal, no quarried stone, no permanent walls. This is not poverty. It is adaptation.

Their speech is layered. In air, it consists of whistles, clicks, soft consonants, and vowels that carry well across open distance. Beneath that, it has a ground-language: foot-taps, heel-presses, and the striking of resonant staffs against the sward. A staff is not merely a walking aid. It is a voice extended downward. A practiced listener can send a message through the felt for miles, and can tell not only who is coming but from what direction, in what mood, and with how many companions. Children learn this before they learn to speak aloud. Many adults can stand still on a quiet afternoon and “hear” the plain for leagues: the passage of a corkback herd, the filling of a pool, the settling of a thatch-stalker, the slow creak of a distant blister of ground warming in the sun.

They eat with great attentiveness. They harvest young shoots, resin nodules, sweet seed-paste, pool-eggs, edible filaments, and the occasional burstwing or small water-creature. They do not hunt corkbacks except in rare ritual necessity, and even then they approach the animal with songs and apologies, because the corkbacks are understood as fellow gardeners. The Softstep know that if they take too much from one place, the plain will not strike back; it will simply grow quieter. That quiet is more feared than punishment.
