Instead, the living are fed by heat and mineral memory.

The first life in the room is not a creature with a face or a will, but a skin. It grows wherever the water can touch the built stone: along seams between tiles, in the shadowed lips of niches, across the broad worn terraces, inside the mouths of the southern channels where warmth enters with its slow, chalky taste. These are microbial films, old and patient, thinner than breath and tougher than fired clay. They eat what the room gives them: iron leached from dull staples, lime from pale brick, sulfur from deep water, salts from bone pins, heat from the mountain’s hidden veins. They do not need sunlight. They have no green in them. Their life is chemical and quiet, a metabolism more like rusting than growing, more like settling than striving.

The foxfire along certain seams is theirs. It is not a lantern made for seeing. It is a leakage, a faint biological accident, the visible edge of warmth becoming something almost visible. To any eye adapted to the dark, it gives the room a false softness: a suggestion of outline, a rumor of edge. The microbes do not shine to guide. They shine because they are alive, and because the water is heavy with what they live on.

Upon that living skin graze the small armored things.

They come in shapes that seem to have been sanded by centuries. Pale crabs, no larger than a thumb or a palm, move over the floor with a deliberation that makes their ticking sound enormous in the hush. Their shells are not bright or ornate. They have the color of wet ash, of milked stone, of old copper rubbed down to a ghost of itself. The edges of their carapaces are worn round, as if each one has spent its life fitting itself against tile and niche until it became part of the room’s finish. Their claws are blunt, not made for tearing so much as scraping, lifting, testing. When one pauses and taps a claw against the floor, the sound is small, precise, almost ceremonial.

There are limpet-shapes too, though not called limpets by anyone now, flat-bodied grazers that adhere to wall and terrace with muscular mouths. They crop the microbial films so slowly that their feeding is less like eating and more like polishing. Behind them, the stone is left cleaner and faintly slick. Some of these creatures have shells of mineralized mucus, layered over generations until they resemble little ceramic caps. Others have no shell at all, only tough skin the color of old bone, and they hide in shallow cups where the water current carries them dissolved food.

In the channels live the filterers.

They are among the room’s quiet engineers. Bivalves with shells like worn coins sit half-buried in the grooves, their mouths opened just enough to draw the weak current through them. They taste the water continuously. They take in bacteria, fine mineral floc, shed skin, dissolved organic threads, whatever the circulation brings. When disturbed, they close with a soft click that becomes, in the chamber’s acoustics, the settling of a stone valve. Some have lived so long in one place that their shells have become continuous with the channel walls, edged by mineral deposit and microbial crust until it is difficult to say where animal ends and architecture begins.

Other filterers are softer. Tube-dwellers anchor themselves in the corners of niches and along the lower terraces, their bodies sealed into narrow cases of cemented grit. From the mouths of these tubes extend pale feeding crowns, not leaves, not fronds of any green thing, but animal structures, fine as hair and sensitive as fingertips.
