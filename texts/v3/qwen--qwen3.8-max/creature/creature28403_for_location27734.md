What lives there does not live as a single population or a simple tribe of creatures. It lives as a pressure, a chord, an accumulation of small hungers and large patiences. The first thing any newcomer would notice is not a body, but the weight of sound: the world is already occupied long before one sees its occupants.

The smallest lives are the most numerous. They fill the seams, pores, hollows, and hidden channels of the plant-made terrain. Insects and insect-like creatures are the first architects of noise and the first engineers of decay. They rasp inside bark furrows, chew through dried leaf-leather, mine the pith of old branch joints, and nest in the walls of hollow internodes. Some are no larger than grains of pollen and live in vast colonies along the shaded undersides of broad leaf platforms. Others are long-bodied, glossy with resin, and armed with mandibles capable of cutting fibrous wood. Their sounds are not incidental; they are constant. The clicking, ticking, stridulating, and drumming of countless small bodies forms the base layer of the landscape’s roar.

Many of these creatures use the terrain as an instrument. A beetle may strike the inside of a dried seed-case with its head, making the whole shell ring like a dull bell. A winged thing may drag its legs across a ribbed bark ridge, turning a small territorial call into a rasping noise that carries across open heat-shimmer. Larvae chew in synchronized bursts inside dead heartwood, producing a grinding rhythm that can be heard from a distance as a low, living abrasion. Some moths or moth-like fliers do not sing at all; instead, they beat their wings against taut membranes of bark, creating percussive flutters that ripple through nearby hollows.

Swarming life here is not merely abundant but meteorological. At certain hours, or in response to flowering pulses, clouds of pollinators rise from hidden places and move across the tableland like weather. They may be tiny, iridescent, and fast, or broad-winged and slow, drifting on thermals between the spaced giants. When such a swarm descends on a flowering platform, the noise becomes physical. The air thickens with wingbeat. Light dims in patches. The scent of pollen, warm sap, and fermented fruit becomes almost suffocating. For a short time, the swarm is the dominant fact of the place: a moving, humming mass that makes even larger animals still and wait.

The larger fauna is less continuously visible but more startling when it appears. Because the great plant forms stand far apart, animals often seem to materialize from emptiness. One moment a bark plain lies open under the sun; the next, a line of grazers crosses a ridge, their bodies low and broad, mouths working over tough leaf mats. These grazers are patient, heat-tolerant creatures, shaped for slow feeding on fibrous surfaces. Some have wide, suctioning feet that grip polished bark. Others have blunt grinding plates instead of teeth, or flexible lips that strip leaf-felt without tearing living tissue too deeply. They tend to move in processions, not because they are highly social in the way of pack animals, but because the safest routes and best feeding surfaces are ancient and repeated.

Climbers and bridge-walkers live along the root platforms and fallen trunk-forms. They are long-limbed, cautious, and highly aware of resonance. A false step on a hollow stem can send a boom through a territory, attracting predators or provoking rivals. These creatures test surfaces constantly, tapping with claws or forelimbs before committing weight. Some have pads that can spread their mass over fragile leaf-leather. Others have hook-like digits for catching the rough edges of buttress roots. They nest in crevices, in the crotches of roots, or inside hollows they widen over generations. Their young are often carried in mouth-pouches or bound to the body with sticky secretions until they can grip.

Flying, gliding, and falling creatures move through the upper air. Bird-like forms dart between crown-spines, but they are not delicate in the way of many lowland birds. They need strong chests and tough wings to handle the thermal arguments of the tableland. Some are broad and buoyant, riding warm columns while scanning for insects, fruit, or carrion. Others are faster and harsher, with hooked beaks or serrated mouths and eyes adapted to seeing movement through glare and haze. Bat-like creatures occupy the hollow internodes, emerging at dusk in noisy curtains. Their calls add a layer of slurred clicks and squeaking echoes to the evening chorus. Insect-like gliders, too numerous to count, drift from crown to crown on membranes that may be evolved wings or modified body-flaps, depending on the lineage.

Predators live inside the noise rather than outside it. They cannot rely on silence alone, because the place is never silent. Instead, they hunt by pattern-breaking. A predator here listens for the wrong rhythm: the sudden cessation of a local chorus, the panicked vibration of prey, the disturbance of a seed-case, the too-quick scrape of claws on bark. Some are ambush hunters that fold themselves into bark seams or hang beneath leaf platforms, motionless until a shadow passes. Others pursue prey in short, explosive bursts, using the confusion of swarms or weather cells as cover. The most dangerous are not always the largest. Some small predators are acoustic specialists, capable of imitating distress calls or mating raspings, drawing victims into hollows where the sound becomes disorienting.

Scavengers and decomposers are everywhere, though often unseen. In this world, death is not buried in mineral soil; it is entered. A fallen creature becomes a site of habitation almost immediately. Beetles, larvae, fungal threads, rootlets, and moisture gather around it. A carcass may be drawn into a crevice by scavengers, or it may remain where it falls until the plant substrate slowly folds it in. Over time, its presence becomes part of the texture of the ground: a swelling in the leaf-felt, a darker patch of bark, a hollow where something nests. Nothing is truly discarded here. Even death is a kind of occupancy.

The plant forms themselves are not merely scenery for these lives. They are hosts, instruments, barriers, reservoirs, and slow participants. The colossal stalks, ribbed trunk-forms, and root platforms are alive in ways that matter to everything else. They may not move quickly, but they respond. Some close pores when touched. Some release scent when wounded. Some open flowers only after specific vibrations, whether from wind, wingbeat, or footfall. Some shed bark plates on long cycles, creating sudden cliffs of fallen material and new shelters below. Some store water deep inside swollen roots, allowing entire communities of small creatures to persist through hot intervals. To live here is to live inside a body that is itself alive, though not alive in the same tempo as the animals.

The people of this place—those person-like inhabitants whose traces are scattered across the tableland—are few because the world does not permit many. They are not absent, but they are restrained. Their presence is light by necessity and by custom. An outsider might pass through large stretches of the landscape without seeing one, noticing only an oddly smooth path along a root slope, a lashing mark healed into bark, or a faint line of worn knots leading upward into a branch crotch.

They are shaped by heat, height, and sound. Their bodies tend to be lean and long-limbed, adapted to long distances and careful climbing. Their feet are broad and gripping, with soles textured for polished bark and toes capable of curling around root ridges. Their hands are sensitive and strong, with fine tactile awareness for testing cordage, detecting moisture, and reading vibrations through surfaces. Their hearing is acute, not only for ordinary speech but for changes in ambient sound: the pitch of a hollow, the alteration of an insect chorus, the distant behavior of wind through crown-spines. Some among them may have slight protective features against pollen and dust—narrow eyelids, closable nostril slits, or fine facial hairs—but these are practical adaptations, not monstrous differences.

They do not wear much, because the heat discourages heavy covering, but they are rarely naked. They wear belts, pouches, wrappings, and harnesses made from woven plant fibers, shed bracts, insect skins, and softened bark cloth. Their garments are practical first and ornamental second, though ornament exists in subtle forms: a beetle-shell button, a rare metal ring, a seed-shell pendant, a knot-pattern identifying lineage or route. They often carry scent as much as tools. Resin, crushed leaves, fermented fruit, and warmed sap may be rubbed on skin or gear to mark identity, repel insects, or mask a hunter’s presence. In a world governed by sound and smell, decoration is not merely visual.

They live in small, flexible groups rather than dense settlements. A band may consist of a few individuals or a dozen, depending on season, water availability, and the current pulse of flowering or insect emergence. These groups are not always based on kinship. Some form around shared knowledge of water; others around skill in listening, hunting, weaving, or fire-keeping. A group may split when resources thin or when the acoustic pressure becomes too great. They understand crowding not only as a lack of food but as an excess of noise, conflict, and vulnerability.

Their camps are temporary and modest. They prefer platforms lashed into branch crotches, shelters woven from shed bracts, or hollow internodes widened and cleaned over generations. They do not quarry. They do not dig into the world as if it were dead stone. They seek existing hollows, folds, seams, and crotches, then improve them lightly. A home might be a roof of overlapping bracts stretched over a root shelf, a sleeping hammock of fine root-fiber, a windscreen of woven leaf-leather, or a sealed chamber inside a hollow stem lined with fungal felt to dampen sound. If a camp is abandoned for too long, the plant world begins to reclaim it: rootlets stitch across openings, vines or fungal threads bind loose materials, insects move into unattended knots, and the structure becomes a small ecosystem.

Their technology is intimate, portable, and repairable. Cordage is central. They make ropes, nets, belts, ladders, and snares from fibers stripped carefully from living or shed plant parts. Knots are not merely functional; they are mnemonic. A skilled person can tie a record into cord: distances, water sources, dangers, flowering times, safe routes, forbidden hollows. These cord-maps are updated constantly, because the living terrain changes. A knot-bowl that held water last season may be overgrown; a root bridge may have shifted; a hollow may now belong to predators. Memory must be mobile.

Their cutting tools come from hard organic materials: flaked dense heartwood, sharpened thorns, seed-shell blades, beetle mandibles, and occasionally rare metal. Metal is precious, not common. It may be found in deep root nodules, traded from far away, or recovered from some mysterious fallen source. It is worked with hammerstones of hardened heartwood and kept for edges that must last: a ceremonial blade, a fire-maker’s striker, a leader’s small knife, an heirloom point set into a wooden shaft. A metal edge here is not a sign of industry but of rarity and care. It is wrapped, oiled, hidden, and passed down.

Fire is both treasured and feared. Everything around them is plant matter, and even when much of it is damp or massive, the risk of spreading flame is real. They use fire sparingly and with elaborate caution. Hearths are built inside dense, partly carbonized bark rings or hollowed heartwood basins lined with wet pith. Fuel is selected for low spark and slow burn. Firekeepers carry embers in closed seed-shells packed with moist fiber. Cooking is often brief: parching pollen, roasting larvae, heating resin to make adhesive, hardening thorn points, or warming water in thick-walled seed vessels. They may also use sun-heated surfaces for slow cooking, especially in midday when bark plains radiate heat. Open flame is never casual. A person who mishandles fire is not merely foolish; they are dangerous to the entire living ground.

Water shapes their movements and their knowledge. Because there are no broad rivers or standing seas, they become specialists in hidden moisture. They know which knot-bowls fill after brief rain, which leaf cups hold clean condensation at dawn, which pitcher-like growths can be safely tapped, and which root swellings store water through the hottest intervals. They learn to read subtle signs: the sheen of wax on a leaf surface, the behavior of insects around a hollow, the smell of damp pith, the color of bark after mist. They carry water in seed-shell vessels, hollow stems, or resin-coated pouches, but they do not rely only on what they carry. Their true wealth is knowledge of where water returns.

They tend these water places without owning them in a heavy sense. They clear debris, reinforce lips with cordage, cover some cups to reduce evaporation, and discourage parasites from breeding in stagnant pockets. They may encourage certain pitcher plants by feeding them small insects or by pruning surrounding growth, but they do not force the landscape into fields. Their cultivation is closer to gardening hollows and maintaining relationships than to farming open ground. A person’s status may depend partly on how many water sources they know, how reliably they can find moisture in a dry pulse, and how carefully they preserve those sources for others.

Their food comes from a shifting mosaic of gathering, hunting, and tending. They collect pollen, fruit, sap, seeds, edible fungi, tender shoots, and stored plant matter. They hunt insects, larvae, bird-like nestlings, gliders, and smaller grazers. They may trap creatures with resin nets, fiber snares, or acoustic lures. Sound-luring is especially important. A hunter might imitate the rasp of a mate, the distress call of prey, or the vibration that triggers a flower to open. They also know how to use the landscape’s own noise to mask movement, stepping in rhythm with insect pulses or waiting for a gust to shake the crowns.

They eat according to cycles. Dawn meals may be light: dew, fruit, pollen cakes. Midday may be a time of rest, hidden chewing, and minimal fire. Dusk brings more activity, more hunting, more exchange. During flowering events or insect emergences, food can become suddenly abundant, and groups gather in loose clusters to harvest, preserve, and trade. During lean intervals, they rely on stored sap, dried larvae, seed kernels, and water knowledge. Hunger is not unknown, but it is managed by movement and restraint.

Their communication is shaped by the overwhelming sound of the world. Ordinary speech exists, but it is not always sufficient. They use layered methods: spoken words, chest-resonant hums, hand signs, touch patterns, drummed codes, and body rhythm. In a noisy environment, clarity matters more than volume. A message may be passed by tapping a hollow stem in a pattern that carries farther than a shout. A warning may be a sudden change in personal sound, a sharp click repeated three times, or a silence so deliberate that others notice it as wrong.
