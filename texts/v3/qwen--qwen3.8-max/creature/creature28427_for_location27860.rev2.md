No one lives there in the ordinary sense.

There are no hearths, no shelters, no paths worn by repeated feet, no signs that hands have ever arranged one stone upon another. If a person stood in that basin, they would be a visitor only, and a brief one. The place does not invite settlement. It has no surplus to give, no green thing to harvest, no timber, no soil that remembers seed. It is warm, mineral, and already complete.

But it is not empty.

What lives there lives small, slow, and close to the stone. It is life shaped not by soil and sunlight, but by heat, vapor, salt, pressure, and chemical difference. It hides in seams, pockets, damp shadows, and the thin margins where one mineral condition gives way to another. It does not gather into abundance. It does not flourish loudly. It persists in isolated refuges, each one tiny in comparison to the wide, humming emptiness between them.

The truest inhabitants of the basin are not animals at all.

They are films, skins, crusts, and living surfaces.

Across the warm rock, where moisture appears and chemistry remains stable, there are thin microbial layers so subtle they can be mistaken for staining. These are the basin’s primary lives. They coat the inner walls of old vent tubes, line shaded cracks, spread across damp mineral shelves, and form delicate laminations where brine seeps meet cooler air. Some are black and glossy, like a varnish laid over basalt. Others are pale, chalky, or rust-colored. Some look like powdered ash bound together by a faint oil. Others resemble brittle lace, mineralized almost as soon as they grow.

These films are not green in any leafy sense. If there is any greenness among them, it is faint, metallic, and uncertain — a coppery stain, a blue-gray sheen, a shadow of color caused by pigment and mineral rather than grass or moss. They feed on heat and chemical gradients. They take what the stone offers: sulfur, iron, salt, hydrogen, trace gases, dissolved metals, warm water carrying faint nutrients. They are patient, ancient things. They do not bloom and crash like life in richer places. They maintain. They thicken by almost imperceptible degrees. They become part of the surfaces they inhabit.

They are also the basin’s food.

Where these films persist, the next tier of life becomes possible.

In the clearest brine pools — those shallow, warm mirrors with edges crusted white — tiny crustaceans live in small, isolated populations. They are translucent, almost glassy, and so light in body that they seem more like condensed water than flesh. Their limbs are fine as hairs. Their bodies show only a faint rust-colored trace where digestion occurs, and perhaps a darker bead where a simple nervous knot gathers sensation. They have no need for speed. They move in brief flickers, pausing, drifting, then flickering again, as if responding to some intermittent signal no larger creature can perceive.

These brine dwellers graze the living films that line the bottoms and sides of pools. They scrape with small, specialized mouthparts, eating microbial layers without destroying them entirely. In a basin where nothing grows quickly, they cannot afford to exhaust their food. Their numbers remain low. A pool the size of a basin may hold only a few dozen. A larger pan might hold a few hundred, separated into little clusters around damp ledges and mineral ridges. They do not swarm. They do not school. They are present as scattered, quiet points of motion in water otherwise still.

Their isolation is profound. Each pool is almost a world of its own. The warm dry flats between pools are barriers as severe as oceans. Over time, each refuge may produce slightly different forms: some broader, some more slender, some with longer antennae, some almost motionless, some more inclined to drift upward when the water warms. Yet none of these differences become dramatic. The basin does not encourage extravagance. It trims life back to utility.

Where the crustaceans exist, there may also be predators, though few.

One is a brine flatworm, transparent and nearly invisible until it moves. It lies against the pool floor like a smear of condensation, then flows forward with terrible slowness over a crustacean too sluggish to escape. Another is a small leech-like creature, clear-bodied and thin, which drinks from its prey and becomes briefly visible only when food fills it with cloud. These predators are rare. They do not hunt widely. They wait. Their existence is intermittent and local, tied to the fortunes of a single pool or a cluster of connected seeps.

Beyond the pools, in the damp cracks and condensation seams, live the basin’s mites and wingless insects.

These are small, pale, flattened things, built for narrow spaces. They hide in fissures during the drier intervals and emerge when vapor condenses on cooler stone. Their bodies are often dusted with mineral grains, salt crystals, or fine iron powder, so that they resemble moving specks of the terrain rather than animals. Some have no eyes. Others have only rudimentary light-sensitive spots, enough to distinguish shadow from glare. None have wings. Flight would be useless here, where there are no flowering plants, no fruiting bodies, no soft vegetation, and no reason to cross the open flats except by accident.

The mites are the more numerous. They graze on microbial crusts, lick mineral films, and scrape organic matter from damp stone. Some are so flat that they can slip into pores barely wider than their bodies. Some carry grains of salt on their backs, either by accident or because their cuticles encourage crystallization. When they move across dry-looking ground, they leave tiny tracks that vanish almost immediately as warm air stirs the dust.

The wingless insects are fewer and stranger.

There are pale, elongated things like midges that never fly, with long legs held close to the body and antennae that taste moisture in the air. They wait near drip-channels and inside old vent throats, appearing only when condensation gathers. When droplets fall in their slow, rhythmic sequence, these creatures become active for a short while. They drink from wet stone. They feed on softened films. They mate quickly and quietly, if they mate at all, perhaps only when chemical conditions signal stability. Their eggs are laid in mineral pores, cemented by salt or mineral crust, and may remain dormant until the exact local pattern of dampness returns.

There are also beetles or beetle-like creatures, though they are not beetles in any familiar sense. Their bodies are low and hard, often coated in a dull mineral bloom. They move little. Some seem to spend most of their lives pressed into shaded crevices, collecting moisture on their backs and channeling it toward their mouths by the shape of their own shells. They do not burrow deeply. They do not build nests. They simply occupy the same cracks again and again, generation after generation, until the crack itself seems to belong to them.

Near the vent chimneys, where steam throttles through rock and the air carries a constant hiss, life becomes still more specialized.

In the inner walls of old vent tubes, where heat is steady but not lethal and condensation runs in thin films, there may be worm-like larvae or segmented things with tough, heat-resistant skins. They cling to stone in narrow grooves, bodies flattened against the rock, feeding on microbial layers that grow where vapor and mineral-rich water meet. They are not beautiful. They look like pale wires, or like strips of wet mineral skin given motion. When steam pulses through a throat, they flatten themselves tighter against the wall, riding the burst rather than fleeing it.

Their presence affects the sound only slightly, but it is there if one listens with impossible care. Their bodies can change the tone of a narrow passage by a fraction. Their slow contractions may alter a drip’s path. Their tiny movements may cause a crystal crust to tick a moment earlier than it otherwise would. They do not make the basin’s industrial chord, but they live inside it, as small living parts within a vast acoustic machine.

Some of these vent-dwellers never leave the tubes where they begin. They hatch in damp inner chambers, feed there, reproduce there, and die there. To be carried outside into the open flats would be death. The open basin is too dry, too exposed, too uniform. Their world is the width of a fissure and the length of a warm stone throat.

In places where faint electrical potentials gather, life takes on another character.

Along magnetite-rich seams and quartz-bearing ridges, where heat and pressure create tiny currents, there are microbial veils that orient themselves to invisible forces. They may form faint metallic lines, barely visible except when light strikes them at a shallow angle. They are not conscious, but they respond. They thicken where gradients are stable. They thin where discharges occur too often. They are among the closest things in the basin to living circuitry.

Small creatures learn these places too.

Some mites avoid charged crusts, sensing the prick of static before a spark appears. Others seem drawn to them, perhaps because the charge indicates moisture, chemical difference, or the presence of microbial films. When brief blue sparks cross salt crusts in the dark, they may reveal for an instant the shapes of these hidden residents: a mite frozen mid-step, a pale insect pressed into a crack, a translucent body in brine turning away from light. The sparks do not endanger most of them. They are part of the basin’s ordinary cycles, small discharges in a system that never accumulates enough power to become violent.

The rarest visible lives in the basin are larger than the mites and crustaceans, but only just.

One might call them saltbacks, for lack of a better name.

They are low, broad, slow things, no larger than a hand or perhaps a plate, depending on the refuge that produced them. Their bodies are plated not by manufactured armor but by mineral accretion: salt, dust, iron stain, and dark varnish gathered over a tough living cuticle. When still, they look like flakes of weathered basalt or broken crust. They are noticed only when they move, and they move rarely.

A saltback does not hunt. It does not flee. It crosses the open flats at a pace so slow that it seems less like walking than like settling. It may spend a long interval motionless beside a damp seam, allowing condensation to gather on its back. When it feeds, it presses a soft underside against microbial crust and scrapes with patient, rasping movements. It eats little. It drinks little. It stores heat and moisture in its body. It can endure the dry stretches between pockets of dampness better than most other visible creatures.

Saltbacks do not gather. One may find a single individual in a wide stretch of basin, then none for kilometers. They leave no trails that last. Their weight is slight, and the warm dust closes behind them. They make no nests, no burrows, no markers. They are solitary tenants of the open mineral plain, almost indistinguishable from the stones they cross.

If anything in the basin deserves the word “who,” it is perhaps these creatures — not because they are intelligent in a human sense, but because they possess a kind of presence. They seem to belong completely. They do not struggle against the basin. They do not try to change it. They have been shaped by it into forms that resemble its own stillness.

There may be others, glimpsed rather than known.

A pale ring of mites around a persistent drip.  
A faint shadow moving beneath the surface of a brine mirror.  
A crust that seems to rise and fall by less than a breath.  
A thread of living slime that contracts when touched.  
A cluster of eggs like salt crystals in a sealed pore.  
A vibration in the stone that may be geological, or may be a signal.

But these remain uncertain. The basin does not reveal its life generously.

No people live there.

Not because the warmth would kill them at once, but because there is nothing for them to hold. A human body could walk across the basin. It could endure the heat for a time. It could drink from some brine pockets if the water were not too saline, or collect condensation from cool rock. But a person could not remain without bringing everything with them: food, shelter, tools, memory, purpose. And all those things would feel foreign in that place. The basin resists possession by subtraction. It offers no materials for building, no fuel, no game in abundance, no plants to weave or eat, no softness in which to make a home.

If intelligence were native there, it would not be like human intelligence.

It would have to be patient. Mineral-skinned. Slow in desire. Without fire, without tools, without need for speech that carries far. Perhaps it would not speak at all, or would speak only in pressure changes, in chemical traces, in rhythms tapped against warm stone. It would not build, because the basin already behaves like a machine and leaves no room for construction. It would not store wealth, because nothing accumulates. It would not remember in stories, because there are no artifacts to inherit. It would only continue.

That is the rule of life in the basin: it continues without adding.

When creatures die, they do not leave much behind. There are no bones in abundance, no shells piled in beds, no carcasses drying in the open. The mineral world takes them quickly. Salt crystallizes over them. Iron dust settles into their outlines. Microbial films grow across what remains. Heat and vapor finish the work. The dead become crust, stain, film, or dissolved chemistry. They return to the basin’s circulating system as nutrients, salts, and trace organics. Nothing is mourned. Nothing is displayed. Death is simply another form of maintenance.

The sounds of the living are absorbed into the greater sound.

A crustacean’s flicker makes no noise a human ear can separate from the water. A mite’s footsteps are inaudible. The scraping of a saltback’s mouth, if it makes any sound at all, is lost in the drone of heated ground. Vent larvae may add the faintest wet clicking to the hiss of steam. Eggs may crack softly when humidity shifts. Predators move without alarm. Even the small blue sparks, when they occur, seem less like interruption than like another note in the basin’s constant chord.

Life does not soften the place. It does not make it gentle, fertile, or familiar. It makes it slightly more complex, slightly more intricate, and more complete.

In the end, what lives there is best described as a scattering of tenants.

Microbial skins on warm stone.  
Filaments in sulfur-stained seams.  
Halophilic films in salt crusts.  
Translucent crustaceans in shallow brine.  
Pale mites in condensation cracks.  
Wingless insects waiting for local drizzle.  
Larval things in vent throats.  
Glassy predators in isolated pools.  
Slow mineral-plated crawlers on open flats.  
Living currents in magnetite seams.  
Small bodies, sparse and quiet, each attached to one refuge, one chemical gradient, one damp rhythm.

They do not transform the basin. They do not build within it. They do not make it less industrial, less empty, or less machine-like. If anything, they deepen that impression. They seem like components that arose from the system itself: valves of flesh, regulators of film, tiny moving crystals, brief animal interruptions in a landscape of heat and stone.

The basin is not alive as a forest is alive. It is not alive as a reef is alive. It is alive in scattered points, in hidden seams, in thin wet margins. Its life is restrained, chemical, and almost mineral. It exists in perfect agreement with the place’s deepest rule: everything continues, and nothing changes.

What lives there is patient.  
What lives there is sparse.  
What lives there is warm to the touch of the world, and barely more visible than the stone itself.
