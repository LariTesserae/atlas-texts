What lives there is not, at first, a single creature. It is a scattering. It is a set of small presences that seem less to occupy the pocket than to be the pocket’s first attempts at having an inside.

The inhabitants are mostly pale, mostly quiet, mostly at the edge of being mistaken for mineral accident. They do not crowd. They do not build cities. They are distributed in thin incidents across the walls, the ceiling, the brief pools, the damp seams where the stone softens enough to allow life. Each one belongs to a narrow condition: a little moisture, a little salt, a little darkness that is not darkness, a little stability before the next change.

The smallest lives are the ones that first seem like light behaving badly.

There are the pale springtails, if one may call them that, because they are pale and because they lift themselves from the wet film of the stone in tiny absent-minded leaps. They hang above the damper corners in loose clouds, not flying, not falling, but suspended as if the air there had learned to carry them. Each is no larger than a grain of sand, translucent, with a belly that seems to hold a bead of moisture. When the pocket is dry, they gather around the salt crusts and become almost invisible; when moisture returns, they brighten, and their movement is the first sign that the stone is breathing again. They feed on fungal stain and mineral bloom, on whatever the water brings and does not keep. They are the pocket’s weather made into small bodies.

The mites are slower and more deliberate. They cross the walls like thoughts crossing a closed eye. Their bodies are glassy, flattened, just substantial enough to cast a delayed shadow. Where they walk, they leave a line of chemical brightness, a trail that lasts only long enough to suggest intention. They are not builders in any visible sense, but they map. They test the wall’s hardness, the salt’s edge, the dampness of a fissure, and then they pass on, leaving the map to erase itself. In dry intervals they range farther, because the wall permits them; in wet intervals they stay close to the faint channels, riding the border between stone and vapor.

Higher up, the larvae hang from the ceiling by threads of their own making. They are blind, soft, and pale, and their bodies pulse with a weak light that is not quite bioluminescence and not quite reflection. They seem to be listening with their whole length. The mucous threads are their roads, their anchors, and their instruments; when moisture thickens, the threads shine and the larvae extend downward, tasting the air. When the pocket dries, they draw themselves up into tighter coils and become like small seeds stuck to the ceiling. They are the builders of the curved galleries that cling to the wall: not grand constructions, but patient accumulations of sediment glued with body-fluid, made by bodies that never leave the wall to become anything else. They do not seem to expect emergence. Their life is the gallery, and the gallery is enough.

In the temporary pools live the small amphipod-like creatures, if pool is the word for a lens of water that may not outlast the next dry breath. They move in quick, nervous jerks, but their haste is not panic; it is the speed of lives compressed into a very brief permission. They hatch when moisture gathers, feed on whatever has dissolved into the cold lens, and if the water remains long enough, they lay eggs that can wait for the next wetting. If it does not, they shrink into the sediment and are taken back into the stone. They are among the pocket’s most mortal inhabitants, and also among its most patient.

More unsettling are the flat, pale things in the shallower basins, shaped like salamander nymphs but wrong in the way a word becomes wrong when repeated. Their gills spread like frost ferns at the sides of their necks, and they move sideways, with a patience that makes the water seem hurried. They do not swim so much as negotiate with the surface. When a bead of condensation falls near them, they turn their heads slowly toward it, as if judging whether the pocket has spoken. They can flatten themselves into the clay until only their gills remain visible, and then they look less like animals than delicate stains left by cold. They watch the air. It is hard not to feel watched back by them, though they have no eyes that one can see.

The wingless midges are the quietest of the visible fauna. They rest on the walls in sparse arrangements, each body reduced to a small, glassy scale. They do not swarm. They do not call. They wait for dampness, for one another, for whatever minimal life is possible on a bright, cold wall. When one dies, another may take its place so slowly that the constellation seems unchanged. They are prey, but not often; their smallness and their stillness protect them.

Predation here is not violent in any loud sense. It is a redistribution of stillness.

The whitish pseudoscorpion beneath its crust of hardened clay is one of the pocket’s older patience-forms. It may remain for a long interval without moving, its pale pincers folded like unused tools. When a springtail lands too close, or a midge settles on the wrong brightness, the wall seems to rearrange itself by one degree. Then the predator is where the prey was, and the prey is gone. There is no sound, no drama, only a tiny tremor passing through the marl and then the pocket resuming itself.

The long, soft-bodied thing in the narrow crevice is harder to describe because it is almost never seen whole. It is a hunter of vibrations. It slides through fissures no wider than a straw, its body extending into the stone’s thin secrets, reading the pocket by touch. It hunts larvae and the smaller pool things, but it does not seem to chase. It arrives where a tremor has promised food. In dry intervals it withdraws deeper, and then the pocket feels more empty, as if one of its nerves has gone quiet. In wet intervals, when everything moves and drips and trembles, it becomes active, and the larvae hang more carefully from their threads.

The beetle-like creature with the wet-paper shell patrols the edges of the damp basins. Its movements are careful and exploratory, its antennae moving through the bright air as if tasting the mineral light. It is both scavenger and hunter, taking midges when it can, feeding on shed skins and softened debris when it cannot. Its shell changes with the pocket’s moisture: glossy and almost translucent when wet, dull and fibrous when dry. It is not beautiful, except in the way that adaptation is beautiful when it has given up everything unnecessary.

The green life is barely green.

The rootlets from above are not residents but visitors, pale threads that descend through the ceiling and stop there, unwilling or unable to enter fully. They bring with them the memory of another world: soil, rain, sunlight, weight. The creatures sometimes gather near them, not to eat them but because they mark a direction toward something else. In one corner, the white moss forms a coin-sized patch, so pale it seems made from condensed breath. It brightens when wet and becomes almost mineral when dry. The lichens are scattered discs on the wall, gray-green at the center, brittle at the edge. They are slow, drier than the rest of the pocket, and they seem to belong more to the entrance than to the deep interior.

The fungal filaments are the least visible and perhaps the most necessary. They lace through the marl without fruiting, leaving only faint branching stains, like veins, like frost, like a script that has not yet decided what it wants to say. They prepare the stone for the small animals. They turn debris into food, and food into film, and film into something the next moisture can carry.

The structures made by these inhabitants are not objects so much as residues of repeated living. The curved larval gallery is a body’s path made hard. The spiral mucus case, no larger than a seed, is a shelter shaped by a single turning body. The broken cases on the floor are not ruins in the ordinary sense; they are available things, already polished smooth, ready to be reused or slowly reabsorbed. The cluster of egg-like spheres in the niche is abandoned, but abandonment here does not mean loss. It means that the making has passed into the pocket, and the pocket will decide whether the spheres become life, sediment, or nothing.

The arranged grains along the ledge are perhaps the most mysterious, because they suggest intention without hands. Some creature needed a line, a boundary, or a signal, and so it sorted what it found. The line may last only until the next dampening. It may be remade many times. It is a thought expressed as mineral placement, then forgotten, then expressed again.

Even the remains are gentle. Shed skins curl against the wall like pale scrolls, holding the shape of bodies that have already moved on. A molted carapace in a basin fills with condensation and becomes a tiny vessel. Broken larval tubes soften at the edges and return to the clay. Nothing rots here in the ordinary way. The pocket is too dry for putrefaction and too mineral for ordinary decay. It absorbs. It folds the dead back into the walls, the floors, the crusts, the faint stains. This is why the place feels clean. Not sterile, but clean in the way a healed wound is clean: the injury has become part of the surface.

But the inhabitants are not only the creatures one could, with effort, classify.

The pocket also contains lives that are harder to separate from its light and geometry. There are delayed shadows that belong to moving bodies but arrive late, as if they had been resting elsewhere. There are reflections in the temporary pools that show sky, grass, or ceilings not present in the pocket. It is not clear whether these reflections are memories of the surface above, dreams of the stone, or simply the water’s habit of imagining. The small animals sometimes pause beside them. The frost-gilled things lean over the lenses and seem to watch what is not there. When the water evaporates, the reflections do not vanish so much as withdraw, like animals returning to a hiding place.

There are also trails of light that cling to the creatures, especially the larvae and mites, so that movement leaves a brief veil behind. In the pocket, such things are not decorations. They are part of the living population, if population is the right word for a set of presences that includes bodies, traces, delays, and reflections.

If one insists on naming a single dweller, the best answer is not any one of these.

The single dweller is the pattern they make when they are all left alone. It is distributed and shy. Its body is the pocket’s spacing: the distance between a mite and a larva, the pause between one bead of water and the next, the emptiness around a pseudoscorpion’s ambush. Its senses are the chemical trails, the tremors in the marl, the moisture following weakness in the stone. Its breath is the ceiling’s slow sweating. Its memory is the arranged grains, the abandoned cases, the delayed shadows. It does not have a face, unless a temporary pool can be called a face. It does not speak, unless a line of sorted grains is speech. Yet it is aware in the minimal way of systems: it contracts when watched, expands when ignored, and alters its behavior according to dampness, attention, and the small pressures of living bodies.

This dweller is not a spirit added to the place. It is the place becoming capable of having inhabitants. It is the first interior learning that it can be inhabited. In dry intervals it seems to sleep, though sleep is too soft a word. It becomes still, pale, and hard; the salt crusts lift; the threads tighten; the pools vanish. In wet intervals it seems to remember itself, though remember is perhaps too human a word. The ceiling opens, the mist comes, the larvae extend, the amphipods jerk into brief existence, and the frost-gilled things lift their heads as if receiving a message they have waited for without knowing how to want it.

And if the question asks who lives there, not merely what, the answer becomes even more uncertain.

Perhaps no one in the human sense lives there. Yet the pocket has a shape that suggests a folded body. In some intervals, especially when the geometry is unstable and the entrance has become a pale smear no larger than a coin, there seems to be a resident near the far wall. It is not clearly visible. It is pale, compressed, shoulder-width, as if a person had entered once and then given up the need to remain a person. Its head is lowered, not in grief, but in fit. Its arms are drawn close. Its stillness is so complete that it might be a formation of marl, except that when the pocket shifts, it is somehow elsewhere without having crossed the space.

If this being exists, it is not a keeper or a god. It is more like a pressure retained by the pocket: the memory of outside weight translated into inside silence. It may be the descendant of an entrant, or the pocket’s imitation of one. It does not eat as animals eat; it seems to take in moisture through its stillness. It does not make tools; its only construction is the way it remains. When observed, it becomes smaller, more wall-like. When the attention slides away, it gathers a little more presence. It is not hostile. It is simply adjusted to a place where being seen is a pressure.

One should not settle on this image too quickly. The pocket does not confirm it.

What lives there, then, lives as the pocket lives: intermittently, carefully, and without final form. The inhabitants are not owners. They are occurrences. They appear when moisture permits, hide when the stone tightens, and leave behind only skins, threads, cases, stains, and the faint sense of arrangement. They do not accumulate into a society. They do not build monuments. They make enough. They make only what a body can make by secretion, repetition, and need.

When the moisture withdraws, they become part of the pocket’s hidden brightness again. The springtails dim into the salt. The mites slow to a halt. The larvae close into their threads. The amphipods vanish with their pools. The frost-gilled things flatten into clay. The wingless midges become indistinguishable from the wall. The beetle retreats to the driest edge of the basin. The soft-bodied hunter withdraws into the crevice. The pseudoscorpion becomes wall again.

The light remains for a while, thin and precise, as if waiting to see whether anything else will happen.

Then the pocket becomes still. It is not dead. It is not empty. It is simply holding its inhabitants inside itself, the way a seed holds a forest, the way a closed hand holds a gesture it has not yet made.
