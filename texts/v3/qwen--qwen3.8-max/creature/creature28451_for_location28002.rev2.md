What lives there has learned to inhabit a finished thing.

The mountains no longer make themselves, and the living have inherited that lesson. Nothing there is trying to become taller, sharper, or louder. Life has folded itself into the softened contours, close to the stone, close to heat, close to water, close to other bodies. It is a crowded quiet, a society of small warm presences arranged inside a vast cool shape.

The first thing a visitor notices is not any single creature but the composite life of the place itself: the city behaving like a slow, breathing animal. Its roofs are a mossy pelt. Its channels are veins. Its cisterns are organs of storage. Its fog combs are sensory hairs lifted into the weather. Its engines are warm glands. The people are not separate from this body; they are its hands, its eyes, its habits, its memory. They move through it, maintain it, argue inside it, love inside it, die into it. To live there is to be part of a system that has long since abandoned the dream of expansion and become exquisite in maintenance.

The people of the tableland are not tall, as a rule. Generations at elevation have made their bodies economical: broad chests, efficient lungs, steady hearts, hands that are often cold but extremely precise. They are not fragile. They have the dense, compact strength of those who climb wet stairs, haul cable, wipe mesh, and carry sealed vessels all day. Their faces tend to be pale or greyish, but not colorless; cold reddens the cheeks and noses, engine heat flushes the skin, and many have a faint blue undertone at the temples where the thin air and damp settle into the body. Their eyes are adapted to pearl light. They do not squint often, because there is little hard sun to fight, but when a break in cloud sends a sudden beam across the basins, they turn away with a reflex as old as childhood.

They dress in layers that are never entirely dry and never entirely clean, though they are cared for constantly. Dark coats of oiled wool or woven moss-fiber hang over inner garments of soft cloth. Hoods are common, not for drama but because a hood saves the face from mist and catches a little warmth around the breath. Shoulders are often beaded with fine droplets. Cuffs are worn shiny from wiping valves, rails, and combs. Boots are soft-soled, because wet stone is slick and quiet footsteps are a courtesy. Copper fasteners, glass beads, and little polished stones are favored ornaments; bright things are small and kept close. Many people carry a personal vessel at the belt: a cup, a flask, a folded trough, a waxed pouch. Water is not heavy there in the way of rivers, but it is always being passed from one careful hand to another.

Their voices are low. This is not because they are joyless, but because the air itself teaches volume. In damp fog, loud sounds smear and lose direction. A shout becomes a blunt, stupid thing. So people speak near one another, often with mouths half-turned away, conserving words as they conserve drips. Children learn early to listen before answering, to recognize footsteps by the sound they make on different surfaces: stone, timber, iron grating, moss-packed stair. They can tell by the pitch of a pipe whether it is full or empty, by the hiss of a comb whether the fog is thinning, by the creak of a cable whether the morning lift is carrying cargo or people.

They are not silent people, though. Their quiet is full of humor, complaint, gossip, tenderness, and sudden laughter that is quickly smothered. In workshops, in bath-steam, in crowded roof-kitchens, voices overlap softly. They joke about dry spells, about leaking roofs, about the foolishness of outsiders who think fog is merely weather. They tease one another about the number of cisterns in a dowry, the quality of a household’s moss, the absurdity of a new pipe fitting. They are practical, and their poetry is practical too. A compliment might be: “Your roof holds water well.” A blessing might be: “May your lamps stay lit and your drains not choke.” A curse, if meant seriously, would be something like: “May your fog pass you by.”

They live densely, but not without order. A household is rarely a single family in a single room. Households stack and interlock. Several related groups may share a stair, a courtyard, a flue, a cistern, a rooftop path, and a set of responsibilities. In the hollow districts, homes sink into the ground in terraces, each roof becoming the doorstep of the house above. In the rim districts, buildings lean into one another for warmth, connected by short bridges and enclosed walkways. A person’s identity is partly spatial. They are from this basin, that stair, the court beneath the third viaduct, the alley where the old gneiss rises through the floor like a whale’s back. Names are often paired with places in everyday speech. “Mura of the lower trough.” “Senn by the green flue.” “The Kess children who live above the paper mill.”

Privacy exists, but it is negotiated rather than assumed. Walls are thin in some ways, thick in others. Sound is managed by hangings, moss panels, and careful placement of rooms. Sleeping alcoves are small and warm, often near a shared heat source. In cold spells, households open partitions and sleep together in one heated chamber, not from sentimentality but from economy. Heat is too precious to waste on empty corners. Visitors are not automatically embraced, but they are assessed by a simple standard: do they understand how to be indoors in a place where warmth belongs to everyone? A guest who hoards heat, wastes water, or speaks too loudly at night will not be punished dramatically. They will simply be invited less often.

The most respected work is work that keeps the place alive without violence. Fog-comb tenders are among the first people one sees at the rims, though they are often half-hidden by cloud. They climb the lattices before dawn, check the mesh, clear ice or debris, and listen to the way the air beads on the wires. They wear copper chains with small counters or notched tags, because their shifts are measured by accumulation: how much condensation, which direction the fog is moving, when the combs should be angled, when the troughs should be opened. Their hands are always damp. Their eyes are used to looking into grey distances where shapes appear only when they are very close. They have a reputation for being solitary, but this is not quite true. They simply spend much of their time in conversation with weather.

Roof-moss shepherds are another quiet authority. They walk the tops of buildings with soft tools and long knives, shearing moss where it grows too thick, encouraging it where it has thinned, pressing new mats into cracks, and checking that root-like filaments have not blocked drains. They know the different species by touch: fleece-moss that holds water like wool, wire-moss that strengthens banks, cup-moss that seals old stone joints, black sedge that tolerates foot traffic. Their boots leave faint patterns on green roofs. Children sometimes follow them, learning not to step on cushion plants or disturb nesting voles. The shepherds are often the ones who decide whether a roof can bear another tank, another walkway, another glasshouse. Their word is not law in the formal sense, but no sensible household ignores it.

Channel walkers work below and between. They know the shallow old channels that look like streets until water suddenly fills them. They know where a cloudburst will run, where a drain will choke, where a sealed reservoir breathes through a hidden pipe. They carry listening rods and lanterns with sealed panes. Their clothes smell of wet iron and moss. They are the people called when a courtyard floods unexpectedly or when a fountain court goes dry. They also have a slightly eerie reputation, because they spend so much time listening beneath the city. They can place a hand on a wall and say whether water is moving nearby, and they often speak of pipes as if the pipes have moods.

Engine tenders live closer to warmth and noise. Their districts have the low hum of condensers, the smell of hot metal, the glow of furnace mouths. They are not loud people, despite working among machines. Their skill lies in detecting small changes: a bearing beginning to dry, a belt slipping, a vane out of balance, a cooling fin blocked with lichen. They sleep in shifts near their engines, sometimes with one hand resting on a casing, feeling the vibration. In many families, engine tending is inherited, not by pride alone but by exposure. Children grow up knowing the pitch of a healthy condenser the way others know the sound of the sea. They learn to oil, to wipe, to adjust, to wait. They also learn that machines in this country are not meant to dominate the air. A machine that roars is a badly made machine. A good machine breathes with the city.

Lamplighters are beloved in a way that surprises outsiders. In a land of dimness, light is a public good. Lamplighters move through districts at dusk with long hooks, sealed candles, oil flasks, and little ladders. They know which lamps must burn all night near stairs and bridges, which can be dimmed after midnight, which red markers must remain visible above fog towers. Their work is weather-dependent. If fog thickens, extra lamps are lit. If the air clears, some are extinguished to save fuel. They are often greeted from windows, offered cups of warm broth, teased for being slow or hurried according to the mood of the street. Children imitate them with sticks, pretending to light the mist.

There are many other lives woven into the city’s labor. Paper makers boil moss fiber and reclaimed cloth in stone vats. Glassblowers work in small, insulated sheds, making lenses, lamp panes, droplet jars, and fine tubes for instruments. Metalworkers repair more than they forge; every part is used until it cannot be used, then melted, re-cast, buried into a wall, or made into a hinge for a cistern lid. Weavers make dense cloth that sheds mist. Fungal growers tend dark beds in vaults, turning compost, controlling damp, harvesting pale caps by lamplight. Greenhouse keepers cultivate dwarf tubers, wax-leafed vines, and berrying shrubs behind glass slick with condensation. Funicular crews manage cables and brakes. Causeway sweepers keep the old paved links from becoming slick. Reservoir guards do not guard against thieves so much as against carelessness; their task is to make sure no one contaminates the water, opens a sluice wrongly, or lets panic spread during a dry spell.

Water is the center of law, though law here is less a set of punishments than a web of shame, habit, and memory. No one owns fog. No one can own fog. But everyone owns the duty to catch it, pass it on, and keep it clean. A child’s earliest lessons are water lessons: do not let a drip fall uselessly; do not wash hands over an open drain unless the water is being caught; do not pour waste where it can reach a cistern; if you find water where it should not be, call someone before you touch it. The gravest offenses are not dramatic. They are negligent: a cracked trough left unrepaired, a roof gutter blocked by moss, a visitor who thinks a little spill cannot matter. In the old districts, households keep notched tally sticks or bead-strings recording cistern contributions. These are not currency exactly, but they become part of reputation. A family known for careful water is trusted. A family known for waste is watched.

Because water is hidden in so many places, children grow up with a sense of secret abundance. They know that beneath a quiet stair there may be a covered tank. They know that a courtyard wall may contain a pipe that sweats in the morning. They know that a sealed fountain court can hold enough drink for a district in a pinch. This gives them a particular relationship to wealth. Wealth is not displayed as flowing water. That would be obscene. Wealth is reliability: a full cistern, a well-kept comb, a roof that catches cleanly, a household that can share without fear. The richest people are not those who splash; they are those who can afford to be generous.

Food follows the same principles. Nothing is grown extravagantly, but much is grown attentively. The terraces and roofs produce low, hardy crops: alpine grasses with small seeds, cushion plants whose leaves can be dried for flour, dwarf tubers roasted in insulated pits, dark leafy vines trained along warm pipes, berries with waxed skins that keep well in cool cellars. Fungi are perhaps the most important cultivated food. They grow in vaults, under staircases, in disused rail tunnels, in shaded courts where engine warmth and damp meet. Some are pale and mild; some are dense and smoky; some are dried into cakes for travel. There are also lichen preparations, though these require patience: soaking, boiling, fermenting, grinding. Lichen porridge is common, humble, and flavored with whatever syrup or fat a household can spare.

In the warmer industrial districts, where heat leaks from pipes and floors, there are more delicate gardens. Hanging vines, mushroom beds, and low shrubs provide fresh greens and fruit for workers. In certain reservoir courts, where water is not destined for drinking, small pale fish and flat-bodied crustaceans are kept in troughs. They are not abundant enough to feed everyone, but they provide taste and fat. Insects also enter the diet, though outsiders sometimes misunderstand this. The silvered moths that gather near lamps are not eaten as adults; their larvae are harvested from controlled fungal mats and warm vents, then dried and ground into meal. It is ordinary food, not a delicacy, and children eat it in cakes without thinking much about it.

Meals are warm whenever possible. Kitchens are often communal at the level of a courtyard or building stack, because heat used for cooking can also warm rooms, dry clothes, and feed a bathhouse. The smell of the city at meal hours is not sharp but soft: steamed mushroom broth, toasted moss-grain, hot stone, faint vinegar from preserved leaves, the mineral scent of heated water. People eat with bowls close to their chests. They drink warm infusions more often than cold water, because cold water is precious and warm drinks help the body keep its heat. There is a custom in many districts of beginning a meal by passing a small cup of freshly caught condensation. It may be only a mouthful. It is not about quenching thirst. It is about remembering where life comes from.

Animals live there too, but they have learned the same discretion as the people. The thin air, the cool dimness, and the lack of tall cover make large fauna rare. What exists is small, quick, secretive, and often pale. Roof voles are everywhere, though one rarely sees them directly. They tunnel through the deepest moss, creating tiny tremors in the green surface. They aerate the roofs and help distribute seeds and spores, but they also chew through mats and block gutters. Children are taught to listen for their runs and to press the moss back gently without crushing it. In some districts, voles are tolerated to a point; in others, they are culled quietly, their skins used for small linings and their bodies fed to the hunters that keep them in check.

The hunters are weasel-like creatures with grey coats and long, flexible bodies. They slip between cisterns, factory yards, vent shafts, and roof gardens, following warmth and scent. People have different names for them in different basins: pipe-martens, flue-cats, grey rippers, fog-weasels. They are not pets, but they are not wholly wild either. They nest near engines where heat leaks through walls. They accept scraps, provided the scraps are offered without fuss. They are valued because they hunt voles and large moths, though they also steal eggs and small fish when they can. A worker who sees one sleeping behind a boiler often leaves it alone. A household with too many voles may try to attract a marten by leaving a warm gap and a little blood-scented cloth near the entrance. It is not friendship. It is negotiation.

Birds are few but memorable. The broad-winged fog kites ride the currents that rise over the rims and sink into basins. They do not flap often. They tilt, glide, and vanish into mist, reappearing minutes later over another district. They feed on insects near the mesh towers, especially at dawn when condensation traps small flying life. Their nests are built on viaducts, old bridge undersides, and sheltered ledges where the wind cannot tear them apart. Children watch them with longing, because flight in that country seems both easy and impossible. There are also smaller birds, dull-plumaged and quick, that live in roof sedge and eat seeds, larvae, and moth eggs. They are not brightly colored. Brightness would be wasted there. Their songs are brief and low, often only a few notes, as if they too have learned not to spend sound unnecessarily.

Moths are perhaps the most important animals after humans, though they are rarely honored in grand ways. The silvered moths gather around lamps and engine heat, their narrow wings catching light like wet metal. They pollinate many of the cup-shaped flowers that open only when fog thins. Their larvae process moss, fungal mats, and dead plant matter. Their life cycle is woven into the city’s seasons. When the air dries, moss releases spores in faint green smoke; when the air wets again, fungal fruiting bodies open; when the lamps are brightest, moths swarm and predators gather. Some districts keep moth lures—dim, warm lanterns above controlled beds—so that pollination and larval harvest can be managed. Moths are also part of superstition. If a silver moth lands on a new cistern lid, it is considered a good sign. If moths abandon a district’s lamps for several nights, people begin checking the combs and the water tables, uneasy that some change has gone unnoticed.

There are smaller lives too, innumerable and mostly unseen. Pale beetles move under cistern lids. Flat-bodied insects live in the overflow channels, their bodies pressed against stone to avoid being swept away. Soft-bodied larvae clean the slimy films inside troughs. In the warmest glasshouses, tiny flies hover over berrying shrubs. In the oldest reservoirs, where mineral water has deposited glittering films over centuries, there are organisms no one can easily name: not quite lichen, not quite mold, perhaps something older, living in the boundary between stone and damp. They glow faintly in some places. They may be alive. They may be chemical. The residents usually do not insist. They have learned to distinguish between what must be understood and what must merely be respected.

Vegetation is not separate from the city; it is part of its skin. Moss lies over stone like fleece, softening edges and holding warmth. Lichen maps walls in pale greens, golds, ash-greys, and rust-reds, growing so slowly that its patterns become a record of time. Cushion plants swell in cracks, their dense bodies keeping moisture through dry hours. Dwarf ferns unfurl in shaded seams between buildings, their fronds no longer than a finger. Sedge and grasses stitch terraces together. Cup-shaped flowers open reluctantly, turning toward weak light. In engine-warm districts, dark-leaved vines climb pipes and walls, their leaves waxed against damp. Roofs are meadows. Courtyards are gardens. Abandoned rail lines become green corridors where insects hide and children learn to walk softly.

People tend these plants with the same care they give machines. They know that moss insulates, holds water, reduces sound, and protects stone from frost. They know that lichen can indicate clean air or old damp. They know that certain fungi, if left unchecked, will rot timber, but if cultivated properly will feed a household all winter. They prune, press, harvest, replant, and compost. In the dry intervals, when the air thins and the city seems almost thirsty, they cover sensitive beds with oiled cloth. In the wet intervals, they open channels and let the roofs drink. There are seasons of spore smoke, when the air turns faintly green and people wear veils over their mouths. There are seasons of flowering, when the basins smell briefly sweet and the fog kites circle lower to catch insects drawn to the blossoms.

Sound is part of life there, but it is not used carelessly. The city has its own acoustic ecology. Dripping is the most common music: single drops, quick strings of drops, the hollow plink into metal, the softer tap into moss. Bells exist, but they are small and often muffled, used for signals rather than celebration. A bell may mark the opening of a sluice, the arrival of a fog tide, the closing of a workshop, or the death of a neighbor. Because sound behaves strangely in damp air, people trust repeated patterns more than single notes. A bell can seem close and then vanish. A voice can travel along a wall but not across a courtyard. So messages are often visual as well: lamps blinking, flags of oiled cloth, painted boards, hand signals from bridge to bridge. In some districts, children learn a simple sign language for foggy days, not because they cannot speak, but because sight and sound each fail at different times.

Their art is small, durable, and often functional. They carve no great statues into the mountains, because the mountains are already finished and because fresh carving feels rude there. Instead, they polish existing shapes. A stone worn like a seat becomes part of a courtyard. A basalt swell becomes the base of a building. A folded ribbon of gneiss becomes the wall of a shrine or a staircase. Their decorations are made from what passes through their hands: copper wire, glass droplets, pressed moss, woven fiber, old machine parts, bone, polished pebbles. They like patterns of branching and netting, because these resemble water channels, fog combs, lichen, and roof roots. Their most prized objects are often repaired many times. A lamp mended with five different metals may be more valued than a new one, because it shows continuity without waste.

Music, when it happens, is quiet. Breath flutes made from pipe scraps. Thumb bells worn on work gloves. Resonant wooden boards struck softly in roof-kitchens. Stringed instruments played close to the body. In some basins, there are water organs of a sort: sealed pipes through which controlled drips pass, making low hollow notes. These are not for performance so much as for listening. A household may keep one pipe sounding through the night, not because the sound is beautiful in an ornamental way, but because it proves that water is moving, that the channels are alive, that the house is still breathing.

Their beliefs are shaped by the ended mountains. They do not generally tell stories about the land being young, violent, or destined to rise again. Their stories are about finishing, settling, and maintenance. Some say the mountains were once giants who chose to lie down after too much struggle. Others say the world itself grew tired of height and decided to become a floor. Others say nothing so grand: stone wears, water collects, life adapts. But even the practical speak with a certain reverence about the final shape of the land. They do not believe the mountains are dead. They believe the mountains are complete. This distinction matters. A completed thing still demands care. A completed thing can still be inhabited.

Their rituals are often rituals of upkeep. The first fog of a season may be greeted by opening every comb in a district for a measured hour, letting the first condensation run into the oldest cistern. Lamps are lit in sequence at dusk during the dimmest month, so that each basin answers the next like a slow chain of stars. When a new channel is cut or an old one repaired, water is poured through it while workers stand silent, listening for leaks. When a child is born, a small measure of water is placed on its lips—not enough to drink, only enough to acknowledge that life depends on what is caught and shared. When a person dies, the household closes a valve or covers a lamp for a short time, depending on local custom. The dead are not buried extravagantly. In many districts, they are wrapped in oiled cloth and moss, carried to a roof garden or a quiet stone niche, and allowed to return their moisture slowly to the city’s skin. There are no grand graves. There are copper drip-rings, notched names, and places where sedges grow especially thick.

They are not without conflict. Density creates friction. Households argue over roof rights, over the angle of a comb, over the direction of runoff, over whether a new glasshouse will steal warmth from a neighbor’s wall. Districts compete for fog exposure. Industrial terraces sometimes clash with reservoir courts over heat and purity. Funicular crews and channel walkers may despise one another’s work rhythms. There are thefts, though rarely of water directly; water theft is too dangerous to the whole and too socially ruinous. More often there are small resentments: a ladder moved, a lamp extinguished too early, a child taught to use the wrong trough. These conflicts are usually resolved by councils of elders, guild stewards, and neighbors, because open violence in a place so interdependent would be like cracking a shared vessel. The punishment that matters most is loss of trust.

Outsiders are rare but not unknown. The old causeways and viaducts bring occasional travelers, traders, scholars, refugees, and curious pilgrims who have heard of the finished land. Outsiders are often too loud at first. They look for horizons and find only undulating grey-blue distance. They ask for rivers and are shown cisterns. They ask for forests and are shown roof moss. Some leave quickly, unsettled by the quiet. Others stay and slowly change. The city teaches them by small corrections: a hand on the elbow to stop them from spilling water, a low voice reminding them to shut a door against heat, a child staring at them until they lower their voice. Those who remain usually develop the same habits as the native residents: soft boots, careful hands, an ear for pipes, a fondness for amber light.

There is also strangeness in the living of the place, but it is subtle. In certain basins, the fog engines create pockets of private weather. A street may remain dry while another, only an arm’s length away, is hung with a fine drizzle produced by overtaxed mesh and cooling fins. Children play in these boundaries. They call them “ghost rains,” though they do not believe the rain is haunted; they know it is mechanical. Yet knowing the cause does not remove the feeling that the city is dreaming small dreams of weather. In deep reservoirs, the mineral films sometimes glow faintly along old veins, and workers whisper that the stone remembers ore, heat, pressure, or older industries. Bioluminescent moss may grow in places where chemical residue has settled, producing a cold green light that seems to breathe. The residents do not make too much of this. They mark dangerous places, repair what can be repaired, and leave the rest alone.

Some people claim there are lives in the fog itself—not spirits, not monsters, but patterns that behave almost intentionally. A fog bank that lingers over a particular bridge. A cloud that seems to lean into a comb. A mist that parts for a funicular and closes behind it. Most sensible people say this is weather moving around solid forms. But even sensible people sometimes pause when a wall of fog arrives exactly at the hour a lamp-lighter needs it, or when a dry spell ends just as the last cistern is emptied. In a place where life depends on condensation, gratitude easily becomes attribution. The residents do not worship the fog, but they speak of it with the same wary respect one might give an old, changeable neighbor.

Love there is quiet and practical. It is shown in mending a coat, saving a dry pair of gloves, clearing a drain before a storm, bringing a bowl of hot broth up a long stair. Courtship often happens along work routes: on bridges, in lamp-lit stairwells, beside cistern courts, under the shelter of fog towers. Lovers meet in glasshouses where condensation runs down the panes, or in bathhouses where warmth loosens the shoulders. They may exchange small objects: a polished stone, a glass bead, a coil of fine wire, a cutting of moss that will grow on a windowsill. Marriage, where recognized, is often an agreement about shared infrastructure: whose cistern will be used, whose heat will be shared, whose roof path will be maintained. This does not make it cold. It makes it real. In a place where survival is collective, love cannot afford to be only a feeling. It must also be a way of keeping the walls dry.

Children are valued highly, but not indulged endlessly. They are needed, and they know it early. They learn to carry small vessels, to wipe low combs, to sort moss, to feed moth larvae, to watch for leaks. They also play. They invent games with drips, racing droplets down windowpanes. They build tiny dams in roof gutters and are scolded when the dams overflow. They imitate fog kites by running with arms spread along ridge paths, then collapse laughing into sedge. They tell stories about the sleeping basalt animals, and some refuse to climb certain rounded hills because they believe the stone might stir. They are not afraid of the dark in the way lowland children might be. They are afraid of dryness, of careless fire, of broken seals, of silence where water should be moving.

The old are especially important. In a society of maintenance, memory is a resource. An elder may remember which channel flooded thirty years ago, which roof moss died during a long dry spell, which engine part failed and why. Their knowledge is stored not only in words but in habits: the way they listen to a pipe, the way they touch a wall, the way they read cloud over the rims. Elders often sit in warm courtyards, wrapped in layers, watching workers and offering brief corrections. They are not always gentle. Some are sharp, irritated by cold and age. But even the sharp ones are listened to, because a mistake made from ignorance can cost water, heat, or life.

At evening, the living city becomes most visible. Lamps bloom along bridges. Windows glow in near-touching rows. The fog thickens, and districts become islands. Voices lower further. Machinery hums in vaults. Moss holds the day’s last warmth. Somewhere a child is carried to bed with a silver moth cupped carefully in both hands. Somewhere a flue-marten slips between tanks, hunting. Somewhere a fog comb beads with new water, and a tender opens a trough by feel alone. In the lower factories, workers wipe down tools and bank heat for the night. In the glasshouses, vines give off a faint green smell. In the reservoirs, water settles into stillness without becoming dead.

What lives there is not abundant in the way of jungles or river deltas. It is not flamboyant. It does not fill the eye with movement. But it is intensely alive, and it is alive together. The people, the voles, the martens, the moths, the birds, the mosses, the fungi, the engines, the water, the light, the quiet—all of it forms a dense web of small dependencies. Each life is limited, and each limitation is answered by another life. Where stone has finished rising, living things have begun their endless work of holding, catching, warming, repairing, and passing on.

If one had to give the country a single living face, it would not be a human face alone. It would be a hooded figure on a roof at dusk, wiping moisture from a comb, then pausing to look across the basins. Behind the figure, windows glow. Below, water moves through hidden channels. Around, moss softens every edge. Above, fog arrives like a slow tide. The figure is cold, probably tired, probably thinking about a leaking valve or a child waiting indoors. But there is also satisfaction there: the satisfaction of being needed by a place that cannot spare anything. The ended mountains do not ask for heroes. They ask for caretakers. And caretakers live there, in the damp and the quiet, keeping the final shape breathing.
