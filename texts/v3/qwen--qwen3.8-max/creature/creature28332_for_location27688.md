Moisture returns not as rain in the ordinary sense, but as a local gesture of the same body touching itself again. It beads on the underside of the fixed cloud-sheet, gathers on cable sheaths, clings to the tips of sedge blades, and falls in short vertical threads so fine they seem less like weather than like the world sweating. It collects in the angular basins, darkens the fractured rock, and passes again into the capillary veins of the high marsh. There is no storm in this. There is no clearing. There is only the circulation of wetness within a system that has learned to keep itself exactly wet.

In such a country, life has shed the old expectation of animals.

What lives there is not a tenant moving through a landscape. It is the landscape maintaining itself as a living condition. If one must speak of inhabitants, one must speak in plural and singular at once: colonies, networks, films, tuned structures, slow growths, self-regulating mechanisms, and finally, out of all these, something like a single vast resident with no face, no hunger, no eyes, and no voice except the layered sound of its own working.

The first apparent citizens are the plants.

They are not beautiful in the soft, floral sense. There are no broad petals here, no warm colors, no nectar, no sweetness, no invitation. The green life of the marsh is mineral, stiff, patient, and acoustic. Mosses form the primary flesh of the ground: deep cushions, low mats, green-black terraces that hold water at their roots and mist at their surfaces. They do not merely endure the wet cold; they organize it. They slow the water down. They spread it evenly. They bind the sharp chips of young rock into a surface that can bear weight without becoming solid in any comforting way. Underfoot they behave like flesh over empty rooms.

Sedges stand in black-green tufts where the surface is thinner and the wind has a cleaner path. Their blades are stiff enough to ring. That is not a metaphor. When the air moves over them, they produce high, faint metallic notes, not unlike the sound of wet glass or struck ceramic. These sounds are not incidental. The sedges live partly as sound-organisms. Their seed cases and spore stems are built to tremble at particular frequencies. Some open only when the drone from the distant turbine halls reaches them across the marsh. Some release their microscopic burden not when the sun warms them—the sun does not warm them—but when the ground carries a certain low throb through water and rock.

The low cushion plants are harder to see as individuals. They grow in tight hemispheres, their small leaves thick, lustrous, and cold to the touch. They look carved from dark soapstone or pressed from wet copper dust. They survive by holding still. Their bases remain saturated; their upper surfaces shed excess moisture into the air. They do not compete in the way warm-climate plants compete. They extend. They persist. They fill space with slow green density until a hollow below is roofed over and a new platform of life forms above a dark interior.

Liverworts crust the rocks in patches like hammered metal gone green. They are among the most important lives in the place, though their importance is not obvious.
