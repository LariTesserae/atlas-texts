The light feels old, though it has no morning.

What lives there is not one thing, and not any single kind of thing. It is a crowded finality: life that has been compressed into the last possible arrangements, packed into every crack, every shadowless interval, every space between plates and bodies and grains. The tableland is not empty. It is overfull in the way of a finished text, where no further sentence is needed and every mark has already been placed.

The first inhabitants you notice are the low grazers, because they are the ground’s nearest imitation of itself. They are broad, flattened beasts, carapaced in dull mineral scales the color of worn feldspar and dark silt. Their bodies lie close to the pavement, as if they have learned that height is an unnecessary argument with the air. They have many short legs, set wide beneath them, and those legs move so rarely that the animals seem less to walk than to be repositioned by long, invisible deliberations. Their mouths are rasping slots, not made for biting but for scraping: they pass over the black wiregrass, over the copper-colored nap in shallow depressions, over the thin mineral films that form on stone, taking in what can be taken without tearing anything away.

They stand flank to flank in fields so wide that, from a distance, their backs become a second pavement. Some are pitted with age. Some have copper-thread moss beginning on their shells, so that the boundary between animal and terrain has softened into a shared color. Some are so still that you would swear they were rocks, except for the faint tremor of their mouthparts or the almost imperceptible settling of one plate against another. When they do move, they do not hurry. They shift into the space beside them as though the space had always been prepared for them.

Their eyes, where they have eyes, are dark and shallow. There is no alarm in them, no bright animal calculation. They look without grasping. They regard you the way a hillside regards a stone: not with hostility, not with welcome, but with the simple fact that you have entered an already occupied extent. Their bodies give off a mild heat, dry and enclosed, and when you pass close enough to smell them, they carry the scent of warm dust, old rope, and a faint oily sweetness like sealed resin.

They are not all alike. Even within the same packed fields, there are variations. Some have plates arranged in overlapping rows, like flattened roof tiles. Some have shells so smooth they seem polished by hands. Some bear narrow ridges along their backs where wiregrass seeds or mineral flakes have lodged and begun to grow. A few are so old that their undersides have begun to adhere to the ground, not by injury but by patience, as if the final stage of their kind is to become part of the pavement they fed on for so long. Around these, smaller creatures gather: beetles moving along the seam where body meets stone, mites threading through the shadow under a belly, pale things pressing close to the warmth of a life that has almost stopped being separate from geology.

Above them, the air is inhabited too.

The drifting veils are the next vastness. They are thin, translucent membranes, buoyed by internal gases and charged surfaces, moving with a slowness that makes flight seem like a long-held thought. Some are no larger than a spread hand. Some are broad enough to cast shadows over whole herds. They resemble old skin, flattened jellyfish, sheets of dried tissue that have forgotten whether they were once part of something larger. Their edges lift and settle without wind. Beneath them trail fine filaments, almost invisible, which brush the ground and gather dust, static, shed particles, organic powder, and whatever falls from the packed bodies below.

They are not graceful in any gentle sense. There are too many of them. In some directions, they overlap until the sky becomes a layered ceiling of faint veils, one behind another, their forms blurring into a soft, dry canopy. Yet they do not darken the light. The illumination remains even and cool, as if the veils are not obstacles to it but merely another condition within it. Their shadows, when they cast shadows at all, are thin and uncertain. Sometimes a veil high above will cast a shadow that belongs to another veil lower down. Sometimes a veil will pass over you and you will feel nothing, though the dust on your clothes has been arranged into fine parallel lines.

The veils feed without hunger. Their trailing filaments collect what the world releases: shed plates from the grazers, dead mites, copper threads broken from the moss, microscopic organisms suspended in the dry air, mineral flakes too fine to see. Occasionally, one descends low enough that its underside brushes the backs of the herds. When that happens, tiny creatures rise to meet it, climbing onto its membrane as if onto a moving floor, riding it for a while before dropping away again. Some veils carry their own populations: colonies of pale mites living along the trailing threads, folded moth-like beings resting in the creases, minute organisms embedded in the membrane itself, so that each veil is not one body but a drifting district.

Between the herds and the veils, the air is also threaded with narrower lives. There are ribbon-like creatures that attach themselves to the black wiregrass and vibrate faintly, drawing static from the filaments. There are soft-bodied things in the cracks of the pavement that emerge only partly, their front ends lifted like blind questions while the rest remains anchored below. There are beetles with shells like chipped obsidian, moving through the spaces between hooves and plates with no apparent fear. There are mites in numbers too great to count, moving across the backs of larger animals as if those backs were provinces. There are rounded, eyeless things that seem to feed on pressure and warmth alone, settling wherever bodies touch and taking what little difference there is between one living heat and another.

Every animal is a landscape. Every shell is a floor. Every hide has tenants. Every crack is a corridor. The density of life here is not merely horizontal, stretched across the plain, but vertical: air, surface, underside, crevice, body, body within body, dust upon dust. Nothing is vacant. Even the spaces between creatures are full of smaller motion, too fine for casual sight. If you kneel and look closely, the ground becomes a city of minute limbs, shed fragments, slow crossings, and dry, patient mouths.

And yet there is no noise.

That is one of the deepest qualities of what lives there: it has been made quiet. The sounds are small and stay near their sources. The rasp of mouths on mineral filaments. The soft tick of carapace plates settling. The faint grain-noise of countless tiny limbs moving through dust. The whisper of a membrane overhead adjusting its position by fractions. If one of the larger beasts shifts, the sound is not a thud but a dry settling, like stone remembering its own weight. If something dies, if death can even be named here, it does not cry out. It loosens. It becomes available.

There are predators, though the word fits poorly. They are rare, long-legged, and quiet, with narrow heads and mouths made for precise removal rather than tearing. They move among the herds without causing disturbance. The grazers do not scatter when they pass. The veils do not rise away. Even the small creatures do not flee in any urgent sense. The predators do not chase. They select what is already prepared to be removed: a plate loosened by age, a filament weakened beyond repair, a body that has begun to merge too deeply with the ground and must be separated before it disturbs the balance. Sometimes they stand for a long time beside a grazer, their heads lowered, as if listening to the internal state of the animal. Then they make one careful motion, remove something almost too small to see, and move on.

Predation there feels like maintenance. It lacks triumph. It lacks fear. It is less the taking of life than the correction of an edge.

The vegetation, too, lives in this same finished way, though it is sparse enough to seem like memory. The black wiregrass stands in separate colonies, stiff and metallic, rooted in cracks, drawing up trace minerals and static charge. It does not sway. It does not bend easily. When touched, it feels more like fine wire than plant. The copper-colored nap in the shallow depressions is warm where the light gathers, dry and dense, almost furry, but not soft. The isolated shrubs with their wax-sealed tubes store no water; they store oils, solvents, and dense internal gels, and their branches rise in patient angles like instruments left behind by a vanished science. They are not beautiful in the ordinary sense. They are necessary. They are life reduced to the least possible gesture of persistence.

These plants, if they can be called plants, are not separate from the fauna. The wiregrass is used by the ribbon-creatures, by the beetles, by the small tethered beings that climb its filaments to reach charged air. The copper nap grows on stone and shell alike, spreading over old carapaces and abandoned tools until the distinction between living surface and mineral surface becomes a matter of degree. The wax-tube shrubs provide shelter for mites and nesting places for folded, moth-like creatures whose wings, if they have wings, are never fully opened. Nothing eats greedily. Nothing grows extravagantly. Everything is allowed only as much as the closed system permits.

But the people, if there are people, are the hardest part to describe.

They are rarely seen, and perhaps that is not because they hide but because they have learned how not to be noticed. Their marks are everywhere in small measure: low cairns, three stones supporting a fourth, curved ribs driven into the ground and stretched with dried membrane, cords of twisted wiregrass, flaked glass blades, bone needles, polished toggles, bowls carved from dense biological shell, pins of soft copper worn smooth by use. Yet these things are not evidence of settlement in any familiar sense. They are anchors. Agreements. Small treaties with a place that does not wish to be altered.

The people who made them, or still make them, are not like travelers who have arrived and imposed themselves. They seem to have become adapted to the tableland’s final equilibrium. Their bodies, when glimpsed, are dry and sealed. Their skin is not wet, not even faintly moist. It has the look of fine-grained hide or polished stone, cool to the eye, warm only where the light has rested on it. They do not sweat. They do not pant. Their breath makes no mist. Their joints move with a slow, enclosed precision, as if their fluids are heavy oils and brines rather than anything that would evaporate. They are not tall. They are not imposing. They have the proportions of beings who have forgotten how to make themselves large.

Their faces are difficult to remember afterward. Not because they are blank, but because they are not emphatic. Their eyes are dark and shallow, like the eyes of the grazers, but with something more in them: a patience that has become a kind of perception. Their mouths are small. Some have rasping plates instead of teeth. Some seem to eat little, or only rarely, taking in dust-cakes, shed membrane, oil-rich seeds from the wax-tube shrubs, mineral curds gathered from cracks, or the fine organic powder that falls from the veils. They carry no water. Their bodies do not ask for it. If they are injured, they do not bleed in any familiar way; they leak a clear, oily substance that smells faintly of old rope and warm stone, and smaller creatures are often seen gathering at the edge of it before the wound seals.

They make no fire. That is one of the clearest signs of their adaptation. Fire would be too much of an event. It would demand fuel, produce ash, create a center, leave a scar of heat and light in a place where all energy is already distributed too evenly. Instead, they use the world’s existing warmth: the faint heat stored in stone, the gentle warmth of grazing bodies, the places where copper nap has gathered light. Their shelters are not houses but temporary agreements with refuge. A curved frame, a stretched membrane, a few stones to hold the edges down. Enough to lie beneath. Enough to become a roost for moth-like creatures within a few days. Enough to be mistaken for geology soon after.

Their tools are humble because humility is survival there. A blade is flaked not to conquer but to open what is already open. A needle is made not to sew grand garments but to perform small repairs on membranes, cords, and shelters. A bowl is carved not for abundance but for holding a little dry food, a little dust, a few copper threads. Metal is rare and soft, used for pins and ornaments, for fastening rather than cutting. Nothing is made to dominate the landscape. Everything is made to pass through it without leaving a wound.

They do not build tall. They do not raise monuments. They do not carve their names into stone. If they mark anything, they do it with cairns so low that they barely interrupt the horizon. These cairns are not memorials. They are stabilizers. A stone placed here so that direction can be remembered without being claimed. A stone placed there so that a path can be recognized without being counted. Some cairns are so small they seem accidental, but their spacing is too deliberate for accident. They form interrupted lines, sparse constellations across the plain, leading nowhere in particular and yet preventing the land from becoming entirely indifferent.

Their language, if they have one, must be quiet. Perhaps it is made of taps on stone, of pressure against the ground, of small gestures with the hands, of shifts in posture that carry meaning without sound. If they speak aloud, their voices are likely dry and brief, quickly absorbed by the packed presence of the herds and the dust. They would not shout. Shouting would be a kind of violence. They would not answer distant calls, because they know that some calls are not calls but seams in the place speaking through itself. They would know the rules: do not build high, do not mark freshly, do not kill more than can be carried without dragging, do not count the herds too long, do not follow your own footprints if they appear ahead of you.

Some of them may no longer be separate from the herds.

This is not a metaphor, or not only a metaphor. In a place where reality has worn through, where two herds can occupy the same ground and pass through one another without interference, the boundary between person, animal, and stone becomes uncertain. There may be figures moving among the grazers whose pace is too slow for ordinary travelers and too deliberate for beasts. There may be shapes beside cairns that look like people until you realize they have not moved in a very long time, and then you see copper nap beginning along their shoulders and a beetle moving across their folded hands. There may be those who have chosen, or been chosen, to become part of the stillness: not dead, not sleeping, but settled into the final arrangement, their bodies gradually mineralizing, their awareness reduced to pressure and warmth and the long patience of stone.

If so, they are not tragic. The place does not permit tragedy in the ordinary sense. Tragedy requires a sharp edge, a fall, a before and after. Here, there is only continuation. What lives there continues in the last possible shape. What ceases to move becomes ground. What ceases to speak becomes silence. What ceases to be one thing becomes several things at once, worn smooth into coexistence.

That is where the reality of the inhabitants becomes difficult to trust.

Some of the herds are doubled. You may see two fields of grazers occupying the same space, one slightly displaced from the other, their bodies passing through one another without disturbance. Both cast shadows. Neither shadow matches the light exactly. Some animals have one shadow; some have two; some have none. Some veils are high overhead and also low enough to brush the ground far away, without contradiction. Some stones have more sides than their shapes permit. Some cairns are visible from angles that should not reveal them. Some footprints appear ahead of you, already ancient, and they may be yours.

The inhabitants of this place are not only the beings with bodies. They are also these settled impossibilities. The doubled herd lives. The mismatched shadow lives. The footprint that has not yet been made lives. The voice calling from very far away, if it is a voice, lives in the sense that it belongs to the place and must be answered by nothing. The wrongness there is not violent. It does not flicker or collapse. It has been eroded into position. It is part of the ecology.

Even the predators seem to understand this. They move through overlapping herds without confusion. They step around shadows that have no bodies and ignore bodies that cast none. When they find an animal that has begun to exist too much in two places at once, they do not attack it. They wait. They touch it lightly with their narrow mouths. Sometimes the animal becomes singular again. Sometimes it becomes stone. Sometimes it simply becomes less visible. The predators do not seem to distinguish strongly between these outcomes.

The largest inhabitant may be the plain itself.

Not as a god. Not as a mind in any human sense. But as a total living arrangement: the herds, the veils, the mites, the wiregrass, the cairns, the dust, the silence, the pressure, the old light, the settled wrongness. The tableland does not think, but it remembers by not changing. It does not speak, but it produces rules. It does not welcome, but it includes. Everything that enters it is eventually distributed into its system: heat, sound, motion, intention, even identity. If you stay long enough, you feel yourself being made available to it. Not consumed. Not attacked. Simply considered as material.

This is why the fauna does not flee from you. You are not recognized as danger. You are recognized as another object entering the field. If you move slowly, the grazers permit you to pass between them. If you kneel, the small creatures may approach, curious only in the sense that they orient toward warmth and pressure. If you remain still long enough, copper-thread moss may begin to consider your boots as a possible surface. If you speak, your voice is absorbed. If you weep, the tears will vanish almost at once, not by drama but by the dry air taking what little moisture you have offered. If you bleed, the scent of old rope and warm mineral will gather around you, and the smaller tenants of the plain will come to harvest the event.

The people, if they are watching, will not intervene. They will not rescue you, because rescue implies disturbance. They may leave a small cairn near you if you become too still. They may place a stone beside your body to hold a direction. They may take nothing from you except what is already ready to be taken. If you are fortunate, they will allow you to become ancient in the same instant as your own fresh mark. If you are not, they will simply wait until you are part of the available space.

What lives there, in the end, is not a single species and not a single image. It is the packed condition itself: dry mouths moving over mineral filaments; veils trailing dust from a sky without weather; beetles threading between hooves; predators making precise corrections; wiregrass standing in black colonies; copper nap warming over old shells; low cairns holding directions without naming them; quiet beings with oil in their veins and stone in their patience; doubled herds grazing through one another; shadows that have outlived their bodies; footprints arriving before their makers; the plain, immense and finished, continuing without increase or decay.

It is crowded because it has reached its last arrangement.

It is silent because sound has no future there.

It is alive because life, even at the end of all its possibilities, still prefers to persist in the smallest permitted form.
