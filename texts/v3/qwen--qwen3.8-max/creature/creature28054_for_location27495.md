Whoever lives there is not one kind, and perhaps not one life in the way an outsider would expect. What lives there is a congregation of cold, wet, patient beings, layered so deeply through the basin that the city cannot be separated from them except by a kind of violence. To ask who lives there is to ask who counts as a citizen in a place where residence is accretion, where a wall is also a rookery, where a canal is also a migration route, where a machine is also a nest, and where the sound of breathing is part of the architecture.

The first inhabitants, if one must order them, are the least visible. They are the microbial skins and mineral varnishes that coat the newly exposed rock. They live on dissolved stone, on heat rising from joints in the ground, on thin chemical gradients that would mean nothing to a surface creature accustomed to sunlight. They are black, blue-black, grey, sometimes faintly phosphorescent when disturbed, and they spread in films, crusts, threads, and gelatinous sheets. They do not soften the place. They make it slicker, colder, more mineral. But they are the foundation of everything that follows. Where their films thicken, other mouths arrive. Where their metabolisms exhale tiny amounts of warmth or change the chemistry of a surface, fungal filaments follow. These fungi are not like soft forest mushrooms. They are austere, slow, cold-tolerant systems: pale or dark threads binding wet silt, penetrating porous stone, breaking down waste, stitching the basin’s few organic materials into forms that can be reused. Together, microbes and fungi form the living skin of the city. They are not the city’s rulers, but they are its first and last laborers. Without them, the rock would remain sterile. With them, it becomes edible, inhabitable, slightly alive.

Above and through this living skin move the grazers. They are among the most numerous visible creatures in the basin. In the shallow marshy margins where mineral water spreads over gravel beds and terraces of wet silt, broad fronts of them pass slowly across the stone. They are flat-bodied, low, pale or slate-colored, with carapaces dulled by moisture and mineral dust. They do not look like insects, nor like crustaceans, nor like anything that evolved under a green sky. Their bodies seem shaped by pressure and scarcity: broad to distribute weight, flat to shelter under ledges, slow because heat is precious. Their mouths are soft rasps, more like wet velvet or muscular files than jaws, and they graze bacterial films from the rock with a patience that seems ceremonial. They leave polished trails behind them, long shining tracks that fill again with condensation. Those trails become routes for other creatures. They become channels for water. They become remembered lines in the city’s growing plan.

These grazers do not act alone. They move in grazing fronts, not because they have leaders, but because moisture, mineral bloom, and microbial scent create shared gradients. When one part of the front finds richer film, the others turn toward it over long intervals. When the air thickens with engineered fog, they pause and let the particles settle on their backs. When a warm pulse rises from a maintenance shaft, they spread themselves over the stone to absorb it. They are not decorative. They are maintenance. They keep surfaces clean enough for water to flow in predictable films. They keep microbial growth from choking narrow channels. Their own waste becomes food for fungi and smaller organisms. Their shed plates, broken shells, and dead bodies accumulate in hollows and become part of the sediment that other creatures carry into construction. Even their deaths are useful. In a place without soil, their bodies become a kind of slow mineral wealth.

In the deeper water, in canals, cisterns, reservoirs, and flooded vaults where the city’s hydrology gathers, other inhabitants take over. There are blind eel-like forms, thick-bodied and insulated by translucent fat, moving through the dark water in patient chains. They do not swim so much as circulate. Their movements are tuned to the flow of mineral-rich water, to pressure changes caused by condensation engines, and to the faint subsonic pulses of the city’s stabilizing fields. They draw water through their mouths and filter it, separating fine particles, microbial clusters, dissolved compounds, and suspended sediment. What they excrete is often useful: fine mineral mud, cleaned grit, calcium-rich slurry, or dark metallic silt that settles in predictable layers. Other creatures collect these deposits. Machines, or things very like machines, direct them into channels where they can become mortar, lining, or new stone.

These canal forms are among the quietest residents, but their silence is deceptive. Their bodies create subtle pressure waves as they move, and those waves are felt by other creatures. When a chain of them turns in a cistern, the water itself seems to change its mind. In the largest reservoirs, vast floating masses of filter-feeders lie near the surface, not quite motionless, their breathing creating faint waves that lap against the stone. Their presence alters the acoustics of the water. A newcomer listening near a cistern would hear not only drips and echoes, but a slow, liquid respiration, as if the reservoir were a sleeping organ. Some of these aquatic beings may be engineered, or at least shaped over long ages to live in concert with the city’s systems. Their fat stores heat. Their filtering keeps the water chemically stable. Their migrations through pipes and overflow channels help prevent blockages. They are fauna, but they are also infrastructure.

On the vertical surfaces of the city, life becomes denser and more architectural. The walls are not bare. They are inhabited in layered rookeries, colonies of creatures that cling, secrete, and accumulate. Some resemble shell-less mollusks fused to stone, their bodies pressed into crevices, their margins sealing against cold and dryness. Others are segmented and wormlike, furred with fine filaments that trap heat and moisture. These wall-dwellers excrete small beads of mineral cement, sometimes accidentally, sometimes in ways that seem too regular to be mere accident. The beads harden into crusts, ridges, and knobs. Over time, the colonies make the walls themselves change. A smooth fused surface becomes ribbed, knobby, organ-like. A retaining wall thickens where generations of cementing creatures have lived and died. A bridge pier becomes textured with living collars that strengthen it against vibration. The city does not merely contain these colonies; it is partly built by them.

The rookeries are crowded in a way that feels almost geological. Each layer of bodies rests upon older layers. Some creatures remain fixed for long periods, feeding on films that drift through the air or on dissolved minerals carried by condensation. Others move slowly across the colony surface, cleaning filaments, rearranging beads, or tending eggs laid in sheltered pockets. The sounds they make are small but immense in aggregate: tiny clicks, wet shifting, the faint tapping of hard parts against stone, the hollow percussion of thousands of bodies settling at once. When a district grows warmer, the rookeries respond. When a pressure field changes, the colonies reorient. When construction advances nearby, they may be incorporated into new surfaces rather than removed. To build in this place is not to clear the wall of life. It is to invite the wall’s existing life into a new shape.

In crevices, beneath eaves, inside maintenance shafts, and in the narrow gaps between stacked dwellings live the climbing things. They are perhaps the most unsettling residents to an outside imagination, because they suggest a kind of communal intelligence without resembling anything familiar. They are eyeless. Their bodies are not mammalian, not reptilian, not insectile in any simple sense, though they borrow qualities from all those possibilities. They are squat, insulated, low-slung, with gripping limbs or pads adapted to wet stone. Some have fine filaments like fur. Others have chitinous plates that overlap loosely as they move. They rest in masses, their bodies rising and falling almost imperceptibly with the slow rhythm of the settlement. They conserve heat by clustering. They communicate by touch, by pressure, by chemical traces, and by short sounds too low or too soft for an untrained ear. They are not frantic. They do not swarm in the manner of panicked insects. They move with etiquette, with pause, with constant adjustment to one another.

These climbing things may be among the closest things the city has to caretakers, though they do not rule it. They clear dead matter from crevices. They carry mineral pellets to places where colonies need reinforcement. They groom one another and remove fungal overgrowth from their own bodies. They tend eggs in warm recesses, moving them slowly from colder zones to warmer ones when the city’s heat systems pulse. They seem to remember routes not by sight but by texture, echo, and scent. A wall worn smooth by their passage becomes a road. A ledge polished by their resting bodies becomes a shelter. If there is something like culture in the basin, it may begin here: not in language or ornament, but in repeated patterns of care, in the maintenance of warmth, in the communal tending of young, in the shared memory of safe places.

The air itself is inhabited. There are no bright wings, no songs of daylight birds. Instead, heavier, slower aerial forms move through the darkness between towers and terraces. They are wide-bodied, dark-furred or dark-shelled, with membranes or wing surfaces that do not beat quickly but shift with long, deliberate strokes. They navigate by sound and pressure. They feel the shape of the basin through echo. They sense thermal leaks rising from deeper heat systems, condensation engines, resonant fields, and inhabited vaults. Many of them barely fly in the ordinary sense. They glide from one warm shaft to another, riding invisible columns of air, using the city’s own metabolism as weather. When they turn, their wings make low percussive thumps. When they call to one another, their voices are not melodious but deep, sometimes bell-like, sometimes hollow, sometimes more felt than heard.

These aerial creatures connect the districts in ways the ground-bound cannot. They move above the crowded ledges, through the thin cold air, crossing open spaces where only sound reveals the far walls. Their calls return from cliffs, towers, unfinished viaducts, and reservoir surfaces, giving them a map made of reverberation. Some species are solitary, drifting like dark buoys through the basin. Others move in loose groups, their wing-thumps answering one another. Smaller aerial fauna swarm near condensation engines and fog-generating structures, feeding on aerosols, spores, microbial particles, and each other. These swarms are not beautiful in a gentle sense. They are necessary. When they move, they make a soft rushing noise, like sand poured endlessly from a height. Their bodies become part of the city’s airborne ecology, distributing microscopic matter, cleaning certain volumes of air, and feeding larger predators when those predators can catch them.

The fauna has also entered the infrastructure so thoroughly that the infrastructure seems partly biological. Drains are lined with resting bodies. Pipes contain slow migrations of eggs or larvae, carried by water or moving deliberately through moist channels. Reservoirs hold floating masses whose breathing creates surface waves. Stairwells are polished by the passage of countless feet, claws, bellies, and dragging tails. Machines pause when large bodies move through them. Channels widen into resting pools not because of an engineer’s drawing, but because generations of animals have needed those places to stop, breed, molt, or shelter from colder currents. Certain humming structures seem designed as much for incubation as for stabilizing pressure fields. Certain acoustic towers are not only markers but roosts. Certain cisterns are not only water storage but breeding grounds. The boundary between civic construction and habitat has become thin, then porous, then nearly irrelevant.

There may be larger beings in the basin, though they are fewer and harder to distinguish from the city itself. They move so slowly that their actions appear as geological events. A section of wall shifts over many hours. A ramp settles into a new angle. A fused sheet of stone becomes seamless where a broad, heavy body has passed over it, secreting mineral compounds or activating fields too subtle to see. These larger residents might be animals, or they might be living machines, or they might be something produced by long coexistence between fauna and technology. They have no faces in any ordinary sense. Their sensing surfaces are membranes, pressure organs, chemical receptors, and resonant chambers. They do not speak, but they modify the city’s sounds. When they move through a district, the local hum changes pitch. Water flows differently around them. Smaller fauna gather at their edges, feeding on particles stirred up by their passage, cleaning their surfaces, or simply moving along the warmth their bodies retain.

If these large beings have intention, it is not human intention. They do not command. They negotiate. They respond to stress in stone, to chemical imbalance in water, to acoustic interference, to overcrowding, to the need for a new resting pool or a widened channel. They may be what guides the mineral fog and the microscopic builders that deposit calcium, silica, and darker metallic compounds. Or they may only be part of the process, another layer of living machinery without a center. It is difficult to tell. That difficulty is important. In this place, intelligence may not reside in a single head, a single face, a single voice. It may be spread across rookeries, migrations, pressure fields, microbial films, and the long memory of worn stone.

The ecology is strange but coherent. Energy enters through chemistry, heat, and the advanced systems that maintain the settlement. Microbial films oxidize minerals. Fungal threads break down organic waste. Fauna grazes the films, preys on other fauna, scavenges dead matter, and feeds on the outputs of the city’s engines. Nothing is ornamental. Every swarm, rookery, grazing herd, filter-chain, and wall colony has a function, even if that function is not immediately obvious. Some animals maintain walls by depositing mineral crusts. Others clean channels by consuming slime. Others generate weak electric fields, perhaps for navigation, perhaps for communication, perhaps because the city harvests those fields as part of its stabilization. Some creatures may be partly engineered, but “engineered” is a word that fits poorly here, because there is no clear workshop, no separate hand. The city’s technologies behave like weather, like geology, like living mathematics. The animals have become part of that behavior. The technologies have become part of the animals.

Sound is the medium through which most of this life is known. The basin is loud, but its loudness is not frantic. It is slow, layered, persistent. Water speaks everywhere: dripping from ledges, trickling through joints, sluicing down gutters, lapping in cisterns, hissing where it meets warmer stone. Beneath that is the low continuous voice of the city’s systems: resonance engines, pressure fields, condensation lattices, subsonic stabilizers. Their tones are felt in the chest as much as heard. Then comes the fauna: the wet shifting of countless bodies, clicking mandibles, soft calls, wing-thumps, the hollow percussion of rookeries settling, the strange bell-like cries of blind aerial creatures answering one another across the dark. When one district shifts, another answers. The noise rolls through the basin in long waves. The inhabitants live inside that noise. They are made by it and make it in turn.

Because of this, whoever lives there lives acoustically before visually. Identity, territory, safety, danger, and direction are matters of echo, vibration, scent, pressure, and taste. A wall is recognized not by color but by the way it returns a call. A cistern is known by the pitch of water against its sides. A rookery announces itself by the accumulated percussion of thousands of small bodies. A thermal shaft is found by the change in air pressure and the faint drone of rising warmth. The inhabitants do not need light to know one another. They know one another by the disturbances they make in the shared medium of the city. To be alone here would not mean being unseen; it would mean making no echo, producing no pressure, leaving no scent, causing no water to move. That is the true emptiness.

The pace of life matches the place. It is slow. Water arrives by condensation and seepage. Rock is exposed gradually. Construction advances in minute accretions. Fauna breeds, molts, migrates short distances, and dies in accumulated layers. Machines operate over long cycles. The loudest sounds are often slow sounds: a deep groan of settling stone, a long call across the dark, the endless dripping that wears basins into bowls. The inhabitants do not rush. They are not idle, but their activity has a different tempo. A grazing front may take hours to cross a terrace. A wall colony may spend generations thickening a single curve. An aerial creature may drift for a long time between calls. The large slow beings, if they move at all, may be mistaken for parts of the architecture until their motion becomes undeniable.

There is little room here for softness in the sentimental sense. The place is cold, wet, crowded, and indifferent to comfort as a sunlit world understands it. Yet it is not without tenderness. Tenderness appears as heat shared in clustered bodies, as eggs moved to warmer recesses, as grooming, as the patient clearing of a blocked channel, as the way machines pause when living bodies pass through them. It appears in the polish of a stair worn smooth by countless safe passages. It appears in the shape of a resting pool widened over time. It appears in the acoustic architecture of quiet zones for incubation and loud zones for construction, where one form of life makes room for another by altering the sound. Nothing is decorative, but many things are careful.

If there are beings that could be called people, they are not people in the shape of surface walkers. They would have to be shaped by pressure, not daylight. Their bodies would be insulated, low, patient, adapted to cold and scarcity. Their senses would be tuned to echo and vibration more than color. Their societies would be built around warmth, water flow, acoustic territory, and the management of living infrastructure. They would not separate themselves from the animals around them, because the animals are labor, population, weather, and architecture all at once. They would not speak only with voices, because voices here must carry through damp stone and crowded air. They would tap, hum, press, scent-mark, and listen. Their history would not be written on pages but accumulated in layers: mineral beads, polished trails, shed carapaces, fused stone, fossilized colonies, chemical strata in cistern walls. Their memory would be the memory of accretion.

But perhaps it is misleading to search for people at all. Perhaps the true resident of the basin is not a single species. Perhaps it is the composite life of the place: the microbial skin, the grazing fronts, the canal filterers, the wall colonies, the crevice clusters, the aerial drifters, the infrastructure migrants, the large slow mason-beings, and the technologies that behave like living systems. Perhaps the city’s citizen is the entire basin considered as one breathing, dripping, echoing body. That citizen has no single face. It has countless mouths, none of which are human, and countless ears, none of which require daylight. It thinks in migrations, in pressure gradients, in thermal pulses, in the slow adjustment of stone and sound. It remembers by building over its own past. It speaks in water, echo, and low mechanical drone.

To live there is to be part of that composite. A body living there would not stand apart from the city. It would be wetted by the same fog, chilled by the same air, guided by the same echoes, crowded by the same fauna, and sustained by the same mineral chemistry. Its presence would change the sound slightly. Its heat would be noticed by nearby creatures. Its movement would polish a stone or disturb a film or cause a pressure field to adjust. If it remained long enough, it would become part of the city’s texture: a route, a warmth, a scent, a worn place, a call answered in the dark.

So whoever or whatever lives there is a multitude. It is the cold film on basalt. It is the grazer leaving a shining trail. It is the blind eel-chain turning in a canal. It is the wall colony adding bead after bead of cement. It is the eyeless climber resting in a crevice with a hundred others. It is the wide-winged drifter calling across the basin. It is the swarm that sounds like falling sand. It is the egg migration in the pipes. It is the floating filter-mass breathing in the reservoir. It is the large slow being that may be animal, machine, or both. It is the acoustic field they make together. It is the city learning its own shape through them.

If one must picture the inhabitant of this place, do not picture a single figure standing in the dark. Picture instead a crowded, breathing, dripping darkness in which every surface is touched, every channel is used, every echo is occupied, and every drop of water passes through many lives before it settles. Picture a cold, loud, slow ecology of stone and bodies, advanced but not sterile, strange but not chaotic, unfinished but not incomplete. The true citizens are the countless lives moving through the walls, the channels, and the air. The city does not merely house them. It is, in large part, what they do.
