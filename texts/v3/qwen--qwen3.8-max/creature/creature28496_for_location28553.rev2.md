What lives there is pale, patient, and distributed. It does not fill the basin in crowds. It appears in figures and pulses: a single mirror-keeper crossing a white floor, a line of crustaceans moving along a newly wet seam, a handful of children learning to make their hands mean water, mist, wait.

The basin is inhabited by people, by animals, by living films and fungal felts, and by the habits that keep all of them from destroying one another. Nothing there lives alone.

## The people

The human inhabitants are human in shape, but altered by long agreement with the basin. They are neither huge nor stunted. Their bodies are compact and sinewy, built for low ceilings, long walks, kneeling beside pools, and moving quietly over stone. Their feet are broad, the soles thickened and soft in a way that lets them cross wet rock without slipping and without making much sound. They walk as if they have always known the ground would listen.

Their skin is pale, but not uniformly white. It carries faint ash-blue, gray, and bone-colored undertones, and in the mildest light it can look almost like polished limestone dusted with fine salt. Mineral powder settles into their hair, their clothing, the creases of their hands. They smell of wet chalk, cold salt, hide, shell, fish, and fungal felt. It is not an unpleasant smell. It is clean in a severe way, like the air after thin rain in a stone chamber.

Their eyes are large and light-sensitive, adapted to the basin’s diffuse brightness. They are not the eyes of creatures born into total blackness, because the basin is not black. They are the eyes of creatures who live in cold, reflected light, where glare can arrive suddenly from a wet wall or a shifted mirror panel. They blink often. Many wear visors or brow-bands made of layered hide or thin shell when working in the light-yards. Their irises are pale: gray, bone-white, faint blue, sometimes almost colorless. In certain angles, when light passes through the shallow mirror pools, their eyes seem to catch the brightness and hold it for a moment too long.

Their hands are their most noticeable feature. The hands of basin-dwellers are wide, strong at the wrist, exact in the fingers. They are always doing something: testing damp, smoothing hide, adjusting cord, tapping rhythm, arranging shells, feeling stone for seepage. Their fingertips are calloused but sensitive. They can tell whether a floor will take a footprint, whether a crust is dry enough to harvest, whether a mirror panel has drifted from its angle, whether the air is about to bead into mist.

They do not wear much that is ornate, but what they wear is chosen carefully. Clothing is made from cured hide, stitched fish skin, shell plates, fungal felt, and woven or twisted fiber. Garments are tied rather than fastened with hard fittings, because everything must be adjustable, removable, and repairable. In damp districts they wear capes of pale felted fungal thread, which shed mist and dry slowly. In the water courts they wear close layers that can be loosened quickly if a channel fills. In the light-yards they wear smooth shell discs or polished calcite fragments at shoulder or breast, not chiefly for beauty, but to test reflection. A mirror-keeper’s clothing often bears tiny scratches and pale stains from abrasive sand.

Ornaments exist, but they are quiet. Beads of chert, fish vertebrae, small polished shells, fragments of old antler, knots of sinew, thin plates of shell worn until they gleam. Nothing jangles. Nothing is meant to catch the eye by noise or violent color. The beauty of their adornment lies in texture and restraint: a row of pale shells arranged by size, a cord worked in a pattern that identifies a household or a water-line, a scrap of translucent fish skin sewn into a hood so that light passes through it like cold fire.

They are not a numerous people. Their population is spread thin across the basin because the basin itself is generous only in patches. A district may seem empty for a long time, then suddenly reveal three or four figures working near a new seep, their bodies small against the pale stone. They do not experience emptiness as loneliness. They understand space the way the basin teaches it: as something that fills and empties according to moisture, light, and animal movement.

## Their signs and rules

The people of the basin speak, but not loudly. Their voices are low and sparing. Much of their language is made with the hands: signs for water coming, light blocked, crust ready, pool filling, animal near, path changed, dwelling must move, wait, listen, cover, open. Some signs are very small, only a movement of fingers against the thigh. Others are broad and slow, used across wide plazas where figures are far apart.

They also communicate by tapped rhythm on bone, shell, or stone. These taps are not drumming in any festive sense. They are precise signals. A sequence might mean that a cistern is accepting water. Another might warn that a hollow is flooding faster than expected. A third might call a work crew to adjust a mirror before the light path shifts away from a drying rack. Because stone and damp absorb sound, these tapped messages carry oddly and do not carry far in the same way surface sounds do. The people know exactly where to strike, how hard, and how often.

Messages can also be left as arrangements of objects. A row of shells on a sill. A cord knotted and hung across a ramp. A chert flake set point-down in dust. A polished pebble placed at the edge of a water channel. Such placements are not mysterious to them. They are part of the ordinary grammar of the city. A child learns to read a threshold before it learns to make a full sentence with its hands.

Loudness is considered clumsy, but not forbidden. A shout is reserved for danger: a collapse, a sudden flood, a person lost in a mist-thickened hollow, an animal panic near a fragile breeding pool. Otherwise, loudness is a waste of the basin’s quiet and a disturbance to the fauna. Children are not punished for noise so much as corrected by being made to watch what noise misses. They are taught that a sudden sound can scatter fish, drive grazers from a crust, or frighten scavengers from a cleanup they need to complete.

Their rules are shaped by light and water.

Light is common. No person may block a light-path without cause. If a shelter must be raised where a beam crosses, a mirror-keeper is consulted, and another path is found or the shelter is set aside. To steal light is not a crime of theft alone; it is a failure of maintenance. The basin lives by shared brightness.

Water has right of way. If a seep opens beneath a platform, the platform moves. If a channel returns to an old route, the route is abandoned or bridged. No dwelling argues with moisture. The people do not regard this as hardship. They consider it good sense. A house that resists water will be broken by water. A house that moves with water may stand for generations.

Ancient stone is not cut wantonly. The basin’s rock is already finished. It has been worn into its final softness. New construction sits lightly on it, rests on adjustable feet, leans without deeply anchoring, or uses older holes and grooves inherited from previous ages. If a stone must be shaped, it is shaped slowly, with abrasive sand and patient pressure, not by violent breaking. The people dislike making fresh scars. They prefer to fit.

## Their work

The inhabitants are always working, but their work has the quality of tuning rather than conquering.

Mirror-keepers are among the most respected. They maintain the angled stone faces, shell-bright panels, and shallow pools that pass daylight through the basin. They wipe dust from reflective surfaces with soft hide and fungal cloth. They test angles by holding up polished shell fragments. They know which panels brighten a district at mid-depth of day, which ones fail when mist gathers, and which must be covered during thin rain to prevent scattering. Their hands are often stained white. Their eyes are trained to notice a dimming before most people feel it.

Water-tenders read damp. They watch the ceiling ribs, the sink-throats, the hairline cracks, the floors that darken slightly before a seep arrives. They maintain grooved catchment stones, dew-channels, hide-lined troughs, and shallow basins. They know the basin’s memory: where water liked to go a hundred years ago, where it may go again, where it has not gone in living recall but might still return. They do not command water. They invite it, divert it, delay it, and give it room.

Felt-tenders cultivate fungal threads in damp cracks and alcoves. They encourage pale wool-like mats to spread over stone, harvesting only what is needed and leaving enough for regrowth. They know which felts are good for insulation, which for cordage, which for lining containers, which are edible when prepared carefully, and which should be left alone because they support grazing fauna. Their work is slow and intimate. They often kneel with their faces close to stone, breathing quietly, checking moisture with the back of a hand.

Crustacean-herders guide the blind, raking creatures of the shallow pans. They do not drive them as surface herders drive animals across open land. They lead them with moisture. A damp line laid across stone can turn a herd. A dry line can stop it. A tap on a shell-plate can call attention. A change in mineral smell can move them toward a feeding pan. The herders carry long poles tipped with bone or shell, not to strike, but to touch water and redirect movement.

Fish-keepers tend the deeper cisterns. They maintain breeding pools, remove excess silt, protect young fish from larger predators, and time the harvests so that the schools are not exhausted. They know fish by movement rather than by individual markings, because many of the fish look alike: pale, unpigmented, quick in reflection, nearly weightless in the water. They use nets of twisted fungal fiber and sinew, and traps made from shell ribs and stretched hide.

Bone-shapers, shell-workers, hide-scrapers, net-makers, dust-watchers, path-tenders, stone-settlers: all these roles exist, but they are not always separate people. In a sparse city, one person may be a mirror-keeper in the morning, a felt-tender at midday, and a fish-net mender when mist makes the light too uncertain for delicate reflection work. Their skills are layered. Their attention is their trade.

## The living surfaces

Before the larger fauna, before the people, the basin is inhabited by living skins.

Microbial crusts spread across the pans in gray-white sheets, bone-pale patches, ash-blue films, and faintly luminous slicks where moisture lies longest. They are the first food. They coat the stone, soften it, feed the grazers, and create the conditions for everything else. They are not plants. They have no leaf, stem, root, or pollen. They are surface-life, patient and wet, responsive to light and damp.

Fungal felts grow in damper cracks and shaded hollows. They look like pale wool or soft mold pressed into cloth. Some are thin as breath. Some thicken into cushions. They break down dead chitin, old hide, fish waste, fallen organic dust, and the remains of the basin’s inhabitants. They are essential. Without them, the basin would choke on its own leavings. The people respect them with the same seriousness that surface cultivators might give to soil.

These living surfaces are tended like fields, though they are not fields. The people do not clear them; they encourage them. They scatter fine organic dust into pans where it is needed. They adjust water films so that crusts do not dry too long. They move grazing animals off exhausted patches and return them later. They harvest microbial sheets carefully, leaving enough behind to regrow. They dry some sheets into thin, pliable food-leathers. They cultivate others purely as animal feed.

The basin’s agriculture, if it can be called agriculture, is a husbandry of surfaces. It is not based on sowing green rows but on knowing where life will thicken next.

## The fauna

The animals of the basin are abundant but uneven. The city can seem almost lifeless, then a seep opens, a mist descends, a pool fills, and creatures appear as if summoned from the stone.

### Blind crustaceans

In the shallow water pockets, blind crustaceans move with slow, deliberate legs. They are broad-bodied, pale as milk, with long antennae and flattened limbs suited to raking microbial crust from stone. The people call them by signs rather than names, but if one were to translate, they might be “rake-claws” or “water-scrapers.” They do not see. They taste and touch. Their antennae sweep ahead of them, reading mineral traces, animal residue, and the chemical signature of fresh moisture.

They are central to the basin’s food web. They graze the living crusts, stir the bottoms of pans, and become food for fish, scavengers, and people. Their molted shells are prized. The people collect empty molts rather than killing unnecessarily when they can, because a molted shell gives material without reducing the herd. The shells are light, tough, and curved. They become scoops, trays, visors, armor plates, and pieces of portable shelter. When the crustaceans move in large numbers across newly wet stone, their rasping legs make one of the basin’s characteristic sounds: a faint dry hiss, like sand remembering water.

### Translucent worms

When the air changes pressure, translucent worms rise from cracks and wet marl. They are almost glassy in the reflected light, their bodies faintly visible as moving threads. The people watch them closely, because their arrival often means that water is about to shift. They are not harvested in great numbers. They are indicators, and also food for fish and some larger grazers.

Children are taught to notice them before they notice anything else. A pool may look unchanged, but if glass-worms are rising along its edge, the basin is preparing for something. The worms teach the people to expect change without demanding proof.

### Plate-grazers and shelfbacks

Flat, pale creatures like eyeless beetles move over wet stone, grazing on microbial crust. Some are small, no larger than a hand, with soft plates and rasping mouths. Others are broader and slower, with overlapping shell-plates and thick, fleshy undersides. These larger grazers are sometimes herded into temporary pens made from stone ribs and stretched hide.

They are not beasts of burden in the ordinary sense. They do not pull heavy loads across long distances, and they are not ridden. But they are useful living tools. When a pan has grown thick with old crust and needs cleaning before a new water pulse, the people guide them across it. When a district must be kept clear of excessive film that might rot in a dry spell, the grazers are moved through it in controlled bursts.

Their shed plates are collected and used as shallow dishes, wall panels, and protective coverings. Their hides, where hide can be taken without waste, are cured and stretched. Their droppings feed fungal felts. They are among the closest things the basin has to grazing animals, though what they graze is not grass but stone-skin.

### Cistern fish

In the deeper pools and cisterns, fish without pigment turn in silent schools. They are not beautiful in a surface sense. They are beautiful in the way of pale things adapted to stillness: narrow, sleek, almost transparent at the fins, their bodies flashing when light passes through water. Some have eyes reduced to dim shadows beneath the skin. Some sense pressure and movement more than shape. They feed on worms, larvae, tiny crustaceans, and flakes of microbial matter.

The people manage their breeding carefully. A cistern can support many fish for a time, then suddenly become too poor if moisture fails. Fish-keepers know which pools are stable, which are temporary, and which must be allowed to rest. When fish are harvested, almost nothing is discarded. Flesh is dried on racks in light-shafts or cured in mineral brines. Bones become needles, awls, fishhooks, and tapped signal rods. Skins become translucent panels, pouches, and patches for hide shelters. Sinew becomes cord. Even the waste is returned to fungal mats or breeding pans.

The fish are one of the reasons the city remains sparse. They require water, and water is not constant. The people cannot concentrate too heavily where fish cannot live.

### Wingless damp-crawlers

Near wet walls, wingless insects cluster in pale knots. They are soft-bodied, slow, and pale gray or ash-blue. Their larvae feed on fungal felt and dead organic matter. They are not greatly valued as food, but they are important. They help break down the basin’s smaller deaths: old crustacean limbs, discarded hide scraps, fungal overgrowth, shed skins.

Their clusters also tell the people where moisture is strongest. A wall thick with damp-crawlers is a wall worth watching. A wall abandoned by them may be drying too deeply. The people do not exterminate them unless they threaten breeding pools. They are part of the basin’s quiet digestion.

### Soft-footed scavengers

The larger scavengers come down from higher galleries when moisture returns. They are among the most distinctive animals of the basin. They are low-bodied, pale, cautious, and mostly mute. Their heads are blunt, their eyes closed or reduced to useless pale seams, their noses and whiskers highly sensitive. Their feet are soft-padded, allowing them to move across stone without sharp sound.

They are neither fully wild nor fully domestic. The people tolerate them because they clean. They eat carrion, waste, dead fish, spoiled crust, and things that would otherwise rot in hidden hollows. In a city without ordinary soil, such creatures are necessary. They are the basin’s undertakers and sanitation workers.

But they must be managed. If they enter breeding pools too eagerly, they are guided away with taps, scent-baits, or barriers of hide. If they become too bold near drying racks, children are sent to move them with slow hand-signals and soft pressure. The people do not hate them. They understand that scavengers are part of the same patience that governs everything else.

Sometimes a scavenger will adopt a district for a long time, sleeping in a dry alcove and appearing whenever waste accumulates. It is not petted. It is recognized. If it dies, its body is given back to the basin like any other dead thing.

### Hunters and smaller predators

There are hunters too, though fewer than grazers and scavengers. Blind white things wait in upper cracks for insects. Pale leech-like creatures lie in the edges of pools. Quick-footed rakers prey on worms and young crustaceans. Their numbers are kept low by the basin’s limited abundance. They are not exterminated unless they threaten a managed pool, because predators keep the smaller populations from surging too wildly.

The people watch them the way they watch weather. A hunter is not evil. A hunter is a limit. Limits are useful.

## Pens, herds, and husbandry

The animal pens of the city are not permanent corrals. They are temporary arrangements: stone ribs set in old sockets, hide screens tensioned with cord, shallow barriers that can be moved when water shifts. A pen may hold young crustaceans protected from predators. It may contain a herd of plate-grazers resting after feeding. It may keep scavengers out of a breeding pool for a few days. It may shelter fish in a cistern during a dry interval.

The people do not keep animals simply to own them. They keep them to place them correctly. A herd is a tool for cleaning stone. A breeding pool is a promise of future food. A pen is a pause, not a prison. If moisture changes, the pen changes. If the animals need to move, the people move them. If the people need to leave, the pen is folded and carried away.

This husbandry has no fields. It has no barns of timber. It has no fences meant to last forever. It is based on timing. The people know when crustaceans molt, when fish spawn, when worms rise, when fungal felt thickens, when scavengers descend. Their expertise is not domination but rhythm.

## The taste of their life

Their food is pale, cool, and careful.

They eat microbial sheets dried into thin leathers. They eat fungal felt prepared in ways that require patience: soaked, scraped, softened in mineral water, sometimes fermented in sealed hide pouches. They eat crustacean flesh cured in brine or dried over light-warmed stone. They eat fish, often dried or thinly sliced. They eat the eggs of certain pool creatures when abundant. They do not waste. Even the less pleasant parts are rendered into pastes, pressed into cakes, or set aside for animal feed.

They do not farm in the surface sense. They do not crave bread, fruit, or green things, because these are not part of their memory. Their idea of plenty is a wet pan thick with living crust, a cistern flashing with fish, a fungal alcove heavy with pale wool, a drying rack full before mist returns. Their idea of scarcity is not hunger alone but dryness: too long without seepage, too many pools failing, too much dust and not enough moisture to bind it.

They are not starving. They are not affluent. They are balanced. Their existence depends on noticing small signs early enough to adjust.

## Their homes and movements

Their dwellings are low, open, and movable. Platforms rest on adjustable stone feet. Screens can be lowered or raised. Hide shelters are loosened and re-erected. Storage courts are kept on slight rises or behind moisture barriers. Sleeping places are arranged so that light can reach them without striking the eyes too harshly. A household may have a small mirror panel positioned to catch the first pale beam of the day and carry it inward.

Because the city is sparse, homes are not pressed tightly together. There is room between them. Air moves. Mist gathers and disperses. Sound does not pile up. A person can stand inside a dwelling and hear the tiny sounds of the basin: beads of water falling from a ceiling rib, a crustacean leg rasping over stone, the soft slap of a hide sheet adjusting in damp air, the distant tap of a signal.

They move often, but not frantically. If a seep opens under a platform, the household does not lament. It begins to disassemble. Cords are untied. Hide sheets are folded. Storage jars are lifted. Stone feet are repositioned. The dwelling is carried a short distance and settled again. Sometimes this happens several times in a generation. Sometimes it happens within a single season. The people do not measure permanence by how long a structure stands in one place. They measure it by how well it can continue.

Travel between districts is by foot, by low hand-drawn carts, or by shallow rafts where temporary water allows. The carts are made of bone, shell, hide, and polished stone, with runners or disc-wheels shaped from hard materials. They are quiet. They are not meant for speed. Rafts are used only briefly, when a pan fills enough to float them. A journey may become impossible not because of distance, but because water has withdrawn from a route. The people accept this. They turn aside. They take another path. They wait.

## Children, elders, and the shape of a life

Children in the basin are taught attention before they are taught labor.

They learn to watch dust in light shafts. They learn to feel changes in air on their cheeks. They learn to recognize the first movement of glass-worms, the smell of a new seep, the sound of water finding an old channel. They learn hand-signs early. A child’s first useful sign is often water. Their second may be wait.

They are not allowed to make careless noise, but they are not made silent through fear. They are shown what silence permits. A quiet child can see animals move. A quiet child can hear a cistern fill. A quiet child can notice a mirror drifting before the light fails. They learn that their smallness is useful. They learn to step where footprints will erase.

Elders become specialists of perception. Some can tell where water will gather by the feel of stone under their palms. Some can predict a mist-fall by the behavior of damp-crawlers. Some know the old routes of channels so well that they can walk a dry floor and point to where water ran three generations ago. They are not necessarily rulers. Their authority is practical. When an elder says move, the city moves. When an elder says wait, the city waits.

Names among the basin people are not usually spoken aloud. A name is a hand-shape, a tap-rhythm, or a small arrangement of objects. A mother might call her child with two taps on a fish-bone and a sign like falling mist. A lover might make a name by touching the wrist and forming a private gesture. A mirror-keeper’s name might include the angle of a beam. A water-tender’s name might be the sound of a drop striking a pool.

When a person dies, they are not hidden. They are returned. The exact custom varies by district, but the principle is the same: the basin takes back what it can. Some dead are laid in dry alcoves where mineral crust slowly covers them. Others are placed in shallow pans where scavengers and water reduce them to bone. Bones may be ground into tools if the family permits, not as desecration but as continuation. If not, they are returned to the stone. Nothing is wasted. Even death is a form of maintenance.

## The animals and people together

The most striking thing about life in the basin is how closely people and animals respond to the same signals.

When worms rise, fish-keepers prepare. When crustaceans shift toward a new damp line, herders follow. When scavengers descend from upper galleries, waste is set in deliberate places so that cleaning will happen where it is needed. When fish turn toward a newly filled cistern, net-makers are not far behind. When wingless insects cluster thickly on a wall, felt-tenders know the wall will stay damp and may be seeded with fungal thread.

The people do not think of themselves as separate from this web. They are inside it. Their waste feeds fungal mats. Their dead feed scavengers. Their careful harvesting keeps grazing populations from exhausting a crust. Their movement opens paths for animals and closes others. Their mirrors change where light lies, and where light lies changes which surfaces live most strongly.

They have learned that even a small action can alter the basin. A blocked light-path can dry a pan too quickly. A loud noise can scatter a school of fish at the wrong moment. A careless footprint can seal a micro-crust before it spreads. A well-placed stone can guide water into a neglected trough. They live with this consequence constantly. It has made them precise.

## The temperament of the inhabitants

The people of the basin are calm, but not passive. They are capable of urgency. When water arrives suddenly, the whole city can move at once: racks turned, screens lowered, pens shifted, nets spread, children guided to safe terraces, hides thrown over stored goods. They work quickly and quietly. There is no panic unless something has gone badly wrong. Their emergencies are handled with the same hushed intensity as their ordinary labor.

They are not emotionally cold. Their affections are expressed in small ways: a hand-sign repeated gently, a polished shell offered, a path cleared for someone older, a mirror adjusted to bring light into a sleeping corner, a portion of dried fish saved for a child. They value reliability. They value noticing. They value the person who repairs a cord before it breaks and moves a dwelling before water forces it.

They are also stubborn. Their stubbornness is not loud. It is the stubbornness of people who have decided that certain things will not be violated: light will not be stolen, water will not be fought, ancient stone will not be scarred without need, noise will not be wasted, life will not be taken without use. If an outsider were to arrive from the dry surface above, the basin people might seem strange not because they are hostile, but because they are so deliberate. They would not shout. They would not rush. They would watch the stranger’s feet, hands, and breath to see whether the stranger understood enough to be trusted.

## What lives there, in the end

What lives there is a pale urban ecology made of people, fauna, living films, and careful attention.

The people are the city’s hands. The animals are its appetite and movement. The microbial crusts and fungal felts are its slow foundation. The water is its restless will. The light is its common inheritance. The stone is its memory.

None of these lives alone. The basin survives because its inhabitants are tuned to one another. The city does not impose itself on the underground landscape. It negotiates with it. It slides with change. It retunes itself after every mist, seep, flood, and dry interval. It remains sparse, bright, cool, and quietly alive.

If one stood in a wide plaza there, one might see only a few figures at first: a mirror-keeper adjusting a shell-bright panel, a child carrying a folded hide, a herder tapping bone beside a slow line of pale crustaceans. Then mist would gather, light would shift, a pool would fill, and the basin would suddenly seem full of life — not loud, not crowded, not green, but abundant in its own severe and patient way.

That is what lives there: a people and a fauna and a living mineral skin, all keeping faith with the quiet turbulence of an ancient bright underworld.
