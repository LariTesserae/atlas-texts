What lives there is not one creature, not one species, not one shape that can be cleanly separated from the rest. The question itself—*who lives here?*—arrives too simply. The place is inhabited the way a lung is inhabited by breath: thoroughly, continuously, without a single tenant holding the whole claim. Yet if a visitor were forced to point to one presence, one dominating fact of life in that high warm dark, it would be the beings who are best called the Foldwarmers, though they have no name that translates into spoken sound. Their name, if one insists on the word, is a shared pressure: a low double vibration in the chest, a scent of warm fur and resin, a pattern of contact repeated so often over generations that it has become identity.

They are difficult to see at first. In that dim green-black light, the eye searches for outline and finds only texture: a swell of fur that might be moss, a curve of membrane that might be leaf, a slow shifting warmth that might be the breathing of the basin itself. Then the texture resolves. A broad head lifts. A row of heat-pits opens slightly. Eyes reflect the faintest fungal glow with a dull, wet silver. Something large and patient becomes aware of you without looking.

The Foldwarmers are not shaped like people. They are shaped like the place: rounded, layered, close. Their bodies are low and broad, warm-blooded but not in the sharp, high-metabolism way of open-country animals. They conserve heat. They hold it in deep chests, in folded flanks, in dense pelts that trap moisture and warmth together. Their frames are not brittle-boned in the ordinary sense; much of their support is cartilage, tendon, and thickened fascia, giving them a settled firmness rather than a skeletal hardness. They feel, when touched, like living furniture: soft over strong, warm over enduring.
