What lives there is not one kind so much as a hush made of many bodies.

The first citizens are the moths.

They cover the young basalt in panels so continuous that, at a distance, they seem to be part of the rock’s weathering: ash-colored patches, smoke-dark smears, soft gray bloom over the blades of stone. Up close, each one becomes a creature folded into patience. Their wings are broad and blunt at the edges, furred with hollow hairs that catch mist before it can fall. Their bodies are short, thick with stored damp, their antennae fine as spider-silk and constantly trembling in the thin air. They are not beautiful in the way valley moths are beautiful, with painted eyes and bright dust. They are beautiful the way lichen is beautiful, the way frost is beautiful: by necessity, by adaptation, by having become almost indistinguishable from the place that permits them.

When the mist thickens, they drink without moving. Their wings lift only a fraction, just enough to expose the furred undersides, and the moisture combs itself into their hairs. Then the droplets travel inward, bead along the seams of their bodies, and vanish into them. The whole wall appears to breathe then. A slow inhalation passes from panel to panel, not as flight, not as panic, but as opening. When the air thins again, they close. The gray settles. The stone returns to being stone, or seems to.

They make almost no sound. At most there is a soft pressure in the air when many thousands of them adjust their wings at once, a feeling like breath held in the mouth. Their silence is not absence; it is discipline. A sudden noise can break them loose. A sharp cry can lift a whole sheet of moths from the wall and send them spiraling over the open side into the pale absence below. For that reason, whatever lives in the amphitheater has learned to live quietly.

The second citizens are the larvae.

They are pale, segmented, and slow, no larger than a finger joint, with dark polished heads that look wet even when they are not. They live in the roofs, in the seams between fitted stones, in protected cracks, in woven sleeves hung beneath the terraces. They eat dead moss, shed moth scales, fungal threads, frass, and the soft films that gather where dripwater darkens the basalt. In return, they spin.

Their silk is unlike ordinary silk. It is thin and mineral, almost colorless when fresh, but where the persistent drip touches it, it hardens into a pale, stony thread. Over time, those threads accumulate into cornices no wider than a reed, into buttresses like dried rivulets, into shutters, tubes, roofs, and delicate arches over vulnerable channels. The settlement is therefore not entirely built. It is partly grown. The hands of the keepers set the first stones and cut the first gutters, but the mouths of the larvae finish the work in places too small for fingers.

They are not guided by any plan visible to them. They follow damp, warmth, texture, and scent. But the keepers know how to ask them. A wetted cord laid along a seam will draw a line of spinning. A clay plug set in a crack will encourage a roof. A screen of woven grass, lightly misted, becomes a nursery wall by morning. The larvae build where conditions tell them to build, and the conditions are carefully arranged.

They are also, in a sense, the town’s memory. Their hardened threads record old water paths. Where a drip once fell for years, a ridge remains. Where a crack once widened, a lace of silk bridges it. Where a roof collapsed, the new repair is visible as a paler patch over the older dark. The architecture is written in their bodies.

The third citizens are the beetles.

They are flattened, hard-shelled, and dull in color, like seeds of wet bark. They move along the walls with a deliberate, side-to-side motion, grazing fungal films from the stone. Their mouths are small and tireless. Where they have passed, the surface shines faintly, cleaned to its mineral grain. They do not make much noise, but if one listens very closely, one can hear the faintest rasp, as if the rock were being combed with a bone needle.

They are important because they keep the walls from being smothered. Too much fungus would clog the fine channels, choke the larval seams, and sour the surfaces where moths prefer to roost. The beetles prevent that. They are the grazers of the vertical country, the small herd animals of the stone.

The fourth citizens are the snails.

Their shells are thin as skin and translucent, so that when light seeps through them, one can almost see the pale animal coiled inside. They live along the gutters, the cistern lips, and the damp corners where water pauses before deciding where to go. Their trails are silver at first, then vanish as they dry. They leave behind a faint mineral residue, a ghost-line of calcium that helps seal hairline leaks.

They are slow even by the standards of the place. A snail may spend a day crossing a basin rim. Yet they matter. Their mucus binds dust. Their bodies clean the narrowest channels where no finger or needle can reach. In dry intervals, they seal themselves into tiny whorls and wait. When mist returns, they unfold as if the stone itself had become tender.

The fifth citizens are the salamanders.

They live in the cisterns and in the deeper basins where water stands long enough to become still. They are blind, or nearly so; their eyes are pale points beneath skin as smooth as wet clay. They do not need sight. They taste movement. They wait with folded legs, their gills barely stirring, reading the water for the fall of eggs, the tremor of larvae, the chemical signature of rot or health.

The keepers watch the salamanders closely. If a salamander rises too often to the surface, something is wrong in the basin. If it remains motionless for days, the water is stable. The creatures are not pets, exactly. They are indicators, living instruments, small pale judges of the town’s hidden condition.

The sixth citizens are the frogs.

They are almost colorless, their skin a damp gray-green that becomes whatever they sit upon. They perch above drip lines, waiting for adults that fall from the roosts or for winged insects carried sideways by rain. They do not croak. Even their mating calls, when they occur, are low, brief clicks, swallowed almost at once by the moss and stone. A shout here would be violence; a frog song would be too much. So they have become quiet too.

The seventh citizens are the mites.

They are small, velvet-bodied, and fast, hunting moth eggs in the seams. They are not numerous enough to destroy the future of the swarm, but numerous enough to keep it from overflowing its own supports. They are the thinning hand. They move through the hidden spaces where wings cannot go, in the dark joints of the town, and they take what is soft, exposed, or excess.

The eighth citizens are the predators.

There are beetle-larvae with hooked jaws that drop from crevices into crowded moth panels. There are pale spiders no larger than a thumbnail, waiting in the lee of hanging nets. There are occasional hunting flies with long legs and metallic eyes, entering from the cloud fields above when the air changes. These creatures are not welcomed openly, but they are not exterminated either. They cull weakness. They prevent a roost from becoming too dense, too sick, too still. When a predatory larva drops, a hole opens in the moth-panel, soundless and slow. The surrounding moths shift. The hole closes. The wall continues.

And then there are the keepers.

They are human, or near enough to human that the word still applies, though the place has edited them. They are few. They are slight. They move with their weight low, knees bent, feet padded in boots made of compressed moss, shed silk, and softened hide. Their hands are broad, patient, always busy. Their fingers are stained with clay, plant juice, and the pale dust of mineral silk. They wear dark clothing because brightness would disturb the roosts; their garments are muted as the stone, sewn close, wrapped to keep from snagging on edges. They do not shine. They do not rattle. They have learned to make themselves soft.

They may once have been ordinary mountain people who found this wound in the rock and learned to live by its strange economy. Or they may have been changed by it over many generations, made smaller, quieter, more deliberate. It is hard to know. The place does not encourage histories told aloud. Whatever their origin, they now belong to the amphitheater as much as the moths do.

They speak mostly with hand signs. A flat palm pressed downward means be still. Two fingers drawn across the wrist means water is moving. A cupped hand lifted to the mouth means mist is coming. When sound is necessary, they use breath-clicks, tongue taps, and short exhalations, each carrying a narrow meaning. Their voices exist, but rarely. A spoken word is reserved for emergencies, and even then it is no louder than a leaf turning over.

They do not rule the place. They tend it.

Their work is endless, but it is not heavy in the way valley work is heavy. It is precise. They clear blocked gutters with bone needles. They lift clay plugs and let cisterns breathe. They replace reed screens where the fibers have gone soft. They tighten ropes twisted from mountain grass. They hang new nets where condensation gathers best. They guide mist through woven meshes so the moth-panels do not shift too violently. They harvest hardened silk only after it has matured and been abandoned, and even then they take sparingly. They gather fallen moth scales in shallow trays, not for decoration, but for tinder, insulation, and the lining of egg-shelves.

They know the mountain’s weather not by the sky alone, but by surfaces. They read the darkening of channels. They feel the first bead of moisture on their cheeks. They watch how the larvae behave: if the young spin quickly, wet is coming; if they retreat into sleeves, the air will thin. They observe the salamanders, the beetles, the angle at which fern fronds uncurl. Their knowledge is not abstract. It is in the fingertips.

They live in the low oval chambers set into the terraces, but even their houses seem designed less for human comfort than for the continuation of the swarm. The roofs are thick with moss and larval silk. The walls are fitted without mortar, allowing tiny passages for beetles and mites. Sleeping niches are lined with soft shed fibers. Hearth-places are sealed clay chambers where stones are heated away from open flame. There is no bright fire here. Heat is stored, contained, passed from stone to stone. A lamp, when one is lit, gives only a dull red glow beneath a mica cover, less than a coal, enough for close work and nothing more.

Their food is gathered, not feasted upon. They cultivate fungal films on shaded trays. They collect the small white seeds of cushion plants. They harvest bitter greens from pockets of leaf-mold. They eat roasted beetle larvae when the beetle population grows too thick, and they eat certain cistern algae scraped into clay cakes. They do not kill moths except in dire need. They may take eggs, but only from shelves where too many have been laid. Everything they take is measured against what the place can replace.

Their children learn stillness before they learn speech. They are taught to walk with the outer edge of the foot first, then roll inward, so that no heel strikes stone. They are taught to breathe through the nose, to keep tools wrapped in cloth, to never toss an object from one terrace to another. They are taught that dropping a bone awl is not a small mistake. It is a shockwave. It can ripple through a roost and empty a wall of wings. A child who learns this early is praised with a touch of the hand against the crown. A child who forgets is not scolded loudly; they are led to the open side and made to watch the pale absence below until they understand what falls.

Their rituals are quiet too. They mark the arrival of mist by lifting a small clay bell without striking it, merely letting it move from one hand to another. They mark the first hard frost of the year, rare at this height, by placing a single drop of cistern water on a warm stone and watching it vanish. When a keeper dies, the body is wrapped in shed silk and moss and carried to a terrace root-pocket, where the beetles and the damp may take it back into the town. No lamentation is shouted. The mourning is a long stillness, a communal pause, the whole settlement listening to itself for a time.

The keepers are not lonely, but they are few. Their social life is made of small tasks done near one another. One repairs a net while another guides a drip-channel. One counts eggs by touch while another replaces a roost screen. They move through the terraces like thoughts through a careful mind, not needing to fill the gaps with noise.

Yet they are not the true majority.

The true majority is the swarm, the crawl, the pulse of animal life that exceeds every human intention. There are more moth wings than hands. More larval mouths than fingers. More beetle tracks than footprints. The place is thick with lives that do not need names to be real. The biomass of small bodies hidden in the walls likely outweighs every plant, every human, every visible structure. The terraces are not merely architecture; they are habitat. The cisterns are not merely reservoirs; they are nurseries. The gutters are not merely waterways; they are feeding paths. The empty courtyards are not vacant; they are resting places, assembly points, future roosts waiting for the next shift in moisture.

Even the empty parts live.

An unused basin, full of still water, becomes a world for blind salamanders and wriggling larvae. A stair with no human foot on it becomes a highway for beetles. A ledge where no one stands becomes a shelf for moth eggs, a hunting post for pale frogs, a molting ground for hungry beetle-larvae. Nothing here is merely unused. It is only assigned to another scale.

This is why the town feels wrong to ordinary human eyes. It was not made for the convenience of upright bodies, broad shoulders, loud voices, and open hearths. It was made for softness, for moisture, for wing-hairs, for spinning mouths, for creatures that live by slow accretion. The keepers understand this. They do not force the place to become a human town. They keep themselves small enough to fit inside its logic.

When mist comes, everything quickens.

The upper nets darken first. Water beads along their woven meshes and runs into channels no wider than a finger. The channels carry it down, from basin to basin, from cistern to cistern. The larvae emerge from sleeves and seams, their pale bodies gleaming faintly. They spin faster while the air is wet, laying down new bridges where the old ones have thinned. The moths lift their wings in panels, drinking. The beetles pause, then resume grazing with renewed purpose. The snails unfold. The salamanders press closer to the water’s edge. The frogs turn their translucent throats toward the drip lines.

The keepers move into this change with practiced ease. They do not hurry. Hurry would be noise. Instead, they flow. One lifts a clay plug. One tightens a net rope. One slides a bone needle into a blocked channel and frees a thin line of water. One replaces a screen before it can tear. One watches the moth-wall for any sign of panic, any ripple that spreads too fast. If a panel begins to shift, the keeper raises a hand, and the others still themselves until the disturbance passes.

When the mist thins, the place exhales.

The stone darkens to a polished sheen, then slowly lightens as it dries. The moths settle. The larvae retreat into woven sleeves. The predators become more active, crossing exposed ledges while the air is clearer. The salamanders sink deeper. The beetles continue their rasping work. The keepers gather what has fallen: broken reeds, shed silk, exhausted moths, a handful of eggs laid in the wrong place. Nothing is wasted. Everything is carried somewhere else, sorted, placed, returned.

At night, if night can be said to exist under such a dull seam of cloud, the town becomes even stranger. The lamps are few and dim. The darkness is not black but mineral, a deep gray-green full of soft movements. One hears drops falling at long intervals. One hears the cisterns breathe. One hears the faint settling of ash-like wings. If a human breathes too loudly, the sound seems enormous, almost rude. So the keepers breathe less. They sit in their oval chambers, mending tools by the glow of covered embers, listening to the place keep itself alive around them.

There are moments when the mountain reminds them that it is young. A flake of basalt breaks from the rim with a glassy tick. A seam opens in the wall. A shelf of scree slides a little farther toward the pale absence below. The sound is small but it travels. The moths lift. The keepers freeze. Then the rock settles, the dust fades, and the town resumes. Later, the keepers will pack the new crack with clay and guide larvae toward it. Within days, silk will bridge it. Within seasons, moss may take hold. The wound becomes another part of the settlement.

This is what lives there: the moths, first, with their stone-colored wings and their mist-drinking patience. The larvae, second, with their mineral silk and their blind architecture. The beetles, snails, salamanders, frogs, mites, and predators, each in their small office of cleaning, sealing, tasting, thinning, hunting, and balancing. The keepers, human and changed, who move through all of it with padded feet and bone tools, necessary but not central, reverent without ceremony, authoritative only in their restraint.

And beneath all these lives, there lives something harder to name.

It is the town itself as a living metabolism. It is the relationship between water and wing, stone and silk, silence and survival. It is the way the amphitheater does not merely contain creatures but composes them, the way the mist calls and the walls answer, the way every small body is held in place by countless other small bodies. It is not a hive, exactly, because no single queen commands it. It is not a city, exactly, because its streets are too small for human feet. It is not a wilderness, because it is tended, repaired, and loved in the only way such a place permits: softly, carefully, without claiming.

To stand there long enough is to feel oneself become peripheral. The place does not need a witness. It needs only its own quiet continuation. But if one remains still, padded, breathing low, the life there may gradually accept the stillness as another kind of stone. Then the moths will lift a fraction nearer. Then the larvae will spin within sight. Then a keeper may glance at you, make a small sign with two fingers, and mean something like: be gentle, be quiet, be part of the balance.

What lives there, in the end, is not merely a list of creatures. It is a negotiated hush. It is a high, damp, newborn organ of the mountain, beating slowly with wings, mouths, drops, and hands. It is the sound of ash settling. It is the pressure of countless wings against stone. It is the town that breathes because everything inside it has learned not to shout.
