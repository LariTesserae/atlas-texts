What lives there does not present itself as a single portrait. It presents itself as pressure, movement, small appetites, and the constant low negotiation between stone, water, heat, and bodies. The first answer is not a face but a film, a spore, a trembling bead of moisture that has learned how to become a skin.

The smallest inhabitants are already at work before anyone names them. On the fresh-cut rock faces, on the damp concrete, on timber bracing and the backs of pipes, there are microbial colonies too fine to see: bacteria in rust-colored blooms, thin fungal threads, mineral slime, algae where even the weakest lamp gives enough light to tempt them. They live on wetness, on trace minerals, on grease, on dust carried in by boots, on the exhalations of larger bodies. They are the beginning of the settlement’s true food web, though no one speaks of them except when a surface turns slick or a smell changes. They make stone less sterile. They make the new place old.

Then come the plants, if one can call them that. There is no garden here, no green abundance, only the most stubborn fringes of vegetable life. Moss forms dark cushions where pipes leak and lamp brackets sweat. Liverworts spread over concrete in places where condensation never quite stops. Pale surface roots pierce cracks in the ceiling and hang blind in the air, feeling for moisture that is not soil. In drains and troughs, algae darkens into a soft, sliding fur. Fungi appear in corners and on rotting timber: small, tough caps; white knots; brown shelves; powdery growths on forgotten grain, cardboard, oiled wood, or guano. None of this life is ornamental. It is opportunistic, patient, and quiet. It lives off what the settlement cannot prevent: leaks, warmth, waste, shadow.

The insects make this life visible.

They are not occasional visitors; they are weather. Midges and moisture-flies gather under every caged lamp in pale, shifting clouds, turning light into a living haze. Moths with damp, ragged wings beat against shades and fall onto hot metal, where they give off a faint, brief smell of singeing. Beetles move in the mud at the pool edge, along gutters, under loose boards, in the dark spaces behind pipework. Cockroaches and cave crickets occupy the seams and recesses, some glossy with damp, some dry and dust-colored, all quick and indifferent to human concern. Spiders build between pipes and railings where air currents are steady, and their webs collect condensation until each strand looks like a line of tiny glass beads. Mites and springtails live in the guano, in rotting insulation, in the soft black dust beneath the bat roosts. Everything here is being processed by something smaller. Dead insects become larvae; larvae become beetles; beetles become food for rats, frogs, or the pale things in the pool. The air itself feels metabolized.

The bats are the chamber’s largest wild citizens.

They hang in the overhangs and upper shelves where the rock is driest or where heat collects, their bodies forming rough black masses that seem at first to be part of the stone. Then one shifts. Then another unfolds a wing. Then the whole cluster trembles with small papery sounds and high, sharp cries that bounce off the walls. They are not dignified in the human sense. They are crowded, restless, and utterly practical. They roost where warmth rises and insects gather. They feed in the lamplight if the shafts do not let them out, or they vanish upward through dark passages when the air changes, returning later with the smell of outside on their fur. Where they settle, guano thickens the rock in pale streaks and crumbled heaps. That guano is not waste so much as foundation. It feeds flies, larvae, beetles, mites, fungi, and the faint, crawling ecology that exists wherever enough organic matter collects in a warm dark place. The smell near the roosts is strong, ammoniacal, fertile, almost agricultural. To disturb the bats is to disturb the whole upper atmosphere of the chamber: wings break from the ceiling in panic, squeals rise, insects scatter, and something vulnerable is exposed in the machinery of the place.

Near the pool, life changes again.

It becomes slower, darker, more aquatic. Water beetles skim the margins with quick, nervous strokes. Larvae wriggle in warm shallows where mineral sediment collects. Worms and small crustaceans live in the mud and among the crushed shell-grit, feeding on detritus that washes down from ledges and pipes. Frogs or toads—pale-throated, heat-tolerant, accustomed to dimness—crouch on wet stone with their forelegs planted and their throats pulsing. Their calls are low, throbbing sounds, more felt than heard at a distance, and they carry across the water with surprising authority. They eat larvae, flies, and anything small enough to fit in their mouths, and for this reason they are tolerated by the people who work near the pool. Beneath the surface there are other lives. Fish may exist there if the water connects to deeper groundwater, or perhaps only blind pale things without common names: eyeless scavengers, translucent shrimp-like creatures, flat larvae that have never needed sight. The pool does not reveal them easily. It shows only broken copper reflections of lamps, then darkness, then occasionally a ripple or a slow turning motion that suggests something feeding below.

Rats know the settlement better than most of the people do.

They travel along pipe racks, behind brick walls, beneath gratings, and through the narrow service gaps left by builders who intended to finish the work later. They are not usually bold in the open. They prefer edges, shadows, and routes with escape. But they are numerous, clever, and always listening for the movements of larger animals. Their claws tick on metal. Their teeth test wood, insulation, stored grain, abandoned cloth, and anything with a smell of fat. They are pests, but also participants. They eat spilled food, dead birds, insect larvae, bat droppings if nothing better is available, and the occasional unlucky creature caught in the machinery. Where rats live, there is a different quality of silence: insects seem to know when something is moving nearby. A sudden stillness in the dark can mean they are passing. The people who live there watch for them, trap them, curse them, and, in a grudging way, respect them. A place with rats is a place with food, heat, and shelter. A place without them might be dead.

Birds are accidental residents, seasonal and brief.

If upper shafts remain open, swifts, pigeons, or swallows may enter, drawn by air currents, mistaken space, or the hope of roosting. They are awkward in the artificial light. Their wings seem too large for the low ceilings, their movements too sudden for the damp air. Some find perches near vents and leave droppings and feathers on walkways. Some become confused, circle until exhaustion, and fall. Others are driven back by noise, heat, or the pressure of the fans. Where birds have been, there are dry rustles, white splashes, and the occasional feather stuck to a wet wall. If one dies, the smaller inhabitants find it quickly. Insects arrive first. Rats come later. Within days, the bird becomes part of the chamber’s economy.

But the people are the most anxious inhabitants.

They are few, far fewer than the settlement was built to hold. Their presence is scattered across iron walkways, brick rooms, pump halls, and dormitory alcoves. You do not encounter them as a crowd. You encounter them as single figures: someone checking a gauge by the light of a portable lamp; someone wiping condensation from a valve wheel; someone pausing on a catwalk to listen to a fan; someone dragging a hose, carrying a coil of wire, or scraping algae from a drain grate. They move with the care of people who know that a slip, a short circuit, or an unknown leak can become serious in a place like this. They are not romantic explorers. They are maintainers. They are custodians of a wet, noisy, half-finished room that must be kept just barely livable from hour to hour.

Their work is divided by necessity rather than elegance. There are pumpkeepers, who watch the intakes, clear debris from grates, and learn the pool’s moods by the way its edge darkens or recedes. There are ventilation tenders, who listen to the fans, oil bearings, replace belts, and treat any change in airflow as a personal offense. There are drainwalkers or gutter-workers, who follow the channels with scrapers and rods, because blocked water here does not merely puddle—it finds new routes through wiring, foundations, and unfinished walls. There are electricians and lamp-tenders, who fight moisture, insect bodies, and corroded contacts to keep the amber lights alive. There are masons, shotcrete patchers, timbermen, and general laborers, who deal with the rock’s slow adjustments: cracks, shifts, falls of dust, newly weeping seams, and the constant mineral staining that makes every surface look older than it is. Someone keeps stores. Someone watches the food against mold and rats. Someone, whether by duty or temperament, keeps track of the bats, makes sure roosts are not disturbed, and notes when the guano becomes too deep or too dry. Someone else sets traps and records where the rats are moving.

They know the settlement by its sounds more than by its appearance.

A pump engages differently when the water level changes. A fan develops a wobble before it fails. A pipe gives a duller knock when it is half full. A motor under strain has a thinner note. A drip that once struck tin but now strikes water means the basin has filled. A silence where there should be machinery is worse than any alarm. The people who live there carry these distinctions in their bodies. They can wake from sleep and say what has stopped, what has started, what is about to break. They talk about leaks as if they were weather systems or difficult animals. They give names to troublesome places: the weeping seam, the hot corner, the bad stair, the drain that breathes, the lamp that eats moths. They mark valves with chalk, tags, tape, and memory. Their knowledge is practical, local, and slightly superstitious, because in a place like this, superstition is often just habit wearing work gloves.
