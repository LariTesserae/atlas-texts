What lives there is not one kind of inhabitant, but a crowded agreement.

The first answer, the one an outsider would give, is: people. Careful people. Quiet people. People who have become as narrow and deliberate as the paths they move through. They are human in ancestry, mostly, but ancestry is not the important fact. The hollow has worn the meaning of that word down, polished it, and fitted it to its own needs. They are the descendants of those who learned to live where water is counted, where heat is close, where silence is a kind of structural safety.

They do not move like open-sky people. Nothing about them is wide or casual. Their bodies are compact, dense through the chest and shoulders, with hands made patient by fine work. They turn sideways before they squeeze through a gap. They duck without thinking. Their feet are soft-soled or bare, because a bare foot can feel a leaking pipe, a warm seam in the stone, a loosened grate, the faint vibration of an engine turning far below. Their skin is often darkened by heat and mineral dust, stained faintly green along the wrists and throat by plant sap and algal ointments, sometimes gleaming with the faintest film of sweat that they have learned not to wipe away carelessly. Moisture is not discomfort here. It is wealth.

Their eyes are large and slow-blinking, tuned to amber lamplight and the weak blue-green glow of living things. They do not stare. They half-see, as the place itself half-sees. Faces emerge from shadow only when someone pauses near a lamp: a forehead sheened with humidity, a mouth moving in a low word, a strand of hair bound with waxed thread or copper wire. Many of them wear their hair short or tightly wrapped, not for fashion but because loose strands catch on tools, vines, and switchgear. Others braid small objects into it: seed capsules, fuse-wire, beads of fired clay, tiny polished nuts from plants that grow in the upper gardens.

Their clothing is built for thirst.

They wear layered wraps, vests, and hooded mantles made of woven root fiber, pressed fungal cloth, waxed canvas, and salvaged textile scraps. The inner layers are often lined with moss or capillary thread that draws sweat from the body into small collection pouches at the hip or chest. These pouches are emptied into household reservoirs with a seriousness that outsiders sometimes mistake for ritual but which is closer to bookkeeping. No one in the hollow exposes more skin than necessary to dry air. No one discards a breath-cloth lightly. Some sleep with soft masks or dampened veils over their mouths so that even the moisture lost in sleeping breath is caught, condensed, returned.

They smell of warm stone, moss, hot metal, faint sulfur, and the green-black smell of leaves pressed too close together. They do not smell clean in the way rain makes clean. They smell maintained.

They are not numerous. The hollow could not sustain a crowd in the careless sense, and they know this. Their population is kept in balance not by cruelty alone but by custom, by exhaustion, by the intimate knowledge that every added body is another heat source, another breath, another mouth, another claim on the drip lines. Children are few and precious, but they are not treated as ornaments. They are treated as future maintenance. From the time they can walk, they are taught to listen. They learn to hear the difference between a healthy engine and one whose bearing is drying. They learn to recognize the smell of overheated insulation. They learn not to shout, not to run, not to strike stone sharply, not to slap metal when frustrated. They learn that haste is a form of waste.

Their voices are low. Not because they are meek, but because the hollow punishes sharp sound. Speech is softened by habit and by architecture. The moss absorbs; the leaves scatter; the narrow spaces turn a loud word into a hundred small echoes that seem to press back against the speaker. Most conversation happens in a murmur, and much of it happens without sound at all. They have a rich language of hands: fingers opening near a lamp to ask if power can be spared; a thumb drawn along the wrist to signal a slow leak; two knuckles pressed gently to a pipe to ask whether the heat is rising as expected. Children learn these signs before they learn the formal names of things. In some districts, people tap messages on rails wrapped in cloth, but only rarely, and only softly, because the young rock remembers fracture and does not love percussion.

They have many names for themselves, and none that are used too loudly. In the lower tiers, where the air is warmer and the engines breathe their slow metallic breath, they may call themselves the Wick-born, because so much of life depends on threads that draw water from one place to another. In the middle tiers, they speak of the Dripward, meaning those who keep the falling moisture in its proper paths. In the upper gardens, where condensation sails hang like dark leaves and ventilation shafts whisper, they sometimes simply call themselves the Warm Ones. Outsiders, if outsiders ever arrive and are spoken of at all, may call them the Hollow People, but that name is considered blunt. It flattens them. It makes them sound like a feature of the terrain rather than a knot of intentions.

They live by roles, but roles here are not rigid cages. Everyone tends something. Everyone is responsible for a portion of the settlement’s metabolism. There are thread-setters, who string capillary wicks along walls and adjust them so that beads of condensation crawl toward collection jars instead of evaporating uselessly. There are gutter-keepers, whose concern is the tiny channels of copper, ceramic, and sealed wood that carry water through the tiers. There are root-tenders, who train living vines around unstable stone, prune invasive growth away from moving machinery, and graft useful plants onto older stock so that they can survive in mineral-rich crevices. There are lamp-feeders, who tend batteries, timers, filaments, and the fragile algae trays that give off weak light in exchange for warmth and exhaled air. There are stone-listeners, often older people with great patience, who press their cheeks or the flat of their hands to fresh rock and attend to its settling. There are engine-sitters, who do not so much repair machines as sit with them, feeling their rhythms, knowing by the warmth of a casing whether a piston is tiring.

No one is only a farmer. No one is only a mechanic. No one is only a priest, if priest is even the right word, though there are practices here that have the gravity of worship. The settlement cannot afford specialists who are ignorant of everything outside their craft. A root-tender must understand why a pipe sweats. An engine-sitter must know when a vine is pulling too tightly against a bracket. A lamp-feeder must know when the moss around a cable is beginning to char or sour. Knowledge in the hollow is not divided into clean categories. It braids.
