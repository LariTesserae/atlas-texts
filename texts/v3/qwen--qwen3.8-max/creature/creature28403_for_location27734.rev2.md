## Inhabitants of the High Plant Tableland

What lives there cannot be reduced to a single kind of creature, because the place itself is layered. Life exists at several speeds, several weights, and several degrees of separateness. Some of it is so slow and so structural that it seems like land. Some is so numerous and so loud that it seems like weather. Some is human-shaped, but only barely, and only briefly. To describe the inhabitants is to describe a series of pressures rather than a roster of species.

The first and most ancient inhabitants are the plant forms themselves.

They are not merely scenery. The root-felted plains, the leaf-layers hardened into fibrous stone, the bark ridges worn smooth by wind and passage, the buttress roots standing as cliffs: all of these are living substance brought to the stillness of terrain. The ground is made of old life. It is stitched by fungal threads, bound by rootlets, compressed by time, colonized and repaired over immense stretches of becoming. What appears to be geography is actually accumulated habitation. The tableland is not built from dead matter alone; it is built from matter that has not fully admitted death.

The great plant bodies are the most visible of these ancient inhabitants. They stand far apart, separated by heat shimmer and long corridors of open air, and each one is less like a tree than a landform with a pulse. Colossal stalks rise into the upper haze. Broad crowns spread like roofs over local weather. Ribbed trunks hold warmth long after the sun shifts. Root platforms extend outward as terraces, ramps, shelves, and hollowed courts. Their bodies are so vast that they do not simply exist in the landscape; they organize it. A crown can make shade into a valley. A root swelling can make a hill. A split in an ancient stem can become a canyon. A hollow internode can become a cave.

These great plant presences are slow, patient, and strangely alert. They are not conscious in the way animals are conscious, but they are responsive. Surfaces close slightly when touched. Resin wells where tissue is injured. Bark seams widen and seal over years. Hollows change their resonance as wind passes through them. Flowers open only when certain vibrations reach them. Some crowns catch mist and drip it down into lower root terraces. Others shed heat in slow waves after midday, making the air above them tremble. They drink when they can, store what they gather, and lose water through hidden skins in the hottest hours. They are alive in the manner of old mountains, except that their stillness is biological.

They are also hosts. Each major plant form is a world to other life. Its bark seams shelter insects. Its crown-spines snag passing clouds. Its leaf cups hold rain. Its dead branch joints become perches, drumming surfaces, nesting hollows, or eventual fall. Its roots form bridges, slopes, and shelters. Where it sheds, other life enters. Where it heals, other life waits. The great plants are not lonely despite the emptiness between them. They are separated by distance, but they are crowded with dependents.

Beneath and between them lives a quieter population of small organisms whose work is to make the plant world into ground. Fungal networks stitch fallen leaves into durable plains. Rootlets bind loose fibers into coherent slopes. Mites and tiny larvae work through dark layers of compressed leaf-leather, breaking down chaff, spreading spores, opening paths for moisture. Bacterial fermentation warms fallen fruit and old pollen deposits. Small beetles clean bark flakes. Worm-like creatures move through damp hollows where plant matter has softened. These lives are seldom seen, but without them the tableland would not hold its shape. They are the patient engineers of the substrate. They turn old tissue into place.

Then comes the second habitation: the fauna.

It arrives not as a scattering of animals but as pressure. The sound alone is enough to tell an outsider that this is not an empty world. The air is filled with a continuous weave of insect noise: stridulation, rasping, clicking, wing-beat, percussion, chewing, high thin cries, and low vibrating pulses that pass through the body before the ear separates them into sources. Much of it is hidden. Some comes from creatures no larger than seed grains, lodged in bark seams or under flakes of old skin. Some comes from swarms so vast that they change the light.

The insects and insect-like creatures are the most numerous inhabitants. They fill every seam, cavity, and surface. There are bark-seam stridulators that make a constant glassy rasping, almost like heat itself given sound. There are hard-shelled beetles that strike hollow stems with their heads or elytra, producing territorial knocks that carry across open distances. There are winged hoppers that rub their bodies against ribbed bark, using the landscape as an instrument. There are pollen gnats, tiny and numerous, that rise in pale clouds when flowering structures open. There are resin-flies with lacquered wings, sap-drinkers, leaf-miners, seed-borers, chaff-eaters, and fungi-tenders. There are predators that hunt by vibration, reading the noise of the plant plain the way desert hunters read tracks in sand.

Pollinator clouds are among the most overwhelming events of the tableland. They do not exist constantly at full strength; they arrive in pulses. A flowering event may begin with a change in resonance, a swelling of scent, a subtle alteration in the way the crown holds heat. Then the clouds come. They can darken the air above a broad platform of blossoms. Their buzzing becomes physical. It presses against the chest. It makes the fine hairs of the body stand. It can shake loose pollen into visible drifts. When such a cloud descends, the noise is not merely loud; it is architectural. It fills the space between the great plant bodies and makes the open distance feel solid.

These pollinators are not gentle visitors. They are hungry, urgent, and numerous enough to alter the behavior of the landscape. Some flowers open only when their wings produce a particular frequency. Some seed-cases release their contents when vibration reaches a threshold. Some fruiting bodies drop heavy seeds in response to the combined weight and sound of feeding swarms. In this way, the fauna participates in the reproduction of the plant world. The insects are not simply living on the tableland; they are part of its functioning weather.

Water pockets have their own inhabitants. Knot-bowls, hollow stems, pitcher-like growths, waxy leaf cups, and shaded crevices are not merely reservoirs; they are small worlds. Mosquito-like creatures breed in them. Tiny larvae filter microscopic growth. Minute beetles patrol the edges. Mites wait on the rims. Sometimes a pitcher throat contains a whole succession of lives: eggs laid above the water line, wriggling forms below, scavengers entering after death, and larger visitors coming to drink. These pockets are so valuable that they are defended, visited, and remembered. They are also dangerous, because every creature that needs water must reveal itself there.

The larger fauna of the tableland is adapted to openness, heat, noise, and scale. Because the major plant forms are widely spaced, animals often appear suddenly. One moment there is only shimmering distance; the next, a procession of bodies crosses a bark ridge or rises from a hidden hollow.

There are grazers that crop tough leaf mats. Many of them are broad-bodied and low, with wide feet that distribute weight over fibrous ground. They move slowly, not because they are dull but because the material they eat is difficult and because sudden movement attracts predators. Their trails become polished over generations. They may travel alone or in loose groups, but when they gather at a fresh growth or after a seed fall, they can seem like a herd of living boulders. Some have skin or shell textures that resemble dried bark, helping them disappear against the plains. Others rely on stillness, heat tolerance, and acute hearing.

There are climbers that travel along root bridges and buttress slopes. These creatures are quicker, more agile, and often highly social. They use hook-like claws, long fingers, or adhesive pads to move over polished bark and steep root walls. Many feed on insects, eggs, fruit, nectar, or fungi. Some live in family groups and communicate through patterned calls that cut through the general noise. They are noisy themselves, because silence would make them vulnerable. Their calls are not only signals but claims of presence: I am here, I am moving, I am not prey, I am not alone.

There are gliders and soarers that ride the thermal columns rising from sun-warmed bark plains. Bat-like, bird-like, and insect-like forms move between the crowns, sometimes disappearing for long distances into heat haze. They nest in high places, under crown-spines or in the joints of old branches. Some hunt aerial insects near swarm edges. Some feed on flowers and fruit. Some wait at the margins of pollinator clouds and take individuals as they rise. Their flight paths are shaped by the tableland’s own circulations: warm columns, cool downdrafts, wind corridors, and rain shadows cast by single broad canopies.

There are hollow-dwellers. Many animals have widened the openings of old internodes over generations, turning natural hollows into dens, roosts, nurseries, or defensive refuges. These cavities accumulate sound. A colony inside a hollow stem can make the whole structure resonate. At dusk, such places may erupt with calls, wing-beats, and scrambling movement. During the heat of midday, they may seem dead, though inside there is sleeping, nursing, grooming, and slow digestion. Hollows are valuable, contested, and sometimes inherited. They are also dangerous if they become too dry, too cracked, or too attractive to predators.

Predators here must hunt through confusion. The landscape is loud, visually broken by heat shimmer, and full of overlapping signals. Many predators therefore rely on pattern recognition. They listen for irregularities: a frightened call, a broken rhythm, a sudden silence, a vibration that does not belong. Some ambush near water pockets. Some stalk grazers along polished trails. Some hunt by mimicking calls or by moving in rhythm with the general chorus. Others are pursuit hunters, using bursts of speed across short distances, because long chases across open plant plains are risky. The largest predators are not necessarily the loudest. Often they are the ones whose approach causes other sounds to change.

Scavengers are everywhere, though they are often hidden by the speed with which the tableland reclaims matter. Fallen fruit, dead insects, shed bark, carcasses, broken eggs, and abandoned nests are all resources. Beetles, flies, worm-like larvae, crawling omnivores, and opportunistic vertebrate-like creatures enter fallen masses and convert them. Nothing remains waste for long. The biological character of erosion is partly due to this cleaning: surfaces wear under footfall, mandible, root pressure, sap flow, heat cracking, and the slow abrasion of wind-carried chaff. Dead tissue is entered, cleaned, inhabited, and converted. The dead become ground, shelter, food, or sound.

The fauna uses the plant architecture as an instrument, and this is one of the defining features of life there. Hollow stems become drums. Dry seed-cases rattle when touched by wings or bodies. Taut membranes of bark vibrate in response to calls. Certain creatures strike wood to mark territory. Others rub body parts against ribbed surfaces to produce amplified raspings. Some make resonating chambers of their own skulls, shells, or throat pouches. Some flowers and fruits rely on sound, releasing pollen or dropping seeds only when particular frequencies disturb them. At peak times, the whole landscape becomes a resonant chamber. The noise can be beautiful, maddening, and dangerous all at once.

Because of this, communication among the larger fauna is loud, patterned, or tactile. Calls are not subtle songs so much as structured signals designed to survive interference. Many creatures repeat phrases, strike rhythms, or use body percussion. Some communicate through vibrations transmitted through bark and root. Some use touch in crowded nests or swarms. Silence is suspicious because it often means that something has caused other lives to stop. A sudden gap in the chorus can be more alarming than a scream.

There are also moments when the fauna seems to become weather. Swarms moving across the sparse expanse can cast shadows, trigger local responses in plants, and create their own pressure in the air. A pollinator cloud, a predator-following prey cloud, or a migratory passage of canopy grazers can feel like a single vast organism temporarily assembled from many bodies. These events are not minds in the human sense, but they have direction, density, and consequence. They fertilize, strip, frighten, feed, and vanish. Afterward, the tableland returns to its apparent stillness, though the stillness is only the outer skin of ongoing life.

The third habitation is the people.

They are few, scattered, and light in their marks. If the tableland had a dominant species in the usual sense, it would have left heavier scars: cleared ground, permanent walls, quarried structures, broad roads, dense settlements. It has none of these. The ecosystem does not favor permanent, heavy construction. To build too much is to invite growth, decay, insect attention, or structural failure. To clear too widely is to provoke the living substrate to close the gap. The wisest inhabitants are those who learn to make brief, repairable, intimate marks.

The people who live there are human or human-like, but the place has shaped them. They are lean and heat-adapted, with bodies that conserve water and limbs suited to climbing, balancing, and moving over uneven living surfaces. Their feet are broad and sensitive, often bare or lightly protected, because the ground must be read through texture: the difference between a stable root platform and a decaying leaf layer, between a dry bark ridge and a slick sweating surface, between a hollow that will hold weight and one that will collapse. Their hands are long-fingered and practiced. They know knots, lashings, pegs, thorn anchors, and the behavior of fibers under tension.

Their senses are tuned to the tableland’s conditions. Their hearing is acute, not only because the world is loud but because changes in sound carry vital information. A shift in the insect chorus may signal predators, weather, flowering, or the approach of a swarm. A new resonance in a hollow stem may mean that water has entered it. A change in the calls of gliders may mean that a thermal corridor is opening or closing. Their eyes are accustomed to glare, heat shimmer, and the deceptive bending of distant forms. They often judge distance not by sight alone but by sound, memory, and the feel of air movement.

They wear little that is heavy. Their clothing and gear are made from woven plant fibers, shed bracts, insect leather, seed shells, bark strips, and occasionally parts taken from large insects or other animals. Resin adhesives bind small repairs. Thorns serve as pins, hooks, or fasteners. Beetle mandibles become cutting edges. Dense flaked wood becomes blades where no metal is available. Metal, when present, is rare and precious. It may come from deep root nodules, traded fragments, or fallen material of uncertain origin. It is not mined from open stone, because there is little stone in the ordinary sense. A metal edge might be hafted in hardened heartwood, carried for a lifetime, and then passed on as an heirloom or traded for water knowledge, resin, or safe passage.

Their shelters are light. A platform lashed into the crotch of a branch may serve for a season. A windbreak made from shed bracts may protect a sleeping place for a few nights. A ladder of braided aerial roots may connect a route between root terraces, but it is maintained by use, not by monumental construction. A rope line anchored with thorns may guide travelers across a difficult slope. A hollowed seed-shell may become a water vessel. A hearth is contained within a ring of dense bark and built over carbonized pith, because fire is both valuable and dangerous. Nearly everything around them can burn, even if much of it is damp, living, or too massive to ignite easily. Fire must be small, watched, and contained. It is used for warmth at high cool dawns, for cooking, for hardening points, for signaling, and for keeping certain creatures away. But it is never treated casually.

They do not quarry shelter; they grow it, lash it, find it, or borrow it. Hollow internodes are prized. Fallen branch joints can become temporary camps. Buttress roots provide windbreaks. Old seed-cases may be used as storage vessels if they are not already occupied. Everything is examined for habitation before being used, because cavities are contested and many creatures object to being displaced.

Their water knowledge is perhaps their most important inheritance. There are no broad rivers or standing seas here, so water must be known by its hidden locations. Which knot fills after rain? Which pitcher can be tapped without triggering a defensive response? Which leaf cup holds clean condensation at dawn? Which root swelling stores moisture through the hottest intervals? Which hollows are safe to drink from, and which are fouled by nesting creatures or fermentation? This knowledge is not abstract. It is carried in songs, rhythmic patterns, route memories, and taught gestures. Children learn not only where water is, but when it appears, how long it lasts, and how to take it without drawing predators or destroying the vessel that provides it.

Their food is gathered, hunted, and followed rather than farmed in the heavy sense. They eat pollen, nectar, fruits, seeds, fungi, insects, eggs, larvae, small game, and occasionally larger animals taken opportunistically. They know which fermented fruits can be used and which become dangerous. They know which insects are rich, which are poisonous, and which become abundant during emergences. They follow pulses: flowering events, seed falls, insect emergences, migrations of canopy grazers, predator swarms that reveal prey locations. They do not merely move through the tableland; they move with its cycles.

Their presence is modest because the world absorbs marks quickly. Carvings made on living surfaces heal into swollen ridges. Abandoned structures are overgrown, chewed, nested in, or folded back into the living mass. A path remains open only through repeated passage. A camp remains usable only if it is tended. A cache remains hidden only if the local fauna does not discover it. Therefore their making is intimate, practical, and replaceable. Tools are made to be carried, hidden, repaired, or discarded without leaving permanent scars on the terrain.

Their language reflects the acoustics of the place. It is loud enough to carry, but also highly patterned. In open stretches, calls may be shaped to cut through heat shimmer and insect noise. Near hollow stems, drum codes can travel farther than voices. In dense chorus conditions, hand signs, touches, and rhythmic taps become important. They likely have different registers of speech: ordinary talk, hunting signals, water calls, warning patterns, ceremonial rhythms, and silence-language used only when silence itself is meaningful. Silence, after all, is not neutral here. It can be a signal of danger, reverence, ambush, or mourning.

They may also use sound as a tool. If certain flowers open to particular vibrations, then people can learn those vibrations. If certain seed-cases release contents when struck, then rhythm becomes a method of harvest. If hollow stems resonate in ways that reveal water or structural weakness, then a staff or knuckle can be used to listen. Their technology is not only physical; it is acoustic. A person on the tableland may carry a striking stick, a rattle, a thorn-comb, a small drum, or a resonant seed-shell not merely as an instrument of music but as an instrument of survival.

Their social life is likely small-scale and fluid. Dense populations would strain water pockets and attract too much predatory or insect attention. Bands may be small, often moving separately and gathering only during abundance: a major flowering, a swarm emergence, a seed fall, or a rare season of widespread condensation. These gatherings would be intense, loud, and temporary. They would involve trade, storytelling, mating, dispute resolution, shared labor, and perhaps ceremonial percussion performed on the living architecture. When the pulse ends, the groups scatter again. The tableland does not support cities, but it can support festivals.

Their ethics would likely center on lightness. To live well is to leave little. To take without killing the source. To open a path without wounding the world. To drink without draining. To make fire without setting off catastrophe. To mark without scarring. To listen before speaking, because the place is already speaking. Their wisdom would be practical and spiritual at once: the recognition that they are small bodies inside a vast living body, and that the living body tolerates brief habitation better than domination.

They may regard the great plant forms with awe, fear, and kinship. They might not worship them as gods in a simple sense, but they would certainly recognize them as elder bodies. Some stories may say that the tableland was once a crowd of walking beings who slowed into terrain. Others may say that the great plants are listeners, or that the weather cells they generate are their breath. Practical knowledge and myth would blend easily here, because the plant giants really do hold water, create rain shadows, catch clouds, store heat, and respond to vibration. To speak of them as alive is not superstition; it is observation.

There may be other persons too, not human in shape but person-like in their capacities. In the hollow internodes, one might find small communal beings that have widened openings over generations and now maintain them with thorn tools and woven fiber. They may be pale from living inside stems, long-fingered, large-eared, and highly sensitive to resonance. They may farm fungi on damp walls, collect condensation in internal channels, and communicate through taps that outsiders mistake for wind or settling wood. They would not build outward in a way that challenges the landscape. Their architecture would be internal, careful, and acoustic.

Such beings, if they exist, would share the tableland’s logic: lightness, sound, hiddenness, and dependence on living structure. They might trade with the human-shaped inhabitants, avoid them, or regard them as dangerous competitors for hollows and water. Their traces could easily be mistaken for animal work, because they too would leave modest marks. A neatly widened cave opening, a polished threshold, a knot-bowl kept clean of debris, a seed-shell vessel tucked into a crevice: any of these could be the work of hands, claws, or something in between.

Even the plant substrate may have inhabitants that blur the boundary between organism and place. Some surfaces close when touched. Some hollows change resonance when wind passes. Some flowers open only when certain vibrations reach them. It would not be surprising if there are slow, distributed sensitivities in the root platforms or fungal networks: not minds, but capacities to register pressure, damage, moisture, and use. The tableland may not think, but it remembers in the way living systems remember: by growing around injury, closing a gap, reinforcing a useful path, or withdrawing support from a structure that is no longer viable.

To live there, one must learn the rules.

Water must be known by its hidden locations. Shelter must be grown, lashed, or found, not forced from the world. Fire must be precious and contained. Sound must be read as information. Movement must respect scale. Distances that look open are not necessarily safe or quick to cross. The ground is uneven, alive, and full of hidden inhabitants. A plain may be a fallen leaf stitched into stability, but it can still soften, shift, or harbor creatures beneath. A hill may be a root swelling that stores water and resists cutting. A canyon may be a split in an ancient stem with its own drafts, echoes, and dangers. A cave may be a hollow internode already occupied.

The inhabitants are those who have learned these rules. The great plants live by endurance and slow response. The insects live by pressure and number. The larger animals live by movement, timing, and acoustic awareness. The people live by lightness, memory, and restraint. The hidden small lives bind everything together. The fungi and rootlets make ground. The borers and scavengers make openings and clean death. The pollinators make reproduction possible. The predators keep the noise from becoming a single unbroken roar. The people, if they are wise, make themselves brief.

There is a strange grandeur to all of it. One can stand on a broad, warm bark plain and see only scattered colossi, heat haze, and pale sky. The visible world seems spare. Then the sound presses in: millions of unseen lives stridulating, striking, chewing, calling, feeding, mating, warning, and moving. The chest vibrates with it. The emptiness becomes full. The stillness becomes tense. The ancient plant terrain becomes present not as a backdrop but as a body.

Whoever lives there lives inside a resonant organism that has become a world.

Whatever lives there has learned that survival is not a matter of conquering the landscape, but of entering its rhythms lightly. The place belongs to the slow giants first, to the noisy fauna second, and to the careful makers last. Its truest inhabitants are those who can be loud when necessary, silent when wise, and always brief enough to be absorbed by the living tableland without leaving a wound that will not heal.
