What lives there is not first a body. It is a disturbance.

It is a change in pressure, a roughening of the dark, a vibration that arrives through stone before any shape can be assigned to it. In that low country, life does not appear as form so much as resistance to silence: a scraping, a pulsing, a crowded breathing, a thousand tiny impacts layered until they become a single continuous weight. If anything can be said to live there, it is this first fact made flesh: darkness can be inhabited so completely that it becomes a substance of bodies.

The truest answer would not begin with animals at all, but with the living ground.

There are films on the stone. Black, blue-black, iron-rich skins stretched over the wettest surfaces; pale crusts that look like salt or ash where mineral seepage has dried into slow bloom; slick, oily membranes trembling faintly over the mouths of cracks. They are not plants. They have no leaves, no stems, no memory of light. They are bacterial and archaeal mats, chemistries made patient, feeding on dissolved iron, sulfur traces, cold exhalations from deep rock, the faint nourishment carried by condensation. They grow so slowly that their growth is almost geological, and they are eaten so constantly that they are also almost animal. They are the first mouths of that basin. They make the stone edible.

Over them, and through them, move the grazers.

They are low, broad, slow things, built like living coins or wet shells pressed close to rock. Some are no larger than a fingernail; others spread as wide as a hand. Their bodies are eyeless, compacted, armored in dull plates of chitin and mineral accretion. They have no faces in any familiar sense, only blunt anterior ends equipped with rasping mouths, rows of hardened scrapers working in endless rhythm against the microbial films. They move by muscular contraction or by many short limbs moving so slowly that the motion seems less like walking than like settling. Their shells are pitted, scarred, powdered with pale dust, sometimes overgrown with newer films. When they pass over stone, they leave behind polished trails, cleaner lines in the dark, and those trails become routes for others.

Some of these grazers seal themselves to the rock when the air dries, rimmed with a bead of hardened mucus, waiting. When condensation returns or a thin seep darkens the stone again, they unseal and resume feeding. Their eggs are laid in damp depressions, often in mineral-crusted rings around pools, and they hatch in sheets when moisture is sufficient. The young are not quite like the adults: softer, more translucent, moving in denser clusters, grazing on the thinnest films until their rasping parts harden. They are prey for almost everything larger, and yet their numbers are so immense that they remain the visible foundation of the basin’s animal life.

Where the films give way to fungal threads, another set of inhabitants appears.

The fungi here are not green either, not plantlike in any comforting way. They are pale, grayish, sometimes nearly transparent, spreading as fine networks through accumulated debris: shed skins, crushed shells, dead fliers, dung, the powdery remains of old bodies. In damp overhangs they hang in curtains or form felted masses that move faintly when disturbed, not because the fungi themselves move, but because they are inhabited. Tiny creatures live in those curtains. Threadworms with no eyes and no color feed on spores and decaying organic matter. Mites and mite-like crawlers move through the filaments, their bodies dusted with fungal powder. Some are so small that they seem to be part of the texture of the air itself. The fungal curtains are not plants; they are metabolisms hanging in the dark, and they are as crowded as the stone floors.

The pools have their own nations.

They are black, shallow, cold, often so still that they seem to be holes cut into the rock rather than water resting upon it. Their rims are crusted with pale mineral deposits and darkened by microbial films. Their interiors are alive. There are filterers anchored there: soft-bodied things with feathery limbs, each limb fringed with fine hairs, each hair catching particles, bacteria, dissolved organic traces. They spread those limbs like fans and pulse them slowly, drawing water across their mouths. Some are rooted by suction; others can detach and drift a short distance before anchoring again. Their bodies are often translucent enough that the movement of their guts is visible if some accidental glow disturbs the water.

There are swimmers too. Flat, disc-shaped forms that move by contracting their edges. Long, segmented water-worms with hooks and bristles, hunting among the larvae. Blind beetles or beetle-like things whose legs have become oars, whose bodies are sealed against desiccation when they must cross wet stone between pools. There are larvae in such numbers that the water seems thickened by them: writhing, chewing, filtering, dying, hatching again. Some are the immature stages of the fliers above; others never leave the water at all. When a pool begins to dry, the life inside it accelerates. Feeding becomes frantic. The stronger consume the weaker. Eggs are laid quickly in the remaining damp. Some larvae encyst in hardened cases, waiting for the next rain. Others die, and their bodies feed the survivors until the water disappears entirely.

Pool predators are patient and vicious. Some lie half-buried in the sediment of corpse-dust and mineral mud, sensing vibration through the water. Some are ambush hunters with folded limbs and sudden mouths. Others are not hunters at all but traps made of hunger: broad, depressed creatures resting in hollows, their bodies nearly level with the pool bottom, their jaws opened only when something brushes their sensory hairs. In crowded pools, predation is not rare. It is continuous. Every movement risks attention. Every ripple carries information.

Above the pools and the stone floors, the air itself is inhabited.

The fliers are perhaps the strangest evidence that this place is alive. They rise in curtains, columns, spirals, and drifting veils. Many are pale, almost colorless, their wings narrow and veined, their bodies light and hollow. Some are like blind moths, though they have no interest in light and no memory of flowers or leaves. Their adults often do not feed. Their mouthparts are vestigial or absent. They emerge from larvae, rise, swarm, mate, and die, sometimes within hours. Their entire adult existence is a brief expenditure of energy saved during earlier stages. They live to become airborne, to find others of their kind by scent, vibration, or the pressure of wingbeats, and then to fall.

Their swarms behave like weather. They thicken in humid air, thin out when the stone cools too sharply, rise in response to the chemical signal of wet rock, and collapse when some predator’s vibration passes below. They are not led by sight. They turn together because they feel one another: air displacement, wingbeat rhythm, chemical trails, the faint electrical disturbance of many bodies moving in close formation. When a swarm moves, it makes a sound like fabric tearing very far away. When it descends, it can darken the already dark air. When it dies, it falls as nutrient snow.

The dead fliers accumulate in drifts. Those drifts are not quiet. They boil.

Scavengers arrive almost instantly, because in a place this crowded, nothing is truly far from consumption. There are small, fast-moving things with sharp mandibles; worm-like forms that enter carcasses through soft joints; larvae that hatch into meat and begin eating before they are fully formed; crawling multitudes so dense that a corpse becomes a moving mound instead of a still object. The process is not grotesque in the way a surface world might call grotesque. It is efficient. It is necessary. It is one of the basin’s main forms of circulation. Nothing is buried. Nothing is allowed to remain. Death here is not an ending but a redistribution, quick and total.

The fliers that do feed as adults are predators of the air. Some have broad, scoop-like mouths fringed with bristles, adapted for taking smaller fliers out of the swarm. Others have hooked mandibles and powerful wings, hunting along the edges of columns. A few produce clicks or rasps in flight, not for illumination but for orientation: sounds that return altered by nearby stone, by water, by the bodies of other creatures. They do not see the dark. They feel it answered back to them. Their heads bear no large eyes, only sensory pits, feathered antennae, tympanic membranes stretched over hollows in their shells. They listen to the basin as much as they move through it.

On the ground, the predators are even more numerous.

They live in cracks, hollows, dried channels, and the spaces between clustered bodies. Many are flattened, built to slide into narrow stone seams. Their bodies are long, segmented, armored in matte plates that do not shine unless wet. They have no eyes, or only useless shadows of eyes, and instead possess long sensory filaments, bristles, or vibration-sensitive organs along their limbs and carapaces. Some hunt by pursuit, moving quickly when prey disturbs the stone. Others wait.

The ambushers are especially strange. They choose worn hollows, potholes, or shallow depressions where movement naturally gathers. They settle into them so completely that they resemble a piece of the basin’s own erosion. Their limbs fold beneath them. Their mouths wait downward or inward. They may remain motionless for hours, days, even longer, slowing their metabolism until they are barely distinguishable from cold stone. When the right vibration arrives—the frantic scrambling of wounded prey, the irregular footfalls of a young grazer, the fall of a dying flier—they move with terrifying speed. The strike is not visual. It is mechanical. Something touched the wrong pattern in the dark, and the pattern was answered by jaws.

Some predators hunt by deception. They tap their limbs or mandibles against stone in rhythms that mimic the movements of prey or the distress signals of injured animals. They create false tremors. They wait for curiosity or hunger to bring another creature close. In a world without light, a lie is not a visible thing. It is a vibration. It is a pattern of pressure in the rock. The basin is full of such lies.

There are also creatures that hunt in loose aggregates, not truly social, not organized by intention, but drawn together by chemical trails and hunger. When a large body falls, these scavenger-predators arrive in waves. They may compete violently, tearing at one another as readily as at the carcass. The noise around such feeding sites rises into a fever: snapping, splashing, wet tearing, the hiss of bodies sliding over mineral crusts, the high thin sounds of smaller creatures driven away or caught. These are among the loudest places in the basin, and louder still when rain begins and new movement spreads across the stone.

Among all this, there are larger forms.

They are not common, because the energy of the basin, though immense in total, is slow and dispersed. But they exist. Some are broad, low, heavily plated beings that move with a ponderous patience over the rock. Their backs are ridged and pitted, often colonized by microbial films, fungal threads, or smaller crawling organisms. They may stand motionless for long periods, their bodies blending with benches and basin rims. When they move, it is because moisture has shifted, because a breeding swarm is rising, because a corpse-drift has formed, or because some deep internal rhythm demands relocation.

Some of these large creatures feed by taking in whatever is densest: swarms, larvae, soft-bodied grazers, the living scum of pools. Their mouths are not delicate. They are grates, scrapers, sucking throats, bristled filters. Others are scavengers, following the smell of death and the sound of feeding. They do not need speed. They need endurance. Their bodies are built to survive long intervals without food, to retain moisture, to withstand the pressure of crowded smaller life moving over them.
