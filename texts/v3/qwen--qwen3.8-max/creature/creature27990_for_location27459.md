## The Lives of High Mere

What lives in High Mere is not one creature, nor even one kind of creature. It is a wet commonwealth: a set of lives arranged around water, root, silence, and care. The people are there, yes — few, slow, adapted — but they are not the only inhabitants. Fish inhabit the channels. Eels inhabit the seams. Birds inhabit the bright upper skin of the pool. Insects inhabit the margins where air and water argue. The plants inhabit everything. And beneath all of them, in the oldest compressed floors of leaf and root, the past inhabits the place too, not as memory alone but as structure.

To ask who lives in High Mere is to receive a layered answer.

First: the people.

Then: the animals.

Then: the green city itself.

Then: what has become part of it.

---

### The Understory Folk

The people of High Mere might once have been called human, if the word had been soaked long enough to soften.

They are human-shaped, but shaped by the pool. Their bodies are long and unhurried, made more for gliding than for running. Their limbs have the lean patience of things that have learned not to waste warmth. Their chests are broad, their lungs large, their breathing deliberate. They do not move quickly unless they must. Quickness costs heat, and heat is precious in cool water.

Their fingers are dexterous, with slight webbing between them, enough to help in swimming but not enough to spoil fine work. Their toes are longer and more grasping than a lowlander’s would be, because root-stairs, wet stems, and slick platforms are their daily ground. The soles of their feet are toughened and textured, able to find purchase on bark that has been submerged for generations. Their skin is cool to the touch, often green-brown or tannin-dark, sometimes marked by faint mottling like light through leaves. In some families, a soft olive cast remains on the shoulders and cheeks, as though the water has stained them with its own color.

Their eyes are large and clear, accustomed to the green dimness under the surface. They are not night-eyed, exactly, but they have learned to read movement in water where a stranger would see only drifting particles. They blink slowly. They do not stare with suspicion so much as they register: a ripple, a shift in current, a fish turning where it should not turn. When they rise into open daylight, they often narrow their eyes for a moment, not in pain but in adjustment, as if the sky is louder than the water.

They do not have gills. That is a common mistake made by those who hear stories of them. They breathe air, as their ancestors did. What they have instead is discipline: trained lungs, slowed pulses, a practiced stillness. They can work long beneath the surface on one breath, not by supernatural means but by knowledge. They know where the air pockets are. They know which chambers hold breath beneath their domed roofs. They know which roots carry oxygen-rich water near them. They know how to move without wasting air. Children learn buoyancy before they learn to walk properly. They learn to be calm in water before they learn to name things.

They call themselves, in their own resonant underwater tongue, something close to “the Understory.” Outsiders, when outsiders come, call them Merefolk or Rootfolk. The Understory is a better name, because it acknowledges that they live beneath the brighter world, under the surface, under the leaves, under the first obvious facts of the place.

They are few. There may be only a few households in the whole basin at any one time: perhaps a few dozen people in all, sometimes fewer. Their population is not limited by cruelty or scarcity alone, but by design. Too many bodies would foul the channels, disturb the spawning fish, overharvest the reeds, and turn the clear water cloudy with too much life pressing against it. Their home can support abundance, but only if abundance is spaced. They understand this. They live in intervals.

A household in High Mere is not necessarily a crowded house. It may be one chamber, several platforms, a fish pen, a drying line, a woven gate, a listening post, and a stretch of open water between it and the next household. Families are often extended but not densely packed. A mother, her children, her sister’s children, an elder, an apprentice, a bonded fish-tender, a visiting weaver: these may belong to one household, but they will not all sleep in one room. They will distribute themselves through the water like notes in a chord.

Their etiquette is based on distance and signal.

To approach another household without sending a sound first is rude, because it startles the water. To swim directly through someone’s tethered reed curtain without asking is a trespass. To harvest from a root wall without first tapping the old rhythm of permission is poor practice, even if no person sees. They have signs for crossing currents, for asking passage through a weir, for borrowing a tool, for declaring that a channel is being cleaned, for warning that a storm is thickening above the pool. Much of this is done with knocked rhythms on root columns, or with hollow reed calls, or with the slow lifting of a woven marker.

They are not a silent people. They are a people who understand that sound travels.

In air pockets, their voices are strange: wet, rounded, amplified by curved plant walls. They speak slowly there, because every word seems to continue after the mouth has finished making it. Children are taught not to shout in the deep chambers unless there is danger, because a shout can confuse the fish and make the city seem wounded. Their laughter exists, but it is often soft, sudden, quickly contained — a ripple more than a crash. When they sing, they often sing in intervals that match the resonances of the hollow stems, so that the city seems to sing with them.

### Their Bodies, Their Gear, Their Patience

They dress simply. Their clothing is made from woven reed fiber, cured bark cloth, plant leather, and occasionally fish skin worked until it is supple and pale. In the water, they wear close wraps that do not snag: short kilts, breast-bindings, sleeveless tunics, nets that can be tightened against the body. On surface platforms, they may wear looser capes against wind and rain, because the air above the pool can be thin and cold. Their garments are fastened with carved bone pins, shell buttons, seed-case toggles, and cords twisted from strong vines. They favor colors that belong to the pool: green, brown, black, dull gold, the gray of wet bark.

They dislike loose ornament that might catch on roots. Beauty, for them, is not display but integration. A person may be beautiful because their swimming is economical, or because their braided hair does not trail into their face, or because their tool belt is arranged so well that one can find a blade by touch in green darkness. Still, they have ornaments. Shell beads from far below may be worn at the wrist. A rare copper hook, inherited and polished dull, may hang at a belt for ceremony. A carved antler comb might be tucked into hair on a day of council. Some elders wear small living vines wound around their arms, not as decoration but as habit, as if their bodies have begun to become part of the architecture.

Their hair is usually kept controlled. It is oiled with fish oil and plant resin, braided, wrapped, or bound. Children often have short crops until they learn to manage it underwater. Adults may wear long braids weighted with small seed-beads so the hair does not float into the eyes. In certain households, a strand of hair is woven into a living wall when a child comes of age, so that the wall remembers them even if they later leave the basin.

Their tools are simple, precise, and few.

They use blades of obsidian and dark volcanic glass, traded upward through many hands from distant lower places. They use shell scrapers, bone awls, antler picks, hardwood levers, sharpened reeds, and needles made from fish bone or the rare inherited bronze. Metal is not common. Metal is remembered, wrapped, passed down, and used only where its strength is worth its strangeness. A bronze needle may be older than several generations. A copper hook may be spoken of almost as a person. They do not smelt. They do not forge. Fire is dangerous in a city made of plant matter, and underwater it is impossible. On the highest surface platforms, small guarded flames may be kept in shallow hearths of wet peat and shell, but even there fire is treated as a guest that must never be allowed to become a host.

Their technology is not poor. It is patient.

They do not cut when they can persuade. They do not nail when they can lash. They do not build a wall when they can train a root to thicken over ten years. Their greatest tools are pruning, bending, soaking, weaving, waiting, and listening. A doorway may begin as a wound in a root mass, but it becomes a doorway only when the plant is coaxed to close around it in a smooth ellipse. A platform may begin as lashed reeds, but it becomes a floor only after living stems have grown through it and made it stable. A chamber may be hollowed from a great seed-case, then anchored, then softened, then planted at its edges until it no longer looks like an object but like a room the city grew around itself.

This is why nothing in High Mere looks new.

Even the youngest dwellings have a settled look, because their makers do not impose a final shape. They begin shapes and let the place finish them.

### Their Work

The daily life of the Understory is work, but not work that looks like labor from the outside. It resembles tending.

There are fish to watch. There are reeds to harvest in rotation. There are channels to clear of silt and fallen leaves. There are weirs to adjust. There are air plants to encourage. There are root walls to inspect for weakness. There are woven gates to tighten after storms. There are eggs to protect, larvae to move, young fish to guide into safe channels, older fish to cull when necessary. There are birds to discourage from certain pens without harming them. There are otters to acknowledge. There are currents to listen to. There are sounds to answer.

Their occupations are often named by what they listen to as much as by what they do.
