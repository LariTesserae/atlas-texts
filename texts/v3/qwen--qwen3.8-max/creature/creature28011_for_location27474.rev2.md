Those who live there are not numerous, and they do not live as life lives in wetter, younger places. They do not drink, do not crowd, do not build upward in defiance of the plain. They have been edited by the terminal age of the land until living has become a matter of smallness, conduction, patience, and permission.

The first residents are the ones that barely seem alive at all.

They are the mineral lichens, the copper-gray rosettes, the dark scales, the brittle filaments in the micro-cracks and on the lee sides of rare stone plates. They cling to surfaces so faintly textured that only a close eye can tell where plain ends and organism begins. Their colors are not the colors of leaf or sap. They are metallic and smoked: ash-blue, graphite, tarnished bronze, dull olive, sometimes a pale silver that brightens when charge moves through the air. They do not grow toward water, because there is none. They grow toward gradients: warmth cooling into cold, light striking a surface at a particular angle, a faint potential difference between two grains of dust.

They are slow beyond ordinary patience. A single rosette may spend centuries widening by the breadth of a fingernail. Their life is not spent in reaching but in remaining. They receive the dry storms without resistance. When turbulence passes over them, they may lose flakes, brittle arms, whole sections of a pattern. If the fragments settle in a suitable place, they continue. If not, they become dust again. This is not tragedy to them. They do not have the density for grief.

Yet they are not passive. The arcs, broken grids, and concentric rings they form are not accidents of scattering. They are records, or perhaps calculations. Each lichen colony answers to the field-history of the plain: the direction of old charges, the memory of storm paths, the faint metric inclinations left by buried machines. When a storm collapses nearby and the air briefly tastes of static, the oldest colonies alter their sheen almost imperceptibly. It may take decades for the change to become visible, but it is there: a shift in orientation, a new line of dark scales, a ring completing itself after a long interruption. If the plain thinks, the lichens are its slowest memory.

The wiry tufts live faster, though still barely.

They stand only a few centimeters high, dry as filament, more like mineral wire than grass. They appear in sparse clusters where the regolith holds a faint fracture or where a buried artifact gives off enough residual structure to organize dust around it. They do not sway, because there is little wind. They stiffen. When charged air moves through them, they become alert: each filament lifting a fraction of a degree, each tip brightening with a small cold light. At such moments they look less like plants than exposed nerves, or instruments remembering what it means to sense.

They are brief by the plain’s standards. Some last only a few seasons. Some die in a single storm, crumbling into powder after releasing their seed-grains into the turbulence. Most of those grains never take root. They travel for years, cross the breadth of the plain, pass over lichen and artifact and empty distance, and finally settle in places where nothing permits them to become anything. The tufts do not require success. Their existence is not an argument for abundance. It is a proof that even here, form can still risk itself in small quantities.

The most visible moving life belongs to the veil-drifters.

They are sheet-like beings, broad as a cloak or smaller, nearly transparent, with the look of smoked mica or old glass made flexible. They move centimeters above the ground on electrostatic lift, never touching unless forced to, never leaving tracks. They are silent. They have no limbs, no mouths, no visible organs. Their bodies are thin fields of layered silicate and conductive filament, so light that the plain’s weak turbulence can carry them for kilometers. When the sun passes through them, they cast only a faint moving stain, like the shadow of a thought.

They graze on charged dust and on the faint energy fields generated by the storms. They do not hunt, flee, or fight. They drift. Sometimes one will hang motionless for hours above a patch of regolith, its margins trembling in a rhythm too fine to hear. Sometimes several will travel together in a loose procession, spaced so evenly that they seem measured rather than gathered. They follow invisible lines of potential, the same lines that make pebbles lift and grains arrange themselves during turbulence. To watch them is to realize that the empty plain is not empty at all; it is crossed by slopes and currents that only such bodies can reveal.

They are not social in any warm sense. They do not call to one another. Yet they do communicate, if communication can be so attenuated. When two veil-drifters pass near each other, their surfaces may flicker with faint interference patterns: bands of dull blue, smoke-gray, pale bronze. The display lasts a moment and means nothing that a human eye could decode. It may be recognition. It may be a reading of charge. It may be nothing more than two fields acknowledging one another by distortion. They do not linger afterward. One drifts east, the other west, and the distance closes over them.

They roost, if such a word can be used, near the small artifacts and marker needles. There, the residual structures create stable pockets in the plain’s turbulence. A veil-drifter will fold itself against the base of a black ceramic cairn, becoming a dark sleeve of translucent matter, and remain there while storms pass overhead. In the deep clarity before dusk, several may gather on opposite sides of the same stone, not touching, all oriented toward whatever faint field the stone preserves. They look less like creatures resting than like offerings left by a religion that forgot its gods.

The harder beings live closer to the lichen.

They are compact, multi-plated things no larger than a hand, and they move with a deliberation that makes insects seem frantic. Their bodies are built of overlapping ceramic-dark plates, each one fitted with impossible precision. Beneath the plates are finer layers, fibrous and metallic, capable of holding charge and releasing it in minute pulses. They have no eyes in any ordinary sense, but their fronts are sensitive to light gradients, field distortions, and the mineral signatures of living crusts. They do not walk so much as decide where walking will next be permitted.

They are the gardeners of the plain, though they do not garden as a farmer does. They tend by subtraction, by alignment, by pressure. A plated creature will approach a lichen colony and remain there for hours, pressed against the mineral growth. Its plates may lift slightly, exposing charged surfaces beneath. A faint transfer occurs. The lichen’s pattern shifts by micrometers over days. A broken grid becomes more regular. A ring closes by one degree. The creature may have fed, or communicated, or performed some act of maintenance that has no human analogue. It is impossible to tell.

They also move grains. Not many. A single creature may carry only a few particles at a time, each smaller than a seed, lifting them with static adherence and placing them in shallow depressions or along invisible boundaries. Over centuries, this produces patterns: low alignments of dust, tiny arcs, cleared channels around the bases of marker posts. The plain’s turbulence often erases these works, but the creatures repeat them. They are not thwarted by erasure. Repetition is part of the work.

They do not seem to die often. When one does cease moving, its plates lock, its charge leaks slowly into the ground, and it becomes a small dark object among the dust. Lichen may eventually grow over it. Its body may become a calibration point for younger patterns. Nothing scavenges it. Nothing mourns it. It simply joins the residue of finished things.

There are smaller lives still, too fine to see without instruments and perhaps too old to be called individuals.

They exist in the regolith itself: microscopic filaments, crystalline mites, self-limiting machine-grains older than the last mountains. They are not alive in the sense of hunger or reproduction, nor are they entirely artificial. They have passed beyond that distinction. They are the latent engineering in the ground, the dispersed lattice that permits and forbids. They smooth footprints. They dampen vibration. They prevent large structures from persisting. They do not attack; they correct. They do not punish; they relax configurations that have become improbable.

These micro-presences are the plain’s immune system and its etiquette. They are why the bedrock remains untouched while the surface churns. They are why a footprint disappears with a faint ripple instead of remaining as a wound. They are why an ambitious tower would not be smashed but gently forgotten, its materials losing coherence until it becomes a low swell of dust. The larger creatures of the plain do not fear them, because there is no violence in them. There is only rule.

The storms have their own inhabitants.

Some of the shapes that rise in the turbulence are not shapes at all but temporary bodies: dust lattices bound by charge, light bent into momentary architecture, fields organizing matter into ribs, sails, columns, and script. Most vanish in seconds. Some last minutes. A few persist long enough to seem alive. They lean into the silence like unfinished sentences. They build themselves into tall forms, hold a posture, then collapse into ordinary dust.

These storm-forms may be creatures, or they may be processes wearing the appearance of creatures. The distinction is less meaningful here. When a dust column branches and moves as if searching, when a sheet of glittering particulate slides over the ground and pauses at a marker stone, when grains assemble into a lattice that resembles writing, it is difficult to deny them a kind of agency. But they do not endure. They are born from turbulence, live in the interval of charge, and die when the field relaxes.

Occasionally, during intense storms, larger forms are seen far off: tall, jointed shadows moving through the dust, or bright ribbon-like flickers crossing the upper air. The tall shadows are not solid. They appear to have limbs because the dust aligns along intersecting fields, not because bodies support them. They move with a slow, impossible stride, crossing kilometers without seeming to step. They may be assemblies: veil-drifters, charged dust, lichen-spores, machine-grains, and storm energy briefly coordinated into a single visible event. They may be the plain’s rare pilgrimages. They may be acts of repair performed at a scale too large to understand.

The ribbon-flickers are higher and stranger. They move through the thin air above the plain, sometimes at dusk, sometimes in the pale clarity before noon. They are bright only at their edges, as if their centers refuse to be seen. They leave no trails. They make no sound. When they pass, the hair on a traveler’s arms may rise, and nearby artifacts may hum with postponed action. Some say they are creatures that never descend. Others say they are memories of creatures, preserved as motion after the bodies became unnecessary.

If there are people on the terminal plain, they are not people in the ordinary sense.

They are the Attenuated, the ones who learned that to live there one must become almost optional. They do not arrive as soft, wet, thirsty beings and remain unchanged. The plain does not permit that. If a traveler stays, the place enters them: dryness settles into their joints, their breath becomes quieter, their thoughts slow to match the intervals between storms. They learn not to build, not to dig deeply, not to carry too much mass. They learn to wait for distance. They learn that arrival is not a matter of crossing space but of being accepted by it.

The Attenuated do not have cities. They do not have villages. They do not even have camps in any familiar sense. Their dwellings are the low domes, the black cairns, the shallow shelters grown from fused dust, the marker needles that hum faintly when touched. Some occupy a single structure for centuries. Others are distributed among several calibration points, their presence moving from node to node as fields shift. They do not require many bodies. They do not require bodies at all times. Sometimes a shelter is inhabited only as a pattern of stillness inside it: a pocket of air sealed from turbulence, a faint warmth in the wall, a shadow that remains a fraction too long after the light changes.

Their forms, when they choose to appear, are small. A dark polyhedron no larger than a child’s head, set into the ground at an angle that ignores the horizon. A slender ceramic frame no taller than a person, mostly empty space. A ring of pale posts surrounding a patch of dust where nothing settles. They prefer edges, seams, thresholds, points where the plain’s ancient engineering can be touched without disturbing it. Their materials are smooth, cool, slightly too perfect. They repel dust without seeming to. They have no doors, because they do not need to keep anyone out. They have no windows, because they do not need to look.

They think slowly.

Not because they are dull, but because rapid thought would be a waste of potential. Their minds operate across long intervals, aligning themselves with the plain’s own rhythm. A decision may take a year. A memory may be held for centuries in the orientation of a marker stone. They do not speak aloud. Sound is too costly, too crude, too brief. They communicate by minute changes in field strength, by the timing of shadows, by the arrangement of dust after a storm, by the hum of an artifact when a question has been placed into it. Their conversations are not exchanges but adjustments. One presence alters a calibration point by a microscopic amount. Another notices decades later and replies by easing a nearby lichen colony into a new arc. That is enough.

They are custodians, but not masters. They do not command the storms. They do not direct the veil-drifters. They do not instruct the plated creatures. They listen. They maintain the small anchorages. They ensure that nothing rises too high, gathers too much, or persists too loudly. If an object becomes too prominent, they do not destroy it. They arrange for it to become less necessary. A charge path shifts. A storm repeats over the same spot until the object’s coherence loosens. Dust covers it in patient layers. The plain continues.

Their art is temporary and invisible.

They make arrangements that last only until the next turbulence: pebbles separated by size, grains aligned into a lattice, a veil-drifter guided into the shadow of a cairn at a precise moment. They create lenses of fused dust that focus ordinary light into brief, uncolored clarity. They compose silences. They edit shadows. They leave nothing that can be called a monument. To them, monumentality is a failure of understanding. The highest achievement is to make something so well that it does not need to remain.

When they meet, which is rare, they do not gather in crowds. Two presences may occupy adjacent calibration points for a century, saying nothing. If they exchange anything, it is charge, or memory encoded in field distortion, or the simple fact of attention. They do not touch. Touch would be too definite. Instead, they become aware of one another as one becomes aware of a change in pressure before a storm. Then one withdraws, and the plain is empty again.

They have no young.

Nothing is born there in the ordinary way. New beings, if they arise, arise by condensation, by permission, by long accumulation of pattern. A plated creature may form around a charged grain at the center of a lichen ring. A veil-drifter may unfold from a seam in the regolith after a rare electrical gradient. A marker needle may become inhabited after centuries of standing empty, as if the structure itself has finally matured into presence. The Attenuated do not reproduce. They continue. When they diminish, they leave behind a calibration point, a hum, a rule. When they cease entirely, the plain does not notice except as a small relaxation, a place where attention has lifted.

Death, when it happens, is not decay.

There is no rot, no smell, no softening into earth. There is only disassembly. A body, if it is a body, loses coherence. Its charge returns to the field. Its mineral matter is sorted by the turbulence and laid down elsewhere. A veil-drifter becomes a stain of smoked flakes around a stone. A plated creature becomes a cluster of dark plates, then a pattern in the dust, then a memory in the lichen. The Attenuated, if they end, become rules: a place where storms avoid, a direction that feels correct, a shelter that remains still even when the plain outside churns.

No bones remain. Nothing here is large enough to leave bones.

Even the rare travelers who live there for a time become part of this economy. They learn to move without insistence. They stop expecting horizons to approach. They stop measuring progress by distance. They may sit for days beside a cairn, breathing the thin, filtered air, listening to their own hearts until even that internal noise feels intrusive. If they remain long enough, they begin to perceive the plain’s small crowdedness: the drifters grazing invisible fields, the plated creatures tending lichen arcs, the storm-forms building and forgetting themselves, the micro-machines smoothing the surface, the Attenuated maintaining their silent calibrations. They realize that emptiness was never absence. It was restraint.

The plain does not love its inhabitants. It does not hate them. It has no affection to spare. But it permits them, and in such a place permission is the deepest form of kinship. They live because they have become compatible with finality. They live because they ask for little, leave little, and understand that even the last state of a world can still contain motion.

What lives there, in the end, is not a collection of organisms but a condition of attention.

It is the lichen recording storms over millennia. It is the wiry tufts brightening for a moment in charged air. It is the veil-drifters crossing kilometers without sound. It is the plated creatures placing grains one by one. It is the storm-forms rising into brief architecture and collapsing without grief. It is the rare tall shadows moving through turbulence like unfinished ceremonies. It is the Attenuated in their small anchorages, thinking slowly, maintaining almost nothing, leaving almost nothing behind.

And beneath all of it, in the regolith, in the fused crusts, in the graphite-dark seams and the salt-like powders that were never sea, the ancient engineering continues: not alive, not dead, but attentive. It smooths. It damps. It forbids. It permits. It turns over the plain’s last thoughts in dust and charge and ordinary light.

So the answer to who lives there is not one creature, not one people, not one image.

It is everyone and everything that has learned to exist at the smallest possible margin.

It is the final ecology of a finished world: sparse, dry, conductive, silent, and still, against all reason, in motion.
