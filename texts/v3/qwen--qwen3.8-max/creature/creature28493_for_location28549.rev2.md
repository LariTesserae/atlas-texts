What lives there is not first encountered as a creature. It is felt as occupancy: the pressure of a world already full. The valley has no inhabitants in the ordinary sense of beings who stand apart from their surroundings. The surroundings are the inhabitants, or the inhabitants are the arrangements of the surroundings. To ask who lives there is to ask who lives in a lung, in a boiler, in a forest turned into its own weather. The answer is plural, slow, and mostly nameless.

The first residents are the mats and skins. They are the low carpets of dark moss, the frost-haired growths, the compressed living surfaces that take the weight of every step. They do not live as separate plants so much as sheets, parishes, contiguous bodies. They hold moisture in their upper fibers, shade the tissues below, and seal small wounds with new filaments before the old ones have finished dying. They are patient in a way that makes patience seem less a virtue than a physical condition. Frost forms on them, melts on them, is incorporated into their daily architecture. Where they are trodden, they do not resent the pressure; they compress, then slowly rise again, or they change their grain and grow around the memory of the disturbance.

These mats are the valley’s flesh. Beneath them live older residents: the root-walls, the hardened stems, the buried woody masses that have been pressed and frozen and bound until they resemble timber turned into geology. They are not dead in the way dead things are dead elsewhere. They are retired but still useful. Their tissues no longer grow at the surface, yet they carry fluids through inherited channels, support new towers, anchor runners, and give shape to the terrain. They are ancestors without graves. The valley keeps its old bodies in service. What would be decay in warmer countries becomes here a slow surrender into structure. The ancient wood does not rot so much as it is promoted into infrastructure.

Among these hardened masses live the standing arrays: the erect stems, the jointed columns, the vertical growths that rise in rows and clusters like machinery set in place by some forgotten engineer. They are the most obviously industrial of the inhabitants. They move fluids. They stand under pressure. Their surfaces bead with condensation, and their interiors are tuned to small differences in temperature and elevation. They do not think, but they respond. When cold intensifies, some close microscopic passages. When warmth reaches them, others open. When pressure builds too high, they release vapor with a soft sigh that is more felt than heard. They click and creak inside themselves. They are full of tiny valves, not made, but grown into valvelike necessity.

If one wanted to personify them, one might call them the pump-workers of the basin. But that would be misleading. They do not labor toward an external end. Their work is their living. They lift water because lifting water is what their bodies do. They circulate sap because circulation keeps them from freezing. They sway without wind because internal pressures move through them in waves. From a distance, a field of these stems can seem to breathe. The motion is not graceful in an animal way. It is mechanical, restrained, and strangely deliberate, like the movement of pistons seen through fog.

Higher up, or in places where light gathers despite the clouds, live the broad leaf-plates. They are among the most visible inhabitants, and also among the most protective. Some stand like overlapping shields; others lie in rosettes the width of rooms. Their surfaces are ribbed, polished by frost, darkened by moisture, edged with rime. They are thermal bodies as much as leaves. They catch weak daylight and transmit its warmth inward. They shed cold air along their margins. They fold when the temperature drops too sharply, and when they fold they make small interior chambers where moisture collects and younger growth survives. Under their shelter, tender filaments can persist through conditions that would otherwise stiffen and break them.

These leaf-plates are not gentle, though they shelter. They compete for light and space with the same cold insistence as everything else. A younger growth that rises beneath one may be pressed flat over days. A neighboring plate may be crowded until it cracks along a vein. But the violence is not dramatic. It is the violence of slow expansion. The place has no predators in the ordinary sense, yet it is full of displacement. Lives are not hunted here; they are outgrown, covered, absorbed, or made structural.

In the hollows and cooler districts live the pale tube-thickets. They are among the valley’s filters. Their surfaces are smooth, sometimes almost waxy, sometimes furred with fine frost-trapping hairs. Their interiors are wet and chambered. Moisture enters them as mist, dew, sap, or thaw, and passes through tissues that separate, concentrate, redirect, or store it. They hiss when pressure changes inside them. The sound is not like a voice or a machine exactly; it is more like a landscape remembering that it has pores. Where they cluster, the air smells cleaner, sharper, as if water itself has been peeled into its constituent coldness.

These tube-growths live by gradients. They know nothing in the way animals know, but they discriminate. They are sensitive to humidity, temperature, dissolved salts, the chemistry of neighboring fluids. They close when the air becomes too dry, even if dryness here is only a relative thinning of damp. They open when condensation thickens. They are not passive pipes. They maintain themselves, repair minor ruptures, exude sealing resins when torn. If one is broken by a shifting root-plate or a collapse of ice, nearby growths will extend toward the wound, not out of sympathy, but because the released moisture and chemicals create a gradient too strong to ignore. The valley does not mourn its injuries. It metabolizes them.

Elsewhere, in raised knots and resinous mounds, live the heat-keepers. These are among the strangest residents because they generate warmth in small, localized pulses. They are not fire. They are not volcanic. They are living tissues with metabolisms tuned to produce enough heat to melt frost in their immediate vicinity, keep critical fluids moving, and preserve vulnerable junctions from freezing solid. Around them the air is fractionally softer. Frost retreats in a narrow ring. Ice needles become water, then are drawn back into capillary channels. In the coldest hours, these mounds become tiny centers of activity: filaments orient toward them, condensation beads along their margins, and the rare moving creatures, if they are near, tend to pass close without seeming to choose to do so.

These heat-knots are not hearts, but they serve as hearths. They make possible what otherwise would be interrupted. They allow the valley to remain continuous through cold spells that would arrest less organized life. Their warmth is not generous; it is functional. It is a local defiance of temperature, maintained by chemistry and pressure. They smell faintly of resin and electrified green, and if you touch one, the surface may feel only slightly less cold than the rest of the world, but that slightness is enough to change everything nearby.

There are also the pods and membranes, the breathers and gatekeepers. They live in clusters, often hidden among denser growth, sometimes high on buttressed walls, sometimes buried in depressions where cold air settles. They expand and contract almost imperceptibly. They store gases, regulate internal pressure, and open when thresholds are reached. Some release spores in soft bursts. Others exhale mist. Others close with a faint inward fold, drawing moisture or heat into themselves. They are the valley’s valves. They give the place its rhythm, though the rhythm is too slow and dispersed to be heard as music. It is more like a vast set of adjustments happening in parallel.

If there is anything like a face in the valley, it is not a face but a membrane changing state. These pod-growths do not look alive in the way animals look alive. They look like organs left to operate without a body. Yet they are alive. They repair themselves. They reproduce. They fail. They are replaced. Their deaths are not dramatic: a membrane loses elasticity, a pod cracks, a chamber fills with water and is colonized by finer threads. Even in failure, they become sites of continuation.

The moving residents are harder to see, because the valley is mostly composed of lives that hold position. Yet there are runners, vines, fibrous strands, and creeping mats that move with slow mechanical certainty. They are not animals, but they travel. They extend into gaps, test surfaces, anchor, and pull newer growth behind them. They do not hurry. They do not panic. They are the restless limbs of the stationary majority. When a shelf collapses or a hollow opens, they are often among the first to enter. They do not explore in the sense of curiosity; they explore in the sense of pressure seeking a path. They occupy. They bridge. They make connections between separated masses until what was torn becomes, over time, continuous again.

Then there are the rare animals, if they can even be called that without distorting them.

They are few, pale, and almost apologetic in their presence. The first one might be noticed inside a hollow stem: a translucent insect or wormlike form, nearly clear, its body so adapted to the plant interior that it resembles a moving bead of sap. It has no strong color, no obvious eyes, no wings. It moves with pauses, as if waiting for pressure to decide for it. It feeds on microscopic films, dead tissue, or excess fluid. It may carry spores on its cuticle without knowing it. It is less a separate creature than a mobile fragment of the plant-machine, a bit of the valley’s interior granted temporary motion.

Occasionally, a wingless form crosses an open patch of moss with sudden, mechanical quickness. It is small, hard-shelled, pale brown or silvered, and gone before the eye can settle on it. It does not run like a mouse or scuttle like a beetle so much as it advances in a brief burst, then stops, then vanishes into a crevice. These creatures are not numerous. They are not social. They leave almost no trace. They may live entire lives within a few square meters of compressed litter, eating what the plant-body releases, carrying fungal threads or spore-dust from one fissure to another, performing functions so localized that they seem less like species and more than tasks briefly given legs.

In temporary pools — the cupped leaves, hollow stem bases, and folded plates that fill after cold rain — there are still smaller lives. They are visible only as faint movements, tiny glints, or cloudings in the water. Some exist as dormant stages for long periods, waiting for enough moisture to gather. When a pool forms, they awaken with a speed that seems wrong for such a cold place, multiply briefly, feed on suspended particles, and then, as the water is absorbed or freezes, return to stillness. They are not animals in the grand sense of beasts and flocks. They are pulses of faunal possibility. They prove that the valley has not excluded animal life entirely; it has only reduced it to almost nothing and made it subordinate to the vegetable order.

These rare animals do not dominate. They do not hunt in visible ways. There are no tracks, no bones, no nests, no calls. If they have enemies, those enemies are not predators so much as absorption, cold, or incorporation. A creature that dies here is not left to dry or be scavenged in the ordinary way. It is wetted, chilled, broken down by filaments, drawn into the chemical economy of the surrounding growth. Its body becomes a minor nutrient event. The valley does not permit the dignity of remains.

There are also lives that are neither plant nor animal in any simple sense. The lichen-like growths hint at them. Beneath the surface, fine pale threads move through the living substrate: fungal networks, symbiotic webs, decomposers, mediators. They are the valley’s hidden syntax. They connect tissues that would otherwise be separate. They move nutrients between zones. They enter dead or damaged growth and make it available again. They are not controllers. They do not direct the valley. But they are among the reasons it can behave so coherently. They are the quiet negotiators between hunger and repair, between frost and fluid, between one living mass and another.

In the smell of the place — cold green, wet cellulose, resin, faint ozone — one can sense their activity. They are chemical lives, microscopic lives, film-lives, thread-lives. They work at the scale where water clings to surfaces and cells exchange invisible cargoes. They are not individual enough to be seen as creatures, but they are alive. They bloom in response to thaw. They retreat in hard cold. They seed new surfaces. They are present in every droplet and crevice. Without them, the valley would either choke on its own production or freeze into inert accumulation. With them, decay becomes circulation.

The air itself is inhabited. Not by birds, not by flying herds, but by spores, filaments, seed-dust, ruptured fibers, and suspended moisture carrying living potential. These drifting things are not merely debris. They are the valley’s dispersal, its future, its messages to itself. Some are released by pressure bursts from pods. Some are shed when membranes rupture. Some are torn free by the slow collapse of a shelf and lifted briefly into the cold light. They hang in eddies, catch the pale daylight, and give the atmosphere its faint texture. They are alive in the minimal sense: capable of settling, germinating, joining. Most will not. A few will land where moisture and temperature permit, and then the process of colonization will begin again.

This airborne life makes the valley feel populated even where nothing walks. It is a population of possibility. It is also a kind of memory. Spores and filaments carry inherited forms into new gaps. When a disturbance opens a hollow, the air soon seeds it. When a surface is laid bare, drifts settle on it. The valley’s future is always suspended in its atmosphere.

If one insists on asking who lives there, the deepest answer may be that the valley lives there as a single vast condition. Not as a unified mind, not as a conscious being, but as a coherence. It has no central self, no throne, no face, no voice. Yet it has habits. It has rules. It has preferred responses. It repairs. It routes. It stores. It releases. It overgrows. It corrects. It does not intend, but it persists with such totality that intention seems almost beside the point.

The inhabitants are not merely the visible growths. They are the relationships between the growths. The moss is not separate from the moisture it holds. The stem is not separate from the pressure it moves. The heat-knot is not separate from the frost it keeps at bay. The rare insect is not separate from the stem it passes through. The fungal thread is not separate from the tissues it connects. The spore is not separate from the air that carries it. To live in this place is to be a node in a system so dense that individuality becomes a matter of degree rather than kind.

There are no people there. No tool-makers, no builders, no speakers, no watchers by fire. The absence of persons is not empty; it is filled by other forms of agency. The valley has agency without personality. It chooses in the sense that one growth succeeds where another fails. It remembers in the sense that old channels persist. It responds in the sense that pressure, cold, moisture, and light produce coordinated change. It is not benevolent. It is not malicious. It is not lonely. It has no need for company beyond its own proliferation.

A traveler entering it would not be met. They would be registered. The ground would take the impression of their steps. The air would take the warmth of their breath. The moisture on their clothing would mingle with the mist. If they broke a stem, the wound would smell faintly green and soon be sealed or colonized. If they remained still long enough, frost would gather on their shoulders, and filaments might begin to orient toward the dampness of their body. Not as attack. Not as hunger. Only as the valley’s ordinary fullness recognizing another source of water and heat.

In that sense, even an outsider can become, briefly, part of what lives there. Not a resident, perhaps, but an event within the resident system. The valley does not recognize persons; it recognizes conditions. You are warmth. You are pressure. You are moisture. You are obstruction. You are a temporary shape in a place where all shapes are eventually overgrown.

So what lives there?

The mats live there. The root-walls live there. The stem arrays live there. The leaf-plates, the tube-thickets, the heat-knots, the pods, the membranes, the runners, the buried threads, the spore-dust, and the rare pale errants that move like thoughts through hollow channels. The dead wood lives there as structure. The frost lives there as medium. The moisture lives there as intermittent pulse. The silence lives there as absorption.

And beneath all these lives, if one must invent a final inhabitant, it is the valley itself: a high, cold, green industry without makers; a machine made of growth; a body without organs in the ordinary sense, yet full of functions; a place so complete that it does not need inhabitants because it is, in itself, inhabitation.
