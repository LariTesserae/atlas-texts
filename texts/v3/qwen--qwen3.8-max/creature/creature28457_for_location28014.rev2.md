What lives there cannot be described as a set of separate creatures moving across an empty landscape. It is closer to a pressure of habitation: a saturation of bodies, surfaces, films, warm cavities, slow appetites, and long metabolic labor. Life there does not occupy the world from a distance. It has entered every seam, every pore, every fracture, every bead of water, every warm fold hidden beneath the ice-edged outer skin of the plateau.

The first impression, if one could stand there and somehow remain neutral, is that the land itself is inhabited from within. The mosses and cushion-plants are not merely ground cover; they are neighborhoods. Their dense felts are threaded with creatures so small that they seem less like animals than living details of the surface: bead-crawlers, frost mites, filament-gnats, microscopic grazers that live inside single drops of condensed fog. Some of these tiny lives are aquatic only for a few hours, spending their active phase inside a lens of meltwater before it freezes, evaporates, or sinks into the rock. They have bodies fringed with water-catching hairs, flattened shapes for slipping between leaf cells, and dormant stages no larger than specks of dust. When the cold deepens, they become part of the rime. When warmth returns, they become motion again.

Above that invisible scale move the first of the larger inhabitants: the crust-scrapers. They are low, broad, patient beings, no bigger than a hand or a clenched fist, built like flattened shells with soft undersides and many short gripping limbs. Their mouths are not made for biting but for polishing. They move over rock and living crust with a sideways rasping motion, feeding on microbial films, mineral salts, and the thin sugary exudates released by cold-stressed lichens. Behind them they leave faint shining trails, almost invisible except when the light strikes them from the side. These trails are not paths in the sense of roads; they are feeding marks, temporary chemical signatures that other small creatures later follow, graze again, or use as guides through the glare.

The moss-browsers are larger. They are compact, dense-bodied animals with heavy feet and broad, soft-soled pads that can grip the sharp stone without cutting themselves. Their coverings are thick and layered: not fur alone, but a mixture of filaments, hollow hairs, felted secretions, and living symbiotic growths. From a distance, some of them look like walking cushions of frost-stained wool. Their backs often carry a greenish or silvery cast because they host mosses, lichens, and microbial films that grow better on the animal’s warmth than on bare stone. They do not seem to suffer these passengers. They are benefited by them: the growths help camouflage, insulate, and perhaps regulate moisture. In turn, the animals shed flakes of skin, waste heat, and nutrient-rich oils that feed the coatings they carry.

Their faces are not delicate. They have clustered eyes rather than two forward-facing ones, because the brightness here requires many angles of vision and many kinds of filtering. Some eyes are dark and glare-shielded; others are pale and sensitive to ultraviolet; still others seem to read the polarization of light off ice and wet leaves. Their mouths are set low and sideways, adapted to crop moss from dense mats without tearing out the root-networks beneath. They eat slowly, with a lateral grinding motion, and when many of them feed together the sound becomes part of the plateau’s drone: a soft, continuous abrasion, like stone being polished by patient weather.

Then there are the herds that seem almost electrical.

These creatures move more slowly than the browsers, but they are heavier and stranger. Their bodies are insulated so deeply that their outer surfaces can build up charge as they brush against charged vegetation, spore clouds, and one another. In the brightest hours, when the air is driest and the light most intense, their outlines can blur with faint static halos. Tiny sparks pass between their flanks and the tips of nearby plants. Their hairs stand outward in stiff fans. Smaller organisms ride these fields: mites and spore-hitchers that leap from one charged body to another, or drift briefly through the ionized air as if carried by invisible hands.

These static herds are not aggressive. Their power is cumulative rather than violent. They move through the vegetation with such density and consistency that they alter the local distribution of moisture and heat. Where they pause, warmth gathers. Where they pass, spores are lifted, seeds are released, and microbial crusts are broken open just enough to renew themselves. They are not merely animals; they are slow disturbances. Their presence can change the behavior of fog. Their body heat, pooled and released, can bend mist into small eddies. In places where they gather for days at a time, the air above them becomes dim with suspended life.

The air itself has its own inhabitants.

The flying things are not like birds or insects from gentler climates. They are narrow-bodied, reflective, and astonishingly patient. Their wings are long and thin, veined with structures that scatter light, often appearing as bright lines or mirrored slits when they turn. They do not swarm in panic. They move in rolling waves, rising and falling together as if responding to pressure changes invisible to an outsider. Some feed on airborne spores, others on mist-borne microbes, others on the charged particles that gather around plant tissues during the brightest part of the day. Their mouths, when they have them, are often not mouths at all but filtering fans, electrostatic combs, or absorbent membranes.

Some of the fliers never truly land. They rest in the dense upper surface of the vegetation, anchoring themselves with hooked limbs while their wings fold into protective sheets. Others live almost entirely above the plateau, riding thermal plumes made by the venting heat-organisms below. They drink condensation from their own wing surfaces. They excrete tiny droplets of nutrient fluid that fall into the moss and are immediately absorbed. Their egg cases drift through the air like pale seeds, suspended on silk-like filaments, sometimes collecting in glowing bands where the wind slows over a ridge. These airborne nurseries are among the most beautiful and unsettling sights of the place: clouds of unborn life moving through brightness, each case carrying a future body into the industrial hum of the plateau.

Below the fliers, among the thicker growths, live the moisture-keepers.

These are animals so closely tied to water that they seem almost temporary. They have soft, sealed skins, absorbent undersides, and bodies shaped to collect beads of condensation. Some flatten themselves against cold leaves at night, becoming living surfaces on which frost can form. In the morning, when the frost melts, they drink from their own backs. Others curl into tight discs inside damp hollows, sharing moisture through their touching bodies, reducing loss until the next fog passes. They are not intelligent in any detached sense, but their behavior is precise. They know where the beads gather, which hollows hold water longest, which leaves cup dew, which stones crack with hidden ice. They move along these hidden routes with inherited certainty.

Some moisture-keepers are communal to an extreme degree. They form living plugs in the mouths of damp crevices, their bodies creating a temporary seal that keeps humid air inside. Around them, smaller creatures cluster: larvae, fungal threads, tiny filter-feeders, all borrowing the stabilized microclimate. When the plug disperses, the pocket breathes again. This is not cooperation in a sentimental sense. It is closer to a physiological agreement, a shared dependence made visible.

Where the rock fissures run deep and chemical warmth rises, there live the thermal beings.

They are among the strangest residents because they barely seem like animals at all. Some are rooted or nearly rooted: broad, columnar bodies anchored in stone, divided into layered chambers where symbiotic microbes break down minerals and produce heat. Their surfaces are ridged, folded, or fluted, shaped by long growth rather than movement. From their crowns or vent-openings, slow plumes of vapor rise into the cold air. These plumes are not mere exhaust; they are ecological events. They draw mist downward. They create updrafts that lift spores and small fliers. They warm the surrounding stone enough for other organisms to settle in concentric rings.

Around each thermal being, a community arranges itself. There are heat-moths or heat-motes—tiny fluttering forms that live only in the warm boundary layer. There are vapor-sucklers, soft-bodied creatures that press themselves to the vent-rim and drink the condensed steam. There are crusts that require both warmth and mineral-rich moisture, growing in metallic colors on the heated stone. There are slow, flattened animals that clean the vent-openings, preventing blockages and receiving nutrients in return. The thermal being itself may be solitary in body, but it is never alone in function. It is a power source, a gathering point, a weather-maker in miniature. When several stand along a fault line, their vents together can produce a drifting bank of fog that reshapes the local light for hours.

Not far from these heat-sources, and often in colder zones just beyond their influence, are the refinery growths.

They are not animals in the ordinary sense, nor plants, nor simple colonies. They resemble pale shelves, crusts, or tiered reefs built by living secretion. Their surfaces are pocked with thousands of tiny cavities, each one engaged in some chemical labor: filtering fluid, concentrating salts, producing waxes, exuding antifreeze sugars, spinning structural fibers, or converting waste into usable nutrient paste. They do not move, but they are intensely active. The fluids they release alter the ground around them. They make new substrate. They create pockets where other life can settle. They are part of the reason the plateau can remain so densely occupied despite having no deep rivers or lasting lakes. Much of the material needed by life is manufactured locally, in living cavities, by organisms that are themselves part factory and part terrain.

These refinery growths often have caretakers. Small creatures move over them, clearing blocked pores, eating parasitic films, or transporting secretions from one cavity to another. The caretakers do not build anything. They do not use tools. They are fitted to their work by body and instinct. Their mouths, limbs, and secretions are adapted to the surfaces they maintain. They are as much organs of the refinery as the refinery is a place for them.

The largest inhabitants are rarer, and their slowness makes them seem almost geological.

These are the walking mounds: great dense-backed creatures whose bodies are so insulated, so layered with living coatings, that they resemble moving portions of the plateau itself. They are not fast. They may cross a kilometer in a season. Their backs carry moss, bead-pools, fungal shelves, egg nurseries, and whole generations of smaller animals. When one stops, life accumulates around it. When it moves again, some of that life goes with it; some is left behind to colonize the disturbed ground. Their dung is not waste but a warm, nutrient-rich node, quickly invaded by mats, larvae, and heat-seeking microbes. Their fallen bodies become major events: a sudden abundance of warmth, shelter, and chemistry that can support a dense local community for years.

They are not rulers of the landscape. Nothing here rules in that sense. But they are important because they are large enough to carry continuity across distances. Their slow migrations redistribute organisms. Their bodies connect places that would otherwise remain isolated by sharp stone and cold. They are living transport systems without intention, roads without roads.

Predation exists, but it does not look like pursuit.

The regulators of this place are patient. Some are almost motionless ambushers, hidden in plant mats, waiting for small animals to brush against a filament or enter a fold of tissue. Others are more like biological traps: surfaces that become sticky when touched, ciliated pits that draw in prey with slow currents, or communal feeders that overwhelm by numbers rather than violence. There are thin, cold-blooded cullers that move through dense populations and take only the weak, the overheated, the parasitized, or the chemically unbalanced. They do not chase because there is no need. The density of life provides constant opportunity. Their role is less to kill than to maintain the balance of an overcrowded system.

Some predators are electrical. They possess organs or tissues capable of producing weak pulses, which they send through damp vegetation to detect nearby movement. A few can stun small prey with brief discharges, not enough to burn but enough to interrupt motion. These discharges add to the plateau’s strange sensory atmosphere: little snaps of blue light in shaded crevices, sudden silences where a humming patch of life goes still, the sharp metallic scent of disturbed air.

There are also decomposers, but “decomposer” is too simple a word. Death here is not allowed to remain death for long. A carcass, a shed skin, a broken mat, a frozen flier—all are entered almost immediately by fungal threads, microbial films, larvae, heat-seekers, and chemical scavengers. The process is not grotesque so much as efficient. The body becomes a warm patch, a nursery, a nutrient source. It is not removed from the world; it is reassigned.

If one asks who the people of this place are, the answer becomes difficult.

There are beings here who might be called persons, but not because they resemble humans or make anything with hands. They have no hands in the usual sense, because hands imply a separation between body and world that this place has never permitted. Instead they have manipulating organs that are also sensory organs, feeding organs, heat-exchange surfaces, and communicative membranes. They are broad, low, and dense, built to conserve warmth and move through crowded spaces without damaging them. Their bodies are covered in layered growths, some of which are native to them and some of which they acquire from the environment. To look at one is to see not a single animal but a moving ecology.

They do not build shelters. They enter shelter. They fold themselves into warm seams, press against thermal growths, sink into cushion-mosses, or cluster with others until their combined heat creates a livable pocket. Their presence changes the space they occupy. They do not construct a home; they become part of the home that already exists.

Their intelligence is slow, distributed, and practical. It is not the intelligence of detached planning, but of deep adjustment. They understand moisture gradients, heat leakage, fungal signals, pressure changes, and the needs of the living surfaces around them. They can encourage a moss to thicken by feeding it with secretions from their own skin. They can calm an overgrown microbial bloom by introducing a balancing chemical from glands in their undersides. They can guide a bead of meltwater into a root-mat by altering the angle of a leaf with their breath, or by pressing their body against the plant until its shape changes. None of this is tool-use in the external sense. It is embodied expertise.

They communicate in several ways at once.

Their voices are low pressure waves, felt in the chest before they are heard. These calls can carry across packed vegetation and through stone shelves, especially when the air is still. They also communicate by electrical pulses passed through damp surfaces, by scent-trails, by changes in skin temperature, and by the rhythmic flickering of reflective tissues in bright light. A group of them in conversation is a layered event: throbbing sound, faint static, chemical whispers, and flashes of mirrored skin. To an outsider, it might seem like ritual. To them, it is ordinary coordination.

They do not have property as a concept built around objects. There are no artifacts to own. Instead they have responsibilities. A particular vent-line may be tended by a lineage over generations, not because it belongs to them but because their bodies know its rhythms. A nursery mat may be maintained by certain individuals whose secretions best protect the young. A fog-catching ridge may be visited in sequence by different groups whose movements prevent the vegetation from being damaged. Their society is organized less by possession than by role, timing, and bodily fitness to particular tasks.

Their young are not born into emptiness. They are placed into living systems. Some develop in warm external pouches formed by the bodies of adults. Others are laid into communal nursery mats, where microbial heat and fungal filaments support their growth. The young are often coated from birth with symbiotic organisms, so that their identities are mixed with others before they can move alone. They learn by pressure, taste, vibration, and imitation. They do not learn to make things. They learn to fit.

Their elders are not always old individuals. Sometimes an elder is a composite: a cluster of bodies that have remained together so long that their tissues have begun to share chemical signals and heat patterns. Such clusters move slowly, if at all, and are consulted—or rather, responded to—by others because their accumulated presence stabilizes the local system. They are repositories of pattern. They do not command. They persist.

If these beings have art, it is not object-making. It is arrangement. It is the shaping of a warm hollow, the timing of a call, the coordination of many bodies into a temporary pattern that improves the flow of water or heat. It is the cultivation of a particularly beautiful microbial sheen, not for ownership but for communal effect. Their beauty is functional. Their function is beautiful. The two are not separable here.

If they have war, it is rare and strange. Conflict in such a crowded world is dangerous not because of weapons but because damage spreads. A torn mat, an overheated vent, a poisoned film can affect thousands of lives. Their conflicts are therefore usually resolved by pressure, display, chemical exclusion, or the slow withdrawal of one group from another. When violence occurs, it is quickly absorbed by the wider system. Bodies are not left as monuments. They are reclaimed.

The inhabitants do not experience the plateau as a collection of resources. They are inside its processes too completely for that. They do not extract water from the world; they participate in its movement. They do not take heat; they share it, produce it, conserve it, redirect it. They do not use the brightness; they are shaped by it, reflected by it, warned by it, fed by it. Even their emotions—if one can call them that—are likely tied to atmospheric conditions: the tension before a static discharge, the relief of fog returning, the communal calm of a warm pocket, the collective urgency of a sudden freeze.

What lives there is loud because it is numerous and because every life is working.

The sounds of the inhabitants are not separate from the landscape. The scraping of crust-feeders becomes part of the drone. The wing-whine of fliers becomes part of the brightness. The seed-pops, shell-clicks, and pressure-calls become part of the rhythm. The great heat-beings vent with low sighs. Droplets fall into hidden chambers made by living bodies. Static snaps when charged coats pass too close. A chorus of low voices can rise at dawn, not to greet the sun but to coordinate the redistribution of moisture after a cold night. The whole place answers itself.

What lives there is also luminous in ways that are not always visible.

Some organisms glow faintly in the blue shadows, not with bright fire but with dull metabolic light: fungal threads, microbial films, egg clusters, and the undersides of certain fronds. This light is not for show. It may mark chemical states, reproductive readiness, or heat gradients. At night, when the sky is clear and the cold becomes severe, the plateau can be dotted with dim living constellations. They are not cities. They are metabolisms.

Nothing here is lonely.

That is perhaps the most important truth of its inhabitants. Loneliness would be a physical danger. To be isolated is to lose heat, lose moisture, lose signal, lose function. Almost every creature exists in contact with another body, another surface, another chemical trace. Even the apparently solitary thermal columns are surrounded by dependent life. Even the fliers moving in vast waves are part of atmospheric patterns created by the land below. Even the smallest bead-crawler lives in a droplet made by the collective breathing of the plateau.

If a traveler came, they would not meet the inhabitants as one meets strangers across an empty space. They would be met as an intrusion into density. Small organisms would already be on them. Static would lift their hair. The brightness would force them to squint. The sound would press against their chest. To survive, they would have to stop standing apart. They would have to lower themselves, find a warm seam, move slowly, breathe with the rhythm of the place, and allow themselves to become part of the crowd rather than an interruption to it.

The inhabitants would not hate them for that. They would simply assess them in terms of heat, water, pressure, and function. Can this body hold warmth? Does it carry moisture? Does it disturb too much? Can it fit?

What lives there is not a list of species. It is a packed, slow, luminous system of bodies and responsibilities. It is bead-crawlers in temporary ponds. It is static herds humming with charge. It is mirror-winged fliers rolling through thin air. It is vent-beings breathing steam into the cold. It is refinery shelves making wax, salt, and antifreeze in living cavities. It is walking mounds carrying whole ecosystems on their backs. It is patient regulators keeping density from devouring itself. It is the seam-dwelling people, if one dares call them people, who have no tools, no houses, no roads, and no artifacts, because they have become skilled enough to live inside the living world without separating themselves from it.

They are the children of a young, sharp, bright land that has not learned gentleness but has learned fullness. They do not soften the world. They fill it. They do not build. They continue. They are not quiet, not simple, not pastoral. They are numerous, loud, slow, radiant, damp with shared breath, and so tightly woven into the stone and ice and light that to ask who lives there is almost to misunderstand the question.

The plateau does not have inhabitants so much as it has become inhabited.
