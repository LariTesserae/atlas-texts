What lives there is not, first of all, a collection of separate creatures standing inside a landscape. It is a living settlement in the fullest sense: a green, warm, low-bodied civic organism whose inhabitants are less tenants than functions, less residents than rhythms made flesh. The place is inhabited by the plant-town itself, by the fauna that move through its chambers, and by something harder to name—an acoustic life, a pressure, a distributed awareness that seems to belong to the whole hollow more than to any one body within it.

The oldest and largest thing living there is the plant-organ that forms the settlement. It is not merely the ground, the walls, the stairs, the basins, and the roof. It is the primary inhabitant. It lives as architecture. It lives as infrastructure. It lives as a slow, patient, vegetable municipality. Its body is the town: the worn paths are its skin, the water channels are its vessels, the hollows are its lungs, the seed-cups are its glands, the resonance chambers are its throats. It does not possess the settlement; it is the settlement.

This plant-body is old in the way that living cities are old, not through monuments but through use. It has been shaped by countless repetitions: water falling where water always falls, bodies passing where bodies have always passed, growth thickening where pressure demands thickness, hollows remaining open because they are needed. It is not conscious in a quick or human sense. It does not decide in moments. It decides in pulses, in turgor shifts, in the slow opening of membranes, in the loosening of seed-doors, in the closing of pores when the air grows too tense. Its awareness, if it can be called that, is distributed through vibration and moisture. It knows when the chorus is rising. It knows when a channel is blocked. It knows when a surface has been touched too often and needs to harden, or when a cup must spill before it overfills.

In that sense, the plant-town is not a passive environment. It is a participant. It gives rooms. It withdraws rooms. It holds water. It releases water. It amplifies some sounds and swallows others. It lets certain passages stay open because creatures continue to use them, and it lets other passages close when they are forgotten. The town lives by being lived in. Its hollows are maintained not only by its own growth but by the tiny, repeated labor of the beings inside it. Without them, its pores would clog, its basins would foul, its seed-doors would not open properly, its acoustic chambers would lose their tuning. The plant is the body of the place, but the fauna are its upkeep, its senses, its weather, and sometimes its voice.

The most obvious lives, or at least the most obvious presences, are the cup-dwellers: small glossy frog-like creatures that inhabit the intermittent pools, seed-cups, and waxed basins. They are sleek, dark-bodied, often green-black or blue-green, with limbs that end in round, moistened pads for gripping slick leaf-surfaces and curved vessel walls. Their skin looks perpetually burnished, as if they have just emerged from water even when they are dry. They do not crowd the open chambers. They stay near water, tucked beneath the rims of cups, pressed into shadowed crevices, balanced along the inner lips of basins where the light barely reaches. Only their throats or eyes betray them when they move.

Their calls are sharp, metallic, and startlingly loud for such small bodies. Each cry strikes the curved chambers and comes back changed, multiplied, sometimes arriving from several directions at once. They do not merely make noise; they participate in the hydrology of the place. When enough of them call in the right rhythm, the upper membranes respond. Condensation gathers more quickly along the high veins. Droplets thicken, tremble, and fall. The frogs seem to know this, or their bodies know it, because their choruses often rise just before one of the brief internal rains. They sing moisture into being. Or perhaps it would be fairer to say they sing the settlement’s moisture into rhythm.

They also keep the water clean. They feed on thin films, tiny larvae, algae-bloom, and the soft organic matter that would otherwise choke the cups. They defend certain basins, not with obvious violence but with calls, postures, and sudden darting movements that drive intruders away. Their territories are acoustic more than spatial. A cup may belong to a particular frog not because the frog physically occupies it at all times, but because its call has saturated that hollow so thoroughly that other cup-dwellers recognize the space as occupied. In the central chamber, their voices can create the impression of a hidden congregation: dozens of small lives, invisible but intensely present, ringing from the walls like struck glass.

Another major life in the plant-town is the broad-winged flying fauna. These are insect-like, but heavy and slow compared with ordinary insects. Their wings are wide, translucent in places, veined like leaves, and often mottled in greens, browns, and dull golds. They do not swarm in the ordinary sense. They move through the upper galleries in loose, irregular circuits, passing from leaf-pane to leaf-pane, pausing beneath shelves, hovering just long enough to touch a membrane with their bodies before continuing. Their wingbeats are deep, almost percussive. They make a hum that is felt in the chest as much as heard.

These broad-winged creatures are important to the plant-town’s breathing and reproduction. Their heavy flight stirs the air in the hollows. Their bodies brush against pollen-bearing structures and carry fine grains from chamber to chamber. Their vibrations help loosen seed-doors and encourage certain membranes to open. They are not pollinators only; they are living ventilators, key-turners in a botanical lock. The plant’s architecture has grown around their habits, and their habits have grown around the plant’s needs. When they pass through a high corridor, the leaf-windows may tremble and brighten, as if the room has inhaled.

They are also part of the town’s sound. Their hums form a slow underlying drone beneath the sharper calls of the cup-dwellers and the dry taps of beetles. In the deepest parts of the hollow, where the plant’s vascular fluids move with heavy thumps and creaks, the wing-hums blend into the architecture until it becomes impossible to tell where the body of the plant ends and the bodies of the flying creatures begin. Sometimes a single broad-winged form crossing a shaft of green-gold light is the only visible sign of a population that fills the air with sound.

Closer to the ground, in the soft and polished root-felt, live the pale crawlers. They are not beautiful in any conventional sense. They are soft-bodied, limbless or many-limbed depending on the kind, often colorless or faintly milky, adapted to darkness and pressure. They move through the woven root-mat and fallen leaf-fiber, opening tiny channels, eating shed tissue, clearing clogged capillary routes, and spreading the waxy secretions that waterproof certain basins. They are the settlement’s quiet maintenance. Without them, the ground would become either too dense or too foul. They keep the underlayers breathing.

They rarely appear in the open. Their presence is known by subtle changes: a softening of the path, a new damp line along a channel, a faint shifting in the root-felt, a small opening where none was before. They are not fast. They do not need to be. Their work is slow, constant, and essential. They are the kind of life that a city would call infrastructure if it were made of stone and pipe, but here they are living infrastructure. They are the plumbing made into small, pale bodies.

There are also beetles and other hard-shelled tappers, creatures that live along bark-flanges, woody ribs, and the undersides of shelves. Their bodies are dry, resonant, often black or dull green with polished elytra. They tap on the plant’s harder surfaces, producing percussive signals that travel far through the structure. Some of these taps are territorial. Some are mating calls. Some seem to coordinate the opening of pores or the loosening of old bark-skin. In the hollow acoustics, a single beetle can sound like several, and several can sound like a slow, deliberate mechanism working inside the walls.

These tappers feed on fungal films, dead bark-skin, and the thin growths that try to close breathing passages. They are cleaners as much as musicians. Their percussion is not ornamental. It is part of the settlement’s maintenance schedule. When their tapping becomes regular, other creatures respond. Drip-troughs may begin to gurgle. Seed-doors may loosen. Certain hidden galleries may open as if the rhythm has unlocked them. The plant-town has grown responsive to these signals, and the beetles have grown responsive to the plant-town. Their lives are braided together so tightly that neither could remain itself without the other.

Then there are the predators.

They are fewer than the others, but their presence shapes everything. In a place so loud, predation does not primarily rely on stealth in the ordinary visual sense. It relies on acoustic manipulation. The predators here are slender, patient, often leaf-colored or bark-colored, with bodies folded into the architecture’s crevices. Some are insect-like; some are more frog-like or salamander-like; some seem almost like mobile plant-parts until they move. They do not chase often. They wait. They know the folds of the hollow, the dead zones, the places where sound bends strangely, the corners where echoes arrive late or early.

Their most unsettling adaptation is their relationship to silence. In a settlement defined by roar, resonance, and constant layered noise, a sudden pocket of quiet is terrifying. Some of these predators create that quiet. Not necessarily by producing an opposing sound, but by absorbing, blocking, or folding local vibration in such a way that a small dead field forms around them. When the noise thins, when the chorus seems to step back, when the drips suddenly feel far away and the air becomes tense, something is hunting. The inhabitants know this. The cup-dwellers stop calling. The crawlers freeze in their channels. The broad-winged flyers slow and cling to the undersides of shelves. The whole chamber seems to hold its breath.

The predators are necessary. Without them, the hidden populations inside the walls would grow too dense, foul the basins, overclean the membranes, or exhaust the plant’s shed tissue. They are the settlement’s discipline. They are also part of its strangeness. In their presence, the place feels less like a peaceful green sanctuary and more like a resonant trap, a beautiful instrument that can suddenly become a killing chamber. Their violence is rarely seen. It is usually felt only as a gap in the sound, followed by a small motion, then stillness.

There are smaller lives too, countless and mostly nameless: algae-slicks that line the shallow cups with blue-green velvet; fungal films that must be carefully grazed but not allowed to vanish; mite-like creatures that clean breathing pores; spore-carriers that move through high galleries; tiny membranous grazers that keep translucent leaf-panes clear enough to admit light. They are not individuals in any meaningful sense to an outside observer, but they are part of the living town. They are the dust, the polish, the immune action, the slow hygiene of the place. Without them, the plant-organ would sicken. Its light would dim. Its pores would choke. Its water would sour. Its chambers would lose clarity.

If one asks whether there is a “who” here, something more than fauna, something that could be called a people, the answer is more difficult. There may be. If so, they are not a crowd. They are not a market people, not a tool-making people, not a people of possessions, banners, weapons, or open assemblies. They are sparse, quiet, and deeply integrated into the acoustic life of the hollow. They might be called the Listeners, though that is only a visitor’s name for them. They do not seem to gather in the center in large numbers. They occupy the settlement the way notes occupy a chord: separately, but made meaningful by relation.

The Listeners, if they can be called that, are thin-bodied and light-limbed, built for moving across living membranes without tearing them. Their skin is smooth, mottled green and shadow-brown, often damp-looking even when the air is dry. They have large, sensitive tympanic patches along their throats, shoulders, or ribs, membranes that tremble with the smallest pressure changes. Their eyes are wide and reflective, adapted to the green-gold light beneath the leaf-roof. Their fingers and toes end in soft pads, not quite like those of the cup-dwellers but similar enough to show convergence: everything that lives here must learn to hold onto slick, living surfaces without harming them.

They do not make tools. They do not carry objects. They have no baskets, no spears, no knives, no cords. Their relationship to the settlement is not one of fabrication but of participation. They clear channels with their hands and breath. They clean pores with slow, repeated touches. They guide growth over generations by pressure, by warmth, by the removal of dead tissue, by encouraging certain shoots and discouraging others. They sing to membranes, not magically but practically: certain pitches help certain structures respond. They know where to stand so that their weight opens a passage. They know which veins must not be touched, which cups must be left full, which seed-doors must be approached only after a particular rhythm has been established.

They are not numerous. Their low density is not poverty; it is ecological balance. The plant-town cannot support crowds in the open. Its vast chambers are built for resonance, not mass occupation. A single Listener on a broad leaf-terrace may seem lonely, but that impression is misleading. The Listener is not alone in the human sense. It is embedded in a web of sound. It hears the cup-dwellers, the beetles, the wing-hums, the vascular thumps of the plant-body, and the hidden movements of other lives inside the walls. It may itself be heard by others without being seen. Its presence is announced less by its body than by the way the chamber subtly changes around it.
