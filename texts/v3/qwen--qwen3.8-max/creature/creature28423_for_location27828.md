Whoever lives there is plural.

There is no single body that can be pointed to, no figure that steps forward from the green and says, in whatever way such things might be said, *this is mine, this is me, this is where I sleep and stand and eat and end.* The place is not inhabited by a creature set apart from it. It is inhabited by an arrangement that has become continuous with itself: a wet, rooted, mechanical commonwealth, a slow municipal life made of plants, water, warmth, pressure, and old worked matter. If one had to give the resident a name, the nearest name would be the place itself — the sunken court, the under-garden, the saturated interior — but even that would be too simple. What lives there is not merely located in the hollow. What lives there is the hollow’s own habit of continuing.

The first inhabitant is the floor.

That is not a metaphor, or not only one. The ground is a living surface, a thick knit of roots, rhizomes, moss, compressed leaf-fall, and fine fibrous decay. It is not walked upon so much as it is shared with. It yields, but it does not surrender. It accepts weight and then slowly returns to its own shape. It holds water without becoming mud. It bears the marks of every small disturbance and repairs them by spreading, thickening, binding, and filling in. In a place without animals, without scratching claws or burrowing bodies or restless feet, the floor has developed a patience that seems almost deliberate. Where it is pressed, it grows denser. Where it is cut, it closes. Where it becomes too dry, it draws moisture upward through capillary threads of root and stem. Where it becomes too wet, it lifts itself into low mounds and lets water gather in the channels between.

The floor is therefore not a surface but a population. It is made of countless rootlets, moss cushions, pale rhizomes, microbial films, and decaying leaves in every stage of collapse and renewal. It does not have thoughts in any narrow sense, but it has tendencies. It prefers certain gradients. It moves toward warmth. It grows away from blockage. It seeks contact. It fills gaps. If a stone shifts, the floor will eventually grow over it. If a pipe warms the ground nearby, roots will gather there and thicken around it. If a channel becomes clogged with debris, the water will find another way, and the floor will learn the new route by depositing more growth along its banks. This is one kind of life there: the life of patient accretion, the life that builds by accumulation rather than by movement.

The second inhabitant is the green wall.

The walls are not dead supports over which vegetation climbs. They are themselves woven lives, old trunks and stems grown into one another, folded and braced and fused by long pressure. They hold the earth back. They retain fragments of older construction: brick, stone, rusted iron, broken stair-edges, the buried curve of an arch. But these are only bones now, and the living wall has grown over them. The wall is full of joints that are not hinges, knots that are not fastenings, seams that are not carpentry. Bark has thickened into ridges. Stems have curled into cables. Roots have descended like ropes and become load-bearing. Moss seals every crevice. Liverworts make dark green plaques wherever water runs most steadily.

These wall-growths are among the oldest residents. They remember by shape. A trunk swollen around an iron bracket carries the bracket’s history in its flesh. A stem bent around a vanished pipe preserves the pipe’s absence as a hollow. A fused lattice of wood may hold the angle of a demolished doorway long after the door has rotted away. In this way, the walls are both structure and archive. They do not record the past as marks on paper; they record it as mass, as curvature, as pressure, as healed injury. They live slowly, but they live intensely. Every inch of them is occupied. Every surface has a tenant: moss, leaf, condensation, fungal thread, bacterial slick, the faint green bloom of algae where a lamp shines too steadily.

The third inhabitant is the canopy, though “canopy” is almost too grand a word for the low, dense overhead growth that closes the place in. It is more like a ceiling made of leaves, bark sheets, hanging roots, old grates, glass fragments, and iron ribs. Light comes through it in weak green-gray washes, or in small amber pools where the lamps below catch wet surfaces and return them as glow. The leaves here are broad, dark, and unhurried. They do not flutter in wind, because there is little wind. They move under their own weight, under the weight of water, under slow changes of temperature. They collect droplets and release them. They bend toward whatever warmth is nearest. They shade the machines below in ways that are not accidental: a broad leaf may hang over a valve because the valve gives off heat; a curtain of root-fiber may thicken beside a lamp because the lamp encourages growth.

The canopy is not a roof in the ordinary sense. It is a regulating skin. It keeps the air below from drying. It catches condensation and returns it as drip. It softens whatever light descends from the outer world and turns it into the dim, submarine brightness of the under-garden. It also gives the place its sense of enclosure, its intimate pressure. Under that ceiling, sound gathers. Every drip is near. Every mechanical knock is close. Every creak of wood seems to come from just beyond the next wall. The canopy makes the hollow into a chamber, and a chamber implies a resident.

The fourth inhabitant is water.

Strictly speaking, water is not alive. But in this place it behaves so continuously, so purposefully, so pervasively, that it cannot be treated as mere substance. It is the medium of everything. It carries heat. It carries fragments of plant matter. It carries dissolved minerals and microbial life. It carries sound. It carries memory, in the sense that every channel remembers where water has flowed before. A groove worn into root-fiber, a slicked stone, a blackened edge of wood, a line of pale deposit along a basin wall: these are water’s records. Water is always arriving, always leaving, always returning. It beads on leaves. It slides down walls in thin threads. It stands in black mirrors between root-terraces. It gathers in shallow channels, moves through narrow passages, drops from ledge to ledge, and disappears into grates or root-mesh only to reappear elsewhere as a seep.

Water is also the place’s means of communication. When something changes — a slight warmth, a vibration, a shift in pressure, a lamp burning hotter than usual — moisture answers. Condensation forms. Drip-rates change. A channel that had murmured evenly begins to lap. A dry corner darkens as seepage finds it. A pool trembles without any apparent cause, then stills. These are not messages in the way words are messages, but they are signals nonetheless. The whole system speaks through water. It says: here is warmth, here is blockage, here is fullness, here is need, here is excess, here is balance. In a world without animal voices, water becomes the resident’s voice.

The fifth inhabitant is the machinery.

At first, it may seem strange to call machinery a living resident. But here the machines have ceased to be separate objects. They have been taken into the place’s metabolism. They are not decorations, and they are not intrusions. They are organs. Pumps lift water from lower basins and send it into swollen living reservoirs overhead. Wheels turn slowly in the channels, their paddles black with constant wetness. Pistons move with long, heavy patience. Belts slip with soft hisses. Valves open and close according to pressures that have been learned over years. Engines sit in alcoves, their housings furred with moss, their warmth shaded by leaves that have grown to fit them. Cables pass through root-masses. Pipes vanish into living wood. Gauges are held in place by bark that has slowly closed around their fittings.

The machines are alive in the way a heart is alive when it is still part of a body: by rhythm, by dependence, by function. They do not live apart from the green mass. If the plants dried, the machines would seize. If the machines stopped entirely, the water would stagnate, the channels would choke, the heat would distribute wrongly, and the living fabric would change. So they belong to one another. The machinery provides motion; the vegetation provides repair. The machinery provides pressure; the vegetation provides containment. The machinery provides intervals, cycles, pulses; the vegetation provides growth, sealing, and slow adjustment. Together they form a working body.

The sixth inhabitant is microbial.

No one sees them except as effects: a film on dark water, a bloom on warm stone, a softening of fallen leaves, a faint heat rising from deeper root beds, a patina on old iron, a sweetness in the smell of wet bark. They are the smallest residents, and perhaps the most numerous. They are the quiet chemists of the place. They take what falls, what leaks, what condenses, what dies, and turn it into something usable. They make the floor warm. They help break compressed plant debris into a substance that roots can enter. They stain wood and soften metal. They create the faint slickness on stone lips and the dark richness of the marshy margins. They are not visible as individuals, but their collective labor is everywhere. Without them, the place would become a tomb of undecayed leaves. With them, it remains a workshop of transformation.

And then there is the resident as a whole.

If all these lives are taken together — the floor, the walls, the canopy, the water, the machines, the microbes — something emerges that is not quite any of them alone. The under-garden has a unity. It has a style of being. It has preferences, even if those preferences are expressed only as growth patterns. It has moods, even if those moods are nothing more than shifts in humidity, temperature, drip-rate, and sound. It has a kind of memory, even if that memory is stored in rings of wood, silt lines, healed scars, worn channels, and the positions of old stones.

This whole resident might be called the saturated court, or the rooted assembly, or simply the place-after-it-has-become-itself. It does not stand inside the hollow; it is the hollow’s living condition. It does not look out from a face; it registers the world through surfaces. It knows warmth by the way condensation gathers. It knows pressure by the way water slows. It knows damage by the way channels change pitch. It knows age by the way bark closes over iron. Its senses are gradients: wet and less wet, warm and less warm, bright and less bright, open and blocked, flowing and still.

It does not move as animals move. It has no sudden flight, no darting, no alert startle. But it is not motionless. Its motion is slow and distributed. A root thickens around a bolt. Moss spreads across a gauge. A leaf bends under the weight of a droplet. A pump thuds at long intervals. A wheel turns. A valve releases steam that becomes water again. A lamp flickers as current changes. A floating mat of leaf debris drifts one hand’s width down a canal, catches in a root-mesh, and becomes part of the bank. These are the gestures of the resident. They are small, but they are constant.

It has labor. The place is not merely alive; it is productive. Things are made there, slowly and without spectacle. Fiber is softened and pressed. Sap is drawn and reduced. Starch settles in vats. Oil separates in quiet basins. Strips of bark hang in warm circulating air. Peat is compressed into dense blocks. Water is distilled, lifted, filtered, and recirculated. Electricity hums through cables and keeps the system moving where gravity is not enough. The resident makes these things not with hands but with channels, presses, heat, growth, and time. Its workshops are not rooms so much as organs of circulation. Its factories are not buildings so much as conditions carefully maintained.

It has law. The law is simple and physical. Water must move, but not too fast. Growth must fill every space, but not choke the channels. Heat must be enough to keep the machinery and the green mass active, but not enough to dry or burn the living fabric. Sound is constant, because water, engines, and living wood are never fully still. Light is scarce, so growth must be broad, dark, and patient. Every gap must be answered. Every excess must be drained. Every shortage must be supplied by seepage, condensation, or mechanical lift. The resident does not obey these rules as if they were written somewhere else. The resident is what happens when these rules have been followed long enough to become form.

It has time. Its time is not measured in days, or not only in days. It is measured in drip-rates. In wheel-turns. In reservoir levels. In the slow swelling of living wood around old supports. In the gradual cleaning of channels. In the darkening of metal under moss. In the way a lamp’s glow becomes more coppery as its housing ages. In the way a root advances a finger’s breadth over a stone lip and then holds that gain for years. The resident lives in durations that would feel immense to a more hurried life, but here they are simply the pace of being. Nothing is urgent because urgency would waste water, heat, and growth. Nothing is idle because every surface is already working.

It has speech. The speech is the sound of the place. Drip. Trickle. Lap. The soft slap of water against a root-bank. The creak of a wheel. The thud of a pump. The knock of a pipe adjusting to pressure. The hiss of a belt slipping. The tick of metal warming. The groan of a trunk settling. The rustle of leaves moving under their own weight. These sounds are not random. They are the resident’s continuous utterance. They change with the state of the system. When water is high, the sound becomes fuller, more hollow, more reflective. When water is low, the sound becomes thinner, more intermittent, more precise. When a machine works harder, the rhythm quickens. When a channel blocks, the murmur changes key. To listen there is not to hear silence, but to hear a body maintaining itself.

It has repair. This is one of its deepest qualities. Nothing in the place remains broken for long. A cracked stone is sealed by moss. A rusted edge is softened by growth. A fallen stem becomes a walkway or a dam. A broken pipe becomes a fountain, then a channel, then a margin of reeds. A lamp that fails is surrounded by deeper shadow until another lamp is shifted or another circuit finds its way through root and cable.
