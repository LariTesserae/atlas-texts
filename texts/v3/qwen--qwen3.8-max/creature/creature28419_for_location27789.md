There are people there, but not many. Fewer than the streets suggest, more than the silence would lead you to believe. They are the town’s keepers, though they would not use a word so grand if they could avoid it. They are the ones who walk the terraces before full light, who lift the covers from cistern mouths, who know by ear which gutter is beginning to whisper and which remains dry. If the place is a city without a crowd, they are the small living core that prevents it from becoming a monument.

They are human in the plainest sense. Nothing about them is luminous, prophetic, or uncanny. They have weathered faces and dry hands. Their skin is tightened by cold air and pale light. Their lips crack in the thin wind. Their eyes are used to looking across stone, to following a hairline channel, a bead of moisture, the faint darkening of a wall where fog has breathed on it. They move without hurry, because hurry wastes breath and breath is thin at that height. They have learned to walk so that their feet do not scrape, to set down jars without making them ring, to close wooden doors with a pressure of the hand rather than a shove.

Their clothes are made for coolness and for work. Wool, mostly, in colors that could have been taken from the basin itself: ash gray, buff, pale rust, the dark green-black of stone that stays damp in shadow. The cloth is close-woven and often old, repaired in careful squares. They wear hoods or close caps when the fog comes in, not for ornament but to keep moisture from their necks and shoulders. Their sandals are soft-soled, worn smooth in the same way as the public stairs, and they carry small tools at the belt: a wooden scraper, a bronze pick, a cord, a bit of felt, a clay cup. Nothing hangs loose. Nothing clangs.

They live in the residential terraces above the water plaza, in the courts set around shared cistern mouths. Each court has its own small household, though “household” may mean different things at different times. Some courts hold only two or three people. Others may hold a dozen, sleeping in dark lower rooms and using the upper rooms for storage, work, or quiet. There are rooms in the town that no one uses at all, and yet they are swept. Their floors are kept free of dust. Their shutters are checked. Their thresholds are worn not by present feet but by the memory of feet. The inhabitants do not regard these rooms as dead space. An unused room is still a room. A dry room is a success.

Their number is not fixed by law so much as by water. They know how much can be gathered, how much can be stored, how much can be spared. They do not speak of this often, because it is not a matter for argument. If a child is born, the town does not rejoice loudly; it adjusts quietly, as a basin adjusts to a new measure. If someone leaves down the dry valley and does not return, the town does not lament in public. The person’s tools are cleaned. Their place is kept for a time, then folded back into the ordinary work of the ward. The population remains thin because the place cannot bear weight without changing. The people have accepted this the way they accept fog, wind, and the slow filling of cisterns.

They are organized by care.

Each ward has its duties. Some people are roof-walkers, responsible for the catchment surfaces and the narrow gutters that edge them. They sweep dust from the slabs, clear grit from the channels, and check the overlapping tiles after wind. Some are gutter-keepers, who carry thin rods and small hooks to open blocked downspouts. Some are basin-keepers, who watch the public cisterns, measure the water with marked staves, and clean the mineral crust that forms at the lips. Some are stone-menders. They know where old replacement slabs have been stored, which blocks fit which openings, how to ease a cracked step out without damaging the ones beside it. They do not carve new patterns. They cut only to replace what is already known.

There are fog-readers among them, though again they would probably not use such a word. These are the ones who stand on the rim walk when cloud begins to enter the bowl and can tell by the way the air thickens which roofs will gather first, which terraces will darken, which channels need their covers opened. They do not predict in a grand manner. They notice. They say little. They place a hand against a wall and feel the first change in it.

Their day begins before ordinary light.

Before the basin fills with brightness, there is the sound of water in the dark: a drip here, a faint run there, the small metallic plink of a drop striking a stone lip. The keepers rise to it. They go out into the cold with lamps shaded so that the light does not scatter. They lift covers from jars. They check the overflow mouths. They listen at the cistern doors. A leak is not a nuisance to them; it is a fact that must be found. They follow sound along walls and under paving with a patience that looks almost like reverence, though they would not call it that. To them, a leak is simply a place where the town has begun to forget itself.

When fog has come in the night, the first task is collection. Every surface must be made to give what it has held. Roofs are brushed gently toward their gutters. Railings and parapets are wiped with broad soft strips of cloth. Condensation is coaxed from ledges into channels. Nothing is done violently. The town does not force water; it invites it. The people move from one catchment point to the next with the quiet attentiveness of nurses. They are not trying to make the town rich. They are trying to keep it balanced.

Water belongs first to the town, then to the people. This is not written anywhere in large letters, but it is understood in every hand that lifts a cup. The cisterns must reach their level before private jars are filled. The public basins must be served before washing, before cooking, before the moistening of cloths for faces and feet. When a person drinks, they drink with attention. There is no splashing. There is no idle pouring. Even children learn this early: to carry water is to carry the town’s breath.

They wash sparingly. They do not bathe in open pools or spend precious stores on comfort. They dampen cloths, wipe skin, dry themselves quickly against the cold. Their bodies smell of stone dust, wool, cold air, and the faint mineral sharpness of stored water. They are not unclean, but their cleanliness is exact rather than abundant. It has the character of maintenance.

Their food is simple and mostly brought from elsewhere. The bowl itself does not offer much that can be eaten without stealing water from the town. There are a few protected beds near the upper gate, where coarse sedge and one or two tough-leaved plants are allowed to grow because they mark a seep and do not drink too deeply. Outside the gate, on lower slopes that do not feed the catchment, there may be rough plots of dry-hard grain or pulse, enough to supplement but never to suffice. More comes from below: grain, oil, salt, dried fruit, lamp fuel, perhaps wool and leather when needed. It arrives infrequently, carried by people or animals sure-footed enough for the dry valley path. The town accepts these supplies without ceremony. It does not grow rich from them. It stores them in cool, dark rooms and uses them with the same economy it uses for water.

The keepers are not poor in the sense of lacking. They are poor in the sense of having no excess. Their storehouses are orderly. Their jars are marked.
