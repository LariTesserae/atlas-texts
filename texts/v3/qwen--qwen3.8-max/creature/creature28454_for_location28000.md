What lives there lives thinly, and with great patience. It does not arrive as a green rush, a swarm, a nesting, or a hunting. It appears first as stain, film, glaze, and thread: life so close to mineral that the eye can miss it and still be correct. The basin is not utterly dead, but life in it has been pressed down toward the condition of stone. It has learned to be quiet, cold, wet, and almost invisible.

The first inhabitants are the films.

They are not plants, not mosses, not lichens in any familiar sense. They are skins of living persistence stretched over the wet rock. Some are black and slick, shining like old varnish on the pale stone. Some are pale gray, almost powdery, visible only when the light strikes them from the side. Some are translucent, present mainly as a slight dulling of the stone’s glare. Others are white filaments, finer than breath-frost, clinging beneath stone lips or in the coldest corners where water hangs without falling.

These films are the nearest thing the basin has to meadows, but they do not spread like grass. They do not bloom. They do not root. They do not offer themselves to be cropped. They live, if that word still fits, on mineral gradients: on dissolved stone, on cold light, on the faint chemical differences between one wet surface and another. They may feed on what the water carries upward from deep rock. They may take something from the hard bright sky without turning green. They may simply persist in a state too slow to be called growth. They are the base of the basin’s spare existence, but they do not look alive. They look like weathering that has remembered itself.

In the pools live the flat ones.

They are the clearest animals the place contains, and even that clarity is a kind of concealment. They are low, translucent creatures, thin as peeled ice, shaped like small leaves or discs no wider than a fingernail. They have no obvious eyes, no limbs, no fins, no mouths that can be seen. Their upper surfaces are smooth and faintly glossy, their undersides textured with microscopic fringes that grip the wet stone. They do not swim. They crawl with extreme slowness, moving by almost imperceptible ripples across their bodies, grazing the mineral films one molecular layer at a time.

In bright light they are difficult to see. They are visible mainly as distortions: a slight bending of a quartz vein beneath them, a patch of water that seems marginally thicker than the rest, a moving absence against the stone. If one watches long enough, one may notice that a reflection has shifted without any wind having touched the pool. That may be one of them. If it stops, it becomes nearly impossible to find again.

They are cold to the core, and their bodies seem made of the same clear mineral water that surrounds them. They do not flee. They do not gather. They do not appear to recognize one another. Each moves through its pool as if it were a private thought. When one dies, if death is what happens, it does not float or rot. It thins, dissolves, and leaves behind a faint mineral smear that the next film will grow over. The basin absorbs it without disturbance.

On the damp shelves and seep margins live the rim-walkers.

They are smaller than the flat ones, and more mineral than animal. They resemble tiny mites or wingless droplets that have acquired shells. Their bodies are encrusted with pale deposits, as though each one has been slowly plated by the same water that feeds it. Some shine with a glassy skin of silica. Others are darkened by manganese-like stain. Their legs, if they are legs, are fine and few, more like crystalline supports than limbs. They stand motionless near threads of water, sometimes for so long that condensation gathers on their backs and makes them look like beads on the stone.

They do not graze in any visible way. They seem to sip the film of moisture that moves over the rock, or to draw something from the mineral crust they carry. They do not call. They do not swarm. They do not fight. A rim-walker may stand beside another for a long time without either acknowledging the other. They are not social. They are not solitary out of preference. They are simply sparse, each one an accidental-looking presence in a world too full of water and stone to support congregation.

Sometimes, where the stone overhangs a pool by a finger’s width, there are filaments.

These hang from the underside of wet lips: colorless threads, extremely fine, no longer than eyelashes. They may be fungal. They may be bacterial. They may be colonial bodies, or single organisms stretched into threads too thin to understand. They do not sway except when a droplet passes near them. They do not seem to reach for anything. They hang in the cold spray and become part of the basin’s delicate wet machinery, catching condensation, guiding it downward, joining the endless movement of water from surface to pool to seep to air.

They are among the oldest-looking things in the place. They suggest no ancestry, no kin, no future beyond their own continuation.

Beyond these sparse forms there are others that are harder to name, because they have almost ceased to look like creatures at all. They might be called the still ones, though even that gives them more separate identity than they seem to want.

They are low, broad, saturated bodies. Some are no larger than a hand. Some might be as large as a fallen torso, though none stands high. They resemble small terraces, wet shelves, or stones that have grown a faint internal order. Their surfaces are pale and slick, pocked with tiny basins, crossed by capillary channels. Films grow on them. Quartz-bright veins run through their outer layers. In places their skins are dark and glassy, like old lava polished by water. They are so mineralized that a person could stand beside one and never know it lived.

They do not move in any observable time. If they move at all, they move as stone moves: by minute adjustment, by slow redistribution of wetness, by imperceptible settling into a more perfect balance with the basin. Water enters them through countless fine pores, passes through them, and leaves them unchanged. They do not breathe as animals breathe. They do not eat as animals eat. They exchange. They endure. They are less like bodies and more like places that have become faintly aware of being places.

They have no faces. They have no eyes, no mouths, no hands, no limbs. Yet they are sensitive. If a droplet strikes near one, its surface shivers in a pattern too small to see. If a thread of water changes course by the width of a hair, the still one adjusts around it over a long interval. If the light strikes at a new angle, its films respond not with turning but with subtle alteration of sheen. It perceives the basin not through organs but through vibration, moisture, mineral gradient, and pressure.

The sound is part of them.

Because the basin is deafening, every living thing in it has been shaped around sound. None of the inhabitants has ears. None needs them. The roar enters their bodies as vibration and becomes a condition of existence. The films align themselves in microscopic patterns partly determined by resonance. The flat ones move along pathways where the water’s trembling is most stable. The rim-walkers choose shelves whose frequency suits their mineral shells. The still ones are perhaps the most saturated with sound: their internal channels turn the basin’s endless noise into a faint, distributed pressure, as if they were living instruments that do not play but merely hold the tone.
