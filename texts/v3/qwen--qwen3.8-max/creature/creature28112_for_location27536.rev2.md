What lives there is not a single species, nor even a society of species in any tidy sense. It is a saturation. The city is inhabited the way a reef is inhabited: by accumulation, by attachment, by countless bodies finding a crevice, a current, a warmth, a surface, and then staying long enough to change it. The residents are not merely present in the wet stone architecture. They are part of its continuing formation.

The first inhabitants are the least visible.

They are the films and crusts, the microbial felts and mineral skins that coat every damp surface. They are not green. They do not have leaves or roots or the soft abundance of plant life. They are dark, slick, powdery, leathery, or threadlike. They grow where water lingers: in the joints between bricks, along the lips of troughs, inside the shaded mouths of drains, on the warm backs of pipes, on the undersides of stairs, on the inner faces of cistern walls. They are blackish in the canals, pale where heat rises through cracks, rusty where iron weeps, chalky where mineral-rich water evaporates into crust.

These films are the city’s pasture.

They are also its slow skin. They seal small fissures. They hold moisture against stone. They make surfaces more edible, more slippery, more hospitable to other life. They are the base on which nearly everything else depends. They are grazed, scraped, browsed, filtered, cultivated indirectly, and recycled endlessly. Where they thicken, other animals gather. Where they vanish, the city feels sterile and unused.

The fungal life is almost as quiet, but more architectural. It does not stand up like vegetation. It works through seams and hollows. Pale filaments cross the gaps between stones. Leathery crusts form around warm vents. Dense felts grow inside abandoned junction boxes, in old insulation, behind loose panels, in the sheltered angles where two beams meet. Some fungi are soft and almost skin-like, swelling slightly with humidity. Others are brittle, layered, shell-like, built up over years into hard brown-white shelves. They break down old organic matter, trap mineral particles, and provide nesting material, food, and shelter for a hundred kinds of animals.

The city does not have soil in the ordinary sense. It has something else: a built-up residue of dung, molted shells, fungal threads, dead insects, bird droppings, salt crusts, rust flakes, and powdered stone. This residue collects in low corners, in pipe elbows, in the hollows of old machinery, in the gaps behind roosts. It becomes a living substrate. From it, the city’s food web rises in layers.

The first obvious animals are the scrapers.

They are the limpets, the grazers, the slow rasping mouths of the wet stone. Some resemble snails, but their shells are flatter, rougher, and often mineral-streaked, as if the city itself had grown a cap over their bodies. Others are more like armored slugs, low and broad, with mouths made of hard, comb-like plates. They move across slick walls and shallow floors, eating microbial film and leaving clean trails behind them. In places where their numbers are high, the stone shows their passage as a network of lighter tracks, as if the city had been combed by tiny hands.

These grazers are not beautiful in isolation. They are practical animals. Their shells are often pitted, furred with microscopic growths, or dulled by constant contact with wet mineral surfaces. They cluster in depressions where water beads. They seal themselves to stone when the air thins or the surface dries slightly. When the flow returns, they move again. Their slow feeding keeps the surfaces from becoming too thickly coated, and in doing so they maintain the city’s channels, steps, and walls in a state of usable openness.

Where the grazers are numerous, their predators are never far.

Small wall-crawlers follow them. These are quick, flattened creatures with many legs or with clawed pads adapted to wet stone. They hide in joints and dart out to catch young grazers, soft-bodied larvae, or injured insects. Their bodies are built for narrow spaces. They can enter a crack no wider than a finger and travel through the inner masonry of the city as if through a network of veins. Some have smooth, damp skins that shine faintly in the diffused light. Others are dull and granular, their bodies dusted with mineral powder. Their presence is often heard before it is seen: a faint ticking inside the wall, a scuffle behind a pipe, a sudden scattering of dust from an unseen hole.

The channels have their own citizens.

The water that runs through the city is not empty. It moves too slowly and warmly to be hostile to life. In the gutters and side channels live crustacean-like scavengers, low and armored, with broad carapaces and patient limbs. They are not crabs in any simple sense, but they have something of the crab’s sideways seriousness. They feed on microbial films, dead insects, shed shells, and whatever organic matter settles into the flow. Their bodies are often encrusted with mineral deposits, especially around the joints of their legs. Old individuals look less like animals than like moving stones.

These channel scavengers perform a constant labor. They break down waste. They stir sediment. They clear obstructions not by intention but by appetite. Where they are removed, the channels clog more quickly. Where they thrive, water continues to move with fewer interruptions. Their molted shells accumulate in basins and behind grates, becoming part of the city’s texture. In some places, the ground is made as much from discarded exoskeletons as from broken stone.

Larger water animals exist too, but they are not hurried. In the warmer runoff channels lie flattened amphibious creatures with smooth bodies and wide heads. They rest against the bottom or press themselves into shallow depressions where heat rises through the stone. They do not swim often unless disturbed. Instead they wait, flicking their throats, tasting the water, feeling for vibration. When insects fall onto the surface or small crawlers pass too near, they move with a sudden, fluid snap. Then they settle again.

Their skin is not dry. It is always moist, often faintly glossy, sometimes marked with pale mucus trails that help them slide over wet stone. They breed in warm pipe coils and protected overflow pools, laying egg masses that cling to metal or stone in jelly-like clusters. The young are small, quick, and numerous. Most are eaten. Enough survive to maintain the population. Their calls are soft, bubbling or clicking sounds, rarely loud, but common enough to become part of the background noise of lower districts.

In the deeper cisterns and flooded maintenance tunnels live longer, more elusive forms. They are eel-like or leech-like, pale or dark depending on the water, with reduced eyes and sensitive bodies. They navigate by pressure and touch. They move through submerged ducts where daylight never reaches, following warm currents and the faint chemical traces of food. Some hunt small crustaceans. Some feed on organic sediment. Some are scavengers, drawn to anything that sinks into the still water below the city.

These deep-water animals are rarely seen whole. A resident of the upper city might know them only by the occasional glimpse of a pale shape turning in a flooded shaft, or by the sound of something large and slow moving behind a grate. They are not monstrous. They are simply adapted to spaces no larger body could use. Their bodies have become narrow enough for the city’s hidden passages. They have become patient enough for its darkness.

The air above the channels is full of insects.

They are one of the city’s great visible populations. They rise where warm air leaks from vents, where heat from below meets cooler moisture-laden currents, where condensing screens create upward drafts and sheltered eddies. Many have broad wings coated in fine water-shedding structures. They do not fly with delicate grace. They fly with persistence. They move in columns and loose bands, sometimes so dense that they look like smoke released from the stone itself.

Some are small and dark, little more than living particles in the mist. Others are larger, with heavy bodies and broad wingbeats that make a soft humming sound. Their larvae live in wet walls, in fungal crusts, in accumulated waste, in shallow pools, in the warm spaces around pipes. The adults may live only long enough to rise, mate, and be eaten, but their emergence is constant enough that the air never feels empty. They are food for birds, for wall-crawlers, for amphibious ambushers, for other insects, and even for some of the larger filter-feeding animals that wait where water slows.

The insects give the city much of its motion. Without them, the stone would seem dead. With them, even the quietest alley has a faint tremor of life: a wingbeat against a grate, a sudden rise from a warm seam, a dry ticking of mandibles inside a hollow pipe.

The most visible animals, however, are the roosters.

They are the city’s crowds. They are the bodies on the rails, the shapes along the roof ridges, the masses shifting on high ledges when the mist thins. They are birdlike, but not necessarily birds in any familiar lowland sense. They are narrow-bodied, strong-footed, damp-adapted, and numerous beyond easy counting. Their feathers or feather-like coverings are sleek and water-shedding, not soft or ornamental. They do not sparkle. They do not display bright colors for beauty’s sake. Their tones are mostly dark, smoky, slate-blue, iron-gray, salt-stained white, or dull copper where mineral dust has settled on them.

They are loud.

Their calls are not melodic in a gentle sense. They are practical sounds: contact calls, alarm calls, roost disputes, courtship rasps, feeding cries, the constant negotiation of crowded life. In some districts the noise rises and falls with the light and the movement of fog. At certain hours, when the air shifts and the roosts awaken together, the city seems to speak with one harsh, layered voice. Then it settles again into a lower murmur, broken by individual cries.

These roosting animals have made the city into a nesting ground. Every sheltered ledge, every protected beam, every dryish corner beneath an arch has been used. Their nests are not fragile things. They are built from whatever the city provides: fungal fibers, chewed crust, mud, mineral sediment, shed insect wings, scraps of old insulation, cementing saliva, and accumulated droppings. Over generations, these nests harden. They become crusts. They become shelves. They become part of the architecture.

In some places, the roosts are so old and so thick that they have changed the shape of the buildings. A ledge becomes a ramp. A window becomes a tunnel. A roofline becomes a ridge of cemented nesting material. The animals do not intend this. They simply return to the same places, generation after generation, adding what their bodies and habits add. The city accepts the addition. Stone and nest merge until it is hard to say where building ends and animal begins.

Their dung is not waste only. It is fertility. It feeds fungal growths, insect larvae, and microbial mats. Below large roosts, the walls are often streaked pale and dark with accumulated droppings, and the surfaces there are especially alive. Beetles and larvae work the deposits. Scavengers gather where droppings fall. Channels below roosts sometimes carry nutrient-rich water into treatment pools, where microbial films bloom. In this way, the animals above feed the city below.

The roosters are not the only animals of the upper levels.

The condensing districts attract quick fliers and predators that use the open air around the mesh screens. Some creatures wait where insects must pass. They perch on louvers, cables, and the edges of fog-catching frames. They are patient, still, and sharply attentive. When prey passes, they strike with a speed that seems out of place in the otherwise slow city. Others circle in wide arcs, riding warm drafts from the vents below. They do not soar for joy. They travel between feeding zones, roosts, and nesting sites with the efficiency of animals that have learned the city’s currents.

Predators in the upper city are often smaller than one expects. They do not need to be large. The city provides bottlenecks, perches, shadows, and predictable routes. A predator that knows where prey must pass can survive without great size or speed. Some have long limbs for reaching into crevices. Some have flattened bodies for sliding behind panels. Some have strong claws for gripping wet metal. Their eyes are often large, adapted to the softened light and the shaded interiors. Their calls are fewer and lower than those of their prey.

The middle levels are more crowded still.

Here, where workshops, storage vaults, and habitation blocks press together, life fills the volume in layers. The upper surfaces belong to roosters and fliers. The walls belong to climbers. The floors and drains belong to crawlers. The interiors of walls belong to burrowers. Old ducts become nurseries. Abandoned machinery becomes colony space. Hollow floors become tunnels. Wherever there is a gap, something enters it. Wherever there is warmth, something settles near it.

Many of the animals here have become specialized for the city’s textures. Some have claws shaped for gripping salt-streaked brick. Some have pads that adhere to polished stone. Some have bodies flexible enough to enter ceramic pipes and turn inside them. Some have shells or plates that protect them from rough surfaces and falling debris. Many are nocturnal in the sense that they prefer darker spaces, but the city has no true darkness. There is always some warmth, some sound, some movement, some faint reflection from moisture in the air.

The city’s noise is made partly by them.

A claw taps inside a pipe. A wing brushes a grate. A body slides across a damp ceiling. A nest shifts under the weight of its occupants. A feeding group moves through a wall, making a soft grinding sound. A roost exhales in unison. Somewhere, a metal plate has been loosened by generations of passage and now clicks whenever an animal crosses it. These sounds are not accidental. They are the city’s voice. They mark the fact that the place is not merely standing. It is being used.

Below the middle levels, near the basins and saturated ground, the animals become larger and slower.

The lowest districts are warm, thick-aired, and rich in microbial growth. Wide flats of wet stone support herds of grazing animals. Some are small and numerous, like the wall-scrapers, but here they grow broader and more deliberate. Others are heavier, armored, and almost reptilian in their patience. They move across the slick surfaces with no urgency, cropping films, swallowing sediment, filtering shallow water through specialized mouths.

The largest of them are not fast. They do not need to be. In the crowded basins, surprise is difficult, and escape is often unnecessary. Their size, their armor, and the sheer density of life around them protect them. Some have broad backs that collect fungal crusts and small hitchhiking organisms. Some have heavy limbs with wide feet that distribute their weight over soft sediment. Their eyes are small and calm. Their breathing is slow. They seem to belong more to the architecture than to any idea of wild animal life.

They are moving reefs.

On their backs and along their flanks live smaller creatures: mites, grazers, cleaning insects, fungal growths, sometimes young roosters that have learned to pick food from their skins. When one of these large animals moves, it carries a small ecology with it. When it rests, other animals gather around it. When it dies, its body becomes a site of intense recycling. The city does not waste such deaths. They are absorbed into the system with the same slow thoroughness as everything else.

The basins also have waders and swimmers. Some move through warm shallow water with long limbs, stirring sediment to expose hidden prey. Others float just beneath the surface, their bodies flat and dark, almost indistinguishable from stone until they shift. They feed on insect larvae, small crustaceans, and organic particles. Their movements create soft eddies in the water. They are not graceful in a delicate sense, but they are perfectly suited to the place. Their bodies know the temperature, the mineral taste, the slow circulation of the city’s water.

Predation exists, but it is not theatrical.

It is woven into the ordinary. A wall-crawler takes a young grazer. A rooster snatches an insect from the air. A channel scavenger pulls dead matter apart. A cistern eel takes something that swims too close. A predator waits at a fog screen and strikes when prey passes. Nothing here is innocent, and nothing is cruel in any human sense. The violence is steady, distributed, and necessary. It does not interrupt the city’s calm. It is part of its calm.

Parasites and decomposers are everywhere, as they must be. They live in nests, on skins, in shells, in dung, in old roosts, in fungal beds, in the joints of machines. Some are tiny and almost invisible. Some are worm-like, some mite-like, some larval. They keep populations in check. They break down bodies. They turn waste into forms other life can use. Without them, the city would choke on its own accumulation. With them, every death and dropping becomes material for another cycle.

The machines are not outside this living system.

They have been drawn into it.

Where water flows, animals gather. Where warmth rises, animals settle. Where vibration is constant, some creatures learn to use it. The city’s industry has become part of the habitat, and its fauna has become part of the industry’s continuation. This is not because the animals understand the machines. It is because their habits align with the needs of the water system.

Crustacean-like scavengers clean films from intake grates because they feed there. Their constant scraping keeps channels open. Certain larvae tunnel through accumulated sediment, loosening deposits that would otherwise harden. Heat-loving insects gather around boilers and exchangers, their bodies and nests adding insulation in some places, blocking small gaps in others. Roosters scrape perches clean and in doing so remove mineral buildup from edges. Some animals prefer the warmth of active machinery and therefore move parts by their weight or passage, not often, but enough to matter.

Other animals damage the machines, of course. Nests block vents. Shells jam gates. Burrows weaken brick. Dung corrodes metal. But the system has settled into balance. Enough is cleaned by use, by water, by other animals, and by the slow wear of the city itself. The machines do not operate with crisp efficiency. They operate with tolerance. They allow for life. They have been adjusted by life.

This is why the city feels inhabited rather than abandoned.

It is not that the machines still run as they once did. It is that they run as part of something else now. Their purpose has widened. They no longer serve only their original designers, if they ever did. They serve the water. They serve the warmth. They serve the animals that depend on both. In return, the animals maintain enough of them to keep the cycles moving.

Whether people live there depends on what one means by people.

If one means the old kind of people, the kind that built the city for their own convenience and saw stone as something to be mastered, then no. Those people are not present. Or if they are, they are gone as a governing fact. The city no longer bends around human comfort. It bends around water, heat, fungal growth, and animal passage.

But if one means whoever has adapted to the place and learned to live inside its logic, then perhaps yes.

There may be people still, or something descended from people. They are not numerous. They do not fill the city as the roosters do. They move through it in small groups, or alone, wearing oiled coverings and soft-soled boots that grip wet stone. Their bodies are adapted to altitude and damp: broad chests, efficient lungs, warm hands, careful balance. They are not afraid of the animals. They do not try to clear them away. They negotiate with them.

They live in the drier upper galleries and sheltered middle terraces, where heat can be kept and water guided without flooding their sleeping spaces. They cultivate fungal beds in dark chambers, not by planting green things, but by managing moisture, mineral dust, and organic waste. They harvest edible crusts, insect larvae, crustacean molts, eggs, and salt-rich deposits. They dry fungal sheets. They press nutrient cakes. They filter water through stone and charcoal and ceramic. They repair critical pipes and valves because their own survival depends on the city’s circulation.

They are not farmers in the lowland sense. They do not make fields. They do not clear growth. They guide. They encourage. They redirect. They know which channels must be kept open, which roosts must be left alone, which fungal crusts can be harvested and which must be allowed to thicken. Their knowledge is practical and exact. It is passed through demonstration, chant, gesture, and the worn paths of repeated use.

Their tools are few and durable. Copper, ceramic, polished stone, treated fungal leather, salvaged iron. They do not decorate much. Their objects are shaped by wet hands and frequent repair. Their homes have rounded thresholds, drainage grooves, raised floors, and vents that can be closed against thick mist. Their doors are often low and oval, enlarged by use over time. Their interiors are warm, dim, and filled with the smell of stone, fungus, and animal oil.

They speak softly or in clicks and whistles, because the city is already loud. Sharp cries are wasted. Long calls carry poorly in mist. Their language has developed rhythm and pitch suited to water noise. They can imitate pump sounds, roost calls, and the warning cries of certain animals. Children learn to listen before they learn to speak much. They learn the meaning of a changing drip, the pause of a fan, the difference between ordinary water sounds and the sound of a blocked channel beginning to fail.

If they have religion, it is not about sky or soil. It is about flow. They know that heat must move, water must move, waste must move, bodies must move. Stillness is dangerous. Blockage is danger. Their rituals may involve cleaning a channel, opening a valve, marking a roost as protected, or laying a new stone where water has begun to wear a path too deeply. Their sacred acts are maintenance. Their ancestors, if they remember them, are not conquerors but caretakers. They may not call themselves owners of the city. They may call themselves those who keep the water moving.

Yet even if such people exist, they are not the city’s dominant fact.

The dominant fact is the animal density. The city belongs to the roosts, the channels, the fungal crusts, the grazing trails, the insect columns, the slow basin herds, the wall-burrowers, the cistern shapes, the countless small bodies moving through the mineral architecture. People, if present, are one more adapted population within that larger body. They do not stand outside it. They are not spectators. They are part of the same wet metabolism.

In the end, the true inhabitant of the city is not a single creature at all.

It is the ecology itself.

The city lives as a combined organism. Water is its circulation. Heat is its breath. Stone is its skeleton. Machines are its old organs, still working in altered service. Fungi and microbial films are its feeding surfaces. Animals are its moving tissue. Their dung, nests, shells, bones, wings, and bodies are the city’s continuing construction. Every resident adds something. Every resident wears something down. Every resident leaves a trace that becomes part of the next surface, the next ledge, the next channel, the next nest.

This is why the place feels so full without being frantic. It has no need for suddenness. It has passed beyond emergency. It has become established. The populations rise and fall in slow cycles. The roosts thicken by generations. The channels narrow by deposits and are widened by use. The machines turn at their patient rates. The mist gathers and releases. The water falls, pools, flows, evaporates, condenses, and returns.

Whoever lives there lives inside that return.

They are the wet-bodied, the mineral-fed, the heat-seeking, the patient climbers and slow swimmers, the scrapers and the filterers, the roosters and the burrowers, the small quick fliers and the large quiet grazers. They are the fungal crusts and microbial films no less than the animals that eat them. They are the sounds in the pipes, the shadows in the ducts, the polished stairs, the worn thresholds, the nests hardened into architecture. They are the city’s living surface.

And if you stand still among them, you will understand after a while that you are not looking at a city that has been taken over by animals.

You are looking at a city that has become animal.

Not ruined. Not reclaimed. Not waiting.

Inhabited.
