The basin is lived in first by water, then by sound, and only after that by anything with a name.

At first you would say the true inhabitants are the small ones: midges rising in thin columns from the pools, beetles moving like dark seeds through the moss, frogs calling from the wet margins without showing themselves. Dippers haunt the channels, dipping and rising as if the streams were hinges. Pipits and wagtails flick from stone to stone. Ravens cross the rim with dry, knowing wingbeats. Ibex appear like weather on the higher lip, stand for a moment in the wind, and are gone. Hares flatten into the grass. Stoats move through the seams of rock with terrible, liquid quickness. All of these live there, or pass through, or return by season.

But if you stay long enough, the scattered lives begin to seem arranged around something else.

Not a large presence. Nothing that dominates. The basin would not allow that. What lives there is modest, patient, and so fitted to the place that the eye slides over it at first. It might be mistaken for a boulder crouched beside the pool, or for a shadow the cliff has let fall too far into the grass, or for an old person bent against the wind. Only when it moves — and even then it moves little — do you understand that the hollow has a keeper.

Call it the Listener, if you must call it anything.

The Listener is not tall. It is low and compact, shaped more by endurance than by growth, as if the basin had pressed it into being the way water presses moss into the cracks of stone. Its back is rounded, its shoulders drawn in, its posture that of someone who has spent a long time listening to weather. It does not look strong in the way valley people mean strength; it looks settled. It looks like something that could remain in one place through rain and frost and thin air without needing to become dramatic about it.

Its skin is weathered and cool, browned in places by wind, greenish in others where damp has touched it long enough to leave a stain. The face is broad and quiet, with a low brow, a blunt nose, and lips cracked by cold. The eyes are pale and thin, the color of water moving over dark rock. They are not fierce eyes. They are attentive eyes. They do not so much look at things as wait with them.

Its hair is dark, wetted down, lying in coarse strands like sedge after rain. Sometimes it is tucked under a hood made of woven grass and old hide; sometimes the hood is down and the hair seems less like hair than a growth of the place itself, something the basin has allowed to sprout from the figure’s head as it allows liverwort to sprout from shaded stone. Its hands are broad, the knuckles thick, the fingers pale at the tips from constant wetness. They are careful hands. They know how to lift a stone without disturbing the moss beneath it. They know how to place a branch across a current so that the water accepts it rather than fights it.

The Listener wears layers of hide and woven plant fiber, dark with age and moisture, patched here and there with bits of sedge, lichen, or cured skin. The clothing does not rustle loudly. It has been worn until it has become soft and damp and almost silent. A cord of twisted sinew or hair is tied at the waist. From it hang a few small things: a pouch of leather, a flat stone worn smooth by handling, a bone hook, perhaps a flake of sharp edge kept wrapped against dullness. Nothing ornamental. Nothing extra. Every object has been carried long enough to lose the look of a possession and gain the look of a part of the body.

It lives in the half-sunk bothy on the moraine, though “lives” may be too strong a word. It shelters there. It rests there. It disappears into it when the mist thickens and the basin becomes a gray room with no walls. The bothy is low, built from stones already dark with wet, roofed with sod and hide so that rain beads on it and runs off in thin threads. Inside there is barely room to turn. The floor has been laid with flat stones and covered with dry moss where the water has been coaxed away by shallow hand-cut channels. A small hearth of stacked rocks holds the memory of fire, not much fire, because fire here is difficult and hungry and too visible. Sometimes there is only a little peat glow, enough to warm the hands, dry a cord, or harden the tip of a wooden spade. The smell inside is old and clean: wet wool, cold stone, smoke long gone, and the green black scent of peat.

The Listener does not build much. It repairs. It adjusts. It maintains the small forces that keep the basin balanced.

The cairns across the wetter ground are its work, or at least its care. It does not raise them proudly. It adds a stone when frost has shifted the path, or when a pool has spread over a firm seam, or when the moss has grown so deep that the old way is no longer safe. Each cairn is small, capped with moss, leaning slightly as if the weather has already begun to claim it back. The Listener seems to understand that the basin permits only brief marks. Anything too tall, too solid, too permanent would be an insult to the place. So it builds low. It builds as if it expects the mountain to take the work back, and it does not resent the mountain when it does.

The weir by the deeper pool is also its doing, or its tending. Woven branches weighted with smooth stones cross the water in a curve that is not quite straight, because straightness would oppose the current too bluntly. The Listener has learned to work by leaning with water rather than resisting it. The weir does not trap everything. It guides. It suggests. It lets the pool keep its breathing. When seasonal flow bends it, the Listener repairs it in a slightly different shape, following what the water has taught it. There is no attempt here to master the basin. There is only negotiation.

Its days are shaped by wetness.

When cloud-water drifts over the rim and the basin fills with mist, the Listener sits very still near the central pool, listening. It can hear the difference between rain that is passing through and rain that has come to stay. It can hear, or seems to, the slow change in the pool’s level: a fuller note when the water rises, a thinner one when it subsides. Sometimes the pool rises more quickly than the weather appears to justify, and when that happens the Listener tilts its head as if the mountain has spoken underneath the ordinary sound of dripping. It does not look afraid. It looks confirmed.

When the mist thins and the basin glistens, it moves. It goes from stone to stone along the drier seams, never hurrying, never choosing the soft moss when a firm path can be found. It carries a short staff of hardened wood, not for walking so much as for testing and tapping. The tap of the staff against stone becomes another sound in the basin, one that returns from the cliffs in delayed fragments. To someone standing on the rim, those taps might seem to come from several places at once. That is how the Listener travels: not silently, because nothing here can be silent, but in a way that blends into the basin’s own loudness.

It eats sparingly. The basin gives, but not in heaps. It gathers insects when they rise thick over the pools. It takes frogs or tadpoles only when they are plentiful, and then with a kind of practical apology. If small fish come into the channels, it may guide a few toward the woven weir, but it never takes more than the day needs. It eats the tender tips of sedge, the softer parts of dwarf willow when they are young, perhaps eggs found by accident, perhaps roots washed clean by flood. Nothing about its feeding is lavish. It eats as the basin lives: in pulses, in small allowances, in seasonal gifts that arrive and withdraw.

It is not lonely. That is one of the first things you understand if you watch it long enough. Loneliness would require an absence, and the basin is too full of small presences for that. The Listener is surrounded by frogs it cannot always see, birds it hears before it sees, insects that appear in loose columns and vanish, wind moving through the sedges with broad strokes. Even silence here is not empty. It is only a pause in the water’s speaking.

If anything, the Listener seems most at home when the basin is loudest: after rain, when every shallow channel is alive, when waterfalls hiss in their grooves, when drips fall from the overhangs in irregular applause, when the moss darkens with drinking and the air tastes cold and newly made.
