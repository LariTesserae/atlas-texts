Whoever lives there is not a “who” in any familiar sense. If you look for faces, hands, voices, or the marks of deliberate making, the basin will refuse you gently and completely. There are no builders there. No one has chipped stone into a blade, tied a knot, laid a foundation, or shaped a tool. There are no roads, no hearths, no walls, no vessels, no signals carved into the world for later reading. The place is inhabited, but not owned. It is filled with life, yet almost none of it is mobile in the way a traveler expects life to be mobile. What lives there lives by holding, absorbing, widening, filtering, lifting, settling, and waiting.

The first and most numerous inhabitants are not single creatures at all, but living surfaces. They are the skin of the new stone: thin, patient crusts that seal the basalt, bind the ash, stabilize the pumice, and make the raw land capable of being lived upon. They spread in dark olive, rust, jade, and coppery films, sometimes glossy, sometimes granular, sometimes so fine they seem like a stain rather than an organism. In some places they feel almost dry, though they are never out of water; in others they have a soft, sealed sheen, as if the rock itself had been varnished by time and patience.

These crusts are the basin’s oldest citizens, even though the basin is young. They do not move as animals move. They advance by accretion, by chemical adherence, by the slow overgrowth of one microscopic layer upon another. They taste the rock more than they see it. They respond to mineral seepage, to warmth rising from fractures, to the faintest differences in light and current. Where rain sinks down in cooler columns, they may brighten almost imperceptibly for a time. Where a new crack opens in the stone, they enter it with no haste and no violence, filling the fissure with living material until the crack becomes part of the habitat rather than a wound.

They are not beautiful in the way a flower is beautiful. Their beauty is more like the beauty of a surface that has become fit for life. Without them, the young rock would remain too raw, too loose, too unstable for the thicker growths above. They hold grains together. They quiet the movement of fine sediment. They make it possible for a blade, a strand, or a holdfast to remain in one place long enough to grow. If there is a “people” of the basin, these crusts are the common folk: innumerable, anonymous, indispensable.

Above them live the middle growths, and here the world becomes stranger. They are not quite plants as lowland worlds know plants, though they have plant-like habits. They root, spread, lift, and turn toward light, but they live entirely within warm water and have adapted to it so thoroughly that many of their bodies have become clear. Some are tall, narrow blades with dark outer sheaths and translucent centers. Light enters them and is guided downward, revealing green movement inside their tissues. In bright sun, they can look like slivers of living glass filled with slow circulation. In shadow, they gather what light remains and seem to hold it rather than reflect it.

Others grow as vertical membranes: broad, flexible sheets arranged in tiers, each tier catching a different quality of illumination. These membranes ripple only slightly when touched by current. They are not limp. They have enough internal tension to keep their surfaces extended, yet enough suppleness to avoid tearing when water shifts. Their edges may be scalloped, lobed, or finely divided, and along those edges tiny bubbles sometimes form at midday when photosynthetic activity is highest. The bubbles cling for a time, brightening the margins like beads, then release and rise in slow chains.

Still others grow as hollow tubes, open at the tips. Water enters these tubes through minute pressure differences and passes through their interiors in sluggish internal currents. The tubes are not passive pipes; they filter, absorb, and exchange. Their inner surfaces are often lined with finer living layers that take passing particles from the water without grasping, snatching, or pursuing. Some of these tube-growths are short and clustered; others stand singly, taller than a person would be if a person could stand there, their openings tilted toward the light like pale horns listening to silence.

The jointed strand-forms are among the most conspicuous of the middle waters. They rise from cracks and ledges as beaded cords, each segment swollen slightly with gas or fluid. Some are green, some gray-green, some nearly colorless except for a dark thread of living tissue running through their centers. Their gas-filled joints give them buoyancy, so they do not merely hang; they lean upward in suspended festoons. In dense thickets, whole clusters of these strands hold themselves just below the brighter water, forming loose curtains that sway with the basin’s slow internal breathing.

These growths do not compete as animals would compete. They do not wrestle for space. Instead, they negotiate by occupation and timing. One form may thrive in stronger light; another may do better in the cooler water near a deep trough. One may anchor in narrow basalt joints; another may spread across sealed ash beds. If one grows over another, it happens so gradually that the event has no drama. It is less like conflict and more like weather: a long change in condition, a shift in dominance, a new layer added to an old one.

The canopy above is its own community. It is not a roof but a broken, moving ceiling of broad floating forms and near-floating membranes. Some canopy dwellers rest just beneath the surface with wide upper surfaces exposed to the fullest light. Their undersides trail fine root-like strands into the water below, not to anchor in stone but to absorb dissolved minerals and catch drifting particles. Bubbles collect beneath them, forming temporary silver pools that cling and shimmer until they slip away or are reabsorbed. These pools are not homes in the sense of shelters; they are transient conditions, little accumulations of gas and light that make the canopy brighter and more complex than it would otherwise be.

Life near the surface is warmer, brighter, and slightly more changeable than life below. The highest ridges, where wave-dimples disturb the waterline, support tough, low mats that can endure both full sunlight and the occasional touch of air. These mats are darker, denser, and less translucent than the mid-water growths. They hold themselves close to the rock, their tissues thickened against variation. They are among the most stable inhabitants of the basin, continuing without interruption even when weather above changes and rain stands around the sea like a curtain.

The rain matters to them. When fresh water sinks in slow columns from the surface, many growths respond by leaning into those columns with minute, patient movements. This is not desire in the animal sense; it is a tropism refined by conditions. The rain brings slight changes in temperature and mineral content. It stirs the upper layers. It carries dissolved substances from the thin bright sky above. The basin’s life does not chase the rain, but it does receive it. The whole upper world becomes briefly more attentive when fresh water descends through the warm green.

Among all these rooted and attached lives, animals are rare. The first one notices their scarcity rather than their presence. For long periods, nothing visible moves except swaying tissue, shifting light, and rising bubbles. Then, if one looks closely, the small animal lives reveal themselves: pale specks along the crusts, almost invisible grazers cleaning films from stone, translucent coils living within hollow stems, and tiny forms inhabiting the pores of pumice. They are not numerous enough to form swarms or crowds. Each seems separated from the others by long stretches of uninhabited or fully occupied surface.

The microscopic grazers move so slowly that they seem more like wandering stains than creatures. They have no urgency, no visible eyes, no quick reflexes. They feed by removing excess film from living surfaces, though they do not leave polished stone behind. Their grazing is subtle, a rebalancing rather than a clearing. They may spend what passes for their whole lives within a few handspans of water, never crossing a ridge, never encountering anything larger than a drifting spore cluster.

The coil-dwellers live inside the tube-growths and hollow strands. Their bodies are long, thin, and often coiled in loose spirals adapted to narrow interiors. They move by slow contractions, tasting the water that passes through their homes. Some feed on fine particles carried by internal currents; others live in close association with the tube itself, removing waste or dead tissue and receiving nourishment in return. They are not parasites in any aggressive sense. Their existence is more like a quiet tenancy.

Pumice harbors its own small lives. Because the porous stone is pale, light, and loosely held in places, the organisms living in it tend to be soft-bodied and cautious. They extend into the pores, retreat when currents shift, and contribute to the stabilization of the banks. Some are extensions of larger growths, sending filaments into the pumice as roots might. Others are independent but minute, living entirely within the stone’s hollows. They help keep the pumice from drifting too freely, binding it into place with biological patience.

Larger animals are uncommon enough to become events when seen. The ribbon drifters are perhaps the most familiar of these rare moving forms, though “familiar” is too strong a word for beings encountered only occasionally. They are long, flat, translucent animals that move through the middle water with a slow undulation. They have no fins in the ordinary sense, no obvious mouth, no eyes that read the world as shape and danger. Their bodies are mostly clear tissue, sometimes with faint green or silver traces inside, and their edges lift and fall as if they were thinking through movement rather than commanding it.

A ribbon drifter does not hunt. It seems to feed by absorption and by filtering fine organic particles through its broad surface. It may follow a spore cloud for hours without increasing its speed. It may pass through a shaft of sunlight and become almost invisible, except for the slight distortion where its body bends the light. It has no apparent destination. Its movement is not aimless, exactly, but it is not goal-bound either. It follows chemical gradients, temperature differences, and slow internal currents with a patience that makes animal hunger seem remote.

When a ribbon drifter passes over a thicket, the growths below do not flee. They may sway slightly as the water is displaced, but there is no alarm. The drifter leaves no trace. It does not disturb the crusts or tear the blades. It is simply a moving part of the basin, a rare mobile volume in a mostly still world. One could watch one for a long time and never learn whether it is aware of anything beyond immediate chemical and physical conditions. Perhaps that is not the right question. In the basin, awareness does not seem to require anxiety.

The broad veil-forms are rarer still, and they are the closest thing the basin has to a presence one might call “whoever,” though even that word strains under their nature. They are not single animals so much as colonial sheets, broad translucent beings that drift above the canopy once in a long while. They move by minute contractions of their edges, lifting and falling like a very slow breath. Their bodies are thin enough to seem made of moving water, yet they hold coherence. They cast soft shadows over the plants below, and those shadows pass across fronds and bladders like clouds across a field.

A veil-form may be wide enough to cover a small ridge or long enough to trail over several terraces. It does not descend heavily. It rides the upper water with a kind of suspended neutrality. Its underside may bear fine filaments or absorbent surfaces that gather spores, drifting cells, and dissolved nutrients. It does not grasp. It does not chew. It does not pursue. It simply moves through abundance and takes what is available with almost no effort.

The strangeness of a veil-form lies in its suggestion of perception without personhood. It responds to shadows, to dense spore clouds, to changes in temperature and current. Its edges may alter their rhythm when it encounters a rich patch of drifting organic matter. Its tissues may brighten or dim subtly with light. Yet there is no face, no central organ, no visible seat of self. If it experiences anything, it experiences the basin as a distributed field of conditions: warmth here, density there, chemical difference, pressure change, light falling through clear membranes above.

When one appears, the basin does not change its character. The plants continue lifting toward light. The bubbles continue rising. The rain, if it is raining, continues entering as slow columns. The veil-form simply becomes an event within that continuation. It passes, casts its shadow, feeds without violence, and leaves. Its rarity gives it importance, but not dominance. It is not a ruler of the place. It is more like a rare thought the basin has once in a great while, then releases.

Below the middle slopes and canopies, in the deeper hollows, life becomes quieter and more mineral. The light there is blue-green or simply dim, depending on depth and the density of growth above. The rock is cooler, and the water carries more dissolved substances. In these places, growths are slower, darker, and often heavily encrusted. They do not reach upward with the same brightness as shallow forms. They spread laterally, settle into joints, and incorporate mineral films into their tissues until they seem halfway between organism and stone.

In the deepest cracks, where the sun may never reach, pale filaments live from the basin’s geological warmth. They are among the least visible inhabitants and perhaps among the most important. They take energy from mineral gradients, from faint thermal differences, from the chemical life of the newly lifted stone. They do not make landscapes. They make possibility. They keep the deepest parts of the basin from being sterile. They are the hidden thread connecting the living water above to the still-forming earth below.

The basin’s inhabitants reproduce as the basin itself behaves: by release, drift, fragmentation, and patient accumulation. Spore clouds are among the most visible episodes of this reproductive life. At certain times, often after prolonged warmth or following rain, the water becomes faintly golden with drifting grains. These spores move through the basin in slow clouds, caught by weak currents, lodged under canopy leaves, drawn into tube-openings, or settling onto prepared crusts where they may begin new growth. Some are eaten, but many simply continue until conditions allow them to attach.

Fragmentation is equally common. A beaded strand may break, and each joint may carry enough life to root elsewhere. A membrane may tear in a rare stronger current, and the fragments may settle into new fissures. A portion of canopy may detach, drift for a time, and then become weighted by accumulated growth until it sinks to a new place. Nothing in this process feels like loss. It is the basin’s way of spreading without moving deliberately, of multiplying without making.

Gas bladders play a quiet role in this reproductive life. Some bladders are temporary; they fill, lift, and later collapse. Others are tied to dispersal. Certain seed-like pods rise with trapped gas until they reach the canopy, where they may float for days, accumulating spores or mineral films before finally sinking. When they descend, they carry with them a small cargo of upper-water life into deeper regions. In this way, the basin’s layers are not sealed from one another. They exchange slowly, by buoyant ascent and weighted return.

The sounds these lives make are few and mostly accidental. There are no calls, no songs, no territorial cries. The nearest things to voices are physical events: the dull click of a gas bladder releasing, the faint rub of broad fronds touching, the soft whisper of water moving through hollow tissues. Sometimes cooling stone gives a remote tick or groan, and the sound travels through the water as if the basin were settling in sleep. The inhabitants do not answer these sounds. They do not need to. Their communication, if it deserves that word, is chemical, tactile, and structural: a change in tension, a released compound, a shift in growth direction, a signal written in the language of condition rather than command.

There is little hunger visible in the basin. Animal hunger exists, but it is rare and muted. Most energy remains in place: in tough tissues, mineral-binding mats, gas-filled structures, and slowly turning over canopies. What is shed is reused. Fallen fragments do not vanish quickly. They sink into lower growths, become sites for new attachment, or are broken down by films and tiny grazers until their substance returns to the living layers. The system is not wasteful, but its thrift is not born of scarcity. It is born of youth and slow circulation. The basin has not yet learned to support great wandering populations. It supports itself by staying where it is.

This gives the place its peculiar peace. There is no visible panic. No creature seems to live in constant fear of being overtaken. There is no open chase, no sudden alarm, no flurry of escape. Even the rare moving beings move as if they have no need to prove their motion. The absence of predation as spectacle makes the basin feel older than it is, though its stone is new. It has the emotional quality of a place that has not yet developed sharp animal antagonisms, or perhaps one whose conditions favor other solutions.

The inhabitants solve problems by growth and adjustment. If a surface is unstable, crusts bind it. If light is limited, tissues become clearer and lift themselves toward brightness. If a crack opens, roots and filaments enter it. If rain brings fresh minerals, growths lean into the descending columns. If a spore cloud arrives, membranes catch it. If a fragment detaches, it may root elsewhere. These are not choices made in the way a maker would choose, but they are effective. They are answers shaped by long repetition and immediate condition.

One could say the basin is lived by three broad modes of being. The first is adherence: life that holds itself to stone, seals it, covers it, and makes it stable. The second is translucence: life that admits light into itself, uses clarity rather than opacity, and turns illumination into structure. The third is drift: life that moves without grasping, passing through the basin as a rare animal or reproductive cloud, leaving nothing made behind.

If a single inhabitant had to stand for the whole place, it would not be the veil-form, though the veil-form is the most arresting. It would be something humbler: a jointed strand rising from a fresh basalt crack, its gas-filled beads lifting it toward the light, its clear tissue full of green motion, its lower holdfast sealed into stone by living crust. That strand is not an individual in a strict sense. It is a relationship made visible: stone and skin, warmth and gas, water and light, ascent and anchor. It lives by being held and by holding. It rises because it is buoyed, not because it strives. It remains because the basin allows it to remain.

And if you ask who lives there, the truest answer may be that the basin is inhabited by patience itself, given form. Not patience as a virtue, but patience as a condition of existence. The lives there do not wait for anything in the human sense. They do not anticipate. They continue. They thicken the water with living layers. They turn young stone into a dwelling without making it into a thing. They receive rain, handle light, release spores, and rise by gas. They are sparse where animals are expected to be sparse and dense where growth can be dense. They are quiet where noise is expected and luminous where darkness might have been.

No one speaks there. No one builds. No one leaves artifacts. But the basin is not empty. It is full of rooted radiance, slow drifting flesh, and surfaces becoming worlds. What lives there lives as the place itself seems to live: newly, quietly, and with the deep steadiness of something still learning how to remain.
