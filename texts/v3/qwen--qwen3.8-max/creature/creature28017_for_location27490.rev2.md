What lives there is not one resident but a graded multitude, almost all of it small, almost all of it quiet. The basin is inhabited the way a reef is inhabited: not by a few prominent creatures, but by layers of life fitted into every wet seam, warm surface, slow current, and dripping joint. Nothing here makes a home out of leaves or branches. Nothing roots. Instead, life clings, crawls, filters, hovers, grazes, nests in grit, and uses the hard works of the town as if the town were a reef of metal and stone.

The first and most fundamental inhabitants are the films.

They live wherever water is repeated often enough to leave a memory: under overflow pipes, along shaded joints, inside drainage troughs, on the backs of stairwells, beneath railings where condensation gathers, and in the narrow spaces between concrete panels where the air stays damp long after the sun has thickened into haze. They are bacterial mats, fungal felts, mineral-stained skins, and heat-tolerant microbial crusts. They are not plants. They do not reach upward toward light in the hopeful way of leaves. They spread laterally, in patches, veins, felts, and stains, following the logic of moisture rather than soil.

Some are black-brown and glossy, like wet tar laid thin over brick. Some are rust-orange, especially where iron-rich seepage runs from old bolts, pipe collars, and rail fastenings. Some are pale gray, almost chalky, forming over salt crusts in places that alternate between damp and dry. Others have a metallic sheen in dim light, a faint bronze or coppery bloom that makes them look less like growth and more like a discoloration produced by heat and time. When rain comes, they swell. When the pans concentrate again under evaporation, they slow, stiffen, or retreat into microscopic persistence. They are the base of everything that moves. Without them, the water would be too mineral, too bare, too chemically severe. They turn wet mineral surfaces into something that can be grazed. They make the town digestible.

In the channels, the water itself seems inhabited before one sees any individual body. It is textured by movement. Along the submerged sides of concrete troughs, amphipods move in quick sideways flicks, pale and restless, turning detritus and microbial film into small, bright bodies. Isopods cling to grated floors and vertical walls, flattened and patient, their antennae testing the current. Worms work the soft places where sediment has collected behind baffles, in corners where flow slows enough for fine mineral matter and organic drift to settle. Snails move slowly over surfaces that would look bare to a careless eye, grazing films so thin they are almost invisible. Their shells are often pitted or dulled by the brackish water, but their bodies are persistent.

Where the current slackens, insect larvae gather. They hang just beneath the surface film, suspended in the warm water, rising and falling with minute adjustments. Some live in little cases made from grains of slag, fragments of shell, or particles of rust, carrying their shelter with them. Others are bare, pale, almost translucent, their bodies adapted to water that changes temperature and salinity faster than most creatures would tolerate. When rain briefly dilutes the basin, hatching happens in pulses. A channel that seemed only warm and still can become crowded within hours. Then the fish come.

The fish are the quick silver thought of the place. They are narrow, fast, and flexible, built for shifting conditions rather than clear rivers. Their sides are slim and reflective, but not with the bright cleanliness of open water; their scales have a faint brassy or gray cast, tuned to mineral haze and warm shallows. They move in dense bands through drainage canals, especially where cooler return water mixes with warmer outflow. When light shifts across the surface, they turn together, a single ripple passing through many bodies. They feed on larvae, small crustaceans, and the loose material shaken from microbial mats. They know the shaded places. Under pipe bridges, beneath the dark lips of culverts, and along the cooler undersides of walkways, they wait during the hottest part of the day. At dusk they become bolder, moving into shallower water to feed where the insect hatch thickens.

In the hottest crystallizer yards, fish cannot stay long. There the water is too concentrated, too hot, too demanding. Those places belong to tougher lives: salt-tolerant invertebrates, small crustaceans with compact bodies, brine-adapted worms, and the microbial crusts that can endure extremes which would kill more delicate creatures. Even there, however, life is not absent. It is only compressed. A black film may line a stone joint. A beetle may cross the hot concrete at the edge of a pan. A bird may stand motionless in a shallow corner, one foot lifted, waiting for something to move.

The air above the basin is just as populated as the water, though its inhabitants are finer and more transient. Midges rise in thin columns wherever overflow pipes drip or warm water meets cooler air. From a distance these columns look like smoke, though they have no source of fire. They bend and thicken in the still air, responding to light and heat. Brine flies stand in pale bands along damp walls, their bodies small, their posture patient. They do not swarm noisily. They occupy the margins: wet brick, copper-stained concrete, the lower sides of handrails, the damp shadows behind pump houses. When something disturbs them, they lift in a brief gray veil and then settle again almost immediately, as if the surface itself recalled them.

Beetles move over wet concrete with deliberate mechanical patience. Some are dark and blunt, their shells marked by salt pitting. Others have a faint oily sheen, or a bronzed edge where the light catches them. They are scavengers and hunters both, eating drowned flies, fragments of fungal felt, dead larvae, and whatever organic drift collects in the corners of the town. They are not beautiful in an obvious way. They are beautifully suited. Their legs are made for slick surfaces. Their bodies resist the damp. Their lives proceed in the intervals between machines, under railings, along tank bases, in the service gaps where humans rarely step.

Dragonflies and damselflies patrol the canals. Because there are no stems, no reeds, no leaves, they use what the town provides: pipe flanges, railings, sensor boxes, gate chains, the edges of louvered panels, the rims of manhole covers. They perch with folded wings, watching the smaller insects that rise from the water. Their bodies are bright in brief flashes—blue, copper, dusty green—but even these colors seem muted by the basin’s light. They hunt along the edges where warm water meets shade, where insect life is densest. Sometimes a dragonfly will take a small moth or midge so close to a pump housing that the sound of its wings becomes a fine pressure in the ears rather than a distinct noise.

Bats arrive when the light turns copper-gray. They come down from the eaves of process halls, from high vents, from the gaps under roof parapets, from the dark interiors of disused housing blocks where the louvres have warped slightly and left a permanent slit of entrance. They are small, fast, and heat-tolerant. Their roosts are dry where possible, but not always clean; under them accumulate little pellets of chitin, shed wing fragments, and fine mineral dust. At dusk they skim above the pans and canals, flying low where the insect hatch is thickest. Their flight is silent enough that one often sees them only when they cross a lamp glow: a black flick, a quick turn, then absence. They drink on the wing, dipping to the surface of a pan or puddle without stopping, leaving a tiny disturbance that closes at once.

Birds are present too, but they are not noisy. They have learned the basin’s scale. A wader may stand alone in a pan the size of a street, its neck lowered, its legs set wide against the slick salt crust. It steps carefully, testing each placement, because the surface is not soil and not stone but something between: crystalline in places, slimy in others, treacherous where microbial film covers a metal grate. Some birds are gull-like, standing in still rows on wet pavement or along canal edges, facing the same direction as if waiting for a signal. Others are smaller, more cautious, nesting on flat roofs, in abandoned ventilation housings, on gravelled terraces, and in the shallow depressions of old concrete where rain collects and then evaporates.

Their nests are not made of green material. There is no grass, no twig, no moss. Instead, the birds gather what the basin offers: shell fragments, grit, flakes of rust, shed beetle carapaces, dried crusts of microbial mat, synthetic fibers carried in by wind, thin strips of rubber or insulation. The nests are hard, sparse things, barely softer than the surfaces on which they rest. Yet they work. They drain quickly. They do not rot easily. They hold eggs in place even when wind moves across the open roofs. The young are raised in a landscape without vegetation, learning early to walk on metal, concrete, and salt.

Predator and prey relationships are everywhere once the eye adjusts to the scale. Fish take larvae. Beetles take smaller insects. Birds take fish and crustaceans. Bats take the aerial swarm. Scavengers work the edges: worms, flies, small crustaceans, and the microbial films that digest what remains. Nothing is ornamental here. Every life has a function, and every function is tied to water. The town was built to manage water, salt, heat, and living biomass, and life has entered that purpose so completely that the machinery now partly processes life itself. A scraper crossing an evaporation pan may push aside mineral cake and dead midges together. A filter may catch chitin as readily as sediment. A warm outfall may feed fish as surely as it feeds the next stage of the industrial cycle.

There are places where animals dominate and places where machines dominate, but few places where the two are entirely separate. Along the cooler return canals, fish gather under shaded pipe bridges, their bodies hanging in the darker water. In the hot crystallizer yards, only microbial crusts and the hardiest invertebrates persist. Under the apartment blocks, where condensation drips from ceiling slabs and the air is close, fungal felts spread in dark bands and small insects feed on them. On the roofs, birds rest during the hottest hours, wings slightly spread, silent in the glare. In pump galleries, the walls sweat continuously, and the floor becomes slick with living film unless it is scraped back. The scraping is not a rejection of life but a negotiation with it. Life must be allowed enough to sustain the system, but not so much that it stops the system from moving.

People live here too, but thinly.

They are rare presences: small figures in boots and reflective coats crossing a yard, appearing briefly on a catwalk, vanishing into a pump house. They do not fill the streets. They do not gather in crowds. Their numbers are spaced out by the work itself. One technician may tend a row of sensors on the east side of the basin while another adjusts a gate several blocks away, and they may pass the whole shift without meeting. The district could have housed thousands, but few lights show in the long apartment blocks. Those who remain live in the parts of the town that still serve their purpose: a lit window here, a ventilated room there, a doorway propped open by a rubber mat, a kettle, a logbook, a pair of drying gloves hung near a fan.

Their homes are practical. Windows are louvered rather than glazed, because the air must move even when it is heavy. Rooms have condensation channels under the sills and absorbent strips where moisture beads at night. Floors are bare or covered in black rubber matting. Walls are painted in colors that have faded toward mineral gray. There are no houseplants. No one tries to keep a window box. Instead, life enters accidentally: a moth near a lamp, a beetle under a door, a fine line of fungal growth in a corner where damp persists. The people do not fight this completely. They manage it. They wipe surfaces, clear drains, replace seals, and scrape back growth where it interferes with machinery. But they know they are living inside a wet, breathing system, not apart from it.

Their work is mostly attention. They watch thresholds. They check water level, salinity, temperature, flow rate, oxygen, hatch density, pump vibration, gate position, sensor clarity. Much of the adjustment is automatic, but automation has edges. A relay sticks. A scraper blade wears. A grate clogs with shed chitin. A sensor lens clouds with mineral film. A gate needs to be lowered by a centimeter because the afternoon evaporation is stronger than expected. A small change in one part of the basin can trigger movement in another: a drop in salinity may hatch larvae; a hatch may draw fish; fish may alter where birds stand; birds may foul a walkway or a roof vent; a walkway may need scraping; a scraped walkway may change where water sheets. The people who live there understand this not as theory but as habit. They move through the district with their eyes lowered and their ears tuned. They listen for pumps, for relays, for the wrong kind of silence.

They speak softly. Not because they are secretive, but because the place absorbs loudness. The humidity, the heat, the wide open pans, the insulated machines—all of it makes sound behave strangely. A voice carries only so far before it seems to thicken and settle. A footstep on a grate rings briefly, then disappears into the low electrical hum. So the human presence is often marked by small sounds: the click of a latch, the scrape of a boot on salt-crusted concrete, the hiss of a radio kept low, the creak of a gate being eased rather than pushed. When they meet, they do not embrace or chatter. They compare readings. They note which channel is warmer, which roof has nesting birds, which pump is drawing brine with a different pitch. They may stand for a moment beside a puddle, looking not at each other but at the water, because in this place water is the common language.

Their bodies have adapted to the basin’s pressure. Their skin is always slightly aware of moisture. Their clothes dry slowly. Their hands are marked by salt and grease. Their eyes are used to pewter light, to glare softened by vapor, to distances made uncertain by heat shimmer. They do not expect green. They do not miss it in the way outsiders might. To them, life is not something that must have leaves. Life is a film on a wall, a flicker under water, a bird standing on a roof, a bat crossing a lamp glow, a fish turning in a canal. They have learned to see abundance without softness.

Some of them were born here. They know no other horizon. Their childhood memories are of puddles connecting after rain, of gulls standing in still rows, of the low hum of transformer yards, of being told not to touch certain pipes because they were too hot, of collecting rust flakes and shell fragments for school projects because there were no flowers to press. They may leave sometimes, but many return, unsettled by places where the air is too dry or where green things grow too thickly. The basin’s emptiness is not empty to them. It has spacing. It has room. It has a calm that comes from long use.

The machines are not alive, but they have rhythms that living things answer. Pumps lift water from lower channels into settling tanks at intervals that fish and birds have learned. Scrapers move slowly across evaporation pans, and insects avoid their paths or return after they pass. Gates rise and lower by centimeters, altering the depth of a channel enough to trigger feeding behavior in small crustaceans. Transformers hum, and bats sometimes roost near the warmer walls of their cages. Rail-mounted conveyors carry mineral cake in covered troughs, and beetles wait beneath the joints where spillage collects. The machines breathe in their low electrical way, and the town breathes with them.

Because the district is open and sparsely occupied, each presence feels isolated. A single lit window in a long block. One technician on a far walkway. Two birds in a pan the size of a street. A train of small hoppers standing on a siding, empty but ready. A bat crossing the copper light. A fish breaking the surface with a tiny glassy click. These isolated figures are not lonely in the human sense. They are spaced according to the logic of the basin. There is room between things because the place is built for movement: water moving through channels, salt moving toward crystallization, heat moving upward in slow columns, insects moving from hatch to air, birds moving from roof to pan, people moving from one necessary point to another.

What lives there, finally, is not only a collection of creatures. It is a pattern of habitation fitted to severe conditions. Life here has given up the desire for green shelter. It has accepted hard surfaces, mineral water, hot air, and the constant nearness of machinery. It has become fine-grained, countless, and quiet. It lives in films rather than forests, in swarms rather than herds, in small bodies that can turn quickly when salinity changes or a lamp flickers on early in the muted day.

The basin holds all of it: microbial skins on dripping joints, worms in warm sediment, fish turning in brackish channels, beetles crossing wet concrete, flies standing in pale bands, dragonflies perched on pipe flanges, bats skimming the dusk, birds nesting in rust and shell, humans moving thinly through the works with tools and logbooks and salt-stained gloves. Nothing roots, yet everything holds. Nothing is soft in the pastoral way, yet everything is alive. The town is not empty. It is simply occupied by what can live without greenery, without crowds, without noise—by the countless small inheritors of water, heat, salt, and stone.
