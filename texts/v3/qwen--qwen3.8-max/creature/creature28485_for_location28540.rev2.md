What lives there is not numerous. The hollow does not support a population, and it does not seem to want one. At first, the truest answer appears to be: barely anything.

A translucent snail under a leaf. A pale fly or moth drifting through the upper light as if it has made a mistake. A tremor in one of the shallow pools that might be a reflection losing patience. A bead of water swelling on stone, falling, and being answered by another bead elsewhere. These are the small signs, and for a long time they may seem like all the life the place contains.

But “life” has to be understood differently in this hollow. It is not life as swarming, nesting, hunting, burrowing, building, or leaving traces. It is life as persistence. Life as wetness holding its shape. Life as attention without intrusion.

What lives there announces itself first not as a body but as a change in the listening.

If you stand near the center, where the water passes over the broad black stone in a thin continuous sheet, you may feel that the sound has thickened slightly. Not louder. Not different in pitch, exactly. Only denser, as though the dripping has acquired a second interior. A drip strikes a pool, and the echo seems to pass through something soft before returning. A trickle slides over moss, and beneath it there is a slower rhythm, almost a pulse. You look for the source and see only water, stone, green shadows.

Then one of the shadows blinks.

It is not easy to see clearly at first, because it has been made by the same conditions that make the hollow difficult to see sharply. It lies low in the water, or just under the film of it, and its body takes on the darkness of the wet basalt. When it is still, it appears to be part of the pool’s floor. When it moves, the movement seems to belong to the water before it belongs to the creature. A sheet of moisture shifts. A ripple spreads wider than it should. A rounded darkness separates itself from the stone with unbearable slowness.

This is the inhabitant.

Not a fish. Not quite an insect. Not fully amphibian in any ordinary sense, though amphibian is the nearest word. It is a soft-bodied thing, low and broad, shaped less for travel than for remaining. It has no look of speed. Its outline is blunt and settled, as if it has grown into the contours of the basin it occupies. It may be no larger than a folded cloak, or perhaps slightly longer when it extends itself along a shelf, but it has weight. Not heaviness, exactly, but presence: the sense of a body that displaces water gently and is not in a hurry to move it again.

Its skin is smooth, glossy, and dark, the green-black of old basalt rinsed for centuries. In certain lights there is a mineral sheen across it, not like scales but like a faint dust of wet silver or pale green salt. Along its back, the surface is slightly rougher, and in the oldest places tiny crusts of mineral deposit cling to it, as though the hollow has begun to claim the creature as geology. Moss does not grow thickly upon it, but a fine algal brightness may live in the skin itself, especially where the water runs over the shoulders and flanks. When the light strikes there, the creature seems less like an animal and more like a stone that has remembered warmth.

Its belly is paler, almost translucent in places, especially when it lies over a bright pool. Then you can sense, rather than see, the faint movement of water within it. Not blood in any familiar sense. Something slower. Something distributed. The body appears to work by saturation, by membranes, by patient exchange. It does not seem to contain dryness anywhere.

The head is broad and blunt, without a muzzle. There is no aggression in it. The mouth is wide but soft, made for pressing against wet surfaces, for gathering films, for drinking from thin sheets of water rather than biting. The lips, if they can be called lips, are flexible and finely fringed, and when the creature rests its mouth against a glistening rock face it seems almost to be listening with it. The nostrils are small slits that close when it submerges. They open again with a delicacy that makes the motion seem ceremonial, though nothing here is performed.

The eyes are dark, large enough to gather the dim green light, and set low enough that they can remain just above the water while the rest of the body lies hidden. They are not bright eyes. They do not flash. They have the depth of wet seeds. When the creature blinks, a second membrane passes across them, clear and slow, leaving them brighter for a moment. Sometimes the blink seems to occur a little after the sound of a falling drop, and sometimes a little before. It is difficult to tell. The hollow has enough delay in it to confuse such things.

There are no external ears. Instead, thin membranes stretch along the jaw, the throat, and the upper ribs, pale and almost glassy when wet. These tremble faintly when sound moves through the hollow. They are not gills, though gill-like folds exist too, tucked along the sides where the body meets the stone. The creature breathes through its skin, through those folds, and perhaps through the water itself in ways that are not easily separated from hearing. In this place, sound and water are too close to be different kingdoms.

Its limbs are short and strong, ending in broad hands and feet with rounded pads rather than claws. The fingers and toes are separate, sensitive, and fringed with delicate skin that increases their surface without becoming ornamental. They can press flat against wet rock and hold there without force. They can test the tension of a water film. They can touch a leaf or a pool surface with such care that no ripple follows. When the creature climbs the curved walls, which it does rarely, it moves with the patience of moisture itself, finding holds that are not quite holds, trusting the stone’s dampness more than its shape.

It smells of warm rain on dark rock. There is no animal musk, no sharpness. Only wet mineral greenness, a scent so close to the hollow’s own breath that it disappears unless one comes very near.

The creature spends most of its time near the central stone, in the pools gathered around it. It may lie half submerged while the water passes over its back in a thin sheet, or it may rest just beneath the surface with only its eyes and the pale membranes of its throat exposed. In that position it is almost impossible to distinguish from the wet stone. The water bends over it. Light slides across it. Reflections form around it and break apart. If it is seen at all, it is seen as a darkening that has weight.

It does not hide in the manner of frightened things. It simply belongs to the conditions that make seeing difficult.

It is patient in a way that suggests not virtue but nature. It can remain motionless for long periods while drops fall around it and the hollow multiplies their sounds. When it moves, it does so because something in the pattern of water or vibration has changed. A bead may fall differently. The mist may thicken at the rim. A leaf may strike stone with a new angle. The creature responds to such things as if they were messages, though not messages meant for it.

It feeds simply. It browses the living skin of the stone: the fine algae, the mineral films, the microbial layers that grow where water weeps continuously over rock. It presses its mouth to wet surfaces and draws what is there without scraping, without tearing, without leaving a mark. It may take in fallen insects or small drifting things if they come to the water, but it does not hunt them. It does not chase. It is not a predator. It seems to eat as the hollow itself eats: slowly, by accumulation, by accepting what arrives.

It drinks through its mouth, through its skin, and perhaps through the whole surface of its body. When mist is thick, it may lift its head slightly and let the moisture gather on its face. Droplets run down its throat and vanish into the water. It does not shake itself. It does not wipe away the wetness. It receives it.

The sound of the hollow is not background to this creature. It is the medium in which the creature exists.

Its body is a resonator. When the water strikes the stone near it, the membranes along its throat tremble faintly. When wind enters over the rim and is divided by fern and root and leaf, the creature’s ribs expand and settle in rhythm with the low intermittent hum. It does not make calls. It has no voice in the ordinary sense. But when it exhales slowly underwater, the pools give out a soft, almost inaudible vibration, like a finger drawn around the rim of a cup. The hollow takes that vibration and blends it with the dripping until it is impossible to say which sound came from water and which from flesh.

Sometimes, when a drop falls from a high point and strikes a pool with unusual regularity, the creature’s breathing appears to synchronize with it. Then the hollow feels even more like a body with a pulse. The coincidence is quiet. Nothing announces it. Yet once noticed, it is difficult to forget.

It may be that the creature has learned the rhythms of the place so completely that its own motions have become part of them. It may be that the hollow shaped the creature to be its sounding chamber. It may be that there is no meaningful difference between those two explanations.

If another living thing enters the hollow, the inhabitant does not react with alarm. A moth drifting through the upper light may cause no response at all. A snail moving under a leaf is beneath notice. If a visitor comes on two legs, bringing weight and disturbance, the creature will likely sink lower into the water. It does not flee. It reduces itself to shadow. It waits to understand the pressure in the air, the vibration in the pools, the meaning of the new stillness.

If the visitor remains quiet, the inhabitant may rise again.

Its gaze is not curious in a human way. It does not study. It does not ask. It attends. It seems to perceive by touch and tremor more than by sight, yet there is an orientation to it, a turning toward whatever has changed. When it looks, or seems to look, it does not seem to see a shape so much as a disturbance in the wetness of the world. It may watch a breath move across the surface of a pool. It may watch a bead swell on the rim above. It may watch the light slow itself into silver-green threads through the mist.

It is not tame. It is not friendly. It does not offer itself. But it is not hostile either. It has no need to defend anything. The hollow is not territory to it. It is condition.

There may be others.

This is hard to know. The place is small enough to be taken in from one standing place, but its wet surfaces, pools, and shadows make concealment easy. Perhaps there is only one. Perhaps there are a few, spaced through the hollow like the plants: one near the central stone, one under a fern ledge, one in a high pocket where mist gathers first. They would not crowd. They would not build together or speak together or leave signs of their passing. They might simply exist near one another in the way stones exist near one another, separated by water and sound.

If they meet, it is probably without ceremony. A ripple crosses a pool. A membrane trembles. One dark shape acknowledges another by becoming still in a slightly different way. There is no evidence of affection, conflict, or hierarchy. There is only the fact that the hollow can hold a few such beings without becoming crowded, just as it can hold a few broad leaves, a few ferns, a few vines, and much space.

At rare intervals, when the mist is thick for many days and the sound of the hollow deepens, the creature near the central stone may become even stiller than usual. The water around it seems to gather itself into finer beads. The drips fall more distinctly. The pools tremble with almost imperceptible rings. In such times, if another inhabitant appears, it does so from the water as if the water had decided to remember a shape.

No one sees how they reproduce. Perhaps the clear eggs, if they are eggs, are laid in the mineral terraces where the water lies in thin sheets no wider than a hand. Perhaps they look like beads of condensed moisture until something dark forms inside them. Perhaps the young are so transparent that they live for a long time as nothing more than moving glints in the film of water on stone. They would have to be. The hollow is not generous enough to support many large lives. It can support only a few, and those must be slow.

The young, if they exist, would probably be almost entirely water themselves, with dark specks for eyes and soft threads for limbs. They would cling to the underside of wet leaves or hide in the capillary spaces between moss and rock. They would feed on microscopic films and on the mineral taste of condensation. Most would not last. A dry interval might end them. A too-heavy drip might wash them away. A change in the hollow’s breathing might erase them. But one might remain. One might settle into a shallow basin and stay there for years, growing so slowly that its size becomes a kind of patience made visible.

The inhabitant does not age as surface creatures age. It does not wither into dryness. If anything, it seems to become more mineral with time. Its movements grow slower. Its skin takes on more of the pale crusts. Its eyes remain dark but less often open. It may spend long intervals motionless under the water sheet, becoming difficult to distinguish from the central stone itself. The hollow does not mourn this. It incorporates it.

When such a creature dies, if it dies in a way that can be called death, it probably leaves no bones. The body would soften into the water, into sediment, into the fine mineral deposits that build drop by drop. Perhaps it would leave only a faint stain on the stone, a smooth hollow where its weight had rested, a slight darkening in the algae. Perhaps the water would continue to move around that absence as if remembering a shape. In time, a new crust would form there. In time, another bead would fall in the same place. In time, the hollow would adjust.

It makes no mark while it lives. This is important.

It does not scratch the stone. It does not bend plants into shelter. It does not carry stones, shells, leaves, or water. It does not create paths. It does not leave nests, burrows, middens, or tracks that last. The water erases whatever pressure it makes. The moss closes behind it. The mist fills its absence. If it uses anything, it uses what is already there: a pool, a ledge, a curve of mineral terrace, a sheet of water falling over rock. It does not improve these things. It does not change them. It inhabits them as weather inhabits the hollow.

Because of this, it is easy to mistake it for a spirit, but that word may be too thin and too separate. It is not a spirit imposed upon the place. It is not a guardian set above it. It is not an intention watching from outside. If it is a spirit, it is a spirit made of the hollow’s own logic: gravity, capillarity, warmth, curvature, sound, long time. It lives because those things have repeated themselves long enough to become flesh. Or something like flesh. Something that breathes, blinks, and remains.

It is also easy to mistake it for the hollow itself. Sometimes this is closer to the truth.

The creature seems to be an organ of the place. Not its brain, not its heart, but something like its skin listening to itself. It notices what the hollow does. It embodies the hollow’s pulse. When the mist gathers at the rim, the creature lifts its face. When the wind divides itself among the ferns and makes the low humming, the membranes along its throat answer. When a drip falls into a cup-shaped cavity and gives out a deeper tone, the creature’s body seems to accept the sound and carry it farther. It does not control these things. It does not command them. It is where they become perceptible.

That is why, after a while, it feels less like “what” lives there and more like “who.”

Not because it is human. It is profoundly not human. Its body has no interest in hands that make, eyes that calculate, or mouths that speak. It does not laugh, threaten, beckon, or plead. It does not have a face shaped for expression. Yet it has selfhood. It has orientation. It has preference. It chooses certain pools, certain drips, certain temperatures of stone. It turns toward some vibrations and away from others. It persists. It endures. It attends.

These are enough to make a who, perhaps, when there is no one else.

If you were to leave the hollow and look back, you might not see it. You might see only the rounded walls, the pale tufa, the dark green plants, the shining threads of water, the mist entering where the rim is lowest. You might hear the dripping multiplied, the rustle over ribbed stone, the soft intermittent hum. You might feel that the place was full of sound and yet strangely empty.

But if you had stayed long enough, you would know that the fullness was not only water and echo.

Somewhere near the center, under the thin sheet of water passing over the black stone, a dark shape would be resting. Its back would catch the light. Its throat would tremble faintly. Its eyes would open, or seem to open, just enough to take in the changing air. A bead would form above it. It would fall. The pool would answer. The creature would blink, perhaps a moment late, perhaps a moment early, and the hollow would continue its slow, loud, warm breathing around that one small act of remaining.

That is what lives there.

The listener in the wet room of stone.  
The soft dark animal of the dripping bowl.  
The original remainder.  
The one that never learned to leave.  
The breathing stone that is not quite stone.  

It lives without making, without taking, without needing to be remembered. It lives as the hollow lives: sparingly, wetly, loudly in small ways, strangely in quiet ways, and with a patience so complete that it feels less like endurance than like the natural condition of the world.
