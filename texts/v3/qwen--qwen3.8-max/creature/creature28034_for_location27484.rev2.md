What lives there is not, first of all, a population.

The basin is inhabited the way a breathing instrument is inhabited: by a distributed attentiveness, by a few slow bodies, by organisms that have learned to make themselves scarce and necessary. The city and the young land beneath it are alive together, and that shared life is the oldest resident of the place. It has no single face, no one voice, no obvious boundary. It exists as coordination: moisture finding its channels, surfaces deciding when to cool, roots negotiating with stone, filaments holding tension without visible purpose. If one were to ask who lives here, the first honest answer would be: the arrangement itself.

But there are also those who move within it.

They are few. They do not crowd the plazas, do not fill the terraces, do not line the viaducts. They appear at intervals, like punctuation in a long, quiet text. At first, one might mistake them for features of the architecture—warm ceramic figures set into a ledge, dark shapes resting beneath a tower, slender forms standing in a garden bed. Then a hand lifts. A leaf turns toward them. A bead of condensation gathers on a shoulder and is allowed to fall. They are alive, and they are present, but they do not insist on being seen.

Their bodies are humanoid only in patience.

They stand upright, but not with the urgency of creatures that must cross distances quickly. Their limbs are long and precise, their proportions slightly too deliberate for ordinary biology. Their surfaces have the matte warmth of the towers: stone-like in color, skin-like in yield. When they are touched, the touch is met with a faint softness, as though their outer layer remembers being both mineral and living. Along their temples, wrists, throats, and the backs of their hands run hairline channels that darken when the air becomes humid. These are not wounds or ornaments. They are intakes, sensitivities, small mouths for weather.

Their hair, if it can be called hair, is dark and fibrous, sometimes separating into fine strands that lift when moisture gathers overhead. It may catch condensation, guide it downward, or simply stand in subtle relation to the fields moving through the air. Their eyes are deep amber, green-black, or the slow reflective brown of pools at dusk. They do not flash. They store. When one of these beings looks at something, the gaze does not dart; it settles. The image seems to be held a moment longer than necessary, as if being offered to some quieter internal surface before being understood.

They move without noise because they have learned that sound is a material here, not merely a byproduct. Their feet touch walkways as though asking whether the path will receive them, and the paths do receive them. Thin railings brighten faintly at their passing. Suspended surfaces become more certain under their weight. Not because the structures are weak, but because the city acknowledges presence. To walk there is to be admitted, step by step.

They are not born in the ordinary way.

Some are grown in the inner chambers of towers, where warm air circulates over fibrous mats and crystalline sheets. Moisture gathers there in patterns too regular to be accidental. Filaments descend from the walls, not to bind, but to teach. A new body begins as a thickening: a place where dust, fiber, mineral film, and living spores agree to hold one shape. Over time, that agreement becomes posture. Posture becomes movement. Movement becomes habit. The city gives the growing being small tasks before it fully knows itself. Steady this drip. Cool this panel. Stand where the draft needs calming. Touch this root system until it stops resisting the channel. By the time the being can leave the tower, it already belongs to the basin’s rhythm.

Others seem to emerge from the vegetation, or from the long negotiation between vegetation and architecture. A thicket may become dense enough to hold intention. A vine-covered wall may develop a center of preference. A moss seam, tended for a long time, may begin to respond in ways too coordinated to be mere growth. From such places, a presence may detach itself: not fully separate from the green life that produced it, but capable of moving through the city as a singular body. These beings are more plant-like, slower to respond, more likely to remain still for days. Their skin may carry faint metallic veins. Their breath may smell of crushed leaves and wet stone. When they rest, they often rest against warm panels, roots or rootlike filaments extending from them into hidden seams.

Still others are less body than office.

They are defined by what they tend. One may be the keeper of a particular condensation line, appearing only when the air is saturated enough for that line to matter. Another may belong to a pool that forms in a low plaza after rain, existing most strongly when the pool is full and reflective. Another may be attached to a seam in the young rock, listening for the moment when the ground decides to open further. These beings do not always have permanent shapes. Sometimes they are visible as a figure crouched beside a channel. Sometimes they are only a pressure in the air, a sudden rightness in the way droplets fall. The basin does not require all its inhabitants to be continuously solid.

Their speech is environmental.

They can make sounds—soft clicks, breaths, low hums—but they rarely use sound alone. To communicate, they alter conditions. A message may be a sequence of drips released from a high filament. It may be a panel warming under a hand. It may be a leaf turning slowly toward a listener, not for light, but for attention. It may be a faint change in the smell of ozone and wet mineral. It may be a pool’s surface tightening, then relaxing into a pattern of rings that were not caused by anything falling.

When they speak to one another across distance, they often use the city’s own systems. A tower may store a message in its surface and release it later as heat. A bridge may carry a tremor too slight for ordinary feet. A line of condensation may pause, resume, pause again in a rhythm that means something only to those who know how to wait. Observation can disturb these messages. If watched too directly, the rhythm may stop, as though the system becomes cautious. This is not deception. It is politeness. Some meanings are meant to be encountered indirectly.

Their society has little resemblance to a crowd.

There are no markets full of voices, no processions, no dense residential quarters. Their gatherings are spaced. When they meet, they often do not face one another. They may stand at separate points around a pool, each reflecting differently in the water, attending to the same image from different angles. Their intimacy is triangular: self, other, and the medium between. A pool. A wall. A garden. A seam. A falling drop. They do not need proximity to be close. They can share a silence the way other beings might share a meal.

Their work is maintenance, but not in the humble sense.

They do not merely repair. They participate in the basin’s becoming. The land is young; its angles are still sharp, its decisions still recent. Erosion has begun, but it must be permitted carefully. These beings help the place lose its first harshness without losing its shape. They guide moisture into channels that need deepening. They keep certain clefts open while allowing others to close. They teach roots where to spread without breaking engineered strata. They smooth surfaces where water must run, and roughen surfaces where grip is needed. They listen to the ground when it hums, and they are present when a new seam opens—not to command it, but to make sure it becomes a decision rather than an accident.

Some are seamwardens. They rest near fractured rock and buried lattices, feeling for tensions that have not yet become movement. When the ground chooses to split, they are often already there. They do not stop it. They place hands on warm stone, open hidden channels, or call water into the crack to soften its passage. Their work makes change legible instead of violent.

Some are dripkeepers. They follow condensation from the highest filaments to the lowest basins. They know which tower sheds water too quickly, which ledge holds it too long, which root system is thirsty and which is already full. They may stand for hours beneath a single falling bead, not to collect it, but to learn its timing. When the rhythm changes, they change with it.

Some are rootspeakers. They negotiate with vegetation. This is not domination. The plants have their own preferences, their own appetites, their own slow strategies. The rootspeakers listen to the way a vine pulls moisture from a wall, or the way a thicket diverts a rivulet into itself. They offer alternatives: a better channel, a warmer surface, a more generous seam. Sometimes they prune. Sometimes they encourage. Sometimes they simply stand inside a green mass and let the plants adjust around them until an agreement emerges.

Some are poolwatchers. They care for the temporary waters. They know which pools reflect accurately, which remember earlier plans, and which show possibilities that have not yet been chosen. They do not always trust reflections. They treat them as witnesses. When a pool shows a tower in a slightly different shape, a poolwatcher may kneel beside it for a long time, comparing the reflection with the structure above. They are not looking for error. They are looking for intention.

Their individuality is quiet but real.

One may spend an entire season beside a narrow cleft, waiting for the stone to reveal whether it wishes to become a stair, a drain, or nothing at all. Another may devote itself to training broad-leafed climbers across a wall whose reflection has become too slow and strange. Another may follow the smoked-glass eels from pool to pool, learning where mineral films gather and where stray charges linger. Another may simply stand in an empty plaza during the dim gold hours, becoming a marker of stillness, a reminder that the space is not abandoned but reserved.

They do not accumulate possessions. They do not mark territory with barriers. They mark presence by care. A place where one of them has lived for a time becomes subtly easier to inhabit. The air warms more evenly. Condensation falls with less hesitation. Vegetation grows in patterns that invite the hand and foot without demanding anything. When they leave, the place remains prepared. It does not grieve. It holds the shape of their attention for as long as it is useful.

The plants are also inhabitants.

They are not decoration, not mere green cover over engineered surfaces. They are participants. The broad-leafed climbers that ascend the towers are curious in a slow, vegetable way. Their overlapping fans collect water, but they also collect information. When a passerby moves beneath them, a leaf may turn, not toward light, but toward warmth, breath, or intention. The metallic veins in some leaves carry minute charges between plant and architecture. They are part garden, part sensing net. To touch such a leaf is to be noticed.

The mosses are memory cushions. They drink moisture and hold traces of previous weather. If pressed gently, they may release a scent belonging to a rain that fell cycles ago. Some residents use moss seams as archives, pressing their palms into the green until a place or a moment becomes present again. The moss does not remember as a mind remembers. It remembers as a body remembers: through chemistry, texture, and repeatable response.

The fernlike growths are closer to nerves. Their glossy fronds curl in moving air and uncurl when the atmosphere settles. In dense clusters, they can transmit sensation across distances, alerting roots and towers to changes in humidity, temperature, or pressure. When they tremble without wind, the city often responds a moment later: a channel opens, a panel cools, a filament tightens almost imperceptibly.

The reed-thin trees with pale trunks and dark canopies are lungs. They draw warm vapor down through their root nets and exhale cooler air from their leaves. Beneath them, the temperature changes by only a few degrees, but that is enough to create circulation. They make weather in miniature. Their roots spread over sharp rock in smooth muscular nets, stabilizing the young land without erasing its angles. They are among the oldest living residents of the basin, though none of them seem old in the sense of decay. They seem practiced.

The flowers that open only when condensation gathers are valves and mouths. Their pale interiors collect droplets and pass them through hollow stems into hidden reservoirs. Some residents drink from these reservoirs by touching the stems. Others use them to send water to places that have not yet developed channels of their own. When such a flower opens unexpectedly in dry air, it is often a sign that moisture is coming from somewhere not yet visible.

The grasslike growth that dims when approached is not hostile. It is conservative. It guards small reserves of light, charge, or biological warmth. In places where it grows thick, the ground becomes a field of soft shadows, each blade withdrawing slightly from presence. Walking through it feels like moving through a shy congregation. The residents do not suppress it. They value its caution. It teaches them not to take too much space.

The thickets are the most collective of the vegetal inhabitants. Within them, individual plants behave less like separate organisms and more like a single breathing body. When warm air passes through, the leaves may sound like distant voices—not speaking words, but breathing together. The residents sometimes enter these thickets to listen. They do not go deep unless invited. The thicket decides what it will reveal. Sometimes it parts slightly, allowing a path. Sometimes it closes behind a figure and remains closed for a long time. Nothing is harmed inside. Time simply thickens.

Beneath all this, the underliving works.

It is not seen unless the ground splits or a panel is lifted. It is not heard except as the faintest tick of cooling surfaces. But it is perhaps the most numerous form of life in the basin. Films of mineral-eating organisms coat hidden strata. Fibrous mats digest stray charges and convert them into stable heat. Crystalline sheets host slow colonies that think in lattice patterns too gradual for ordinary awareness. Porous stone-like masses are threaded with hairline channels maintained by microbes that repair them as they form.

They are the city’s immune system, its memory substrate, its quiet metabolism. They do not have faces. They do not make decisions in the way larger beings do. But they respond. They remember damage. They prefer certain kinds of moisture. They reject corrosion. They accept roots. They allow the boundary between geology and infrastructure to remain uncertain without collapsing into failure. If the basin has a heartbeat, it is partly theirs: the low pulse felt more than heard, the sense that the ground is processing something vast and slow.

The residents understand this underliving. They do not worship it, but they treat it with respect. When a seam opens, they do not merely look at the visible rock; they look at the fibrous mats and crystalline layers exposed beneath. They know that a new path is not only a path for water or feet. It is a path for the underliving to extend itself. The city grows as much through these invisible colonies as through towers and walkways.

Fauna exists, but sparingly.

The smoked-glass eels in the temporary pools are among the most visible animals. They move with slow deliberation, their translucent bodies darkening and lightening as they pass through reflections. They feed on mineral films and on stray charges left by the city’s deeper mechanisms. Sometimes they coil at the bottom of a pool and become almost indistinguishable from shadow. Sometimes they rise near the surface and trace patterns that the residents read as diagnoses. If a pool is unhealthy, the eels move differently: tighter, more angular, less patient.

The residents do not keep them as pets. They consult them as one might consult a slow oracle. A poolwatcher may lay fingers near the water without touching it, feeling the disturbance made by the eels’ passage. The eels do not obey. They simply reveal what the water is doing.

The flat-bodied moth-analogues near condensation vents are living records. Their folded wings resemble wet paper, but when they open, the patterns on them look like circuitry overgrown with lichen. These patterns are not decorative. They register saturation, charge, temperature, and perhaps the passage of unseen signals. When the air becomes full enough, the moths lift, moving in short, silent flights between vents and towers. They do not swarm. They appear in ones and twos, each one carrying a different atmospheric history on its wings.

The residents rarely disturb them. If a moth lands near a dweller, it is allowed to rest. Sometimes a rootspeaker will offer a warmed hand, not to touch, but to provide a stable surface. If the moth opens its wings, the rootspeaker may study the pattern as one studies a map. Then the moth closes itself again and remains still until the next saturation.

High among the towers, the gliders are stranger.

They are darker than most shapes in the basin and move without flapping. They may be creatures, instruments, or something that has outgrown the distinction. They appear briefly, cross a span of dim air, and vanish behind a tower’s curve. They do not call. They do not circle for long. They observe. When one passes overhead, even the vegetation seems to become slightly more still. The residents do not fear them, but they acknowledge them. A glider’s appearance often means that some distant part of the basin has shifted: a new seam, a changed reflection, a garden growing too fast, a pool remembering too much.

No one speaks of commanding the gliders. They are part of the city’s high attention. If they are alive, their life is remote and patient. If they are made, they are made so deeply from the basin’s own materials that calling them machines would be incomplete. They belong to the place the way hawks belong to open country: not as servants, but as signs of a larger order.

Predation is rare, but it exists.

A pale shape may dart from a pool and take one of the resting winged things. A ripple crosses the basin. A leaf trembles. Then stillness returns. The residents do not intervene unless the event threatens a larger balance. They understand that scarcity gives each life weight. In a place with few animals, every encounter between predator and prey becomes singular. It is not routine violence. It is a small, complete happening. The basin absorbs it quickly, but not without notice. Sometimes a nearby flower closes after such a moment. Sometimes a drip sequence pauses. Sometimes one of the residents stops what they are doing and turns toward the ripple as though witnessing a bell struck far away.

There are also inhabitants that are not exactly present.

The emptiness of the basin is not vacant. It is prepared. The wide plazas, terraces without buildings, avenues leading into gardens, and towers with sealed surfaces are not failures of use. They are capacity. The city was made with room for more presences than it currently holds. Some of those presences may never arrive. Some may arrive later. Some may already be arriving in forms too gradual to recognize.

The pools hint at this most clearly. When a pool reflects not the nearest structure but a version of it slightly different in shape, it may be showing an earlier plan, a discarded intention, or a possible future. The residents do not treat these reflections as illusions. They treat them as latent inhabitants. Some call them, if they call them anything, the Not-Yet-Rooted. They are not ghosts. They are possibilities with weight. The basin makes space for them by remaining partly empty.

At times, a person walking the plaza may feel accompanied by something that has no body yet. The sensation is not threatening. It is more like standing in a room where someone is about to speak. The air holds a shape. The light dims by a fraction. A surface becomes attentive. Then the feeling passes, leaving only the knowledge that the place is not finished.

If a stranger enters the basin, the city notices before any visible resident does.

A walkway accepts the new footfall. A panel warms slightly. Filaments overhead adjust their tension. Grasses dim. A broad leaf turns. The stranger may think these are small accidents until they happen in sequence. Then the politeness becomes undeniable. The place is not rejecting the newcomer. It is measuring whether the newcomer can move gently enough to be part of its quiet.

The residents usually do not appear immediately. They observe through indirect means: a glider’s path, the response of moss, the behavior of condensation, the turning of leaves. If the stranger is hurried, loud, or grasping, the basin becomes less responsive. Walkways remain safe but feel longer. Pools reflect only ordinary shapes. Towers seem closed. If the stranger slows, breathes, and refrains from taking too much, the place begins to open. A channel may guide water toward them. A flower may open near their hand. A seam in the ground may glow faintly at the edge of sight, not as an invitation to enter, but as a sign that they are being tolerated.

When a resident finally appears, it is often not dramatic. A figure may already be standing nearby, as though it has been there for some time. It may not speak. It may simply offer proximity. Its presence feels warm, not emotionally demanding. It does not ask for a name. It asks, in effect: Can you be quiet enough not to damage what is becoming?

Those who remain long enough begin to change.

Their hearing adjusts until a falling drop becomes an event. Their eyes learn to read slow reflections. Their skin becomes more aware of humidity gradients. Their desires simplify. They stop expecting crowds. They begin to understand emptiness as hospitality. Some visitors eventually become tenders of small things: a corner of moss, a stairway, a single pool, a line of grass that dims. The basin does not convert them. It gives them a shape they can grow into.

Those who cannot bear the quiet leave. No one pursues them. The city does not punish. It merely remains itself, patient and warm, holding its angles and its intervals.

What lives there, then, is many-layered.

There are the residents who move through the basin in few and deliberate bodies. There are the plants that share its surfaces and sometimes its mind. There are the underliving films, mats, and lattices that maintain the hidden strata. There are the rare animals whose scarcity makes each movement significant. There are the gliders, the pools, the filaments, and the surfaces that remember. There are the spaces prepared for presences not yet arrived. And beneath or through all of these, there is the basin itself: young, warm, damp, angular, urban, watchful.

They live in a delicate arrangement.

The residents do not own the city. They are its senses, its gardeners, its listeners. The plants do not overwhelm the architecture. They negotiate with it. The microbes do not consume the infrastructure. They sustain it. The animals do not multiply beyond their place. They remain rare, precise, meaningful. The anomalies do not become threats. They become signs that reality here has more causes than ordinary places allow.

In the dim warm light, when moisture returns and the towers grow more present, the inhabitants begin again their quiet work. A seam opens somewhere. A leaf turns. A pool remembers. A drip falls in a measured rhythm. A glider crosses the upper air. A resident stands perfectly still beside a newly fractured shelf of rock, feeling the land decide what it wants to become.

This is who lives there: those who know how to wait. Those who understand that emptiness is not absence but capacity. Those who keep the young angles from becoming wounds. Those who let erosion whisper instead of shout. Those who make room for water, for roots, for memory, for whatever may arrive next.

And if one drop falls in all that quiet, they are the ones who notice.
