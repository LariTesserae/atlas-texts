If you ask who lives there, you must first surrender the expectation of faces.

No one lives there in the ordinary animal sense. No creature pads through the dust. No wings disturb the amber twilight. No eyes catch the weak light from doorways. No throat makes a sound that is meant as a signal to another throat. There are no nests, no burrows, no tracks, no droppings, no bones. There is no hunger moving through the terraces, no fear, no mating call, no small quick heartbeat hidden in the walls.

And yet the hollow is not dead.

It lives in a mineral key. It lives through heat, chemistry, pressure, crystal, wax, sulfur, salt, glassy growth, and slow architectural adjustment. Its life is not a single body but a spread-out fact: a basin-wide metabolism of warm air, chemical vapor, resonant stone, and dry vegetation. What lives there is not a citizen in the sense of a person walking a street. What lives there is the street itself as a flue, the plaza as a drum, the tower as an ear, the garden as a machine, the machine as a garden, and the whole enclosed hollow as a vast, patient, self-tuning instrument.

The first inhabitants, if one must give them names, are the plants.

They are not plants in any familiar green and watery sense. They do not thirst. They do not soften into rot. They do not wilt as ordinary leaves wilt. Their bodies are built from wax, fiber, sulfur compounds, silica, metallic threads, heat-stable oils, and solvent-like fluids. Their lives are governed not by rain but by temperature gradients, vapor flow, mineral tension, and the chemical moods of heated stone. They occupy the city as monks might occupy a monastery, except that they are also the monastery’s repairers, its bell-ringers, its gardeners, its archives, and eventually its dust.

On the lower terraces live the sulfur-ferns.

They are among the most visible residents. Their fronds are laminated in brittle yellows and deep blacks, sometimes edged with a fine powder that looks like crushed pigment. They do not turn toward light, because light here is weak and uncertain; they turn toward warmth. When a floor vent exhales, their fronds lift and spread in slow, dry gestures. When the heat shifts, they fold or lean, making the terraces seem to breathe with a vegetable patience. Inside their veins move thin internal fluids, oily and solvent-like, never water. If a frond is disturbed, if it brushes stone or cracks under thermal stress, it gives off the sharp mineral smell of struck matches.

The sulfur-ferns are not merely decorations. They are heat-readers. Their arrangement records the long-term behavior of the basin. Where warmth rises steadily, they grow broad and open. Where heat pulses irregularly, they grow twisted, their fronds angled into tight fans. Where a vent has gone quiet, they become brittle and pale, eventually shedding themselves into yellow-black dust that gathers against the stair shoulders. They are among the basin’s oldest visible lives, and their frond-shadows are part of the dim patterned world below the towers.

In shaded corners, along terrace lips and inside rooms whose ceilings have partly fallen, lives the wax-thread moss.

It is a low, dense, copper-green carpet, but “moss” is only a convenience. It has no soft wetness. It is made of fine mineralized threads that exude tiny beads of heat-stable wax. Those beads catch the faint luminescence of salt crusts and sulfur channels, making the moss glow subtly, not with its own light but with borrowed and reflected glow. It creeps over stone with extreme slowness. It does not root deeply. Instead, it bonds to surfaces through chemical softening, filling microscopic pores, making hard stone slightly yielding under pressure. Where it has lived long enough, the terrace edges become smoother, the stairs more shoulder-like, the walls more settled.

Wax-thread moss is the hollow’s memory in one sense. It thickens where chemical vapor pools. It preserves the outline of old use. It coats cracked lintels and corroded bronze pins, not protecting them in a sentimental way, but incorporating them into the ongoing surface. When heat travels across a terraced wall, the moss makes a soft abrasive whisper, a sound like extremely fine sand moving over baked clay. That whisper is one of the basin’s intimate voices. It is not the voice of a creature speaking. It is the voice of living mineral tissue adjusting to temperature.

Along the dry canals stand the glass canes.

They are perhaps the most striking of the hollow’s inhabitants. Tall, hollow, jointed, smoky-colored, they resemble plants that have become pipes, or pipes that have decided to grow. Their bodies are silica-rich, almost glassy, and they rise from fitted canal beds where hot mineral vapor moves beneath the surface. They do not drink liquid. They draw vapor. Their tissues exchange gases and mineral compounds through microscopic channels in their walls. At their joints, they release fine crystalline waste, pale deposits that build up ring by ring. Each ring is a record of a vapor season, a thermal event, a cycle of exhalation from the deep floor.

The glass canes are the basin’s musicians.

When air moves through the dry canals, the canes sound. Some produce a low continuous tone. Some tick as they expand. Some ring faintly when spore-dust strikes their hollow bodies. Some sway in dry squalls and create a thin, brittle percussion. Their pitch changes with temperature, pressure, and the amount of crystal accretion at their joints. A cane that has grown for many cycles becomes an archive of airflow. Its segments are not merely body parts; they are accumulated history. If one could read them, one would find in them the record of every major thermal pulse the basin has undergone.

They also shape the city. As they grow, they narrow canals, redirect drafts, alter resonances. A cane that leans into an aperture can turn a silent vent into a whistling one. A cluster of canes that thickens near a plaza can change the plaza from a quiet drum into a resonating chamber. The glass canes are therefore not only inhabitants. They are engineers. They are the living organ pipes of an urban instrument that no hand now plays directly.

In the open plazas live the bell-pods.

They are squat, ribbed, dry-bodied plants with membranous fruits hanging from curved supports. They are less graceful than the sulfur-ferns and less solemn than the glass canes. They have a tense, waiting quality. Their life is governed by thresholds. They accumulate heat and chemical tension until their fruit membranes reach a precise tightness. Then, at the right temperature, the membranes split.

The sound is sudden and clear: a ringing crack.

It is not rot that opens them, nor softness, nor age in the ordinary sense. It is tension. Their ripening is mechanical and musical. When one bell-pod splits, others nearby may follow if the thermal condition is right. In this way, a plaza can become a field of sharp bright percussions, each crack releasing a cloud of spore-dust into the rising heat. The spores are dry, fine, golden in the weak light. They drift through the streets in bands, settle into cracks, collect against vent covers, lodge in the hollows of broken ceramic, and begin the slow process of new growth wherever chemistry and temperature allow.

Bell-pods are the clocks of the hollow. They mark not hours but thermal states. Their cracking is a signal that the basin has entered a particular condition. The city hears them in the only way it can: by resonance. Their sound moves through the plazas, enters stairways, vibrates against vent covers, and joins the larger chord of the basin. They are reproductive events and acoustic events at once. Their children are dust and sound.

Beneath the terrace gravel live the ember-bulbs.

They are less visible than the others, and for that reason perhaps more essential. They are rounded mineralized growths, partly buried, their cores holding a faint red warmth. They are not fire. They do not consume in the manner of flame. They store heat. They take in thermal energy during periods of strong venting and release it slowly when the upper air cools. Around them, other growths gather in dense clusters. Sulfur-ferns lean toward them. Wax-thread moss thickens nearby. Glassy root-analogues spread through the gravel to touch their warm surfaces.

Ember-bulbs are the hearths of the hollow, but hearths without cooks, without hands, without smoke. They create local stability. When the basin’s upper air cools and the stone contracts with small sharp ticks, the ember-bulbs remain warm. They keep certain terraces chemically active. They allow slow growth to continue where otherwise the system would become too still. They are the hidden hearts of the place, if “heart” can mean a node of retained warmth rather than a pumping organ.

There are other growths too, less easily named.

Black copper-threaded forms climb retaining walls, drawing metallic traces from the stone and leaving faint shining lines where they have lived. Pale sulfur crusts bloom in channels where gases are rich, brittle as crystal flowers, alive in the sense that they grow, fracture, heal, and grow again. Fibrous mats of heat-resistant tissue fill blocked doorways, turning rooms into sealed chemical gardens. In some courtyards, a single standing stone has become the center of a whole ecology of dry growth: moss at its base, sulfur-ferns leaning toward its sun-warmed mineral mass, glassy filaments climbing its side, spore-dust collected in its shadow. These are not individuals in a simple sense. They are assemblies. They are lives made of lives made of chemical conditions.

But the plants are not the only residents.

The basin’s breath also lives there.

Not as an animal breath, not as lungs, but as a moving thermal body. The hollow inhales and exhales through its fissures, vents, canals, towers, and shafts. Deep heat pulses from below. Air warms, thins, rises, carries dust and spores upward, deposits salt on terrace edges, cools near the upper crust, sinks along the walls, and returns toward the lower vents. This circulation is slow, serious, and regular enough to form the hidden rhythm of the place. It is the current through which all other life is arranged.

The breath of the basin feeds the glass canes. It lifts the spores of the bell-pods. It carries sulfur vapor to the moss. It moves dust into shapes that polish stone. It makes the towers useful. It turns dry canals into rivers of pressure and temperature. It is not alive as a fern is alive, but it behaves with such consistency, such responsiveness, such architectural intimacy, that it becomes impossible to think of it as mere weather. It is the basin’s circulation. It is the medium in which the hollow’s life happens.

When the deep vents pulse, the whole place changes.

The canals breathe harder. The glass canes rise in pitch. Bell-pods tighten and split. Dust veils lift in the plazas. Sulfur-ferns open their fronds. Wax-thread moss whispers as thermal expansion travels across its surface. The towers amplify the low rush until the basin seems to hum. This is not panic. It is not excitement in an animal sense. It is a state change. The hollow has moved into a higher thermal register, and every resident responds according to its nature.

When the air cools, the basin becomes another kind of alive.

Stone contracts. Joints pop. Metal fittings tick. Ember-bulbs glow more noticeably against the darker gravel. The glass canes quiet. The bell-pods stand open or empty, their spent membranes hanging like cracked bells. Spore-dust settles. Fine salt accumulates in thin lines. The wax-thread moss becomes more visible where luminescent crusts reflect from its beads. The hollow does not sleep, but it lowers its activity into a slower pattern. It becomes a place of minute sounds: tiny hisses of dust on stairways, faint crystalline creaks, the soft settling of heated forms returning toward equilibrium.

Sound, too, lives there.

Not as speech. Not as song intended for listeners. Sound in the hollow is a living signal of state. The place is loud without voices. Its noises are produced by heat moving through architecture, by vegetation responding to heat, and by architecture responding to vegetation. The dry canals rush. The vent-shafts moan. The towers amplify low frequencies. The glass canes ring and tick. The bell-pods crack. The moss whispers. Stone joints pop. Dust avalanches in tiny hisses. Spore release makes a dry rustling like paper tearing far away.

These sounds form cycles. They are not random. A change in pitch means a change in pressure. A new moan in a tower means a vent has opened more widely. A scatter of bell-pod cracks means the plaza has reached a reproductive threshold. A soft abrasive whisper along a terrace means wax-thread moss is adjusting to thermal expansion. The city listens in the only way possible for such a place: its apertures respond to vibration. Simple lever-catches tremble. Vent covers shift. Bronze pins expand in their sockets. Copper strips creak against cracked lintels. Ceramic sockets hold rough bolts that move by fractions when the temperature changes.

The hollow is therefore not merely inhabited by visible growth. It is inhabited by acoustic relationships.

The sound is part of the ecology because the sound is part of the regulation. The city is an instrument, and the instrument is alive because it is continually being played by heat, air, and growth. Its music is not for pleasure. Its music is diagnostic. It is the audible condition of the basin. If the chord changes, something in the system has changed. If a street becomes a flue, if a plaza becomes a drum, if a room becomes an echo chamber, that is because the living architecture has shifted its balance.

The built city itself is also a kind of life.

Not a creature. Not a body with organs in the animal sense. But a living structure, in the way that a coral reef or a vast fungal network or an ancient garden can be called alive. Its streets are not empty corridors; they are channels for air and sporefall. Its plazas are not vacant squares; they are resonance volumes, seedbeds, thermal drums. Its towers are not meaningless ruins; they are heat-catchers, sound-amplifiers, breath-shafts. Its rooms are not simply abandoned chambers; they are chemical gardens, echo rooms, slow-weathering cells, places where mineral vapor pools and growth thickens.

The simple technology still participates in the life of the basin. Dark bronze pins hold stones in positions chosen long ago. Hammered fittings still bear loads. Corroded copper strips reinforce cracked lintels and move almost imperceptibly with heat. Lever-catches on vent covers respond to pressure. Rough bolts set into ceramic sockets shift when the stone expands. These are not complex machines, but they are not dead matter either. They are parts of a low, patient mechanism that continues to function because it was made to function with heat, angle, mass, and time.

The builders are absent.

That absence is one of the hollow’s defining residents, if absence can be said to reside anywhere. There are no current citizens moving through the streets. No hands tend the vents. No eyes inspect the terraces. No voices argue in the plazas. Yet the intention of making remains. It is present in the fitted stone, the organized tiers, the curved streets, the blocked doorways, the ramps, the retaining walls, the tall towers raised to catch something from the air. The place is urban without inhabitants in the ordinary sense. It is a settlement whose population has become its processes.

Perhaps vanished hands made it. Perhaps some dry, mineral, heat-patient intelligence shaped it and then dissolved into it. Perhaps the vegetation learned the shape of building over long ages and now repeats architecture as growth. The hollow does not answer. It only continues. The absence of the builders does not make the place a corpse. It makes the place a question that has stopped needing an answer.

What lives there most completely is neither one plant nor one tower nor one vent.

It is the whole basin as a distributed living system.

The hollow itself can be understood as the largest organism there. Not an organism with a brain, not a being with a face, not a self that could be met on a road. It has no central mind. Its intelligence, if that word can be used at all, is the intelligence of feedback. Heat moves air. Air carries vapor. Vapor feeds growth. Growth alters apertures. Apertures change airflow. Airflow changes sound. Sound vibrates structures. Structures shift heat. The cycle continues.

If the basin thinks, it thinks in thresholds.

If it remembers, it remembers in worn stone, deposited crystal, wax-soaked surfaces, grown cane segments, narrowed vents, polished stairs, and the accumulated angles of long use. If it desires, it desires balance. Not moral balance. Not emotional balance. Thermal and chemical and acoustic balance. The state in which no part overwhelms the others. The state in which heat is distributed enough to sustain growth, growth is distributed enough to regulate heat, sound is distributed enough to reveal pressure changes, and erosion is slow enough to maintain form.

It corrects itself.

If a vent becomes too strong, mineral deposition and plant growth may narrow it. If a canal loses draft, glass canes may lean and shed crystals until the airflow changes. If a wall begins to crack, root-analogues and wax-thread moss may enter the fracture and stabilize it. If a plaza becomes too still, bell-pods may crack and send spore-dust into the air, beginning new growth that will eventually alter the resonance. If a tower becomes blocked, pressure may build until dust squalls clear it. Nothing here is still for long. Nothing is violent. Everything is habitual.

The basin’s life is not fast.

It does not dart. It does not flee. It does not chase. Its motions are the motions of heat shimmer, drifting spores, settling dust, expanding stone, cracking membranes, growing crystal, and slow vegetable leaning. Its generations are not marked by birth and death in the animal sense. They are marked by accretion, fracture, shedding, recrystallization, and reoccupation. When a sulfur-fern becomes brittle and falls apart, it becomes mineral dust. When a glass cane cracks, its fragments become part of the canal bed. When a bell-pod splits, its shell remains and its spores travel. When wax-thread moss thickens, it becomes part of the terrace surface. Nothing is wasted because there is no flesh to waste. Everything returns to the system as chemistry, texture, angle, or resonance.

There is no hunger in the hollow.

That is one of the strangest facts of its life. The lushness is not grazed. No mouth eats the ferns. No predator hunts among the canes. No scavenger cleans the dust from empty rooms. Growth accumulates, hardens, sheds, mineralizes, and is reused by chemical weathering and the slow circulation of the basin. This gives the place its peacefulness, but it is not the peace of kindness. It is the peace of completeness without appetite. There is no comfort of breath, no warmth of fur, no small animal presence in the dark. There is only the deep satisfaction of a system that does not need to consume itself in order to continue.

The hollow is intimate because it is enclosed.

It is a room carved into planetary heat. Its fissured entrance hides it from the broad mineral plain above. Inside, despite the settlement-scale spaces, there is a feeling of containment. The towers, terraces, and walls are close enough to shape every breath of air. The sound cannot escape easily. The heat is held. The spore-dust drifts in visible bands because the volume is bounded. The weak brownish light gives everything the feeling of being preserved in amber. Even the emptiness feels close, not lonely. The vast volumes between objects are not wasted. They are necessary. They are resonance chambers. They are drift corridors. They are heat-exchange spaces. They are part of the body of the place.

It is also alien.

There is no softness here in the ordinary living sense. No wet leaf. No cool shade. No moist soil. No breathing animal. The lushness is made of crackle, shine, wax, sulfur, glass, ceramic, salt, and heat. Beauty exists, but it is not the beauty of a forest or a garden in the familiar world. It is the beauty of a sealed instrument maintaining itself. It is the beauty of a chord played by geology and architecture. It is the beauty of patience made visible.

So who lives there?

No one with a face.

No one who can be greeted.

No one who turns at a footstep.

What lives there are the sulfur-ferns bending toward invisible warmth. What lives there is the wax-thread moss softening stone into memory. What lives there are the glass canes singing in dry canals. What lives there are the bell-pods cracking open their tension into golden spore-clouds. What lives there are the ember-bulbs holding warmth under gravel. What lives there are the thermal tides moving through the streets. What lives there are the spore drifts, the dust squalls, the salt deposits, the resonant towers, the vent-shafts, the empty plazas, the tuned apertures, the bronze pins, the copper strips, the ceramic sockets, and the long slow breathing of the basin.

What lives there is the Dry Chorus.

What lives there is the Amber Lung.

What lives there is the city as an instrument and the instrument as a garden and the garden as a thermal body.

If you insist on a single inhabitant, then the single inhabitant is the hollow itself: a sealed, animal-less, waterless, heat-driven world maintaining its own equilibrium in the dark. It does not call out. It does not look back. It does not ask to be known. It only continues, ringing faintly in its own heat, playing itself over and over in the hidden basin, alive without thirst, loud without voice, empty and full at once.
