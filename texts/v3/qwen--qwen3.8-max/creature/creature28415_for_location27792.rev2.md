The bowl is not inhabited as a room is inhabited. It is played.

What lives there, beyond the resinous towers and the strange air-fauna, is a people who might be called the Vane by anyone hearing their signal tubes from far below, though they would not use that name for themselves. Their own name is something made of pressure and touch: a pattern tapped against the throat, a double pull on a wrist-cord, a brief palm-flat against the salt ground. If it must be translated into speech, it would be close to *The Tied-by-Their-Own-Breath* or *Those Who Keep the Note Unbroken*.

They are few. The bowl does not allow many. At any season, if season means anything in that amber dimness, there may be only a small number of them: perhaps five, perhaps nine, sometimes fewer than three. They live in loose chords rather than families, each chord bound to one or two of the great resin plants and to the particular sounds those plants make when the air is stable. They do not own the bowl. They attend it.

At first glance, one might mistake them for growths or shadows among the plant towers. They are low-bodied and dense, built close to the ground, with flexible ribs and wide, gripping feet. They rarely stand fully upright; when they do, they are not tall, and they seem to be listening with their whole posture rather than looking. Their bodies are not wet, not soft in the lowland sense. Their skin is smooth, poreless, faintly glossy, often dark green-black, mineral-grey, or pale as salt-dust, and it carries a constant film of aromatic wax. They do not sweat. They do not weep. If injured, they do not bleed freely; the wound beads with a dark, oily fluid that thickens and seals quickly, smelling of heated resin.

Their faces are calm and shuttered. The eyes are large, amber-dark, adapted to the thin copper light, and protected by inner membranes that close against dust and spore-haze. Their nostrils are narrow slits that can seal entirely. Their mouths are valve-like, double-lipped, built to keep dry air from stealing too much moisture and to permit careful breathing through thick atmospheres. Many have small acoustic crests of cartilage along the jaw, collar, or shoulders, not large enough to be grotesque but sensitive enough to tremble when the bowl’s pitch changes. Their throats possess expandable sacs or resonant chambers, sometimes visible as faint swellings when they breathe deeply or signal one another across the noise.

They do not drink water. They may not even recognize water as a comfort. For them, thirst is not a craving for liquid but a dulling of heat, a stiffening of the breath, a loss of resonance in the throat. They satisfy it with other chemistry: warm resin vapors drawn through carved tubes, oily pollen cakes chewed slowly, the rich fats of seed-horn pith, the volatile solvents exhaled by certain leaves, and the metabolic heat released from digesting mineral-laden dusts and plant oils. Their bodies are sealed and efficient. What little moisture they produce internally, they keep with severe economy. They excrete dry salts, fine pale flakes that they sometimes scrape from their skin and use to polish tools or mark stones.

Their hands are their most expressive tools. The fingers are long, strong, slightly adhesive from resin oils, with callused pads and careful nails. They can tie complex knots quickly, even in gusts. They can feel minute vibrations through plant trunks, salt crust, or rope. Almost everything they make is tied, lashed, weighted, or folded low. They do not build monuments. They build attachments.

The low shelters near the sheltered side of the bowl are theirs. These are not houses in any permanent sense. They are sleeping knots, storm pockets, temporary shelters weighed down with salt blocks and sealed with resin. Inside them, a Vane does not lie loose. They lie in a harness, tethered to an anchor stone, tools tied to the frame, breath-hood closed, hands resting on cords that lead to signal tubes or whistles. If the wind shifts violently in the night, the shelter may tear or collapse, and the Vane inside will not fight it. They will roll out, hook themselves to a waiting rope, and move to a lower lee. They expect loss. They prepare for rearrangement.

Their technology is primitive only if judged by the standards of wet, stable lands. In the bowl, it is exact. They use obsidian hooks for tapping resin without tearing living tissue, bone awls for piercing hide or membrane, polished climbing hooks for gripping ribbed trunks, sinew cordage twisted so it sings a little when the wind passes through it, resin glues that harden in heat, and racks where offerings or tools are left exposed to the air. Their most important instruments are sound-tools: hand-carved whistles, hollow tubes, small drums, throat resonators, and signal pipes. Some pipes are worn at the throat; others are planted in the ground near plant bases. Some are meant to imitate the notes of the great stems. Others are meant to warn.

Because the bowl is loud, the Vane do not rely on speech as a lowland people would. They have voices, but those voices are used sparingly and often below or beyond ordinary hearing: pulses felt in the chest, low resonant thrums, short infrasonic calls that travel through the salt floor. For close communication, they use gesture, touch, and rhythmic codes. A tap on the wrist means *hold*. A scrape across the shoulder means *change coming*. A palm pressed flat to the ground means *listen before moving*. Between individuals who have lived long together, conversation can happen with almost no visible motion: a shift in breathing, a finger tension on a shared cord, a slight inflation of a throat sac.

Children learn this pressure-language before they learn to walk far. Their first objects are not toys but tuned whistles and tether-cords. A young Vane is taught to place a hand on a plant trunk and distinguish whether the hollow note inside is rising, flattening, or becoming dangerous. They are taught to read the behavior of the air through the animals: how the pale moths drift, how the blind bristled climbers cluster on waxy leaves, how the ribbon-bodied gliders tilt before a thermal collapses. They learn that in the bowl, safety is not a place. It is a timing.

They are gardeners, but not in the sense of tending rows or flowers. They tend sound, vapor, and growth-pattern. They prune dead fronds from the great plants with hooked blades. They clear salt from the shallow channels so gas can move without building dangerous pressure. They insert carved bone pegs into hollow stems to alter pitch. They stretch membranes between stakes to redirect small gusts. They place stones in alignments that look accidental but are actually listening marks, wind-checks, or reminders of where a rope should be tied during a spore burst. When a seed-horn swells and darkens, they gather near it with nets of stretched membrane and tubes sealed with resin, waiting for the moment it releases its cloud.

They harvest without greed. The bowl is rich, but it is small and volatile. They take resin beads in closed cups, not open bowls, because the solvent evaporates too quickly. They collect oily fruits that grow in resinous clusters near the swollen bases. They gather pollen in sealed pouches. They encourage the blind bristled climbers rather than hunting them, smearing resin trails along leaves to guide the creatures into manageable paths. They sometimes take shed strips from ribbon gliders, membranes left torn on plant thorns, and use them for windbreaks or breath-hoods. They rarely kill larger fauna, because killing is noisy, wasteful, and dangerous in a place where a burst of blood or struggle can attract turbulence. They prefer to gather, lure, guide, and wait.

They also eat air, in a sense. During warm exhalations from cracks in the salt floor, they crouch over shallow basins filled not with water but with fine dust and resin powder. They breathe through tubes into that dust, drawing up volatile heat and mineral vapor. The dust acts as a filter, a heater, and a marker. When the powder changes color or scent, they know the bowl’s chemistry has shifted. A basin left too long may crack or hum. A basin that suddenly goes silent is abandoned at once.

When the bowl changes state, the Vane change with it. A thermal column collapses; the air falls inward before rising. The great leaves turn upward, then flatten. A seed-horn bursts. Dust rises in curtains. The hollow trunks drop in pitch. The pale moths scatter into spirals. In that moment, the Vane do not panic. They lower themselves, hook their harnesses to anchor ropes, cover breathing slits, and signal one another through tubes. Their bodies become part of the turbulence. They lean into gusts as if into a moving wall. They move in the brief pauses between pressure waves. They know which cracks in the salt floor will exhale warmth and which will suddenly scream. They know which plant stalks are safe to climb and which will whip like bent bows.

They do not fear height as a lowland climber fears it, but they respect the rule that height is hunted by gusts. They climb the plant towers only when the note is stable, and only with ropes tied to the base. Up there, they can repair torn membranes, cut loose strangling growth, retrieve trapped creatures, or listen for changes coming through the notch. But they do not linger. The upper air is where the ribbon gliders ride and where sudden drafts strike without warning. If a climber’s tether begins to sing too thinly, others below answer with warning tubes until the climber descends.

Their relationship to the great plants is intimate but not sentimental. The plants are shelter, food, instrument, and danger. A Vane may rest against a swollen trunk during a lull, feeling its deep vibration, and then later cut a dangerous tube from it without hesitation. They do not name individual plants as persons. They name them by sounds, by uses, by histories of damage: *the One That Splits on the Third Gust*, *the Horn That Keeps Dark Spores*, *the Broad Rib That Holds the Rope*, *the Plant Whose Note Warns of Ash*. A plant that dies is not mourned as a body but dismantled carefully. Its hollow parts become instruments. Its wax becomes sealant. Its fibrous ribs become stakes. Even in death, it is tuned.

Their shelters are surrounded by evidence of this practice. Racks hold half-finished whistles and polished hooks. Windbreaks snap softly nearby. Small cairns mark pressure-points where the floor has shifted. Cracked drums are left upright not because they still work but because their broken skins make a useful warning sound when the air grows thin. Offerings are placed on flat stones: a bead of hardened resin, a carved bone fragment, a knot tied in red-stained cord. These are not prayers for stillness. Stillness would be death. They are offerings for continuation, for the storm to remain patterned, for the bowl to keep singing without breaking.

The Vane themselves are hard to describe as either gentle or harsh. They are patient. They are exact. They do not waste motion. Their gestures have a ceremonial economy even when they are performing urgent work. Because the air is always crowded with sound, they have developed a bodily quietness. They do not shout. They do not run unless the rhythm allows it. They do not throw things, because loose objects become hazards. They tie. They fold. They brace. They wait. They act.

They are not isolated from one another emotionally, but their affection is expressed through maintenance. One Vane tightens another’s harness. One clears dust from another’s breathing slits. One repairs the cord worn thin by another’s climbing hook. One presses a warmed resin cake into another’s palm after a long exposure. When a member of a chord is injured, the others do not gather in loud concern. They secure the injured one, seal the wound, tie them to a stable anchor, and continue the work of keeping the group tied into the bowl’s rhythm. Tenderness, there, is the act of preventing someone from becoming loose.

Their young are rare and precious. They do not bear children often; the bowl does not favor abundance. Birth, if that word fits, is bound to heat and resin. A fertile chord prepares a hollow plant base, sealing it with warmed pitch and lining it with dust, shed membrane, and fine fibers. The egg is small, parchment-shelled, resin-scented, and is placed inside the living plant where the deep warmth can brood it. The chord tends it by sound as much as by touch, adjusting nearby tubes so the vibration around it remains stable. When the child emerges, it is dry, alert, and already sealed against the air. It is not washed. It is polished with fine salt and pressed briefly to the throat sacs of each adult in the chord so it learns their resonance before it learns their faces.

A child’s first lesson is not to walk but to be tied. It wears a soft harness almost from the moment it can be carried. It is attached to an adult, to a shelter frame, to a plant base, always within reach but never free to wander into open gusts. It learns the feel of pressure changes before it learns names for them. By the time it is old enough to carry a tool, it can already recognize the warning hum of a collapsing thermal and the dry rattle that precedes a spore burst. Adolescence, if one can call it that, is marked by being given a personal whistle and a plant to tend. The young Vane must keep that plant in good voice through several changes of state. If the plant survives and sings well, the youth is accepted into full chord-work.

Old age among the Vane is not a decline into helplessness but a movement toward listening. Elders become interpreters of pattern. They sit near the salt channels with bare feet and palms against the ground, reading vibrations. They adjust the placement of shelters by small signs. They decide when a plant must be pruned, when a shelter should be abandoned, when the notch is about to inhale too sharply. They do not command. They signal. Their authority is the authority of long attention.

Death is dry. When a Vane dies, the chord does not bury them in deep earth, because the floor is salt, dust, and moving gas. Instead, the body is sealed with resin, tied upright in a sheltered hollow, or lashed to a dead plant frame where the wind can finish what age began. Over time, the body desiccates, becomes light, becomes bone and fiber. Bones may be reclaimed for tools or shelter frames, but only after a period of respectful use, when the dead one’s note has had time to pass into the bowl. Some of their oldest whistles are made from ancestor bone, not as relics of worship but as instruments that already know the chord.

They have little interest in the world beyond the notch, though they are aware of it. The long drop into haze is not spoken of casually. It is the place where broken notes go, where careless things fall, where the wind sometimes carries offerings out of the bowl and into the unknown. They may stand near the broken rim during rare stable hours and play low tubes toward the haze, not to call anything back but to announce that the bowl remains in tune. If outsiders ever climb up through the notch, the Vane regard them with wary attention. Outsiders are usually too heavy, too wet, too loud in the wrong ways. They bring thirst, clumsiness, and objects that come untied. The Vane will not offer water, because they do not have it and do not trust its effects. They may offer resin vapor, a bitter pollen cake, and a harness. If the outsider cannot learn to be tied down, they are guided back toward the drop before they cause damage.

There are stories among the Vane, but they are not told as long spoken tales. They are stored in knot-patterns, in tuned pipes, in the arrangement of cairns, and in the gestures repeated during storms. Their central story is not a myth of origin so much as a pattern of correction: once, the bowl almost went silent. A crack sealed. A plant choked. The dust settled too evenly. The wind lost its edges. The animals stopped moving. The Vane who were then alive had to reopen a channel, cut a dead horn loose, and sound the oldest tubes until the turbulence returned. The story is performed, not narrated. It ends not with peace, but with noise restored.

They do not worship silence. Silence is the enemy. They do not worship stillness. Stillness is rot, blockage, death. They do not worship water. Water, in the few fragments they imagine of it, is a dissolving force, a thing that would soften their bodies, unseal their skin, and erase the careful dryness that lets them exist. If they have a sacred idea, it is *continuity under pressure*. If they have a virtue, it is *remaining shaped while being moved*. If they have a sin, it is carelessness with a knot.

The animals of the bowl live around them and through them. The pale hand-sized moths with gas-bladders are indicators: when they drift low and jerk in short spirals, a gust is coming through the notch; when they rise in lazy chains, the upper air is temporarily stable. The blind bristled climbers are both companions and livestock, tended for resin, wax, and the cleaning they perform on leaves. The ribbon-bodied gliders are watched with something like awe, because their movements reveal the invisible architecture of updrafts. The Vane sometimes tie long membrane ribbons to their shelters in imitation of them, not as decoration but as wind-readers. When the ribbons twist one way, work continues. When they flatten, everyone prepares to brace.

In this way, the Vane are not separate from the garden. They are its most deliberate organ. The plants make sound, resin, food, and structure. The animals carry pollen, consume residue, reveal air-currents, and add to the noise. The wind supplies pressure, heat, and constant change. The Vane listen, repair, harvest, tie, tune, and move between all these things. Without them, the bowl might still exist, but it would be more likely to fall into dangerous silence or destructive strain. With them, its turbulence is maintained as pattern.

If you stood inside the bowl and saw one of them clearly for the first time, you would probably notice the hands before the face. They would be moving steadily, perhaps adjusting a cord, perhaps pressing a small pipe to the salt. Their body would be low, braced, shining faintly with wax. Their breath would be slow through the dust. Their eyes would watch not you, but the air around you. They would not ask you to be still. They would ask, by gesture, whether you could hear the change coming.

The thing that lives there, then, is not only a species. It is a distributed life made of plant towers, drifting fauna, mineral floor, hot breath from cracks, and the small careful people who move among all of it. But if one must choose the dweller most proper to the bowl, choose the Vane. They are the bowl’s hands, ears, and memory. They live there as a note lives in an instrument: not by remaining untouched, but by holding shape while the air moves through them.
