The High Lattice Marsh is not inhabited in the way a city, a field, or even an ordinary forest is inhabited. It is inhabited the way a body is inhabited: by countless small specialists, by symbionts, by cleaners and growers and quiet regulators, each of which performs a task so localized that no single creature can claim the whole. To live there is to be one function among many.

The first residents are not people.

They are the living materials of the place itself: the root lattices, the mosses, the liverworts, the fungal threads, the waxy leaf-skins, the resin ducts, the breathing membranes, the slow green tissues that have been trained into floors, walls, bridges, cisterns, valves, and lamps. These are the true citizens of the marsh. They drink, thicken, scar, heal, lean away from dryness, close around drafts, open toward humidity, and remember injury in the grain of their growth. The constructed landscape is not dead matter shaped once and left alone. It is a population of plants and fungi arranged into long cooperation. In some places, that cooperation is so intimate that it becomes difficult to say where one organism ends and another begins. A terrace floor may be held together by five kinds of root, three kinds of moss, a film of algae, and a web of pale fungus that tightens when water runs across it. A wall may breathe through pores governed by swelling cells. A bridge may flex according to the moisture in its braided cane, becoming stiffer in mist, softer in dry air. The marsh is alive not merely as habitat, but as a vast, patient artifact of many metabolisms.

Within this living structure, animals exist in great numbers, but they are dispersed like rare words through a very long text.

The pools are kept clean by thread eels, translucent and thin as rush stems, whose bodies reveal the small dark beat of their hearts when light catches them correctly. They move through capillary channels and shallow basin-floors, eating slime, dead tissue, and the first bloom of rot. Children of the marsh are taught not to catch them. To harm a thread eel is considered both cruelty and bad sense, because a pool without cleaners soon turns cloudy and begins to smell of sour decay.

On the surface of the black pools rest film skaters: flat, broad-bodied insects whose legs spread their weight so delicately that they do not break the water’s skin. They graze on microbial film, and when something startles them they scatter in sudden silver streaks, making a pool seem to blink. Their movements are one of the ways people read water quality. If the skaters are calm, the pool is stable. If they gather at the edges and refuse to cross, something below has begun to change.

Leaf-funnels and wide-mouthed basins shelter cistern frogs, small amphibians with damp, bark-patterned skin and eyes the color of wet ash. They sit inside the funnels during mist-tides, throat skins pulsing faintly, and their calls are not loud croaks but low vibrations that pass through the leaf-walls and into the feet. The Marsh-born say that a well-tended water garden should be heard more with the legs than with the ears. These frogs help keep insect larvae in balance. Their skins also secrete a mild antiseptic substance that keeps stored rainwater sweet, so their presence is encouraged by leaving rough perches and shaded overhangs.

The wetter terraces support lichen gliders, slow creatures with long limbs and membranes stretched between their flanks, patterned exactly like the pale crusts that grow on old bark. They are not birds, nor bats, nor insects, though they partake of the logic of all three. They feed on moss tips, fruiting caps, and the soft young shoots of aerial vines, moving from terrace to terrace in silent, shallow arcs. To watch one glide is to see a leaf decide, halfway through falling, that it would rather become weather. Their droppings are valuable because they carry seeds and nutrients across gaps, so the Marsh-born protect their roosting hollows and sometimes guide them toward under-fertilized walls with trays of fragrant bait-fruit.

Inside the thicker walls live root-browsers: blind, warm-bodied animals with moist hides and rasping tongues. They are slow, rounded, and furred in places with accidental moss. They feed on excess fungal threads, preventing mycelial mats from choking water channels and air valves. Their grazing is essential. Without them, some inner passages would become white with fuzz and begin to heat from too much decomposition. A root-browser moving through a wall makes almost no sound, only a faint dragging pressure that can be felt if one leans against the bark. The Marsh-born consider them signs of structural health. If a root-browser abandons a wall, the wall is already ill.

At certain hours, especially after a long stillness, the air fills with pale spore-moths. They rise from hidden nurseries like steam given wings. They are weak fliers but numerous beyond counting, and they pollinate the high flowers that open only in dim, humid air. Their wings shed a fine powder that glows faintly blue when it settles on damp surfaces. For a short time, every handrail, every leaf-edge, every bridge-cable seems touched by cold starlight. Then the moths fade, the spores sink, and the marsh returns to its green-gray hush.

Predators are present, but they do not dominate. They are too few, too patient, and too carefully fitted into the rest.

The hush heron is one of the most familiar. It is not truly a heron, but it has earned the name through its stillness. Its legs are long and spread wide to distribute weight across soft floors. Its body is covered not in ordinary feathers but in soft, felt-like filaments that absorb sound. It can stand for hours at the edge of a pool, head lowered, motionless except for a slow blink. When it strikes, it does not splash. Its bill enters the water with a small, precise click, and then it lifts with a film skater, a larva, or a young frog. It swallows without ceremony and often remains exactly where it was. The Marsh-born do not drive it away. A hush heron culls weakness and keeps the smaller populations from exhausting their food. Its presence is considered a sign that a pool has enough life to support a predator.

There are also fold-leaves, creatures so well disguised as wet leaf litter that they seem to be part of the floor until they unfold. Some are harmless filterers, spreading delicate nets across slow currents to catch floating spores. Others are ambush predators, snapping shut around small insects or careless larvae. The Marsh-born learn early where not to place a bare hand, not because fold-leaves are deadly, but because their bite is painful and their saliva can cause a slow, itching numbness. Children call them “patient mouths” and treat them with wary respect.

Among the most useful animals are not wild at all, but semi-domesticated.

Wax beetles are kept in shallow troughs along walkways. These beetles secrete a water-resistant coating as they move, and the Marsh-born guide them over new stems, handholds, and bridge surfaces when waterproofing is needed. The beetles do not understand their purpose, but they follow scent trails and warmth gradients laid out by their keepers. A well-waxed walkway shines faintly in the dimness and remains safe under bare feet for many seasons.

Boring grubs are used more carefully. Certain larvae can tunnel through dense pith and old heartwood, leaving smooth channels behind them. The Marsh-born confine them with resin barriers and clay plugs, allowing them to hollow out air shafts, drainage veins, or small root-conduits. When the work is done, the grubs are either moved to another task or harvested for fat, protein, and lamp oil. Nothing is wasted. Even their castings are pressed into fertile cakes and fed to hungry buttresses.

Thorn mites are kept as living pest control. They are tiny, fast, and aggressive toward rot-mites, dry-lice, and bark-borers. A keeper may carry them in a stoppered seedpod, releasing them into an infected wall with a whispered warning that is more habit than superstition. If the thorn mites thrive, the infection retreats. If they fail, the keeper knows the trouble is deeper than insects and must be answered with pruning, resin, or the harder medicine of fire-free fumigation using bitter fungal smoke.

But the beings who most clearly shape the High Lattice Marsh, who give its vastness something like intention, are the people called the Marsh-born.

They do not have one name for themselves that travels easily into other tongues. In their own usage, they are something close to the Wet-handed, or those who keep the green joints from drying. Outsiders sometimes call them Lattice-kin, and the name is not wrong, because they are kin to the marsh less by blood alone than by long labor, grafting, and habit.

They are not numerous. The marsh could not support crowds without becoming wounded, and their whole way of life depends on not exceeding what the structure can heal. Their settlements are therefore sparse, scattered across the lattice like knots in a net. A household may occupy a single hollow trunk, a cluster of rooms grown inside a buttress, or a set of terraces connected by short bridges. Another household may be visible only as a faint lantern-glow through fog, a day’s careful travel away.

Their bodies have been edited by altitude, humidity, and the need for silence.

They are usually not tall. Height is inconvenient beneath low vaults and across windless spans where balance matters more than reach. They are compact and long-armed, with broad hands and fingers that can grip wet cane without cramping. Their feet are wide, with toes that spread and press independently, the soles textured like leaf veins. This gives them sureness on slick roots and springy moss. Their skin is cool, matte, and often green-gray in tone, not because they are ill but because they live in a world of filtered light and because their skin hosts harmless, beneficial growths: pale lichens, moss-filaments, and fungal patches that help repel infection and retain moisture. They do not scrub these marks away. To be completely bare-skinned is considered strange, almost lonely.

Their eyes are large and adapted to dimness, with secondary inner lids that protect them from mist and falling debris. They do not see sharply at great distance, but they are exceptionally good at reading slight changes in texture, movement, and bioluminescent signal. Their hearing is tuned to low vibrations. They listen as much through the floor as through the air, feeling the creak of a bridge, the drip pattern in a cistern, the tremor of a creature moving along a root-wall. Their spoken voices are soft, breathy, and often below the threshold of an unaccustomed listener. Much of their language is signed, tapped, or touched.

A Marsh-born child learns stillness before elegance. Before being taught to graft, climb, or read the water channels, a child learns not to startle the marsh. They learn to place a foot so the moss does not tear. They learn to touch a wall without slapping it. They learn that a dropped tool is not merely a mistake but a noise that may ripple through nearby life. Play exists, but it is often quiet: balancing games, shadow-puppetry with lantern light, knot-tying, mimicry of frog calls, and the careful construction of tiny living huts from shed bark and moss.

Their homes are not built so much as invited.

A family may spend generations coaxing a trunk to form a room. Young stems are guided, not forced. Branches are persuaded to close into arches. Interior wood is hollowed slowly, over years, by selected grubs, then smoothed and hardened with resin. The result is a living chamber that continues to breathe. Its walls may sweat clean water in dry spells. Its floor may be a layered mat of moss and shredded bark that absorbs footsteps. A window may be no more than a slit covered by translucent leaf-skin, letting in a greenish glow. Furniture is grown in place: seats formed from guided knots, shelves from shelf-fungi, cradles from braided tendrils that can be loosened as a child grows.

A well-made home smells of wet bark, warm moss, and faint fruit. It should not smell stagnant. If a room smells sour, the family knows its air channels are blocked. If it smells too dry, they know the wall has cracked or the outer louver has failed. The house is a diagnostic instrument. Living in it means being constantly read by it.

Their clothing is practical and often alive in some small way. They wear close wraps of felted moss, leaf-leather, and woven fiber, not only for warmth but to protect skin from abrasion and to carry tools. Some cloaks are seeded with slow-growing moss that helps the wearer blend into the lattice and retain humidity. Belts may be made of braided vine that tightens slightly when wet, keeping pouches secure. Ornament is subtle: beads of polished seed, pins of thorn, small living knots woven into hair or sleeves. Beauty is not displayed through brightness or sharpness, but through health, neatness, and the evidence of care. A person wearing a garment whose living seams are thriving is considered well-made. A person whose clothes are dying through neglect is looked upon with concern.

Their food comes from cultivation, not conquest.

They tend fruiting walls, fungal combs, algae films, pith gardens, and shallow aquacultures. They harvest tender shoots, seed clusters, edible mosses, and the custard-flesh of certain high fruits that ripen without strong sun. They gather eggs from marsh insects, take larvae in controlled numbers, and occasionally eat thread eels or small pool creatures when abundance allows. They do not slaughter loudly. Blood is not absent from their world, but it is not made theatrical. It is handled with gratitude and immediate use.

Cooking, where it occurs, is gentle. They use warm compost chambers, fermentation pits, sealed leaf-pouches heated by microbial decay, and steaming baskets placed above slow vents where the lattice exhales humid heat. Open flame is rare and feared. Fire in a living structure is not a tool but a catastrophe. When flame is used at all, it is in tiny, contained clay lamps for special purposes, and even then it is surrounded by wet moss and watched by several people at once.

Their primary technologies are grafting, tying, plugging, guiding, feeding, and listening.

A Marsh-born keeper may carry a small roll of tools: bone knives, wooden scrapers, thorn pins, resin sticks, bark cord, clay plugs, a needle made from a fish-bone or insect spine, and a polished stone used to smooth sap over wounds in living wood. These tools are not crude because they are simple. They are refined by centuries of use. A grafting knife may be passed down through many generations, its handle worn to the exact curve of a family’s grip. A clay valve may be shaped so precisely that it has to be replaced only once in a lifetime.

Their science is attention. They can tell by touch whether a root is drinking well. They can smell the difference between healthy fungal growth and early rot. They can tap a wall and know whether the hollow inside is dry, damp, or failing. They read the behavior of animals as indicators: if root-browsers gather near a certain shaft, the fungal balance there is shifting; if wax beetles refuse a walkway, the surface chemistry has changed; if cistern frogs leave their funnels, the water is becoming bitter. No single sign is trusted alone. Knowledge in the marsh is cumulative, comparative, and slow.

Their social order is not based on crowding or command, but on responsibility.

A person is known by what they keep. A household may be entrusted with a bridge, a cistern, a set of air louvers, a fruiting wall, a nursery hollow, or a stretch of root-cliff prone to drying. This keeping is not ownership in the hard sense. One does not sell a bridge. One does not hoard a cistern. One is bound to it by duty, and that duty is also identity. To introduce oneself is often to name the places one tends: I keep the third drip-channel under the split buttress. I open the eastern leaf-gates when the mist thickens. I listen to the long bridge after storms.

Because the marsh is vast and its people dispersed, communication is one of their most sacred arts.

Near at hand, they use hand signs and facial expression. At greater distances, they tap coded rhythms into buttress roots, sending slow messages through the living lattice. Some structures carry these vibrations remarkably far, so that a warning from one terrace can arrive in another as a faint pulse in the floor. They also use trained insects and scent threads. Certain moths can be guided by smell to familiar roosts, carrying tiny capsules of resin, spore-markers, or written strips of dried leaf. In darker passages, they communicate with lantern moss: a touch here, a chemical feed there, a pattern of blue-green brightening that can be read across a hollow.

Their long-distance language is not rapid. It is not meant to be. Messages arrive like weather. A family may receive a notice from distant kin as a low drumming in their floor during the evening quiet, then spend the night decoding the rhythm by candle-fungus light. Urgency exists, but it is shaped by distance and difficulty. No one expects an answer before the marsh allows it.

Their conflicts are correspondingly restrained.

They do fight, in their way. Disputes arise over water rights, failed grafts, neglected duties, broken promises, or the use of shared passages. But open violence is dangerous in a world made of living tissue. A struggle can tear a membrane, break a valve, bruise a support root, or start a panic among animals. Therefore their quarrels are often mediated by elders, by ritual exchange, or by labor tests. Two households in dispute may each be given a difficult cutting to tend. Whichever graft thrives after a season is understood to have demonstrated better care. This is not seen as magical judgment. It is practical: the marsh rewards attention, and attention is the virtue they value most.

The worst punishment is not death, but unknitting.

A person who repeatedly harms the lattice—who wastes water, burns living tissue, overharvests, or abandons a duty without warning—may be cut off from their responsibilities. They lose their place in the web of keeping. They become untethered. In a world where identity is relational, this is a severe exile. Some such people leave the marsh entirely and try to survive in drier, harsher regions beyond the mist. Others wander from knot to knot, asking for small tasks until they can be trusted again. The Marsh-born do not love cruelty, but they are not naive. They know that a single careless act can spread through a living structure like rot.

Their reverence is directed not toward distant gods, but toward continuity.

They speak of the Long Tending. It is not a doctrine so much as a recognition: every living thing in the marsh has been shaped by previous hands, and every present act will shape what comes after. They do not pray for victory. They pray, if they pray, for balance. They make offerings of nutrient paste to junctions that have been strained. They sing low vibration songs to new grafts, not because sound alone can make them take, but because singing slows the keeper’s breath and sharpens patience. They mark ancestors not with statues but with living scars. A name may be trained into bark as a pattern of cuts and regrowth, readable by touch long after the person has died.

Death among them is intimate and un-wasteful.

When a person is near the end, they are brought to a warm hollow prepared by their household. The dying choose, when they can, which of their remains may be used. Bones may become tool handles, pegs, or small structural pins, but only with consent given beforehand. Flesh is composted. Breath is caught, symbolically, in a sealed seedpod placed among root fibers. After death, the body is laid in a root-cradle and allowed to feed the lattice. In some families, a cutting from the home-tree is grafted over the place where the body rests, so that the house literally grows from the dead. Children are taught not to fear this. To become part of the structure one tended is considered the final completion of duty.

Their love of the marsh is not sentimental.

They know it can kill. A dried-out membrane can collapse. A blocked cistern can breed poison. A storm can snap a bridge and send whole walkways falling into cloud. A rot outbreak can consume a household’s life work in a season. They have terms for disasters that outsiders might not expect: dry-sickness, hollow-fire, root-blindness, the white choking, the long sag. Their courage is quiet. It consists of noticing small failures before they become large ones.

The rarest beings in the High Lattice Marsh are the vast ones.

In the widest hollows, where clouds form indoors and distances blur into green-gray nothing, there are drifters so large that they are often mistaken for weather. The Marsh-born call them cloud-sifters, though this name may be too simple. They are immense, slow, translucent colonies or organisms—perhaps both—trailing veils and filaments through the air. They move without sound, filtering spores, moisture, and microscopic life. Their bodies are not solid enough to be called flesh in the ordinary sense. They seem made of thickened mist and old silk. When one passes through a hollow, the light changes. Droplets form differently on nearby leaves. Small creatures go still.

No one hunts them. No one claims to understand them. They may be remnants of the original lattice, or its accidental children, or wild relatives of the cultivated membranes above. They appear rarely, sometimes separated by many years, and their arrival is treated as a season in itself. Children born during a cloud-sifter’s passing are said to have long patience, though this may only be because their parents learn to speak more softly while it is near.

There are also beings that may not be alive at all, but which the Marsh-born speak of as if they are.

Certain deep root-junctions creak in patterns that do not match wind or weight. Certain hollows answer taps with delays too regular to be accident. Certain ancient trunks seem to close their inner chambers only when particular people approach. The Marsh-born do not insist that these are minds. They say instead that old growth develops habit, and habit can resemble intention. They treat such places with courtesy. They ask before pruning. They wait before entering. If this is superstition, it is a superstition indistinguishable from good manners.

The overall population of the marsh is not easy to estimate because it is not gathered. It is spread through layers, intervals, and hidden niches. A single terrace may seem deserted for days, then reveal itself at mist-tide as a meeting place for dozens of keepers, each arriving from a different direction to open valves, check pools, and exchange news by sign. A wall that looks empty may house root-browsers, larvae, fungi, nesting frogs, and a family’s sleeping alcove all at once. A bridge may be both a route and a farm, its cables seeded with edible moss, its underside sheltering hanging nests of useful insects.

Nothing there lives alone.

This is true ecologically and spiritually. A pool is not only water; it is eels, frogs, skaters, mineral balance, leaf-shadow, and the keeper who clears its edge. A bridge is not only cane; it is tension, wax, graft scars, beetle trails, footfall memory, and the household that sings it calm after high winds. Even solitude in the marsh is relational. A person alone on a distant terrace is still surrounded by the consequences of care: channels dripping, moss glowing faintly under a hand, roots shifting in their slow sleep.

Outsiders sometimes find the place oppressive because it refuses to be mastered. There is no stone to strike, no open field to burn, no clear horizon to conquer. Everything is alive, absorbent, and already in use. A stranger may shout and hear the shout die instantly in wet fiber. They may strike a wall and feel the blow returned as a dull, patient shudder rather than an echo. They may search for rulers, armies, or monuments and find none. They may assume the marsh is empty because it does not assemble itself into spectacle.

But to those born into it, the High Lattice Marsh is dense with presence.

It is full of small faces remembered in bark patterns, of animal habits known by heart, of water voices distinguished by pitch, of bridges that lean differently in grief and joy. It is full of the Wet-handed moving silently through fog, their feet spreading on wet stems, their tools wrapped in living cloth, their attention divided among ten thousand tiny duties. It is full of gliders crossing pale air, of frogs humming into leaf-funnels, of root-browsers working in darkness, of lantern moss brightening under a careful palm.

What lives there, finally, is not one species and not one image.

It is a woven ecology of maintenance.

It is the marsh itself, the people who tend it, the creatures that clean it, the fungi that bind it, the predators that thin it, the vast drifters that occasionally pass through it, and the accumulated memory of all the hands that have taught it to remain soft, wet, balanced, and quiet. The inhabitants of the High Lattice Marsh live by restraint. They live by noticing. They live by not breaking more than they can heal. They live by becoming less sharp, less loud, less separate, until they too seem rounded into the structure, like another edge that time and drip have patiently worn smooth.
