What lives there is not easy to separate from what happens there.

The first answer is almost always wrong: one wants to say the place itself is alive, and stop there. But the place is not merely alive; it is populated. Its inhabitants are not independent of its processes, yet they are not nothing. They have shapes, appetites, habits, deaths. They simply do not possess permanence in the way one expects from a living thing.

The smallest and most numerous residents are the ones that look, at first, like surface. They are the filamentous mats sealing new rock, the electric microbes glittering in wet ash, the mineral-separating colonies that line warm fissures and leave copper-green, iron-red, and silver-gray traces along their own metabolisms. They are not individuals in any ordinary sense. They are sheets, films, nets, crusts, and breathing skins. They line vents, digest fallen tissue, concentrate salts, and pass charge through one another in slow pulses. They are the lowest gears of the country’s engine-life: unseen as beings, indispensable as process. Without them the warmth would not circulate, the minerals would not separate, the moisture would not taste of metal and green sap, and the larger lives would have nothing to graze, absorb, or become.

Above them, and among them, move the more visible inhabitants.

The most conspicuous large animals, if conspicuous is possible in a world that swallows sound, are the broad-bodied grazers. They are low, wide, and many-legged, built close to the living mats they crop. Their undersides are warm and damp, like pressed leaves; their backs are not bare but carry moss, fungal knots, hanging root-fibers, and sometimes entire nursery pools in the hollows between their shoulder ridges. Where their skin can be seen, it is dark and faintly translucent, threaded with vessels that glow dull red or copper when heat moves through them. Along their flanks rise flexible spines, hairs, or filament-fringes that bead with water whenever the air becomes charged. In a storm, every filament lifts and glows faintly, so that a passing herd looks less like a line of animals than a moving field of dim sparks.

Their mouths are broad rasping pads, made for cropping microbial mats and low growth without tearing deeply into the substrate. They do not bite so much as graze in long, slow passes. They have no voices. When a herd moves, one does not hear it; one feels it as a soft pressure through the soles of the feet, as a tremor in the moisture on nearby leaves, as a change in the way the air lies against the skin. Their coordination is nearly perfect but not led. They shift direction by minute electrical flickers along their spines, by chemical traces, by the warmth rising through fractures below. From a distance, a large herd can look like weather: a low dark mass altering the texture of a slope, condensation forming above it, mist parting around it, small organisms scattering in its wake like sparks from a slow fire.

They are mobile heat reservoirs. Smaller creatures gather in their folds, ride in their shadow, feed on the life that grows upon them, and shelter beneath them when fog becomes too dense or too charged. Their bodies collect water. In dry intervals, they stand motionless while condensation gathers on their filaments and runs along hidden channels in their skin to the mouths or throat-sacs of calves pressed against their sides. Their young begin as swimming larvae in temporary pools no larger than footprints. Only a few ever reach a passing adult and attach themselves to a warm fold. There they metamorphose slowly, their bodies flattening, thickening, taking on the broad patience of the herd. When one of these grazers dies, it does not remain a carcass for long. Filaments reach it within moments. Moss leans toward it. The body softens, sinks, and becomes a basin, a nursery, a patch of richer growth. Even in death, it continues to function as part of the engine.

In the tangled vertical places, where vegetation climbs over itself and root curtains hang from ledges, live the softer and more hidden kinds.

There are moth-like things with broad wet wings, though they are not moths in any familiar sense. Their wings are membranes, veined with conductive tissue, and they fold themselves into leaf joints so tightly that they become indistinguishable from swollen buds. They feed on spore clouds, electric films, and the minute life suspended in humid air. When two of them meet, they touch wingtips and exchange a brief flicker of light, a charge, a chemical taste. Sometimes they open their wings beneath a charged fog and ground the air around them, making a small circle of stillness in which droplets fall gently instead of trembling upward. They do not sing. If they communicate, it is by luminous veins, by humidity altered on the skin, by the tiny pressure waves their folded wings make when opened.

There are hanging things that resemble root-fiber but are not plants. They drape from overhangs and dense shelves in soft curtains, pale or dark, sometimes beaded with moisture, sometimes dry and still. A careless brush against one causes it to contract, not with violence but with a quiet, enclosing patience. The curtain folds inward, moistens, and begins digestion in a hush. What is taken is not left as a carcass or bone. It is softened, opened, transferred. These curtain-dwellers are among the country’s ambushes of texture: predators not as chasers, but as surfaces that become interiors.

There are also flattened, dark membranes stretched over frames of bone-white growth. Some seem neither fully animal nor fully plant, but living interfaces. They vibrate faintly in humidity, harvesting electrical potential from friction, mist, and thermal gradients. At times they are still as dead skin; at times they tremble so delicately that the air around them shimmers with almost-invisible charge. They may be solitary or arranged in loose clusters that resemble machinery only by accident. When touched, they may release stored energy as a soft flash, a smell of ozone, or a pressure behind the eyes. Some smaller creatures roost on them, using their warmth and charge. They do not appear to defend themselves. If damaged, they fold, detach, or are colonized within hours.

Where water gathers, even briefly, life becomes stranger.

The amphibious forms are among the most delicate. They have translucent throats that pulse as they breathe humidity directly from the air. Some never leave the shallow basins where they hatch; others move across wet rock in slow, cautious journeys, their bodies flattening to preserve moisture. Their eyes, if they have eyes, are often reduced to light-sensitive patches. They navigate by tasting the air, by feeling electrical gradients, by the memory of condensation on their skin. They feed on floating pollen, larval swarms, shed skins, and the microbial films that form on warm water. In the brief lives of footprint-pools, entire generations may rise, feed, metamorphose, and dissolve back into residue before the water vanishes.

Some pool-dwellers are harder to classify. In basins that reflect a sky other than the one above, there are things that seem to begin as reflections. They are thin, quick, and uncertain: translucent shapes skimming the surface, moving with a half-second delay, as if remembering a body they have not yet been given. They feed on pollen, charge, and the residue left by other drinkers. Sometimes they imitate the outline of whatever bends toward the water. This is not aggression, exactly. It is more like recognition gone wrong. Drinking from such a pool is possible, but afterward the drinker may feel slightly misplaced: their reflection may lag, their shadow may fall briefly in an adjacent direction, or they may remember a path they never walked. The pool-born do not seem to intend harm. They simply exist in a country where moisture carries memory, and where identity is not as sealed as elsewhere.

The wormlike processions are among the most engine-like inhabitants.

They move through moss in slow lines, their bodies producing faint electrical sparks as they pass. They follow mineral seams, charged films, and hidden gradients in the rock. No one directs them. Their regularity is not discipline but resonance. They are part of the reason the landscape feels mechanized without machines: they behave like living circuits closing and reopening. Where they pass, spores brighten, fungal threads answer with light, and tiny discharges crawl over nearby leaves like static moving over fur.

They may also be part of the reason sound behaves so poorly. It is as if they gather stray vibrations and carry them away. During long hushes, one can sometimes see processions moving with unusual slowness, their sparks dimmer than usual, as though burdened. Later, when many lines converge, the stored noise may return all at once: a soft chorus of drips, rustles, breaths, clicks, and distant collapses, none of them loud, but all of them suddenly present. Then the sound fades again, and the country returns to its muffled work.

The storm-centers have their own temporary fauna.

From a distance, these quiet storms look like columns of dim smoke or slow whirlpools in the air. Up close, they are almost silent. Leaves lift. Droplets orbit in loose rings. Small animals freeze or move backward along paths they have already taken. Spores ignite briefly into cold light. The vegetation bends toward the disturbance as if feeding.

In these centers live beings that may not deserve the word “creature” because they last only as long as the tension that holds them. Some appear as rings of water turning around an empty axis. Some appear as negative spaces in the mist, dark outlines where condensation refuses to settle. Some are only patterns of motion: droplets rising, pausing, reversing, then falling all at once when the storm collapses inward. They feed, if feeding is the right word, on electrical imbalance, thermal shear, and the pressure of reality thinning. They may reproduce in the moment of collapse, scattering into spores, charged mist, or microscopic cysts that wait in moss until the next storm calls them into shape. When the rain falls warm and heavy and the basin fills, they are gone. What remains is accelerated growth: new buds, new filaments, new mineral sheens along the wet stone.

Because the country is thin, some inhabitants are not stable individuals.

The same broad grazer with a pale mineral scar may be seen in three valleys at once, but not because it traveled. The same moth-folded sleeper may be encountered twice in the same leaf-joint, one version slightly darker, one version with a notch missing from a wing. The same herd may appear to approach from two directions, then become one, or neither. These are not ghosts. They are not omens. They are local excesses of possibility, revisions made visible. The landscape does not seem disturbed by them. Its residents do not rely on fixed landmarks or continuous identity in the way one would expect. They navigate by tasting gradients: humidity, charge, mineral breath, the pull of heat through stone, the memory left in water. A repeated ridge is less important to them than the warmth exhaling from its base. A duplicated pool matters less than whether its surface reflects this sky or another.

This gives the fauna a strange serenity. They do not panic when distance changes. They do not cry out when a familiar path folds into a different scale. They move through unreality as fish move through water: by pressure, by current, by bodily memory.

If one insists on finding a “who” rather than a “what,” the answer becomes more elusive.

There are beings—never clearly separate from the rest of the life there—that seem to pay attention in a way the others do not. They are not human, and they are not people in any familiar sense. They might be called persons only because they sometimes choose where to direct their awareness.

They are soft-bodied, low, and slow, with broad limbs and wide, heat-sensitive surfaces. Their skin is dark green, black, or deep red-brown, slick with condensation, sometimes threaded with faint bioluminescent veins that brighten when they touch one another. They have large eyes adapted to the dimness, but they do not rely chiefly on sight. Their faces, if one can call them faces, are dominated by tasting membranes, heat pits, and fluttering filaments that sense charge in the air. They have no hands suited to making tools. Their limbs end in broad pads, soft fringes, or clusters of moist filaments that can touch, spread moisture, discharge static, and read chemical traces, but not grip a handle in the way a maker would require.

They do not build. They do not carry objects. They do not wear coverings, arrange stones, or leave deliberate marks. They rest beneath overlapping leaves, in warm hollows, inside fog banks, along the lips of new fissures. They gather at storm-centers and newly opened vents, but their gatherings do not become settlements. They are temporary densities, like eddies in water. When they meet, they press skin to skin, exchange droplets, and let their luminous veins synchronize. This may be greeting, memory-sharing, digestion, or calibration. It is difficult to say. It may be all of these at once.

Their intelligence, if intelligence is the word, is not constructive. It is responsive. They perceive the fine feedback loops of the country: where heat is rising, where a pool is becoming too mineral-rich, where a fog bank is about to deposit new moss, where a distance is beginning to fold, where a swarm is gathering too much charge. They move into such places and, by their presence, alter them slightly. A body warms the edge of a vent until it opens. A membrane discharges static until a swarm disperses. A group stands in a certain relation to a trembling pool until the water stops reflecting the wrong sky. They do not appear to intend these acts as making. They do not celebrate them afterward. They are more like participants in weather than engineers of it.

They have no artifacts, and therefore no history in the usual sense. If they remember, their memory is chemical and electrical: traces left in moisture, patterns in charged moss, residues in water, the taste of a storm that passed through a valley. When one drinks where they have been, one may feel a brief, nonhuman attention—not a message, not a voice, only the sense of being included in a calculation without center. They do not speak. If they produce sound, it is not speech but pressure: a soft click in the teeth, a vibration in the chest, a sudden return of withheld rustles after long silence.

They are neither hostile nor welcoming. If an outsider enters the country, they investigate as the landscape does: by condensation, by heat, by tiny organisms arriving at the edges of shadow. If the outsider is dry, moisture may gather near them. If the outsider is too hot, something may draw warmth away through the air. If the outsider is unstable—leaking memory, carrying wrong reflections, disturbing local distances—they may be avoided, or gently surrounded until the disturbance settles. Sometimes one of these beings will follow for a while as a second shadow, a tremor in moss, a warmth at the corner of perception. It does not feel like being hunted. It feels like being noticed by something that has no face and no need to be liked.

They are not rulers. They are not guardians. They do not own the place. If anything, they are the country’s most sensitive organs of self-adjustment. They are the nearest thing to a “who” because they can attend, choose, and remember in some distributed fashion. But even that may be too strong a claim. They may only be the moments when the landscape becomes aware enough to resemble intention.

Predation exists, but it is rarely dramatic.

There are no roaring hunts, no chases across open ground. The dense vegetation and muffled air do not permit spectacle. Predators are textures, closures, hollows that inhale, curtains that fold, patches of growth that become digestive chambers. Violence is quick, quiet, and rapidly recycled. A creature may step into what seems like a soft hollow and find the hollow closing around it with the patience of a leaf folding at night. There is no cry, or the cry is swallowed into pressure. There is no prolonged struggle. The body is softened, opened, and passed into the production cycle. Even fear, if present, seems to have been bred out of loudness. The animals avoid danger by sensing gradients, by withdrawing along charge-lines, by freezing when storm-tension rises, by moving backward when reality begins to repeat.

Death is not monumental.

There are no clean bones. There are no dry remains. Fallen tissue is covered by filaments within moments. A dead grazer becomes a nursery. A dead membrane becomes conductive film. A dead curtain-dweller becomes a hanging garden. A dead storm-born thing may leave nothing but a faint mineral ring on wet stone. Even collapse becomes substrate. The landscape does not accumulate graves or ruins. It accumulates layers. This shapes the emotional quality of all life there: abundant, close, warm, but without permanence. Nothing is preserved. Everything is processed.

The smells of the inhabitants blend with the air: warm wet skin, crushed moss, green sap, fermented sweetness from cup-leaves, ozone after charge, and a clean sharp note like heated glass. Their bodies are rarely dry. Fur, filament, skin, membrane, and leaf alike become beaded with moisture. Surfaces sweat. Breath carries more than air: spores, chemical signals, minute luminous motes, and the taste of electricity. To be near them is to feel oneself becoming damp, warm, and slightly porous, as though one’s own edges might loosen.

Their light is dim, so their colors are not ornamental in the way one expects. Greens deepen until they are nearly black. Reds glow as if internally heated. Whites are rare and unsettling: fungal, mineral, or the pale translucence of pool-born things. Bioluminescence appears in pulses: a vein in a leaf, a ring of spores, the underside of a crawling animal, the edge of a charged membrane, the slow flicker moving along a herd’s spines. Because the sky is diffuse, their bodies seem to emerge from shadow rather than stand against it. One notices them by nearness, by warmth, by the way moisture leans toward them, not by clear outline.

What lives there, finally, is not a single kind of life but a condition of being inhabited.

It is lived in by microbial films that act like refineries. By broad grazers that move like weather and carry ecosystems on their backs. By moth-folded winged things that exchange light in leaf joints. By root-curtains that digest in silence. By membrane harvesters trembling with stored charge. By pool-born forms that may be animals, reflections, or memories given temporary weight. By electric processions that carry the country’s withheld sound. By storm-center beings that exist only while tension holds. By duplicated creatures that have no original. And by the elusive, soft-bodied attendants who seem, at times, to be the place paying attention to itself.

None of them builds. None of them leaves roads, walls, tools, or monuments. None of them makes a lasting mark in the sense a maker would understand. Yet all of them produce. They distill water, separate minerals, store charge, spread spores, breathe heat, digest collapse, and generate the next layer of the living system. They are engines, but soft ones. They are machines, but grown ones. They are an industry without artifacts, a factory with no product except continuation.

If one asks who lives there, the answer is: no one permanent. No single face, no tribe, no sovereign species. At most, there are the attentive ones, and even they are more relationship than person.

If one asks what lives there, the answer is: everything capable of existing between condensation and collapse. Everything that can feed on warmth, moisture, charge, and instability. Everything that can be made quickly and unmade quietly. Everything that does not need to last in order to be real.

It is a country of crowded lives and hidden faces, of bodies that are also processes, of animals that behave like weather, of persons without property, of deaths that become gardens within hours. It is inhabited by the ongoing. It is lived by the unfinished. It is full to the edge of breaking, and yet it remains hushed, warm, damp, and endlessly at work.
