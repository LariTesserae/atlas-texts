No one lives there, if “live” is taken in the animal sense: no breathing body, no watching eye, no footfall, no nest, no bone, no hunger. That must be said first, because the hollow refuses the usual idea of an inhabitant. It does not house a people. It does not shelter a beast. It has no citizens, no flock, no predator, no parasite, no corpse.

And yet it is not empty.

What lives there is plural, dry, and slow. It is not a single body moving through the streets. It is the streets themselves, the heat moving through them, the growths that alter them, the sound that shapes them, and the long equilibrium that keeps them from becoming mere ruin. If one must give the resident a name, the best name would be something like the Dry Chorus: not a creature, but a continuing.

The first lives one notices are the mineral plants, though “plant” is only a convenience. They have the habits of vegetation without its wetness. They do not thirst. They do not soften into rot. They harden, open, ring, shed, and crystallize. They are the visible population of the hollow, the low citizens that fill the terraces and courtyards with a lushness made of heat and chemistry.

The sulfur-ferns live by leaning. Their fronds are not seeking light so much as seeking warmth; they turn toward vents and sun-warmed stone the way ordinary leaves turn toward a sky they will never see. Their surfaces are laminated, brittle, and faintly oily, and when air passes over them they release the sharp mineral smell of struck matches. They are not gentle plants. They are patient chemical engines, each frond a thin factory for moving solvent-like fluids through capillary channels. Their life is a series of thermal openings: when the floor warms, they spread; when it cools, they fold into tight, sulfurous hands. They do not grow quickly. They accumulate themselves in yellow and black layers, making the lower terraces look as if they are furred with slow fire.

The wax-thread moss lives by coating. It is not so much a carpet as a skin, a communal crust that settles into shaded corners and along the shoulders of stairways. Its copper-green surface gives off tiny beads of heat-stable wax, and those beads do something quiet but important: they soften the mineral surface beneath them, sealing hairline cracks, dulling sharp edges, making the stone less vulnerable to dust and thermal shock. Where the luminescent salt crusts glow, the moss catches that glow and returns it in a muted greenish sheen, so that some terraces seem lit from below by buried lanterns. The moss does not hurry. It thickens where chemical vapor pools, retreats where heat becomes too sharp, and slowly turns architecture into something closer to a living shell.

The glass canes live by resonance. They stand along the dry canals like tall, jointed pipes of smoked quartz, hollow and almost leafless. Their bodies are their means of exchange: they draw hot mineral vapor from the canal beds and deposit crystalline waste at their joints, adding segment after segment over long intervals of high flow. They are not decorative. They are acoustic organs. When air moves through them, they produce thin notes, ticks, whistles, and moans, depending on the pressure and temperature of the breath passing through the basin. Each cane is a record of vapor and heat; each joint marks a period of growth. They sway in dry squalls, their bodies touching with a hard, glassy knock, and when many move together they sound like a city tuning itself in the dark.

The bell-pods live by tension. They are squat, ribbed growths in open plazas, each bearing dry membranous fruits that hang from curved supports. Their reproduction is not soft. It does not involve ripeness in the sense of sweetness or decay. It involves precision. The membranes wait until a particular heat threshold is reached, then tighten, split, and release their spore-dust with a sharp ringing crack. The sound is both birth and announcement. A single bell-pod can startle the air like a struck plate; a dozen together can turn a plaza into scattered percussion. Their children are not seeds carried by birds or water, but dry golden clouds lifted by rising heat, borne through streets and courtyards until they settle into cracks where chemistry and warmth permit another beginning.

The ember-bulbs live by memory. Buried beneath terrace gravel, they store heat long after the upper air has shifted. They glow faintly at their cores, not with flame but with retained warmth, and around them other growths gather. They are nurseries and hearths without fire. When a thermal pulse has passed and the hollow begins to cool, the ember-bulbs keep a small portion of the heat alive. They give the basin a kind of endurance, a way of holding the day’s intensity inside its stone body. Other roots and rootlike mineral growths cluster near them, borrowing their stability, using their warmth as a condition for slow change.

These lives alone would already be enough to call the hollow inhabited. But they are not the whole resident. They are only its visible parts.

The second life of the hollow is the city itself.

The basin is built, and its building is not a dead backdrop. It is an active frame. The terraces are not merely carved shapes; they are heat-shelves, directing warm air upward in broad, slow sheets. The dry canals are not failed rivers; they are ducts. The plazas are not abandoned gathering places; they are resonance chambers and sporefall courts. The towers are not monuments to forgotten gods; they are chimneys, throats, ears, amplifiers. The stairways, ramps, retaining walls, and blocked doorways are all part of a system whose purpose is not habitation by bodies but regulation by heat, sound, and mineral growth.

The simple technology of the place has become part of its living behavior. Bronze pins and corroded copper strips are not ornamental. They bind cracked lintels and hold stones in positions that would otherwise shift with thermal expansion. Lever-catches on vent covers respond to pressure and temperature, lifting or dropping apertures by fractions of a width. Rough bolts in ceramic sockets allow certain stone plates to move without collapsing. None of this is intricate in the way of machines. There are no gears, no engines, no delicate mechanisms. The engineering is older and more patient: mass, angle, aperture, weight, draft. It works because it has been tested by endless repetition. It works because it has been worn into balance.

The city breathes. That is not a metaphor, or not only one. Heat rises from floor vents, moves through the canal beds, climbs the terraces, gathers in courtyards, and escapes through towers in slow pulses. The air thickens, thins, pushes, settles. Vent covers lift. Dust moves in fine veils. Spore clouds rise and fall. The whole basin expands and contracts with temperature, its stone joints popping softly as the heat changes. It has no lungs, but it has respiration. It has no mouth, but it exhales through the fissure above, sending that faint pressure into the mineral plain outside. It has no eyes, but its apertures respond. It has no hands, but its forms adjust.

The city is most alive in its emptiness. The vast volumes of air between objects are not absence. They are working space. They are the pauses inside a chord, the chambers inside a flute, the distance needed for sound and heat to behave properly. A crowded city would choke this place. It needs open courtyards, long silent avenues, empty rooms, bare plazas with only one standing stone. It needs the low density. Its life depends on enough room for air to turn, for spores to drift, for resonance to develop without being muffled.

The third life of the hollow is sound.

Sound here is not decoration. It is state. It is information without language. The continuous rush of hot air through the dry canals tells that the vents are active. A rising moan in a vent-shaft tells that pressure has increased. The ticking of glass canes tells that vapor flow is moving through them. A crack from the bell-pods tells that a heat threshold has been reached. The soft abrasive whisper of wax-thread moss tells that thermal expansion is traveling across the terraces. A tiny hiss of dust down a stairway tells that some surface has shifted or released its burden. The towers take these small events and deepen them, turning local noises into a basin-wide chord.

If anything speaks in the hollow, sound speaks.

It does not speak in words. It does not ask, warn, greet, or mourn. It announces condition. A change in pitch means a change in pressure. A new rattle means a change in spore release. A sudden silence may mean cooling, blockage, or collapse. The city listens to itself through its own architecture: streets become flutes, plazas become drums, empty rooms become echo chambers, towers become resonators. The sound is not random. It follows cycles. When deep vents pulse, the canals breathe harder, the canes rise in pitch, the bell-pods open in scattered cracks, and the towers answer with a low moan. When the air cools, the stone contracts and gives off sharp ticks. When spore release begins, there is a dry rustling like paper being torn far away.

The sound is one of the truest residents of the basin. It has no flesh. It has no permanent body. It is born from heat and structure, travels through air, and dies into stone. Yet it returns. It changes. It can be said to live because it has rhythm, cause, effect, memory, and consequence. The hollow remembers sound the way a bell remembers being struck: in shape, in readiness, in the particular curve that makes the next note possible.

The fourth life of the hollow is heat itself.

Heat is the currency, the season, the weather, the motive force. It is not merely temperature. It is motion. It moves air. It carries dust. It lifts spores. It opens vents. It triggers reproduction. It feeds the glass canes. It softens sulfur veins just enough to make them yield, then lets them stiffen again. It expands stone, contracts stone, polishes surfaces by slow abrasion, and keeps the hollow from becoming still.
