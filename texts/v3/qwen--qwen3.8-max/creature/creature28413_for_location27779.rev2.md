The first answer is: almost no one.

A few workers among the scaffolds. A few insects over the pools. A few amphibians under the wet stones. A bird crossing the upper air without stopping. If you counted only bodies, you would say the basin is nearly empty, a place still waiting to be inhabited.

But that would be a mistake.

What lives there is not yet numerous. It is preliminary. It is the first population of a place that has not finished deciding what it will become. The basin is inhabited by beginnings: by water that behaves like a surveyor, by low plants that act as masons of the smallest scale, by a few careful humans, and by the larger, stranger life of pattern itself.

The most numerous lives are the least conspicuous. The moss lives there. The liverworts live there. The ferns and sedges and creeping mats live there, not as decoration but as the first citizens of the new stone. They have no streets, yet they settle every margin. They have no voices, yet they soften the whole basin with their green, wet silence. They hold the dust in place. They thicken the thin mineral soil. They creep into joints and seams and teach the rock to accept softness. Where wood has stood long enough, they move onto it, darkening its grain, sealing its pores, turning scaffold into something almost geological. In this place, plant life does not rise high; it spreads close, patient, and tenacious, stitching the raw stone into a surface that can someday bear footprints without being wounded by them.

Beneath even that green life, there is another population too small to name comfortably. Films of algae and microbes live on every face that stays wet. Threads of pale growth move through water no deeper than a breath. Invisible spores drift from ledge to ledge and find lodging in the cool damp. In the still pools, minute lives flicker and wheel. In the running channels, organisms cling to the underside of stones, flattened against the current. They are the basin’s first metabolism, the quiet industry that turns mineral water into something faintly sweet and alive. They make the air smell clean. They make the surfaces slick. They are preparing the place for larger bodies by doing the oldest work: turning bare wet stone into a world that can be touched without harm.

Animals are rare, but they are not absent. The place does not yet belong to them, yet they have begun to arrive as if summoned by the moisture. Small flies and midges hover above the pools, quick and solitary, their movements brief against the larger sound of falling water. Beetles and springtails live in the wetter cracks, under loose flakes of rock and beneath the soaked hems of moss. In the shallow basins, mosquito-like things rise in thin swarms when the air is still enough. Where water gathers and stays, there are the first amphibians: small, dark, cool-skinned creatures that shelter under stones and blink slowly when disturbed. They do not sing in crowds yet. There are not enough of them for chorus. Their voices are isolated, occasional, more like tests than declarations.

A few birds pass over the basin, usually high and fast, using it as a landmark or a hunting glance rather than a home. Sometimes one drops briefly to a ledge to drink or to inspect a crack for insects, then leaves. The place is too open, too loud, too unfinished for nesting. It does not yet have the hush animals prefer. But even those passing lives matter: feathers carry seeds, feet carry grit, droppings bring salts and nutrients. The basin is being seeded by accident. Life is entering it in tiny, unintended shipments.

Then there are the humans.

They are few. Fewer than the scaffolds suggest, fewer than the streets imply, fewer than the terraces seem to demand. Seen from a distance, they look almost incidental, small figures moving through a space too large for them. Up close, they are anything but accidental. They move with the unhurried precision of people who know that what they are doing will outlast them. They do not hurry because hurry would be useless here. The basin is not built in urgency. It is built in seasons, in drips, in repeated strikes, in the slow accretion of mineral and habit.

These people are builders, but not in the way one usually means. They are not merely raising walls. They are coaxing a landscape into becoming a city. They are as much gardeners of stone as masons. They understand water first. Before they lay a course of rock, they watch where moisture darkens the ground. Before they cut a channel, they listen to the way water already leans across the surface. They do not impose form on the basin so much as they reveal the forms the basin seems already half willing to accept.

Their bodies have begun to resemble the place. Their hands are thickened and precise. Their feet are accustomed to wet stone, to the faint suction of damp surfaces, to climbing stairs that are still being taught how to be stairs. Their clothes are dark with moisture at the hems: oiled wool, close-woven cloaks, leather hardened by water, cords softened by constant handling. They wear little ornament. What ornament they have is practical: polished stone pins, metal fittings worn smooth, small tokens of resonant rock carried in pouches. Their skin is cool rather than sun-burned, their breath visible in the hollows of the morning. They smell of wet wool, crushed sedge, mineral dust, and the faint green bitterness of moss.

They speak little while working. Not from grimness, but because the place asks for listening. The basin is loud enough already. Water speaks continuously in it. It drips into basins, runs through troughs, falls from terrace to terrace, murmurs in clay-lined channels, and booms in hidden chambers where the gradient and the rock make resonance possible. In such a place, ordinary chatter would be wasted. So the workers reserve their voices for necessary intervals. They call short phrases across the open space, loud and clear, not as casual speech but as signals. The calls go out, strike stone, return changed. The workers listen to the return as much as to the original sound. In their calls is a kind of measurement. They are not only telling one another when to lift, when to brace, when to let a rope run. They are also testing the emptiness, asking the basin whether it is still shaped the way they think it is.

Among them are different kinds of knowledge, though they may not divide themselves into rigid trades. Some are water-readers. They crouch beside channels and watch the movement of leaves, grit, and foam. They know when a flow is healthy, when it is thinning, when it is about to find an unwanted path. They can smell a dry fault before it shows itself. They place their fingers in the current and read temperature as others might read text. They are the ones who decide where a basin may fill and where it must be drained, where a wall may stand and where water has already refused it.

Others are stone-listeners. They carry mauls and smaller striking tools, and they touch rock before they trust it. They tap a block and lean toward the answer. A sound too dull means a hidden seam. A sound too bright may mean brittleness. A long, clean ring tells them the stone is ready to bear weight. They listen not only to individual blocks but to the basin itself: to the way echoes move from terrace to terrace, to the way certain hollows amplify a hammer-blow, to the deep slow booming that rises from resonant chambers after rain. They do not treat this as mysticism. To them, it is procedure. Stone must be heard before it is set. Silence must be investigated. A place that has gone quiet is a place where something has failed.

Others tend the forms. These are the workers who build wooden templates, armatures, and low frames where mineral-rich water can deposit crusts of stone. They know how long to leave timber in place before it becomes part of the structure. They know when to remove a shuttering and when to let it remain, half-swallowed by mineral rind. Their hands are always checking edges, running along damp surfaces to feel the difference between loose sediment and true hardening. They work with a strange patience, because their materials are only partly obedient. Water may deposit where they wish, or it may refuse. They adjust. They redirect. They do not argue with the process. They negotiate with it.

There are also the green-tenders, though they might not be called that except by their function. They are the ones who notice where moss has begun to seal a joint and decide whether to encourage it. They press sodden plants into cracks where soil is too thin. They protect young ferns from being crushed underfoot. They move mats of creeping growth onto unstable margins to hold them together. In a more mature city, such people would be gardeners. Here they are something closer to skin-doctors for the land. They help the basin grow its living surface. They understand that greenness is not separate from construction. In this place, moss is infrastructure. Algae is weatherproofing. Roots are stitches.

And there are those who keep the emptiness.

This is the least visible labor, and perhaps the most important. The city is not being filled as quickly as it could be. Rooms remain roofless. Plazas remain bare. Streets are broader than present need. Terraces open into hollow space. Someone must maintain that restraint. Someone must resist the temptation to close every wall, cover every court, and pack the settlement with immediate use. These people walk the unfinished spaces and preserve their vacancy. They measure what is not there. They protect the silence between sounds. They understand that the basin needs room to echo, room to breathe, room to become legible before it becomes crowded. Emptiness, to them, is not lack. It is capacity. It is the shape of future life held in reserve.

Where these people live within the basin is modest. They do not yet occupy the grand rooms they are preparing. They shelter in low structures at the edges of terraces: thick-walled enclosures of stone and compacted gravel, partly roofed with timber, hides, woven mats, and weighted cloth. Their shelters are not luxurious, but they are carefully placed. They are set where water will not collect, where wind will not strike too directly, where sound will not become confusing at night. Inside, the air is close and warm from bodies and heated stones. Beds are made of dry moss, folded fibrous plants, and layered hides. Tools are stored in recesses cut into the walls. Nothing is abundant. Everything has a place because space is still precious and the weather is cold.

They eat simply. Some food is brought from lower ground, carried up in packs or drawn along rough paths by rope and sledge. Some is gathered within the basin itself: edible watercress-like greens from the cleaner channels, tender sedge shoots, pale tubers from pockets of mineral soil, algae scraped from sunned stones where growth is sweet rather than bitter. They do not hunt much here. There is too little fauna, and the place feels too young for killing to be casual. They may keep dried provisions in sealed cists. They may trade for grain, salt, and oil with people who live beyond the basin’s rim. Their meals are quiet, warm, eaten with hands that are still cold at the knuckles. They drink water constantly, because water is everywhere, but they are careful with it. Even in abundance, they treat it as sacred in the oldest sense: as something that must be observed before it is used.

Children are not absent, but they are few. The settlement is not yet a place of crowds. A child here grows up hearing the basin before learning to speak its names. Such children would learn to walk on wet stone early. They would know how to listen before they knew how to argue. They would press their ears to cold rock and hear the deep slow movement of water inside the ground. They would be taught not to throw stones into pools without reason, not to block channels out of mischief, not to shout without listening for the answer. In this place, play would be a kind of apprenticeship to patience. A child’s game might be to strike a sounding stone and guess where the echo will return. A child’s chore might be to keep moss alive on a shaded wall. Even their laughter would feel temporary against the vast water-noise, small and bright and quickly swallowed.

At night, the basin does not become silent. It becomes differently loud. Water continues to fall, drip, run, and gather. In the dark, sounds seem to detach from their sources. A drip becomes a metronome. A rush becomes a breath. The deep booming chambers feel closer, as if the ground itself has turned into a sleeping throat. The workers sleep in intervals, their rest lightened by habit. Even in sleep, they attend to the place. A change in water-sound can wake them. A wrong silence can wake them. They are not afraid of the noise. They are afraid of the absence of it. To them, a healthy basin speaks. If it stops speaking, something has gone wrong.

There is another kind of life in the place, harder to classify. It is not biological, not exactly, but it is active. The patterns live there. The intentions live there. The streets that have been marked but not paved live there as alignments. The rooms that have been bounded but not roofed live there as measured absences. The wet outlines of future walls live there, appearing after rain in darker lines across flat stone. The survey marks live in the memory of the workers and in the way water has begun to follow them. The city exists already, but as a low-density fact: more plan than mass, more rule than crowd.

This gives the basin a strange population of potentials. The people who will someday fill it are not present, yet their absence has weight. The crowds that may one day cross the plazas are already implied by the width of the walkways. The voices that may one day rise from the roofed rooms are already anticipated by the acoustics of the basin. The market stalls, the processions, the doors, the lamps, the arguments, the festivals, the ordinary noise of habitation—all these exist only as capacity, but capacity is not nothing. In this place, it is a kind of life. The emptiness is pregnant with use. The hollow scale is not deserted; it is reserved.

Even the echoes seem to live here. They are not creatures, yet they behave like them. They answer hammer-blows after a pause. They carry calls across terraces and return them altered. They multiply waterfalls into layered sound. They make a single worker seem accompanied. In the resonant chambers, they stretch a small noise into a long boom, giving the basin a pulse. The people do not worship the echoes, but they respect them. They do not mistake them for spirits, yet they speak as if the place has ears. When they call, they leave time for the answer. When they strike stone, they wait to hear what the stone says. In their practice, an echo is not mere repetition. It is information. It is the basin’s opinion.

Water, too, lives here in a manner that exceeds matter. It is not only falling, flowing, and pooling. It is deciding. It chooses where to bead, where to sheet, where to seep. It reveals gradients too subtle for the eye. It darkens planned streets in lines of damp. It avoids certain foundations and so teaches the builders where not to lay stone. It deposits mineral crusts over wooden frames, turning temporary carpentry into permanent texture. It fills footprints slowly, erasing them with clear water almost as soon as they are made. In this basin, water is the first inhabitant, the first engineer, and perhaps the oldest law. The human workers are not its masters. They are its pupils, its interpreters, and sometimes its hands.

The place also lives in its slowness. Moss advances by fingertips. Mineral crust thickens by fractions. Timber darkens season by season. Stone hardens where water has been persuaded to leave its burden. Streets become streets by being walked again and again. Channels become channels by being cleaned and re-cut year after year. Nothing here is finished quickly enough to be called finished. The life of the basin is therefore not the life of events, but the life of accumulation. It is the life of a surface learning to hold greenness, a wall learning to stand, a city learning to be a city.

So if you ask who lives there, the answer begins small and refuses to remain small.

The moss lives there. The ferns and sedges live there. The microbial films and the cold algae live there. The midges and beetles and small blinking amphibians live there. The passing birds live there in the briefest way. The few workers live there, with their damp clothes, their listening faces, their short calls, and their patient hands. Their children live there, if children are present, learning to move quietly through a loud landscape. Their tools live there: mauls, ropes, levers, chisels, clay linings, wooden frames, leather slings, and little metal fittings worn bright by use. Water lives there as a continuous presence and a ruling intelligence. Sound lives there, filling the hollow spaces with dripping, rushing, booming, striking, and answering. Echoes live there, not as bodies but as recurring shapes. The future city lives there, not yet dense, not yet crowded, but already real in its measurements, its channels, its empty plazas, and its unroofed rooms.

And perhaps the basin itself lives there, in the way a newly begun thing lives: awkwardly, coolly, wetly, with most of its life still to come. It is not yet a home for many. It is a home for preparation. It is a place where the first inhabitants are not citizens in the ordinary sense but conditions: moisture, sound, pattern, intention, restraint. Whoever or whatever lives there lives in the earliest tense. Nothing is settled. Nothing is crowded. Nothing is final. But everything is already attending.

If you stood in the center of that high, wet, echoing basin and listened, you would hear more than construction. You would hear a world being inhabited before it has become inhabitable. You would hear water teaching stone where to be hard. You would hear hands teaching water where to go. You would hear silence being left open on purpose. You would hear the first residents of the place: not only the few small living bodies moving among the terraces, but the vast, patient life of becoming itself.
