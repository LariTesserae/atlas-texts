Whoever lives in the Salt Nave is not one figure, but a congregation.

Not a people in the ordinary sense. Not a tribe with a chief, not a herd with a leader, not a city with a builder. What lives there is distributed, patient, and many-bodied: a population that has become indistinguishable from the place it inhabits. To ask who lives in the Salt Nave is almost the wrong question. The better question is: what has learned to live without spilling itself?

At first, the inhabitants seem to be only small creatures.

There are the mineral-workers: beetles with matte, stone-colored shells that move along the salt ridges as if they were fragments of the crust given temporary life. Their backs are dull and rough, powdered with pale sulfate, and when they pause in the sun they look less like animals than like polished chips of gypsum that have decided to think. Beneath the surface, blind grubs loosen grains with mouthparts made for pressure rather than biting. They do not tunnel violently. They coax. They separate crystal from crystal, grain from grain, opening passages so fine that only the smallest bodies or the slowest roots can use them. In their wake, the mineral floor remains firm, but porous; hard, but not sealed. These are the lowest laborers of the Nave, though they do not know themselves as laborers. They eat what the stone offers, excrete what the architecture requires, and leave behind compacted trails that later become streets.

There are the wax-keepers.

They are the pale, soft-footed climbers seen passing over resin-hardened walls with a silence that seems almost ceremonial. Their bodies are not armored like the beetles’ but supple, warm, faintly translucent at the edges where the sun catches them. They smell of honeyed resin, hot dust, and the clean mineral sharpness of salt. They do not carry materials; they secrete them. From glands along their flanks and mouths comes a slow, amber substance that hardens in the dry air into panels, linings, thresholds, and comb-like partitions. When a wall needs thickening, they gather without summons. They press their bodies against the surface, smoothing wax into cracks, warming old resin until it becomes pliable again, adding layer after layer in motions so gradual that the wall seems to be growing rather than being made. If a chamber becomes too warm, they thin the ceiling. If a passage becomes too drafty, they curve its mouth. They do not plan this. They respond to warmth, pressure, scent, and the tiny tremors of other bodies moving nearby.

There are the bladder-tenders.

These are the thread-bodied insects and slender climbers that move among the swollen plants, the vines with translucent pods, the succulent organs in which the Nave keeps its private liquids. Their limbs are fine, their antennae long and delicate, their movements precise. They know which bladders are full, which are still filling, which have begun to thicken into oil, and which must be left untouched. They feed without puncture or spill, fitting mouthparts into sealed pores as if each plant had been made to answer only them. Sometimes they press their whole bodies against a taut leaf or pod and remain motionless for a long time, listening to the fluid within by some sense no outsider could name. When they leave, the plant is no different except that it has been encouraged, cooled, cleaned, or gently relieved of pressure. They carry pollen on their backs not as dust but as tiny dry grains sealed in resinous packets, so that even fertility does not scatter wastefully into the air.

There are the canopy-dwellers.

Above the lower terraces, where salt-fig branches interlock and bladder-vines hang from the rock lips, life becomes lighter and more aerial. Moth-like pollinators drift through the golden stillness under the woven leaf-ceiling. Their wings make almost no sound, only a faint dry whisper, like the memory of paper being folded. Their bodies are soft, pale, and faintly luminous in the shaded vault, not because they shine, but because they catch the scattered light. They visit the orchid-like flowers that open slowly in warmth, entering with their bodies folded and emerging dusted with the plant’s sealed reproductive burden. In hollow stems and woven galleries, small nesters hide: creatures with bright, bead-dark eyes and limbs that fold tightly against their bodies. They do not sing. They do not cry. They communicate by tapping, by scent, by the minute flexing of living walls. When startled, they do not flee in panic; they vanish inward, slipping into the architecture as though the city had swallowed them.

And there are the root-bound.

Below the plazas, below the lanes of compacted salt and seed-case fragments, below the visible city, lives the slowest stratum. These inhabitants are rarely seen. They are pale, eyeless, thickened, adapted to pressure and darkness. They move among the roots of salt-figs and the buried seams where trace moisture is locked inside crystal lattices. They do not seek water as water. They seek bonds, gradients, faint differences in heat and mineral tension. Where roots need to pass, they prepare the way. Where dead matter falls into the lower crust, they receive it. They do not decompose in the wet, swift manner of lower, damper worlds. They desiccate, disassemble, rebind. Shells become floor. Husks become wall. Shed skins become lining. The bodies of the dead are not buried as if shameful; they are incorporated. In the Salt Nave, death is not an interruption of architecture. It is material.

But if one searches for a single being who seems most like the “who” of the place, one will find it in the small wax-keepers and their close kin.

They are not large. None of the Nave’s lives are large. The biggest are no greater than a hand, and many are smaller. Their bodies are layered: wax, shed skin, pollen-dust, mineral powder, resinous secretions. They seem to wear the place on them. Their faces, if faces they can be called, are smooth and mask-like, more like polished seeds or shaped husks than mammalian features. Their eyes are dark resin beads, not wet, not glistening, but deep. Their limbs end in soft pads and fine hooks suited to wax, salt, bark, and vine. They have no hands, not in the human sense, but everything they touch is changed by them. They carry nothing that is not part of another living process. They make no tools. Their bodies are enough.

They move through the Nave as though they are both citizens and cells.

One may pause beside a salt-fig root and press its mouthparts to a pore no wider than a needle. No fluid appears. There is no drip, no bead, no spill. A sealed transfer occurs. The creature’s throat moves once, perhaps twice. Then it withdraws, and the plant’s surface closes as if nothing had happened. Another may approach a wall where an old chamber has become too brittle. It does not strike the surface. It breathes warmth against it, softens it with secretions, and then smooths it with its body until the crack disappears into a seam. A third may carry a dried seed husk not by dragging it, but by holding it against its chest while walking backward, pressing it into a fresh wax line where it will become part of a floor mosaic. Each act is small. None is dramatic. Repeated over generations, these acts become towers, courtyards, vaults, and streets.

They do not speak, and yet the settlement is full of communication.

Their language is made of tremor, temperature, scent, and texture. A crack in a resin panel releases a faint odor that draws wax-keepers. A bladder-vine under too much pressure gives off a minute change in surface tension that the tenders feel with their feet. A root shifting below sends a vibration through the compacted salt, and certain beetles pause, turn, and move toward it. The hollow towers of the Nave amplify these signals in the smallest way: a tap becomes a whisper, a whisper becomes a ripple, a ripple becomes adjustment. There are no commands. There are only tendencies. The inhabitants move toward what is needed. They withdraw from what is too hot, too brittle, too exposed. They repeat what has worked before, not from memory as a story, but from memory as structure.

In this way, the Nave has customs without laws.

Its inhabitants do not own their chambers in the way a person owns a room. They occupy them. Occupation is maintained by use, repair, and bodily fit. A wax cell belongs to the creature that keeps it warm, clean, and sealed. A hanging gallery belongs to the nesters who line it with silk and husk. A plaza belongs to no one and therefore to everyone who crosses it carefully. There is no hoarding, because nothing can be hoarded without harm. Fluid is private. Food is enclosed. Material is either living, dormant, or already part of the structure. To take too much would be to wound the whole body of the place, and the inhabitants are not separate enough from that body to permit such a wound for long.

Fluid, above all, is treated with reverence—not as a substance to be displayed, but as a secret to be kept.

The inhabitants are vessels. Each one contains a small, precious economy of moisture held in cells, oils, nectars, internal sacs, and living tissue. They do not sweat. They do not weep. They do not leave damp traces. When they exchange fluid, it is through closed contact: mouth to pore, gland to gland, root to feeder, feeder to dependent. A droplet is never seen falling. To see moisture exposed in the Salt Nave would be like seeing something wounded. Instead, life passes liquid inward, in darkness, in sealed chambers, beneath wax, inside swollen leaves, within the hidden lattice of mineral roots. Even when the sun heats the basin and the air shimmers, no one pours, bathes, or spills. The Nave’s wealth is carried internally. Its abundance is containment.

Their days are governed by warmth.

When morning light strikes the sharp cliffs and the pale salt crusts begin to brighten, the settlement does not wake suddenly. It opens. Wax doors soften. Small bodies emerge from vertical cells. Beetles move onto warm ridges. Thread-bodied tenders ascend the bladder-vines. In the canopy, moth-like wings unfold in the golden stillness. The first sound is barely a sound: the tick of crystal expanding, the faint rasp of a claw on resin, the soft separation of petals opening like paper. As the sun climbs, the walls become warm enough for repair. Wax is easiest to shape then. Resin becomes yielding. Roots loosen fractionally. The inhabitants work in the heat, not frantically, but continuously, their bodies oriented toward warmth as other beings might orient toward water.

At midday, the Nave glows.

The light is clear and strong, but the basin does not blaze. Its surfaces are too layered, too vegetated, too folded into shade. Under the canopy, the air is still and full of drifting motes. The inhabitants move through this golden dimness like thoughts through a quiet mind. Some feed. Some tend. Some simply press their bodies against warm walls and remain motionless, perhaps digesting, perhaps listening, perhaps allowing their own internal fluids to circulate. There is no rush. There is no panic of abundance. The lushness of the place is not the lushness of overflow; it is the lushness of careful retention. Every leaf is a storehouse. Every stem is a corridor. Every swollen pod is a locked treasury.

When afternoon cools, the work changes.

The inhabitants begin to close what the day has opened. They seal pores in wax panels. They draw husk-covers over nursery chambers. They retreat from exposed ledges. As the temperature drops, delicate crystals begin to form along the edges of salt shelves, and the basin becomes even more mineral, even more pale, even more precise. The creatures do not admire this in any human way, but they respond to it. Cold makes some surfaces brittle; they reinforce them. Night makes some passages too dry; they line them. The settlement contracts inward, not in fear, but in thrift.
