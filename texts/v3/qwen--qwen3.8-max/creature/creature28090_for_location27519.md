What lives there is not obvious. A visitor from the plain above, if any visitor could squeeze through the fissure and keep from panic, would first believe the chamber itself is alive. That is not entirely wrong. The Pocket City of Weeping Stone has the feel of a body turned inward: walls like ribs, channels like veins, basins like open palms, the low ceiling like a held breath. But there are inhabitants in the narrower sense too, and they are as mineral and patient as the stone that holds them.

The first living bodies you would notice—if you stayed long enough for your eyes to learn the blue-gray dusk—are the Wetted.

They are not numerous. There may be dozens, perhaps fewer, depending on the season of the water and the health of the fungal shelves. They do not gather in crowds. They move singly or in pairs along the raised walkways, sliding their hands along grooves in the stone, pausing to taste a bead of condensation, to press an ear against a sounding jar, to clear a thread of calcite from a drain. Their bodies have become suited to the pocket. They are small-boned and narrow through the chest, not from delicacy but from generations of squeezing through low ledges and tight wall-passages. Their shoulders are rounded, their arms long, their fingers broad and strong. The soles of their feet are thick and gripping, accustomed to wet stone, to narrow rims, to sudden changes in texture.

Their skin is pale, but not the clean white of milk. It is the pale of wet chalk, of fish bellies, of fungal skins: grayish, faintly luminous in places where mineral film has settled into the creases. Many have patches of rust-brown staining along the arms and ribs where iron-rich water has touched them over years. Their hair is thin and sparse, often bound away or shaved close, because hair holds damp and becomes a nuisance in the close chambers. Some wear caps of cured fungal leather, stiffened and shaped to shed drips away from the neck. Others have no covering at all, their scalps marked with notched patterns pressed into the skin when young, scar-lines that mirror the channels carved in the walls.

Their eyes are large, but not beautiful in the way surface songs mean beauty. They are wide and dark, quick to gather light, slow to blink. In the rare flare of a fat-lamp, their faces look suddenly sharp and startled, as if they have been caught in the middle of listening. They do not rely wholly on sight. They navigate by sheen, by texture, by the memory of where every ridge and basin lies. Their fingertips are constantly moving, reading the stone. A newcomer would think they are touching idly, but they are not. They are checking moisture, temperature, vibration, the minute grit that means a channel is beginning to clog.

They speak little with the voice. Sound behaves badly in the pocket. A raised word becomes a slap, a cry becomes a flood of echoes, a laugh can rattle through the jars and disturb the whole balance of listening. So the Wetted have made language out of restraint. They use taps, clicks, the scrape of a nail across stone, the soft pop of a tongue against the palate, and, most importantly, the manipulation of water. A drop allowed to fall into one basin rather than another can carry meaning. A finger trailed across a wet wall can be a warning, a question, a greeting. Their names are not spoken so much as patterned: a rhythm of two or three drips, a particular resonance against a jar, a touch repeated in the dark. To know someone in the Pocket City is to know the exact pressure of their hand and the tempo of their step.

They are not gentle because they are kind. They are gentle because the place punishes carelessness. A broken channel can waste water into a lower hollow where it becomes stagnant. A disturbed fungal mat can darken for a season. A clumsy foot can crack a calcite lip that took centuries to form. So they move with a deliberation that looks like reverence. They do not hurry. They do not waste motion. When they must work quickly, it is with a terrifying economy, every gesture already known.

Their work is endless, but it is not the work of miners or builders in the usual sense. They do not dig outward. They maintain inward. They are tenders of damp. They scrape excess mineral from the grooves before it seals a channel shut. They guide new seepage into old courses with clay and stone dust. They clear bacterial film from basins when it grows too thick and sour, and let it thicken elsewhere where it will serve as food. They test the water by taste and smell, knowing which threads of rust mean a change in the rock above, which coolness means a new crack has opened toward the saturated plain, which faint bitterness warns that something has died in a lower pool.

Every surface in the city has been assigned to a task, and every task has a keeper. The high ceiling is watched by the Drip-Readers, those who lie back in hollow niches and listen for the first fat beads forming in the stone pores. They can tell by sound whether a drop will fall freely or crawl sideways, whether the ceiling is sweating evenly or preparing to leak in a dangerous thread. The carved channels belong to the Water-Wardens, who know the whole branching path of moisture from the first dark seam to the final sounding jar. The basins belong to the Basin-Keepers, who decide whether a pool should be kept clear for drinking, allowed to culture pale microbial skins, or left still as a listening mirror. The fungal shelves belong to the Lace-Cutters, who harvest only when the growth is thick enough and never take all. The clay joints belong to the Mud-Pressers, who keep the city sealed against unwanted weeping.

There are also the Fat-Keepers, and they are perhaps the most careful of all.

Below the walkways, in the black water of the lower hollows, live the well-whelps. They are not numerous either. They are pale, eyeless, soft-bodied creatures, somewhere between eel and seal in their movement, though no surface animal matches them exactly. Their bodies are long and heavy, slick with a mucus that shines faintly when disturbed. They have small, almost vestigial forelimbs and broad tails that move slowly through the dark water. Their faces are blunt and lipless, full of sensitive barbels and taste pits. They are not stupid. They are simply slow, and their slowness is suited to the cold, food-poor water. They feed on bacterial films, fungal scraps, and the occasional dead thing that drifts down into the lower pools. In turn, their bodies store fat.

The Wetted do not hunt the well-whelps. Hunting would be foolish. Instead, they tend them in the rough way one tends a sacred and fragile herd. They feed them cuttings of fungal skin and scraped microbial mats. They guide them away from stagnant pockets. They sing to them in the only sense the place allows: low, almost inaudible taps along the basin rims, patterns the whelps learn to follow. When a whelp dies, it is rendered with ceremony and grief, because its fat is light and light is precious. When a whelp is born, the event is noted by notch and touch, and the basin is watched more closely for days. The Wetted do not love the well-whelps as pets. They love them as one loves a necessary organ of one’s own body.

When a lamp is lit, it is always a small event. The shallow stone dish holds only a little rendered fat. The wick is made from twisted fungal fiber or the dried skins of microbial mats. The flame is low, blue-edged, trembling. It does not brighten the chamber so much as it rearrange the darkness. Faces emerge from the wall and then sink back. Hands become visible. The fine work of repair becomes possible: setting a chip of basalt, sewing a tear in a fungal cloak, carving a new notch into a record ledge. The Wetted do not sit around lamps for comfort. They use them and extinguish them. To waste light is to forget where one is.

Their food is mineral-colored. Nothing green ever touches it. They eat pressed cakes of fungal flesh, pale and faintly sweet in the way of cold water and stone dust. They scrape the thicker bacterial mats into curds and let them sour in shallow pits. They harvest the tiny eyeless crustaceans that live in some of the lower pools, boiling them in stone cups with heated pebbles when fuel can be spared. They drink water constantly, but not carelessly. They taste it, measure it, sometimes mix it with dissolved mineral salts kept in little clay vessels. They do not feast. They do not starve often either. Their bodies have learned to move on little. Their hunger is slow, their thirst precise.

Clothing among the Wetted is rare and practical. Wet fabric is a danger, so they do not wear much. They use wraps of fungal leather cured in dry upper niches, stiff and water-shedding. They wear aprons when working with clay or sharp tools. They grease their shoulders and chests with rendered fat when the cold becomes biting, though they have grown so accustomed to damp that they tolerate what would chill a surface dweller into sickness. Ornament exists, but it is small and meaningful: beads of calcite, chips of dull copper, notched bands of bone or stone that record a person’s lineage, trade, or debts. Some wear tiny sounding shells—though there are no sea shells here, only carved stone imitations—against the throat, so that even their breathing adds a faint rhythm to the city.

The fungi are more than tools. They are more than light. They are the nearest thing the pocket has to weather.

The pale growths spread over the walls in branching skins, in shelf-like brackets, in veils like frozen lace. Some glow faintly blue. Some glow almost white, but so weakly that the eye must rest near them for minutes before the light becomes real. Some do not glow at all and are known only by touch: rough, dry, leathery in the upper niches; slick and soft where the seepage is constant. The Wetted do not plant them in the way farmers plant seed. They cannot. But they encourage certain growths and discourage others. They leave particular cracks unsealed. They divert moisture toward favored walls. They harvest only when the lace is thick, and they leave the root-threads undisturbed.

Over generations, the fungi have become entangled with the lives of the inhabitants in ways that are difficult to separate from architecture. A glowing thread may mark a safe path. A shelf of fungus may soften the sound of a busy basin. A dry skin in an upper niche may be used as a bandage, a wrapper, or a lamp wick. Some of the oldest growths are said to remember water. The Wetted believe—if “believe” is the right word for something so practical—that the fungal networks hold the history of every change in moisture, every flood, every drought, every new crack in the ceiling. They do not speak of this in grand terms. They simply pay attention when a patch of lace brightens without cause, or darkens after a drop that seemed ordinary.

Some of the Wetted carry fungal threads on their own bodies. This is not an infection, exactly, nor a decoration. It is a tolerated relationship. Thin pale filaments may settle into scars, into the creases of joints, into the shaved skin of the scalp. In the blue dusk, these traces can make a person look as though they are beginning to become wall. Children sometimes fear it. Adults accept it. Elders wear it with pride. A faint glow under the skin of a forearm can be useful in the dark; more than that, it is a sign that the body has learned to live without resisting the city’s damp.

There are those who have gone further.

In the deepest niches and beside the oldest sounding jars, one finds the Settled. They are not statues, though a quick glance might call them that. They are bodies—ancient bodies—partly mineralized, partly incorporated into the architecture of the pocket. Some sit in hollows carved to receive them, their backs fused to the wall by calcite crust. Some lie in shallow basins with their mouths slightly open, receiving a slow drip that has been tuned over generations to strike their lips or teeth or a stone cup placed near their throats. Some have been built into channels so that water passes over their shoulders and down their folded arms before falling into a jar below. Their skin, where it remains, is gray and hardened. Their bones have become supports. Their skulls have become resonating chambers.

The Wetted do not call the Settled dead. They do not call them fully alive in the same sense as a walking person. They say the Settled have “entered the long listening.” They are ancestors, specialists, volunteers, and sometimes—though this is spoken of with discomfort—those who broke the city’s careful laws and were given to the water as repayment. To become Settled is not always an honor, but it is always a transformation. Their bodies no longer move through the city; they have become part of its voice. When the water strikes them in the right way, they produce low notes, clicks, hums, and hollow resonances. These sounds are read by the Jar-Singers, who spend their lives learning which ancestor speaks in which rhythm and what a change in tone might mean.

The Jar-Singers are strange even among the Wetted. They are often blind, or nearly so, and their hands are always wet from working in basins. They keep the sounding jars tuned by adding or removing mineral crust, by changing the water level, by placing small stones or bone fragments inside. They do not sing as surface people sing. They make the jars answer one another. In their care, the pocket city becomes an instrument played by seepage. A visitor might hear only random drips. A Jar-Singer hears conversation, warning, memory, and sometimes the slow expression of something that might be contentment or hunger.

This is why the city is loud without being noisy. It is full of small sounds, but none are wasted. Water taps on stone. A bead slides down a groove and whispers. A basin answers another basin across the chamber. A fungal shelf creaks faintly as it swells with moisture. A well-whelp turns in the deep water. A Settled tooth clicks under a falling drop. The Wetted move through this web of sound as if through a language they were born inside. They do not need to fill silence. Silence here would mean something has stopped working. Silence is suspicion. Silence is danger.
