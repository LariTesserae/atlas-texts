The living here are not easy to count, because not all of them arrive as bodies.

Some are figures. Some are motions. Some are pressures held in cord. Some are reflections lying just beneath the surface of still water. If one begins with what can be seen from the low platform, the first answer is the rare shapes that move across the causeways: the caretakers, if that is what they are. They appear human only in the way a lashed frame can suggest a body, or a knot can suggest a face. They are tall without seeming heavy, slender without seeming fragile, their proportions just uncertain enough to make the eye pause. They walk slowly, not from weariness but from care, as if each step must be placed into a prepared space.

Their limbs are bound at the joints with pale cord. Not tightly enough to restrict them, but precisely enough to keep their parts aligned. Where one might expect skin, there is polished clay, resin-darkened fiber, or woven matting so fine it bends like cloth. Where one might expect hair, there are fine strands of reed, black rope, or threads of twisted bark, tied back or woven into caps that shed water. Their fingers are long and smooth at the tips, made for finding slack in knots, for testing the tension of cords, for pressing tiny imperfections out of wet clay. Their eyes are dark beads, shell-bright or water-bright, catching the glare without greed. They blink slowly. Sometimes they do not blink at all.

They do not seem to be numerous. One may walk a long time between them. They are scattered across the basin like the features of the landscape itself: one kneeling beside a planting bed, one standing at the center of a causeway as if listening to its length, one carrying a gourd from a square pool to a row of thirsty reeds. Their work appears unnecessary. They tighten cords that are already tight. They smooth clay that is already smooth. They pour water where water already stands. They touch things more than they use them. Their hands pass over surfaces as though checking whether the world is still holding its shape.

Perhaps they are builders who have finished building. Perhaps they are caretakers who have outlasted the need for care. Perhaps they are only the habit of making left behind after the makers withdrew.

They do not speak loudly, if they speak at all. Their speech is the tick of condensation falling from a leaf, the creak of a mat under weight, the small sound a cord makes when it is drawn just enough. When they communicate across open water, they may not use sound. They alter a tension somewhere, and the change travels along the woven floor until another figure feels it through the feet. A slight pull at a rail. A softened give in a plank. A bead of water shaken loose from a reed. These are their signals. Their language is made of adjustments.

They do not experience days in the usual way. There is no morning here, no evening, no gathering storm, no cooling dusk. Their time is the falling of condensation, the warming of a tile, the slow tick from leaf to basin, the moment when a pool’s surface becomes still enough to reflect a structure whole. They can remain motionless for as long as a heron. When they move, it is because some seam has asked for them. They know the region not by horizon but by joints: how many plank-seams lie between one enclosure and the next, how many knots mark a rail, how many times a channel forks before it reaches a stair. Their names, if they have names, are not sounds. They are locations. They are the third plank after the channel fork. They are the knot beside the water stair. They are the pool where the heron returns.

Some of them look barely outlined, as if the pattern has only recently remembered them. Others are so finished that their surfaces have the same gloss as resin beds, the same pale polish as shell tiles. A few seem worn not by age but by too much attention, their edges softened by repeated smoothing, their cords rubbed into a kind of velvet. None of them look young or old. They look maintained.

They do not seem to hunger. The fruit that hangs in the constructed beds is not harvested except to be moved, occasionally, from one permitted place to another. They do not eat in any way that leaves scraps. They may absorb more than they take. The wet air enters their seams. The heat presses into their clay. The light gives them outline. They are sustained by balance. If anything disturbs them, it is looseness: a cord giving way, a mat lifting, a level altering, a reflection broken too long. Such things do not happen often. If they do, several figures converge without apparent alarm and restore the fastening. Their nearest fear is not violence or flood. It is slackness.

They are not the only living things.

In the canals, fish hang in dark, patient groups, but they are not merely fish. Some move as if their bodies have been woven into being scale by scale. Their fins open with the stiffness of folded mats. Their sides catch the light in ways that suggest fiber rather than silver. They do not feed greedily. They drift through the channels as though checking the water’s memory, stirring only enough to remind it that it can still ripple. When one turns, it may reveal a flank stitched with pale thread, or an eye like a bead of black resin. These fish are not pets and not food. They are moving parts of the basin’s stillness.

Frogs rest on tiled ledges, their bellies pressed to the warmth of the clay. They look alive until one looks too long. Then their skin seems too much like damp green plaster, their legs too much like smoothed tools. Their throats swell and settle, but the sound they make is not a croak. It is a low, wet adjustment, a tiny pressure released. They may be keeping the ledges warm. They may be keeping the clay from forgetting the shape of heat.

Herons stand in square pools like pins holding the sky to the water. They are almost motionless, but their wings, when they open, creak like folded shelters. Their eyes are drops of oil. They do not seem to hunt. They seem to watch reflections more than fish. When a heron lifts one foot, the water below it holds the image of the foot for a moment longer than it should. The bird waits as though for that delayed reflection to catch up.

Turtles line the edges of walkways in rows too regular to be accidental. Their shells have the matte sheen of fired clay, the segmented neatness of fitted tile. They move only enough to remain aligned. They are boundary creatures. They mark where walkway ends and water begins, where a stair is permitted, where a pool must remain square. If one of them shifts, a nearby caretaker may pause until it settles again.

Insects skim the surface films, crawl over reed knots, and gather above flowering beds in subdued clouds. Their humming is quiet, dampened by the air. Some look like frayed ends of cord given motion. Some look like splinters of reed. Some look like tiny knots that have learned to fly. They do not swarm aggressively. They vibrate with restraint. They may be necessary for the flowering stems, or they may be the sound of the place thinking in very small units.

The distinction between born and made is weak among them. A frog may be clay remembering how to breathe. A fish may be a loose strand of the world given a spine. A heron may be a shelter that has decided, for now, to stand on one leg. They do not disturb the stillness. They inhabit the basin as though they were fastened into it.

But there are deeper inhabitants.

Beneath the visible figures, beneath the animals, beneath even the reflections, there are the underlayers. These are the compressed histories of making: old rope pressed into resin, charred timber, shell grit, broken baskets, fired shards, worn cords, thumbprints buried under newer plaster. They are not dead material. They are foundation-presences. They bear weight. They transmit pressure. When a foot falls on hollow planking, the sound does not echo, but it is answered somewhere below by a faint, dull knock. The underlayers speak in that way. They say: we are still holding.

The caretakers listen to them. One may see a figure kneel, press a palm flat to the matting, and remain there while condensation gathers on its shoulders. It is not praying. It is hearing the floor. It is checking whether the old cords beneath are still in agreement with the new surfaces above. The world here has been assembled downward in strata of craft, and every stratum remains inhabited. Nothing has been buried so deeply that it stops participating.

There are also the reflected ones.

They live in the water’s skin, not below it exactly, but within its capacity to remember. When a causeway is mirrored in still water, the reflection may show more arches than the causeway possesses. Those extra arches belong to them. When a pool reflects a sky brighter than the one overhead, that brightness is theirs. When a bird passes overhead twice in exactly the same manner, the second bird may be one of them, repeating the first to make sure the first remains true.

The reflected ones are pale, patient, and easily wounded. A ripple is a blow to them. If the surface is disturbed too long, they lose cohesion. They scatter into fragments of light and line, waiting for the water to become calm again. Then they reassemble, sometimes with a small error: a leaf reversed, a shadow misplaced, a reed pattern repeated too perfectly across a wide field. The visible world accepts these errors without protest. They are part of the basin’s thinness at the seams.

It is possible that the reflected ones are the true caretakers, and the visible figures are only their hands reaching upward into the bright air. It is possible that the visible figures are the true ones, and the reflections are merely their habits copied too faithfully. It is possible that neither is original. Perhaps both are copies of a pattern that no longer has a source.

The open spaces are alive too.

The broad sheets of still water, the bare matting, the pale intervals between shelters, the long emptiness between meaningful features—these are not absence. They are residents of distance. They live as pauses. They keep the figures from becoming crowded. They keep the shelters from touching. They keep the sky wide and the heat evenly distributed. They are the quiet ones, the unmoving ones, the ones made of glare and restraint.

To walk through an open interval is to feel watched by something without eyes. The emptiness does not threaten. It attends. It measures the walker by seams crossed, knots passed, joints traversed. It allows movement but does not encourage haste. In some places the openness seems to breathe very slowly, expanding only by shimmer, contracting only by a faint tightening of light. These intervals may be older than the causeways. They may be the first things made here, if making can begin with the decision to leave room.

And then there are the makers who are no longer visible.

They live in the thumbprints. They live in the scrape marks left by hard-edged flakes. They live in the slight asymmetries that prove the work was done by bodies rather than engines. They live in every cord that still carries the twist of a hand. They are the absent hands, the already-lifted arms, the pressure that remains after the tool has been set down. They may be the truest inhabitants of the basin, because every visible being moves inside the shape they left behind.

The caretakers may be their gestures continuing. The animals may be their attention given smaller forms. The reflections may be their memory lying on the water. The underlayers may be their first decisions, compressed but not forgotten. Nothing here has departed completely. Even withdrawal has been woven into the pattern.

What lives there, then, is not a single people.

It is a population of maintenance.

The figures on the causeways are part of it. The woven fish, the clay frogs, the heron-pins, the tiled turtles, the cord-insects are part of it. The reflected ones are part of it. The underlayers are part of it. The open intervals are part of it. The absent makers are part of it. Even the water’s slow weeping from wattled walls is part of it. All these lives share one condition: they do not seek to alter the basin. They seek to keep it true.

They do not build new cities. They do not expand into denser arrangements. They do not accumulate. Their labor is not progress. It is return. They pour water into water. They smooth clay into clay. They tighten cord against cord. They place fruit beside fruit. They align turtle beside walkway. They wait for reflections to settle. They perform the same small acts with the gravity of rites, because those acts are the basin’s breath.

If there is a society here, it is a society of intervals. Each being is placed far enough from the next to remain distinct, yet near enough to participate in the same equilibrium. There are no crowds. No markets. No quarrels heard across the bright water. No children cry. No elder decays. No procession moves toward a shrine except in the sense that every movement is already a procession toward the next required adjustment. When two figures meet, they do not embrace. They may exchange a tool. They may touch a cord together. They may stand side by side over a pool until the reflection becomes whole. Then they part.

Their emotional life, if it can be called emotional, is composed of satisfactions and uneases so subtle they resemble weather. A snug knot gives something like peace. A smooth plastered surface gives something like approval. A still pool gives something like trust. A slack cord gives something close to sorrow, though not dramatic sorrow: only the recognition that a pattern has become vulnerable. They do not lament. They repair.

They do not seem to dream, yet the basin has dream-qualities. Repeated birds. Extra arches. Paths that do not enlarge with approach. Shadows falling faintly toward the light. These wrongnesses are not attacks on the place. They are its imagination. They are the signs that a world made so completely has begun to generate its own interior weather. The inhabitants live inside that weather. They accommodate it. A caretaker may pause before a row of reeds that repeats too perfectly, then touch one stem until the repetition becomes intentional again. A fish may pass through a reflection that belongs to a different sky, and the water will not boil or break. It will only dim, then brighten.

What do they want?

If wanting belongs to them at all, they want continuation. They want the water to remain at its fixed level. They want the heat to press without burning. They want the mats to stay supple. They want the clay to remember its thumbprints. They want the cords to hold their twist. They want the reflections to be trusted. They want the underlayers to remain in agreement. They want the intervals to remain open. They want the finished state to continue being finished, moment after moment, without needing to become anything else.

This is why their existence feels uncanny. Life is usually associated with growth, hunger, decay, change. Here life has been fitted into completion. It has been made stable. The fruit hangs ripe and does not drop. The reeds stay pale at the cut. The resin keeps its gloss. The water weeps without flooding. The heat presses without drying. The inhabitants move without advancing. They are alive, but their life is the life of a held breath.

Perhaps that is why the rare figures seem less like individuals and more like organs of a larger body. The basin may be alive as one thing, and they are its ways of knowing where it is still tight, where it is still wet, where it is still true. When a figure kneels at the edge of a planting bed, it is not merely tending plants. It is checking the boundary between growth and pattern. When a fish turns in a canal, it is not merely swimming. It is testing the weave of water. When a heron stands motionless, it is holding the sky in place with its stillness. When a turtle shifts along the edge, it is reminding the walkway where it ends. When a drop falls from a leaf, it is continuing the basin’s breath.

And perhaps the truest inhabitant is not any one of them.

Perhaps the truest inhabitant is the maintenance itself.

It has no single face. It passes from hand to cord, from cord to mat, from mat to clay, from clay to water, from water to reflection, from reflection to the figure that pauses to let it settle. It is the ongoing act of keeping the basin exact. It lives in the space between tightening and being tight, between smoothing and being smooth, between pouring and being full. It is not a god. It is not a ruler. It is more like a custom that has become a creature. It feeds on repetition. It rests in equilibrium. It breathes through condensation. It thinks in knots.

The figures serve it, or express it, or are made by it. The animals serve it, or express it, or are made by it. The reflections serve it, or express it, or are made by it. Even the emptiness serves it, because emptiness is what maintenance needs in order to move.

So if one asks who lives there, the answer cannot be only the rare figures crossing the causeways with gourds and coils of fiber. They are the most visible, but they are not alone.

The woven fish live there.

The clay frogs live there.

The herons with folded-mat wings live there.

The turtles aligned like boundary tiles live there.

The insects like frayed cords live there.

The reflected ones with their extra arches and borrowed skies live there.

The underlayers of old rope and charred timber live there.

The open intervals live there.

The thumbprints of absent makers live there.

The quiet itself lives there.

And the maintenance lives there, moving through all of them, keeping the bright wet basin in its long, stable, finished breath.

They live there the way a knot lives: by being held.
