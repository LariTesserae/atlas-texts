What lives in the high drowned country is not easy to count, because very little there begins and ends the way a fish begins and ends, or a bird, or a walking animal. The first truth of the place is saturation. The second is that saturation produces a different kind of self. Most of what lives there is spread out, layered, colonial, membranous, or patient to the point of appearing mineral. If one insists on using the word “people,” one must be willing to apply it to a carpet of black root-felt, a drifting veil of translucent tissue, a thermal rain, a slow chemical decision moving through a forest of swollen stems, or an echo that repeats before the sound that made it has fully arrived.

There are inhabitants. There are even, in a certain sense, persons. But they are not persons of the quick, bounded, tool-holding kind. They are persons of texture, gradient, and long memory. They live in a world where the difference between organism and environment has been worn thin by age, warmth, pressure, and endless biological reweaving. To live there is to be partly dissolved into the medium. To be intelligent there is not to command the world, but to participate in it so deeply that one’s thoughts become currents, one’s memories become sediment, and one’s body becomes a place where other lives settle.

The oldest residents are the undermats.

They are the dark, liver-colored carpets that knit the silt and stone together in the lowest basins. From a distance they seem merely ground. They are not ground. They are living compressions, generation laid over generation, tissue folded into tissue until what appears to be a floor is actually an archive. In some places the undermats are thin as skin. In others they are dozens of body-lengths thick, compacted into a firm, springy substance that yields slightly under pressure and releases faint bubbles of ancient gas when pierced. They are black, rust-stained, olive-brown, and occasionally a deep bruised purple where mineral-rich water has seeped through them for centuries.

The undermats do not think quickly. They do not need to. Their intelligence, if it can be called that, is chemical and structural. They sense changes in acidity, warmth, dissolved sugars, mineral salts, spore density, and the microscopic debris of other organisms. They respond by tightening, loosening, expanding, sealing pores, releasing filaments, or altering the taste of the water around them. A disturbance may travel across an undermat as a wave of altered calcium signals, a souring of the local chemistry, or a slow pulse of warmth. It may take years for the mat to “understand” what has touched it. It may take decades to decide whether to incorporate the disturbance, reject it, or grow around it.

They are the keepers of the drowned country’s memory. Not memory as story, but memory as residue. A leaf that falls into a mat is not eaten in the ordinary sense; it is entered. Its veins are threaded by filaments. Its substance is sorted into sugars, minerals, toxins, and structural fibers. Its pattern is, in some way, retained: the shape of the leaf, the chemistry of the water when it fell, the presence or absence of certain spores at that time. The undermats remember by becoming marked. They are full of such marks. A swimmer passing low enough to brush one might leave behind a faint thermal impression that remains for a season, a slight alteration in the mat’s surface, a memory of foreign salts.

Some undermats are so old and extensive that they behave like one vast communal body. Others are divided into local factions, each with its own color, odor, and rhythm of pore-opening. They do not war, but they negotiate. When two mats meet across a shallow rise, their edges may interlace for centuries, exchanging chemical phrases, testing compatibility, sometimes merging into a blended zone, sometimes remaining separate along a seam no wider than a hand. These seams can be seen from above as faint lines of different hue: rust beside olive, black beside umber, a living boundary written across the floor.

The undermats also make the first kind of building in the drowned country. They build by accumulation and contraction. Over long ages, they draw fine particles together, bind them with filaments, and compress them into living felt. Where they have grown around old structures, they preserve them in softened form. A ring of stones becomes a ring of root-felt. A causeway becomes a raised ribbon of compressed tissue. A hollow sphere becomes a node in a breathing network. The undermats do not intend architecture as shelter. They intend it as continuity. They make places where life can hold on.

Above the undermats, in the middle twilight, live the frond peoples.

They are not trees, though they have the gravity of trees. They are not animals, though some of them move, and some of them hunt, and some of them show what can only be called preference. Their stems are cartilaginous and buoyed, swollen in places with bladders of gas or warm light liquid. Their leaves vary wildly by lineage: broad and torn like old cloth, narrow and feathered like dark plumes, latticed like sieves, ribboned like ceremonial banners, or cupped into hollows that gather drifting spores. Their colors are deep green, bronze, purple-black, oily blue, and sometimes a dim red so dark it seems to be a stain rather than a pigment.

The frond peoples are rooted, but not always permanently. Some groves remain fixed to one rise for centuries, anchored by root-clumps that grip the undermat and exchange nutrients with it. Others are more nomadic. These release their lower holds during certain chemical tides, become buoyant, and drift a short distance before settling again. Their migration is slow enough that it can be mistaken for the swaying of plants in a current, except that over years the grove has moved. It may advance one body-length in a decade, or one full basin in an age. When a drifting grove encounters another, they may intertwine, exchange spores, or release clouds of recognition chemicals that smell, if one could smell them, like wet bark, warm metal, and crushed leaves.

They feed in several ways. Their broad surfaces absorb dissolved nutrients. Some contain microbial symbionts that make sugars from the weak green-gold light. Others are more predatory in a vegetal manner: their fronds curl around dense spore clouds and digest them over weeks; their leaf-pits secrete enzymes into captured organic matter; their root-cilia draw in microscopic life with patient suction. A hunt among the frond peoples may begin when a current carries a rich plume of spores into a grove. The nearest fronds will turn, not with sudden motion, but with a slow thickening of one side, a subtle alteration in buoyancy, a gradual leaning. The spore cloud is enclosed. Digestion may last a season. The grove will brighten slightly afterward, its edges producing more phosphorescence, its bladders swelling with new gas.

They communicate by pressure, chemistry, and faint light. Along the edges of certain fronds, bioluminescence appears in slow pulses: a greenish glimmer, a blue flicker, a cold amber glow. These lights are not signals meant for eyes so much as changes in the local medium, visible only because the water is so saturated with living particles. A frond’s glow may mean that it is ready to release spores, that it has detected unusual warmth, that it is withdrawing from a neighboring growth, or that it is digesting. Sometimes a whole grove will pulse in sequence, not because there is a single mind directing it, but because the chemical state of one stem provokes the next, and the next, until the signal travels across the forest like breath through lungs.

If there is a people most like “persons” in the drowned country, it may be these frond lineages. They have individuality without sharp boundaries. Each grove has a characteristic shape, a local temperament, a preferred density of spores, a way of opening its leaf-chambers. Some are tolerant of drifting organisms and allow small veil-life to nest among their ribs. Some are exclusive, curling their fronds inward and releasing bitter compounds to discourage attachment. Some grow into great arched chambers, their stems bending over one another until they create still pockets of water inside. These chambers are among the most intimate places in the drowned country. Within them, spores hang unmoving, light is reduced to faint biological sparks, and the water tastes of lineage. They may be nurseries. They may be sanctuaries. They may be the frond peoples’ way of holding a private world inside the public sea.

The frond peoples also die in a manner unlike animal death. When a stem fails, it does not simply fall and rot. It is entered by filaments, threaded by rootlets, colonized by tube-growths and pale wormlike tenants. Its bladders collapse one by one. Its leaves become ribbons, then lace, then drifting particulate matter. The grove absorbs part of the substance. The undermat receives the rest. The dead stem may remain upright for years, a softened pillar full of small lives, until it becomes indistinguishable from the living architecture around it. Death there is not a sudden departure. It is a redistribution.

Higher in the water, among the hanging veils and drifting meadows, live the curtain beings.

They are looser, stranger, and less easy to describe as individuals. Some are rooted to floating knots of debris and extend long filaments downward like tassels. Some are entirely free, drifting in broad sheets of translucent tissue. Some form rafts of bead-like bladders, each bead housing a different microbial colony. Some are thin as condensed mist and can pass through the fingers without resistance. Others are elastic membranes that close slowly after being touched, leaving a faint taste in the water. They make the upper layers of the drowned country into a crowded ceiling, a living roof that is constantly brushing against itself.

Among these curtains live the veil-grazers. They are large, rare, and nearly transparent. A veil-grazer may be as broad as a room or as narrow as a cloak, but it has almost no substance. It seems made of compressed fog, with faint thickening along its edges where filaments trail behind it. It moves by subtle contractions, by catching thermal layers, by riding the slow internal weather of the sea. Its body is so thin that light does not so much pass through it as become slightly delayed by it. When a veil-grazer passes through a spore cloud, the spores do not scatter violently; they align, as if drawn briefly into a pattern, then resume their drift.

No one knows whether a veil-grazer is a single organism or a temporary alliance of smaller ones. It may be both. It may be a colony that becomes a body only under certain conditions: a particular temperature, a spore density, a chemical signal released by the undermats below. Some appear only during spore storms, spreading themselves across the darkened water to filter the dense reproductive clouds. Others appear after gas releases, their membranes shimmering with trapped bubbles. They may feed by absorbing dissolved organic matter across their entire surface. They may also feed by creating gentle vortices that draw particles toward them without touching them directly. Their filaments do not seem to grasp. They seem to invite.

The veil-grazers are among the beings most likely to be mistaken for weather. A drifting form may cross a swimmer’s path and leave no sensation except a sudden change in pressure, a dimming of nearby light, and a brief silence in the ordinary rustle of filaments. The water where it passed may taste cleaner, or older, or faintly sweet. Sometimes one of them descends into the frond forests, its translucent body folding around the crowns of the stems without breaking them. When this happens, the fronds often pulse with bioluminescence, and the veil-grazer responds by releasing tiny sparks along its margin. Whether this is communication, alarm, or feeding is unclear. It may be all three.

There are also the rain-walkers.

They live in the strange internal weather of the drowned country, especially where warm liquid rises and condenses into descending curtains of fresher water. These rain-curtains are not air-born rain; they are water falling through water, vertical rivers within the sea. The beings that inhabit them are adapted to boundaries. They live at the trembling edge where one kind of water slides past another. Some are lens-shaped colonies, clear and trembling, no larger than a seed. Some are filament rings that spin slowly in the descending current. Some are soft, bead-bodied things that gather on the underside of floating mats and drip downward in chains, as if gravity has become uncertain and they are testing it.

Rain-walkers are quick by the standards of the drowned country, though still slow by the standards of animal life. They may complete a generation in a single rain-curtain, encysting when the curtain weakens, then emerging when another forms. They feed on dissolved sugars, mineral droplets, and the microscopic life concentrated at thermal boundaries. They are often luminous in faint points, so that a rain-curtain inhabited by them looks like a curtain of cold stars. From within, passing through such a curtain means passing through a storm of tiny living lights, each one touching the skin with a barely perceptible tick.

They are not numerous everywhere, but where they are present they give the place a sense of delicate activity. They gather at the edges of warm columns. They ride the internal waterfalls. They cluster where the sea makes rain inside itself. They are perhaps the closest thing the drowned country has to birds: not because they fly, but because they inhabit weather as a medium of freedom.

The few animal-like beings are even rarer and more ambiguous.

Some are pale, wormlike forms that live inside root knots. They may be animals. They may be specialized organs of larger plants. They may be something older than that distinction. They have no eyes, no obvious mouths, no limbs. Their bodies are smooth, white or faintly pink, segmented by rings of muscle or growth. They move through the undermat by peristalsis, opening tiny channels, pruning dead filaments, carrying spores from one pocket to another. Sometimes they emerge from a root knot and hang in the water like a loose thread, tasting the current. If the water chemistry is right, they withdraw. If it is not, they may remain still for days, as if waiting for the world to become tolerable again.

Others are tubular growths that open only when certain conditions are met. They resemble pale horns, cups, or folded sleeves attached to stems and stones. When closed, they look like swellings in the tissue of the frond forests. When open, they reveal soft inner surfaces lined with cilia. These cilia create minute currents, drawing in particles, expelling waste, or perhaps exchanging signals with the surrounding water. Some of these tubes may be filter-feeders. Some may be sensory organs for the grove. Some may be dwellings for wormlike tenants. The distinction is often impossible. A tube may open, accept a drifting spore, close around it, and later release a small pale form that may have been an animal all along.

There are also the net-growths, black and patient, spread across currents. They are among the predatory forms of the drowned country, though their predation has no urgency. They look like lace made of dark glass or burnt thread. They hang in places where water moves in slow spirals, catching organic matter, dead cells, shed films, and sometimes small drifting organisms. They do not strike. They wait. What they catch, they digest over long periods. A net-growth may remain motionless for a year, then contract by a fraction of its width as it absorbs enough matter to produce a new branch. Its hunt is not a chase but a placement: it chooses a current, unfolds itself, and lets time do the killing.

These sparse animal-like forms are not dominant. They are tenants, maintenance workers, minor agents within a larger living system. The drowned country does not belong to animals. It belongs to the vegetal, the fungal, the microbial, and the colonial. Its drama is not the flash of teeth or the panic of pursuit. Its drama is the slow closing of a frond around a cloud of spores, the gradual spread of a black net across a current, the century-long tightening of a root around a stone.

Yet there are builders.

They are not builders in the way of masons or engineers. They do not raise walls against weather. They do not make tools. They do not leave sharp marks. They are the gesture-makers, the arrangers, the beings responsible for the faint remains scattered across the drowned plain: the rings of fitted stones, the blurred causeways, the spirals of hollow seed-like spheres, the low mounds pierced with tunnels.
