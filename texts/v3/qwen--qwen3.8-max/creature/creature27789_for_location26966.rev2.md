What lives there is not one resident but a slow, wet census.

The basin is tenanted in layers. Some of its inhabitants are so small they exist as texture rather than shape: mites threading between root hairs, larvae curling and uncurling in the dark canals, soft-bodied colonies vibrating inside hollow stems. Others are larger, but no less careful. The place does not hold life the way a nest holds eggs; it holds life the way water holds salt—throughout, invisibly, and with consequence.

If one begins with the beings most responsible for the city’s ordered shape, one begins with the tenders.

They are not tall. Most are no larger than a forearm when curled, and the biggest among them, when they lift themselves fully, reach little higher than a kneeling person’s knee. Their bodies are made for closeness: low, dense, narrow at the shoulders, broad at the hands and feet. They move through the woven causeways one at a time because the causeways were grown for exactly that—a single deliberate body, balanced and unhurried.

Their skin is not like human skin. It has the damp, matte quality of soaked leaf-flesh and liverwort, mottled green, bone-white, and black where sediment has settled into its folds. Across their backs and shoulders grow fine root-like filaments, not quite fur, not quite moss, which tremble when water shifts nearby. These filaments are sensitive. They taste the air before it becomes air. They read condensation before it falls. In older tenders, the growth thickens into soft felted plaques where tiny organisms settle and live, so that an elder at rest can seem less like a creature than a low shelf of vegetation.

Their faces are blunt and broad, with nostril flaps that close in water and a mouth made more for scraping, cutting, and carrying than for biting. Their teeth are small, edged like shell, and used on fibrous plants, cured larvae, and the tough outer skins of reed stems. Their eyes are large, black, and dull rather than bright, adapted not to true sight but to the faintest blue-green emanations of living light. They do not see far. They do not need to. The basin is not a place of distance. It is a place of nearness.

Their hands are their finest instruments. Each hand has more fingers than a human hand, short and strong, with palms that can grip wet stem without slipping. From the palms extend minute rootlets, not unlike the tendrils of young growth, which can press into plant surfaces and sense the movement of water through them. A tender can lay one hand against a wall and tell whether the roots beyond are thirsty, clogged, diseased, or being gnawed from the other side. They work by touch as much as by sight. In the deepest dark, they work almost entirely by touch.

They are amphibious in the way cold marsh things are amphibious: not dramatically, not with sleek speed, but with long patience. They can remain submerged for a great while, their breathing slowed until their bodies seem part of the water. Along their ribs and necks are narrow fronds that flutter faintly when they are young and become stiffer with age, aiding them in water even after their lungs take on more of the work. When they surface, their breath may pale in the cold, but it does so thinly, as if even warmth is rationed here.

They smell of the basin: crushed mint, fish oil, wet fiber, and the faint ammonia sharpness of rich animal life compressed into a small space. This smell clings to their shelters, their tools, their woven platforms. It is not unpleasant so much as intimate. It is the smell of a place that does not ventilate itself to the outside world.

They are not silent because they lack voices. They are silent because silence is survival.

Their speech is mostly pressure, tap, and glow. They communicate by striking root walls in patterns so soft they feel more like a change in the air than a sound. They send messages through water by flicking stems, releasing bubbles against submerged wood, or pressing their palms to the surface and creating pulses too small for an outside ear to notice. When they must use light, they do so through cultivated bioluminescence: a smear of glowing slime on a knot, a string of luminous secretion drawn along a canal edge, a brief pulse from a pouch of living tissue carried against the body. These signals are rare and deliberate. Too much glow is waste. Too much waste attracts the wrong attention.

They have names, but not names spoken into open air. A name here is a rhythm, a touch-pattern, a scent signature, a particular way of disturbing water. To know someone is to know how they move through the dark without breaking it.

The tenders are the basin’s gardeners, plumbers, weavers, and wardens, though none of those words fits cleanly. They do not build in the way surface builders build. They persuade. They tie a young stem in a certain direction and return seasons later to find it has become a rail. They wound a root in a precise place so that scar tissue forms into a lip over which water will sheet rather than drip. They press chewed leaf paste into cracks to encourage slime colonies that will later glow along a threshold. They use bone pegs not merely as fasteners but as guides for living growth, setting them where roots will eventually take the shape desired.

Their tools are few and precious. Shell blades are set into root notches and used for cutting reed, scraping hide, or opening seed pods. Bone hooks hang in rows, pale and polished, each one carved from the remains of an animal that has already passed through the basin’s food web. Thorns serve as awls. Hardened root-wood becomes levers and pins. Fiber is braided into lashings, nets, slings, and carrying bands. Nothing is casual. Nothing is disposable. A broken tool is not thrown away; it is re-tied, re-sharpened, or planted into the structure of a wall where it can serve as a peg, a hook, or a perch.

They wear very little. Clothing in the basin would mostly become another wet surface. Still, they adorn themselves functionally: braided belts of grass, small resin-sealed pouches, shoulder wraps woven from bladderweed when they must carry eggs or delicate cuttings. Some wear living coils around the wrist or throat—trained rootlets that continue to grow slowly, holding shell tools or bone picks close to the body. Their ornaments are practical first and symbolic second. A polished shell disk may mark skill in maintaining a sluice. A knot of pale fibers may mean one has survived a dangerous flood pulse. A smear of blue-green resin may indicate responsibility for a nursery channel.

They are few in number because the basin is small and the balance narrow. There are not enough of them to fill every platform or shelter, and they do not wish to. Empty spaces are part of the settlement’s health. They leave platforms bare so water can move. They leave chambers open so predators can pass through. They leave stretches of black water untouched because some things need stillness to breed, hunt, or rest. Their restraint is not austerity for its own sake. It is knowledge. They understand that a city built inside a living wet pocket can fail by becoming too full.

Their social order is likewise compressed and careful. There is no single chief, no loud authority. Responsibility shifts according to season, need, and sensitivity. One tender may be best at reading the temperature of the reservoir; another may know which root walls are about to overgrow a channel; another may have the patience to tend egg masses without disturbing them. Decisions are made by attention. They gather at the edge of the deepest pool, place their hands on root rails, and feel what the water is doing. If the flow has changed, they respond. If a wall is growing too fast, they prune. If a predator has entered a nursery canal, they reroute movement until the danger passes. They do not issue commands so much as arrive at shared motion.

Their labor is constant but unhurried.

At any given time, one may be clearing a blocked sluice with a thorn pick, another weaving new stems into a low terrace, another pressing shell-grit into a soft bank to improve drainage. Some harvest fibers from the upper root-ceiling where condensation is thickest. Others collect the slow, nutritious drift that gathers in filter hollows: shed skins, dead larvae, broken egg casings, fine organic silt. Nothing is allowed to remain merely dead. Death is moved into growth. A corpse, whether insect, fish, or tender, becomes part of the substrate if it cannot be preserved. The basin has no separate place for waste. Waste is misplaced food. Misplaced food is danger.

They eat what the basin gives, and they cultivate what they eat. Their diet is not generous but varied: larvae caught with fine bone hooks, small eyeless fish drawn from deeper channels, bladderweed seeds pressed into dense cakes, root pith soaked until its bitterness leaches out, soft fungal knots scraped from old stems, and the rich paste that forms where filtered animal matter settles into living moss. They do not cook with fire; fire is unthinkable here. Instead they cure, ferment, dry, and cold-smoke in the limited ways available to them. Racks of drying reeds hold strips of flesh and seed-cakes in the faint currents that move between root walls. Some foods are sealed in resinous sap and stored in cool hollows. Others are wrapped in leaf and lowered into slow water where time and bacteria do the work of transformation.

They are careful eaters. They do not harvest from one channel until it is emptied. They rotate feeding grounds. They leave breeding populations alone for entire seasons. They know which egg masses may be thinned and which must be protected. They understand that predation is not cruelty but maintenance. If they take a life, they do so with the same quiet efficiency with which they tie a knot. There is no ceremony of triumph. There is only use, thanks expressed through restraint, and the return of residue to the living floor.

Their young begin as light.

Egg masses hang beneath causeways and along protected root shelves, jelly-like clusters faintly luminous in the dark. Each cluster contains dozens of curled shapes, some belonging to the tenders themselves, some to other basin creatures whose survival is woven into the same system. The tenders do not always distinguish between these in the way an outsider might expect. They tend what needs tending. They clean egg casings with gentle strokes of their fingers. They adjust water flow so that oxygen reaches the clusters without tearing them loose. They move predators away with soft taps and barriers. They know the difference between a healthy glow and a sick one.

When the young hatch, they are not small versions of the adults. They are translucent, drifting things with slow-beating gills and almost no will beyond hunger and concealment. They live among root hairs and in the finer channels where larger bodies cannot follow. They feed on microscopic growth and on the dissolved richness that seeps from the plant walls. Many do not survive. This is accepted. The basin is a nursery and a trap in the same breath. Those that endure gradually thicken, darken, and develop the gripping hands, valve nostrils, and sensory filaments of adulthood. Even then, they remain small for a long time. Growth here is slow because warmth is scarce and every new body must be supported by the whole system.

Elders are not always easy to distinguish from the architecture.

Their movements become more spaced. Their root-filaments lengthen and whiten. Moss may settle on their shoulders and not be brushed away. Some choose resting hollows near warm root clusters and remain there for long periods, emerging only to feed or advise. There are stories told in tap-rhythm of elders who ceased moving entirely and became part of a wall, but these stories are not quite tales of death. In the basin, the boundary between body and dwelling is not firm. A wall can be alive. A living thing can become structure. The tenders understand this not as horror but as continuity.

They are not the only inhabitants.

The canals belong to other bodies as well. Among them are the pale sluice-kin: long, limbless creatures without eyes, their skins slick and muscle-corded, moving through the water with such smoothness that the surface barely wrinkles. They are predators, but not indiscriminate ones. They eat clogging larvae, soft worms, and small things that multiply too quickly. Their bodies polish the inside of root channels simply by passing through them again and again. The tenders encourage them, leaving certain routes dark and untraveled so they can patrol. Sometimes a tender will tap twice against a submerged stem, and one of the pale shapes will turn, not in obedience but in recognition. They are not tame. They are allied by habitat.

In the deepest reservoir lives something older and broader.

It is difficult to know whether this creature is an animal, a colony, or a piece of the basin that learned to breathe. It lies flat in the black water, moss-backed and still, its body so covered in living growth that it seems like a low island until something about its outline shifts. Its breathing is so slow that the water level around it appears to change only over long intervals. It filters sediment, breaks down dead matter, and provides a stable chemical center for the basin’s water. Egg clusters are sometimes attached to the warmest hollows along its sides, not because it permits this in any obvious way, but because conditions there are favorable.

The tenders treat it with profound care. They do not climb on it except when necessary, and even then only along its less sensitive ridges. They place offerings near it: shell fragments, cleaned bone, knots of fiber, pellets of nutrient-rich leaf paste. These are not worship in the way open places understand worship. They are maintenance directed toward a living foundation. If the reservoir creature is pleased, it is pleased in the way water is pleased: by remaining balanced.

There are wingless marsh-moths in the dark angles of the ceiling and walls. They are furred against the cold, slow-moving, and pale as condensation. They do not fly. They walk with deliberate steps, their antennae tasting humidity, their bodies gathering fine droplets that later fall into channels below. They seem ornamental until one notices that their movements change before the air changes. When they tap against reed walls, the tenders listen. When they gather in one place, the tenders check for drafts, blockages, or new seepage. The moths are not workers, but they are indicators. They belong to the basin’s weather, and in a place without sky, weather is a local thing.

Hollow stems house colonies of soft insects whose bodies are nearly invisible but whose vibrations are known by heart. A healthy stem colony hums at a level felt more than heard. A disturbed one clicks. A starving one goes silent. The tenders use these sounds to diagnose sections of the city. If a wall is drying, if a root is rotting, if a predator has entered where it should not, the colonies react first. In this way, the smallest lives serve as the basin’s nervous system.

There are other predators too, less obvious.

Some live in the plant walls themselves, flattened and patient, waiting for prey to pass within reach. Some are no larger than a finger but can empty a nursery channel if left unchecked. Some hunt by suction, drawing in larvae with a sudden contraction of their bodies. The tenders do not try to eliminate them. That would be folly. Instead they manage their presence: widening one passage, blocking another, moving egg masses, altering glow patterns so prey drift elsewhere. Predation here is not spectacle. It is a small tightening of water, a brief disappearance, then stillness returning. The basin does not dramatize death. It absorbs it.

This is why the place feels so thick with life even when almost nothing is seen.

Every surface is in use. The undersides of causeways hold eggs. The seams of root walls shelter mites. The joints of built structures are traced by bioluminescent slime that feeds on waste and marks safe routes. The floor releases nutrients when pressed. The air carries scent trails, moisture differences, and pressure changes that mean more than light. What looks empty to an outside eye is often full of intention. A smooth patch of water may be a hunting lane. A dark hollow may be a resting place for a predator. A seemingly random knot of roots may be a meeting point, a nursery, a memory, or a gate in preparation.

The tenders themselves are changed by living here. Their bodies leave traces. Their skin sheds fine fibers that feed moss. Their breath adds warmth to enclosed chambers. Their hands smooth root rails until they shine. Their presence alters the chemistry of the water just enough to encourage certain growths and discourage others. They are not outside the ecosystem, observing and directing it. They are inside it as both cause and consequence. The city grows because they tend it, and they live because the city grows. Neither statement is primary.

They have no sharp division between making and belonging.

A doorway is also a fish pass. A roof is also a spawning shelf. A storage pit may hold dried reeds one season and amphibious juveniles the next. A platform built for fine handwork may later be used by cold-blooded creatures seeking the faint warmth gathered near dense root clusters. The tenders do not resent this. They design for it. Their urban order is not exclusive. It is porous. It admits multiple bodies, multiple hungers, multiple rhythms. A route that is a street to one creature is a current to another, a hunting ground to a third, and a nursery to a fourth. The basin works because it refuses to belong to only one kind of life.

The new erosion has made the inhabitants more alert.

The fresh cut is a wound and a gift. It brings danger: a suggestion of outside air, a sharp edge not yet stabilized, a possibility of uncontrolled inflow. But it also brings material. Blocks of fallen plant-matter have already been propped and wedged into retaining edges. Torn root-bundles have been bound before they could fray further. The thin trickle of incoming water has been guided into a root basin and passed through living filters. Some tenders work near the opening in shifts, watching how the bead of moisture forms, where it gathers, whether the cold coming through the cut changes the behavior of the nearest channels. They are not afraid of the new shape. They are studying it. In time, they may grow the entrance into something narrower, more controlled. Or they may leave it partly open, depending on what it teaches them.

They dislike sudden light. The pale suggestion near the cleft makes them uneasy. They move beside it with extra care, keeping low, avoiding direct exposure. If the opening widens, they will respond. If it begins to dry the inner air or admit larger creatures, they will close it with woven reeds, living stems, and clay daub. If it brings useful moisture and no immediate threat, they will let it remain a controlled scar. They do not hate the wound. They know that fresh breaks can become rooms.

If something large enters the basin, the inhabitants notice at once.

A human body, or anything like it, would be immense, warm, noisy, and hydrologically reckless. Its heat would disturb the cold. Its breath would alter the air. Its weight would press water out of the plant floor in awkward surges. The tenders would vanish into wall hollows, under terraces, and behind root lattices. They would not flee in panic. They would reposition. They would watch through root-thread gaps. They would measure the intruder by what it breaks.

If the intruder moves too quickly, they will seal routes and withdraw deeper. If it brings fire or foul air, they will try to close the opening behind it. If it is still, cold-wet, and patient, they may approach. Not warmly. Not with greeting. A tender might emerge from a dark chamber, one hand resting on a root rail, the other near a shell blade. It would taste the air. It would feel the water’s reaction. It would decide whether this large body is a threat, a disaster, or merely an obstacle to be waited out.

They would not try to speak in words. They might test the visitor by making a small vibration through a stem and observing whether the visitor flinches. They might place a glowing knot in view to see if it is respected. They might leave a single bone hook in plain sight as a sign of inhabited territory. If the visitor is gentle enough, they may eventually allow proximity. They will not offer mastery. They will offer tolerance, and in the basin, tolerance is already a serious gift.

Their beauty is not loud.

It appears in the way a causeway curves just enough to slow a current. It appears in a row of bone hooks hanging like pale teeth, each one chosen for shape and balance. It appears in a shelter daubed with dark clay and sealed with resinous sap, low enough to hold warmth without interrupting flow. It appears in a lash-line of braided grass stretched across a gap, holding drying strips of food so that condensation falls beside them rather than onto them. It appears in the faint blue-green lines along root seams, not decorative alone but mnemonic, marking routes, thresholds, and tending stations.

They make art, but their art is mostly adjustment. A beautifully tied knot is art. A gate that opens with water pressure instead of force is art. A nursery glow tuned to the right intensity is art. An empty pool left empty for three seasons is art. They do not carve monuments. The basin would not hold them. Monuments interrupt flow. Instead they make refinements so subtle that only another tender would fully perceive them.

They have rules, though they would not call them laws.

Do not stop water where it wishes to move.  
Do not let a wall die while it is still needed.  
Do not fill a hollow simply because it is empty.  
Do not take more than the channel can replace.  
Do not make noise where silence can carry information.  
Do not let warmth become waste.  
Do not confuse stillness with absence.

These are not written anywhere. They are repeated in practice until they become reflex.

The basin’s inhabitants are shaped by scarcity and intimacy. There is little room for error. A blocked sluice can flood a terrace. A torn root can divert condensation away from a nursery. A dead patch of moss can weaken an entire bank. Conversely, a small repair can stabilize half the settlement. A handful of shell-grit can improve drainage. A new woven mat can become a floor, a ceiling, a hunting perch, or an egg shelf depending on what adopts it. This closeness of cause and effect has made them attentive to the extreme small. They do not think in broad gestures. They think in fingers, knots, drips, and pressure changes.

They are not innocent. They are not gentle in the way surface stories mean gentle. They allow predation. They manage death. They cull, move, abandon, and redirect lives according to the needs of the whole. They will let a canal population fail if saving it would poison the reservoir. They will encourage a predator to remain if it keeps another population in balance. They do not moralize these choices. They live inside consequences too tightly to afford sentimentality.

Yet they are not cruel. Their cruelty would be inefficiency, and inefficiency kills in small places. They show care by precision. They show respect by restraint. They show affection through shared labor, through grooming root-filaments, through offering a tool before it is asked for, through resting close enough for another’s body to borrow a little warmth without disturbing the water.

They are, above all, negotiators.

They negotiate with roots by guiding growth.  
They negotiate with water by shaping slopes.  
They negotiate with predators by offering alternative paths.  
They negotiate with cold by huddling, sealing, and slowing.  
They negotiate with darkness by becoming sensitive to the faintest signals.  
They negotiate with death by turning it into substrate.

If asked what lives there, the simplest answer would be: the tenders, their canal-kin, the reservoir creature, the moths, the larvae, the mites, the hidden predators, and the countless small bodies that move through stem, water, and root.

But that answer would still be incomplete.

What lives there is also the agreement between them.

It is the city as a living contract: plant grown into structure, animal grown into function, water given room without being allowed violence, cold accepted as a constant companion, silence maintained as a form of attention. The basin is inhabited not by a single people ruling over a place, but by a braided population that has learned to exist inside extreme closeness without collapsing.

And if one stands at the fresh cut long enough, listening to the drip from torn roots and the almost inaudible shifting of woven stems, one begins to understand that the basin does not merely contain lives. It is made of lives. Every wall is held together by roots fed by animal residue. Every canal is kept clear by bodies and hands working in rhythm. Every glow is a living signal sustained by hunger and care. Every empty space is empty on purpose.

The true resident is the balance itself.

But that balance has hands. Small, cold, root-fingered hands. It has eyes that shine faintly in blue-green dark. It has a breath that pales and then slows. It has a memory stored in knots, water patterns, and the polished wear of root rails. It moves carefully, one body at a time, through a city that is half grown and half made.

It lives there quietly.

It has lived there long enough to know that the place will survive only if nothing inside it forgets how small it is.
