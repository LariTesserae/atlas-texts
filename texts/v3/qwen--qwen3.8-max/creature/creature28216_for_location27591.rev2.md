What lives there is not abundant, but it is deliberate.

There are people in the valley, though not in the usual sense of a town or a tribe. They are scattered through the basins like tools left after a long shift: a pair of figures moving along a pipe run, a lamp bobbing on a high catwalk, a silhouette bending over a control panel in the blue-green hush of a cultivation bed. They do not crowd. They do not build upward in any proud way. Their presence is fitted into the existing structure of root and steel, and wherever they live, the place still seems mostly to belong to the cold, the plants, and the machines.

They call themselves different names depending on the basin, the crew, or the generation, but most often they are simply called the tenders, or the rootwardens, or, in the older work-speech still used near the pump sheds, the Underhands. The name matters less than the function. They are the ones who keep the valley from choking itself.

Their bodies have been shaped by the place in small, practical ways. They are not monstrous, not dramatically changed, but they carry the mark of long residence below. Their skin tends toward paleness, though stained often at the hands and cuffs with peat-water, oil, sap, or rust. Their shoulders are rounded from ducking under root shelves and working in low access spaces. Their hands are thick-knuckled, palm-calloused, sensitive to vibration. They can tell, by resting a bare hand on a root wall or a pipe casing, whether water is moving behind it, whether an engine is running true, or whether the substrate is beginning to shift. They learn this young. A child who cannot read the floor soon learns not to wander far from a marked path.

They dress for damp and cold, not for display. Their clothing is layered: close underlayers of woven moss-fiber or recycled fleece, then oilskin or waxed canvas, then heavier coats stiffened with age and repair. Hoods are common, lined with felted plant wool, because the condensation rain comes without warning and finds every gap at the neck. Boots are their most valuable possession: high, reinforced, soled against slip and puncture, sometimes with metal toes and removable liners dried over engine housings. Gloves are kept at the belt even when not worn, because a bare hand on cold steel or a splintered root face is a lesson one receives only once. Many carry hearing protection—padded caps, waxed plugs, or heavy ear-flaps tied beneath the chin—though some of the older workers wear little, having gone partly deaf from years in the basins and relying instead on the felt tremor of the place.

They move with a characteristic caution. There is no true running except in emergencies, and even then it is a low, balanced stride, feet testing before weight is committed. The floor may look continuous, but it is not. A firm mat can turn to a soft peat pocket in three steps. A root ridge may be dry on top and slick beneath. A steel grate may be sound or loosened by rust and root pressure. So their walking is attentive, almost conversational: step, test, shift, continue. In louder districts, where the engines press against the chest, they walk even more carefully, because sound can hide the small signals that would otherwise warn of a failing platform or a hidden channel beneath the surface.

Their settlements are few and low. They do not build cities here. The valley does not permit that kind of weight, and the people have learned not to ask too much of it. Their homes are clustered where the substrate is stable, where drainage can be controlled, and where heat from a nearby engine or pipe can be borrowed without danger. A dwelling might be set into the curve of a great root buttress, its roof a patch of corrugated steel and cured root-bark, its walls layered with moss insulation and moisture-proof sheeting. Another might hang partly from a root shelf, suspended on cables, with a grated floor to let drip-water pass beneath instead of pooling inside. Others are half-buried into peat terraces, their entrances set behind heavy flaps or sliding panels to keep out the cold draft and the worst of the noise.

Inside, the homes are small, practical, and surprisingly still. Thick wall-mats dampen sound, though they never fully silence it. The thrum of the valley remains, but inside it becomes a pressure rather than a roar. Lamps glow amber or pale blue; open flames are rare and carefully controlled. Sleeping places are raised, insulated, kept dry. Tools hang in neat order because clutter becomes dangerous in a space where every object must be carried, maintained, or repaired. Water is caught in clean gutters and filtered through fiber beds. Heat comes from coils, heated stone blocks, or pipes routed from machine housings. The air indoors smells of wet wool, resin, faint oil, and the green-brown scent of root-fiber walls. It is not a luxurious smell, but to those born there it is the smell of shelter.

They live by maintenance. That is the center of their existence. The valley does not run itself, or rather it runs itself in ways that would eventually close off, flood, collapse, or darken the spaces they need. So they attend to it. They clear root growth where it threatens sluice gates. They install frames where a load-bearing root has begun to crack. They scrape moss from sensor panels, replace corroded bolts, bleed air from pipes, reset fuses, oil bearings, and listen for changes in the pitch of turbines. They watch water levels in the black pools and note when condensation shelves begin to release too early or too late. They record these things in worn logbooks, on scratched metal tags, or in the knotted cord systems used in the deeper basins where paper rots too quickly.

Their labor has specializations, though in small crews everyone knows a little of everything. There are listeners, who are perhaps the most respected. These are men and women with an almost unsettling patience, capable of standing still for minutes with an ear pressed to wood, pipe, or steel. They can hear where water is gathering behind a root wall, where a compressor bearing is beginning to fail, or where a root column has developed an internal split long before it shows outside. They carry short rods, sometimes with brass bells or weighted tips, and they tap surfaces as a physician taps a chest. When a listener frowns, people stop what they are doing.

There are cutters, who handle the hard work of opening channels and removing intrusive growth. They are not reckless. Cutting in the valley is a serious act. A careless cut can weaken a platform, redirect water into a dry basin, or cause a root shelf to shed condensation unpredictably. Cutters know the difference between a feeder mass and a structural member. They use saws, axes, hooked blades, and occasionally powered cut-heads, but they always mark what they intend to remove before they remove it. Fresh cuts are treated with resin or sealed with damp moss packs, depending on whether the goal is to heal the plant tissue or discourage regrowth. Their hands are often scarred, and many have sap-darkened nails that never fully clean.

There are drip-herders, though that is a rough name for a delicate job. They manage condensation. They stretch cloth and thin metal sheets under high root shelves, angle gutters, clean drip-lines, and predict when the accumulated moisture above will release. This work is dangerous more often than it looks. A condensation shelf may hold for hours, then let go in a cold sheet that can knock a person from a catwalk or flood a low service pit in minutes. Drip-herders learn to read the slickness of surfaces, the sound of gathering water overhead, and the slight change in air temperature before a release. They wear slickers even when the air seems dry, and they never stand directly beneath a heavy shelf if they can help it.

There are lampwrights and engine-tenders, too. The lampwrights keep the fragile network of light alive. They string cable along root hooks, replace bulbs, clean grime from reflectors, and know which circuits can be tapped without overloading old conduit. They are the ones who decide where a new amber service lamp should be placed so that a dangerous intersection becomes passable, or where a red hazard light must hang over a soft peat drop. Engine-tenders live closer to the noise. They work around housings, grates, fans, and transformers. Their clothes are often stained with grease, and their voices, when they use them, are loud and clipped from years of speaking over machinery. They are the first to notice if an engine rhythm changes, and they treat such changes as personal offenses.

Children grow up inside this work. They do not have a separate world of play; they have gradual inclusion. A very young child learns the color of lamps before letters. White for work. Blue-green for cultivation. Amber for service and passage. Red for danger. Later they learn the sound signals: a long fan cycle, a double knock in a pipe, the particular shudder that means a sluice is opening somewhere out of sight. They learn to walk with one hand near a wall or rail. They learn not to touch bright green mats, not to lean over still water, not to sleep with their face pressed against an outer root wall when the condensation is heavy. They learn to carry a lamp, a knife, and a whistle. They learn that if an engine stops suddenly, silence is not peace; silence is a warning.

Their speech has changed to suit the place. In the quieter basins, they can talk in low, economical sentences. In the louder districts, full conversation is rare. Instead they use a rich language of gestures: hand signals for gate, pressure, water, cut, stop, wait, support, danger, return. Some of these gestures come from old industrial practice; others have grown locally, shaped by root and lamp and basin. A flick of two fingers down the side of the thigh means slippery footing. A closed hand tapped twice against the chest means the sound is wrong somewhere. A slow opening palm near the face means condensation is about to fall. At distance, they use lamp codes. Short-short-long for attention. A red flash held steady for halt. Three amber pulses for a meeting point. Children learn these before they are fully fluent in spoken language, and sometimes, when tired or frightened, they revert to gesture even if the air around them is quiet enough for words.

Because noise is constant, they value stillness but are suspicious of it. They do not seek silence the way surface people might. To them, silence can mean a stopped pump, a dead fan, a failed heater, a broken circuit. It can mean air no longer moving, water no longer draining, heat no longer reaching a root junction. So the healthy sound of the valley is a comfort. A child sleeping through the deep thrum of engines sleeps well. An elder waking to an altered rhythm may be out of bed and reaching for a lamp before conscious thought has named the change.

Their food is plain and partly earned from the valley itself. They cultivate slow beds under blue-green lights: dense mats of edible root pith, pale stems, fibrous leaf-buds, and a few hardy grains coaxed to grow in controlled peat troughs. These beds are not beautiful in a garden sense. They look industrial, tended, measured. Some are covered with mesh to keep out dripping water; others are warmed by coils and monitored with dials. There are also wilder harvests: certain mosses can be pressed into bitter cakes; sedges near clean pools yield a starchy core; algae films are scraped from lit panels and dried into dark sheets rich in minerals. Where water connects to older channels, there may be blind fish or pale larvae, but these are never abundant. They are treated as supplements, not staples. Beetles near engine housings are sometimes collected and roasted, especially by children, though adults mostly consider them emergency food or small comforts on a long route. Nothing is wasted. Even spoiled fiber goes back into the substrate after treatment.

Water is everywhere and yet must be managed. Condensation is collected, filtered, boiled when fuel permits, or passed through charcoal and root-fiber beds. Standing water is approached with suspicion. Clear-looking pools may conceal soft peat drops, hidden gates, or sudden suction from an old sluice. The people know which black pools are safe to draw from and which are not. They mark dangerous ones with red cord or a notched stake. They teach children that stillness in water is not calmness; it is concealment.

Their laws are practical, but they have taken on the weight of custom. Do not cut a load-bearing root without installing a support frame. Do not walk on the brightest green mats. Do not camp beneath a condensation shelf. Do not leave a lamp burning in an unmarked hazard zone. Do not block a drainage line with tools or refuse. Do not trust a dry-looking floor near a drip-line. Do not work alone in a red-lit basin unless someone knows your route. These rules are repeated so often they become proverbial. A careless person is told, “You walk as if the roots will catch you.” A reckless one is told, “The cold keeps what it gets.” The sayings are not dramatic. They are the valley’s own kind of seriousness.

They have rituals, though they would not call them that. Before a major cut, a cutter may touch the root with the flat of the blade, not in reverence exactly, but in acknowledgment. When a new support frame is set, the crew sometimes leaves a small metal tag tied with wire at the joint, stamped with the date and the names of those who installed it. If a section collapses, they do not immediately clear all the broken material. They assess it. They let it settle. They watch how the surrounding growth responds. Some fallen root masses are later incorporated into new terraces or used to reinforce weak ground. Nothing here is simply waste if time and pressure can make it useful.

Births and deaths are marked in ways suited to the basin. Births are rare and quiet affairs, attended by those who know how to keep warmth and clean water in a damp world. A newborn is kept close to heated coils or near an engine room’s borrowed warmth for its first days. Names are often chosen from the place: a bend in a pipe, a lamp color, a sound heard during labor, a root formation near the home. Some names are practical call-signs more than anything else, easy to shout close to the ear or signal with one hand.

Death is treated with the same patience. The substrate is not suitable for ordinary burial, and the machinery of the valley makes heavy stone monuments impossible. Instead, the dead are laid in prepared root shelves or dormant peat pockets, wrapped in fiber cloth and sealed with resin where appropriate. Over time, the plant material takes them in. This is not spoken of with horror. It is understood as a continuation of the same process by which the valley holds everything: roots over roots, matter into matter, life folded back into the living floor. Sometimes a personal tool is left nearby—a wrench, a lamp, a coil of cord—so the place of the dead can be recognized. In older basins, one can find rows of faint green rise-lines where generations of tenders have been returned to the substrate. The moss there is often darker, richer, and strangely even. Outsiders, when they see it, sometimes step back without knowing why.

They are not wholly isolated, though they do not depend on frequent contact with any outside. Occasionally there are shipments: oil, spare parts, lenses, insulated wire, medical supplies, seed stock from stable beds, or new tools. These arrive through shafts or service tunnels that most residents know but rarely use. Some of the tenders have seen the surface; many have not. The surface, when spoken of, is described in terms that sound almost abstract: open sky, moving air, harsh light, distance without ceiling. Children sometimes ask about it, but they ask the way children ask about deep water or old wars—curious, but not certain they want to go there. The valley is not a prison to them. It is a condition of life. They have made it livable by learning its limits.

There is little laughter in the large, loud basins, but that does not mean there is no joy. Joy appears in small, practical forms: a repaired pump running smooth, a dry night, a successful harvest from a difficult bed, a child mastering a lamp-code, a shared meal in a hush-house while the engines throb outside. They sing rarely, because song is hard in noise, but they do have work chants for short, coordinated tasks—pulling cable, setting a frame, lifting a grate—built more around rhythm than melody. They tell stories by gesture and by marked boards, because spoken tales get lost in the roar. Their heroes are not warriors but survivors and fixers: the woman who found a cracked penstock before it burst, the boy who led a lost crew out by following vibration through a pipe, the listener who sat for nine hours with her ear to a root column until she located the split that saved an entire terrace.

The nonhuman life is minor but persistent. Pale springtails swarm in the wettest moss, their tiny bodies flashing faintly when a lamp passes over them. Small flies and moths circle the fixtures in weak, broken spirals; they are called lamp-ash by some, because they collect in grey drifts beneath dead bulbs. Near engine housings, flattened beetles move through loose fiber, feeding on decaying root matter and the oily film that gathers there. Workers sometimes tolerate them, since their presence can indicate warmth and stable air. In the deeper pools, where water connects to older underground channels, there are pale larvae and sometimes blind fish, almost translucent, moving with slow caution. These animals are rarely seen and never hunted in large numbers. They feel like exceptions, and the people treat them that way. A blind fish glimpsed in a black pool is not usually caught; it is watched for a moment and left alone, as if its survival matters more than the meal it might provide.

There are also living presences that are not animals at all, and these dominate the valley far more than any person or creature. The plant mass itself is the primary inhabitant. It is not a collection of individual specimens so much as a continuous living system: floor, wall, support, skin, filter, reservoir. The roots hold the ceiling. The moss holds moisture. The peat holds memory. The fungal threads stitch decay into structure. The stiff sedges mark water edges. The dwarf woody thickets break drafts. The tall root-columns bear loads that would crush ordinary timber. The people live inside this living architecture, and they know it. They do not speak of the plants as sacred in any ornate way, but they speak of them as reliable, dangerous, patient, and necessary. A root is not just a thing to be cut. It is a beam, a pipe, a shelter, a record, and sometimes a threat.

Because of this, the tenders have a complicated relationship with growth. They cultivate and restrain in the same breath. They encourage root thickening where support is needed by applying heat coils and nutrient packs. They discourage growth near valves, panels, and moving machinery by cutting, shading, or applying bitter resin barriers. They know that the valley wants, in its slow way, to close over everything. Not with malice. Not with hunger. Just with the patient accumulation of tissue over time. If left untended, channels would narrow, catwalks would be swallowed, sheds would sink, lamps would be covered by moss, and the open basins would become low, green-ceilinged corridors. The machines keep air and heat moving; the people keep the machines running; the plants keep the world from collapsing. It is a three-part dependence, and none of the three can be removed cleanly.

The machines, though not alive, are treated as if they have moods. This is not superstition so much as experience. A fan that changes pitch before failing is said to be “turning.” A pump that runs hot but does not seize is “holding its breath.” An engine that knocks irregularly after a pressure release is “arguing with the water.” These phrases are used without embarrassment. They make the work manageable. They give shape to forces too large to see. A child who asks why a certain transformer hums differently in cold cycles will not be mocked; they will be told to listen again tomorrow. In the valley, attention is a form of respect.

There are dangers that everyone knows and few discuss in detail. The worst is collapse: a ceiling section failing, a root column splitting, a terrace giving way into a hidden water pocket. The second is drowning, not in open water but in soft substrate—a person stepping into what looks like firm peat and sinking into cold, dense fiber-mass that closes around the legs and chest. The third is machinery: a caught sleeve, a burst pipe, a fan housing opened at the wrong time, an electrical fault in damp conditions. The fourth is cold itself, entering slowly, numbing judgment before the body realizes how much heat it has lost. Because of these dangers, no one works entirely alone if it can be avoided. Even the most solitary roles maintain check-ins: a lamp flash across a basin, a marked tag moved from one hook to another, a scheduled return time written in a control shed.

Yet despite all this, the people are not grim. They are serious, yes, and often tired, but they have a groundedness that outsiders sometimes mistake for dullness. They notice small changes: a new green film on an old wall, the first clear drip from a repaired gutter, the smell of heated resin when a joint is sealed well. They take satisfaction in clean work. A properly set support frame is admired the way a fine piece of furniture might be elsewhere. A neatly routed cable line along a root stem is considered beautiful not because it is decorative, but because it will last. They value what holds.

There are also those who live farther out, in the quieter margins where the machinery is older and the basins more dormant. These are often elders, or people who prefer solitude, or workers assigned to watch long-term changes. They live in small shelters near inactive peat shelves, where the noise is lower and the air colder. They keep track of slow shifts: the spread of a moss bed, the sinking of a concrete footing, the gradual tilting of a rail spur, the return of condensation to a shelf that had seemed dry. Their reports travel by hand and lamp across long distances. They are the memory of the valley. When a new route is needed or an old hazard suspected, their knowledge is consulted before any work begins.

Some of them become strange in the way solitary people can, but not dangerous. They may speak more to roots than to people. They may keep a dead lamp hanging in a doorway as if it still works. They may tap a pipe every morning before eating, not from superstition but from a lifetime of checking. They are respected because they have stayed long enough to see the valley change by inches. In a place where change is slow, long residence is a kind of expertise no newcomer can replace.

There are also those who do not live there permanently but pass through: inspectors, traders, engineers from other districts, couriers carrying parts or sealed messages. They are easy to identify. They move too quickly. They look upward too often. They try to speak in full sentences where gesture would do. They flinch at the first heavy drip from a condensation shelf, while the tenders barely glance up. The valley tolerates visitors, but it does not explain itself to them. Without a guide, an outsider can misread a bright moss flat as solid ground, a red lamp as decoration, a quiet engine as an idle one. The tenders know better. They know that in the basin, nothing quiet is necessarily safe.

The truest answer, then, is that the valley is lived in by several kinds of presence at once. There are the people: sparse, practical, noise-worn, patient, bound to maintenance. There are the minor animals: springtails, beetles, moths, larvae, the rare blind fish, all of them clinging to moisture, warmth, and dimness. There are the plants, vast and dominant, forming the living body of the landscape itself. And there is the machinery, not alive but indispensable, so deeply woven into the habits of survival that it has acquired a kind of second nature. Together, they make a community of interdependence: roots holding roofs, engines moving air, lamps marking passage, hands repairing roots, water feeding growth, growth shielding water, noise binding all of it into one continuous pressure.

If you were to stand on a raised root ridge and look across the basins, you would not see them all at once. You might see one lamp far off, moving along a pipe line. You might see the weak blue glow of a cultivation bed under a high shelf. You might see a pump shed with its corrugated roof shining faintly after an artificial rain. You might hear, beneath the roar of fans, the short shouted exchange of two workers too far apart to understand but close enough to keep one another alive by sound. You might feel the floor give slightly underfoot, not like soil, not like stone, but like something that has weight and memory. And if you stayed long enough, you would realize that the valley is not empty. It is only unpopulous. It is filled not by crowds but by attention: the attention of people to machines, of machines to air and water, of plants to load and moisture, of cold to everything.

That is what lives there.

Not a nation. Not a wilderness. Not a factory crew and not a garden.

A scattered, watchful life, adapted to pressure and patience. A people who speak in gestures and read the world by vibration. A green architecture that grows slowly around every intervention. Minor creatures surviving in the margins. Engines that keep the dark breathable. And beneath all of it, the constant, enormous, patient body of the valley itself—root over root, drip over drip, stroke after stroke—holding its inhabitants in place while they keep it from falling apart.
