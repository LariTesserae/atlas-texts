What lives there is not one creature, not a people, not a single ruling shape moving through the stone lanes with purpose and noise. The city is inhabited the way a cathedral is inhabited by sound: thinly, widely, by presences that belong to the architecture more than they own it.

The first and oldest resident is the water itself.

It is the citizen that fills the city without crowding it. It lives in every district, under many tempers and names, though no name is given. In the upper terraces it lives as mist, a blind, wandering population that arrives without footsteps and leaves every edge darkened. On broad shelves it lives as sheetwater, temporary and shining, spreading after rain like a moved congregation. Along vertical faces it becomes threadwater, descending in countless fine lines, patient as prayer. In the small basins it is still water, holding sky in cold mirrors. At night, when the air tightens, it becomes frost, a sleep of white needles on the stone lips. In the morning it wakes again and continues the city.

Water is not merely present there; it is the principal life of the place. It makes the rooms, keeps the channels, polishes the thresholds, fills the cisterns, empties them, fills them again. It has habits. It has routes. It has seasons of being. It gathers, falls, spreads, pauses, seeps, evaporates, returns. If the city has a body, water is the blood and breath of it. If the city has a will, it is the will of water: to move downward, to find weakness, to make smoothness, to persist.

But water is not the only living thing.

The smallest inhabitants are the ones that make the stone seem almost flesh. They are films and skins and microscopic greennesses, older than anything with legs or wings. On the wet mineral faces there are microbial crusts, thin as breath, darkening the rock with olive, black, and green. They live in dampness too fine for larger life to notice. They are the first pastures of the city, the quiet groundwork that makes other lives possible. Where light reaches them, they brighten almost imperceptibly, as if the stone had remembered warmth.

In the moist seams, mosses live like slow households. They do not grow so much as settle, press, and keep. They seal ledges with dark green wool, hold rain in their bodies, soften the harshness of bare mineral. A patch of moss there is not decoration; it is a district. It shelters mites, tardigrades, rotifers, and things too small to be called animals by anyone who has not looked closely. Inside the moss, a droplet can be a world. A single saturated stem can be a street. The moss lives by drinking, holding, and giving back slowly, like a careful resident who never wastes what the weather brings.

Lichens live on the more exposed faces, flattened against stone as if they have chosen to become part of the geology. They are the city’s mapmakers, marking rock with grey, sulfur, rust, and pale bone. They ask almost nothing. They take light, air, mineral dampness, and time. They are so patient that their movement can only be measured in centuries. In the driest intervals they seem dead, brittle, withdrawn. With the next fog they soften and begin again.

Where a little soil collects near persistent seeps, sedges and rushes stand in sparse families. They do not crowd. They grow in disciplined spacing, fine-stemmed and wind-cooled, each one rooted where water slows enough to let sediment rest. Their leaves tremble with passing air. Their roots bind small banks. They are not abundant, but they give the city a hint of meadow, a memory of softer countries far below.

On the knolls beaten by wind, cushion plants live close to the stone, rounded and dense, conserving warmth in their own tight bodies. They look like green stones breathing. They do not reach upward; they endure downward. They are among the sternest residents of the place, shaped by exposure into humility.

Then come the animals, though even they are small, muted, and mostly hidden.

The springtails are perhaps the nearest thing to herds. They move through wet films on ledges and in moss, tiny dark grains of life that leap when disturbed, vanishing into dampness. They are so small that their presence is known more as a stirring than as a sight. In places where moisture has collected organic dust, they graze on fungal threads and decay. Their lives are brief and numerous. They are the city’s invisible cattle, living in the thin pastures of wet stone.

Mites live in the moss and under mineral flakes. Some are rust-colored, some nearly clear, some dark as wet seed. They move with purpose through spaces too narrow for imagination to follow easily. They are scavengers, hunters, wanderers. They belong to the lower economy of the city, where nothing is wasted and every fallen filament becomes food or shelter.

At certain times, when temperature and moisture align, the city produces brief pulses of flying life. Midges rise from wet seams in trembling clouds. Their existence is short and intense. They hatch, mate, lay eggs, and die within intervals that feel almost ceremonial. For a little while, the air above a pool or damp ledge is filled with them, a faint living haze. Then they are gone, and their bodies lie on the surface tension of small basins, becoming food for what comes next.

Blackflies may emerge from moving threads of water, their larvae anchored in channels where flow is just enough to feed them. Mosquitoes, if there are pools stagnant and warm enough, hatch in quiet corners. Their larvae hang beneath the surface, tiny inverted breathers, living in the underside of stillness.

In the larger pools, life is hidden but real. There are larvae in soft sediment, red or pale, curled in mud or dwelling in cases made of grain and silk. There are small worms, thin as threads, moving through organic muck. There may be minute crustaceans, rowing through water with motions too quick and tiny to hold in the eye. They live beneath the mirrors, in the cool underworld of the basins. When a pool dries too far, some of them wait as eggs, patient in sediment, holding life in a paused form until water returns.

Spiders live at the edges of the wettest districts. They choose sheltered angles between stones, crevices where rain does not strike directly, and hang their silk in geometries fine enough to catch mist. Some hunt without webs, moving carefully over damp rock. Some wait in silk-lined retreats for a springtail or fly to come near. Their predation is small, patient, and almost silent. They are the city’s quiet wardens, keeping the tiny populations in balance without drama.

Birds are rare, but when they come they bring a sudden sense of the wider world. A stone-colored bird, low and quick, may drop from a ridge to drink at a basin. It stands for a moment at the edge, alert, neck turning. Its feet leave small prints in mineral mud. It drinks, perhaps snatches a fly or larva, lifts away again. For a breath, the city seems visited by something familiar. Then it is empty again.

Sometimes a larger bird passes over, a raptor perhaps, riding high air above the plateau. Its shadow moves across the wet stone like a temporary storm. Nothing below is taken. Nothing is harmed. The shadow crosses basins, terraces, mossy ledges, and vanishes. The city does not remember it, except in the brief tension that passes through small hidden bodies.

Warm-blooded life is marginal here. It may pass through, but it does not settle in numbers. A small mammal might cross a dry ridge at dawn, leaving a trail of pressed moss and disturbed droplets. A pika or hare might use the stoniest margins, listening from a crevice, but the wet city itself offers little comfort to creatures needing warmth and burrow. They are visitors, not citizens. They belong to the city only as fleeting traces: a track, a bent stem, a warmth already gone.

There are no crowds, no voices, no herds moving across open ground. The population is measured in films, pulses, tremors, and tiny appetites. It lives at the scale of droplets and seams. Its drama is not in battles or migrations, but in hatching, seeping, feeding, drying, waiting, and beginning again.

Yet the city is not empty.

It is inhabited by condition as much as by creature. The air itself feels occupied. Moisture has weight there. Fog has presence. The coolness seems attentive. When a bead of condensation trembles on a ledge before falling, it feels less like an event than like a resident passing through. When a channel fills after rain, the sound is not noise so much as occupation: the city being used again by its oldest inhabitant.

If one asks for a “who” in the ordinary sense, the answer refuses the question. There is no single self there. There is no ruler, no family, no tribe. But if “who” can be stretched to include what persists, what maintains, what returns again and again to the same stone, then the city is occupied by something very old and very plural.

It is inhabited by the wetness.

The wetness has no face, but it has ways. It comes in from the horizon as mist. It gathers on edges. It darkens stone. It joins small streams. It fills basins. It feeds moss. It hatches larvae. It slides over lips of rock and disappears into hidden drains. It is generous and indifferent. It builds and erases with the same movement. It is the mason, the road, the drinker, the drunk, and the drought.

The biological residents belong to it. The mosses live by keeping it. The algae live by turning its light into thin green life. The springtails live in its films. The midges live by its timing. The spiders live by the small lives it permits. The birds live by its pools for a moment and leave.

Together they make a commonwealth without ambition. No one there builds to be remembered. No creature tries to outlast the stone. The moss spreads a millimeter. The mineral crust thickens by a skin. The pool fills. The larva grows. The droplet falls. The frost cracks a seam by a hair. The city continues.

In that sense, the truest inhabitant is not an organism but the whole adjusted system: the wet city continuing itself. It lives the way a long river lives, or the way a glacier lives, though slower and quieter than either. It has rhythms. It has repairs. It has losses. It has recoveries. A ledge collapses; water finds the new angle. A basin silts; a channel cuts elsewhere. A moss patch dries; rain restores it. A population hatches; another waits in eggs. Nothing is permanent, but everything is continuous.

There is a strange dignity to what lives there. It does not need eyes to be seen. It does not need song to be heard. It does not need hands to build. It has made a dense, layered, urban world without grasping. It has filled chambers without entering them in the human sense. It has left traces everywhere without writing.

If a traveler were to stand in one of the courtyards of wet stone and listen, they might feel watched, though no eyes would be upon them. That feeling would not be false. The place is aware in the only way such a place can be: through water moving, through small lives responding, through surfaces receiving weather and changing. The traveler would be briefly another warm body in a cold, wet city, soon gone. The city would not mourn. It would not welcome. It would simply continue.

And so, whoever lives there?

The answer is: the water lives there. The mist lives there. The moss lives there. The springtails, midges, larvae, spiders, and passing birds live there. The stone itself lives there by being altered. The city lives there by being continued.

What lives there is not a single being, but a vast, patient, water-fed habitation: countless small presences gathered around one ancient fact, that in this high cool place, water has made a home and everything else belongs to it.
