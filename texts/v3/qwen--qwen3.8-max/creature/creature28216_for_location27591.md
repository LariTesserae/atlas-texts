What lives there is not first a face. It is not a single creature waiting in the dark with eyes and a mouth and a name. The first thing one notices as an inhabitant is the living ground itself: the rootmass, the moss, the peat, the slow green tissue that makes the valley into a place rather than a hole. That is the oldest resident. It is already underfoot, already in the walls, already holding the ceiling in place with a thousand patient fibers. If the valley has a people, this is their foundation and their body.

Still, lives there.

There are small lives first, the exceptions that prove how severe the place is. Pale springtails move through the wettest moss like grains of frost that have learned to jerk and vanish. Tiny flies gather around the service lamps, their wings damp, their bodies weak, circling in short hopeless spirals until they fall onto the roots and are slowly covered by green film. Near the warmer engine housings, where steel gives off a low persistent heat, flat-bodied beetles work through loose fiber and old bark. They are dark, unhurried, and unlovely, more like moving chips of wet wood than creatures with blood. In the deeper pools, where black water lies still enough to seem solid, there are sometimes translucent larvae drifting in the cold, or a single blind fish turning with a slowness that suggests it has forgotten urgency. These lives are minor. They do not make a society. They do not leave trails or bones or stories. They persist in niches, in leaks, in warm seams, in the few places where the valley’s enormity briefly loosens its grip.

The larger presence is harder to name.

If people live here, they are not numerous. They are not a population so much as a sparse set of functions placed where the machinery and the vegetation require attention. They might be called keepers, tenders, rootwards, sluice-workers, depending on which basin one enters and which old stencil remains legible on a control shed. They do not gather in crowds. The landscape would not allow it. The cold is too constant, the noise too heavy, the space too spread out. Instead, they exist in ones and twos, sometimes in small crews of three or four, moving between pump sheds, catwalks, valve nests, and cultivation terraces with the practiced caution of people who know that the ground can remember a careless step.

They are human, or nearly so, but the place has worked on them. Their bodies are pale, not with the clean pallor of people who simply stay indoors, but with a damp, bluish whiteness that seems lit from beneath by cold. Their hands are rough and strong, stained with sap, oil, rust, and the black-green residue of crushed moss. Their knuckles are swollen from valve wheels and tool handles. Their shoulders hunch against the chill even when they have stopped feeling it. Their clothes are layered, patched, and utilitarian: waxed canvas, rubberized coats, thick socks, heavy boots, straps holding lamps, gauges, wire cutters, and small tools in oilskin pouches. Wherever fabric stays wet long enough, moss or fungal threads begin to grow into it, so that some of them wear a faint living nap on cuffs, hoods, and knees, as if the valley has started to claim them gently and without malice.

They do not speak much. The sound of the place makes ordinary speech a waste of effort. The engines throb through the floor. The fans breathe their huge slow breaths overhead. Water knocks behind root-walls and steel plates. In such a noise, voices become thin things, easily swallowed. So the keepers have developed other ways. They communicate in gestures: a flat hand drawn across the throat for stop, a fist raised for hold, two fingers pointing at the eyes and then at a distant lamp for watch that line. They use lamp signals too—short flashes, long sweeps, colored blinks. White for work. Blue-green for cultivation. Red for danger or restricted access. A triple flash might mean a sluice is about to open. A slow amber pulse might mean a fan is reversing and the air will shift. They learn to read these signs the way surface people learn weather.

When they must speak, they do it close to the ear, in short words, half shouted, half flattened by the din. Their voices are hoarse from disuse and from the damp. They do not waste language. They say things like: “Pressure’s up.” “That mat’s soft.” “Don’t stand under.” “Listen.” “Cover the cut.” “Engine’s wrong.” Even their warnings are practical. Fear here is not dramatic. It is precise. It attaches itself to particular mistakes: cutting a root without bracing it, stepping onto a bright green shelf, pausing beneath a condensation ledge, trusting a still pool, ignoring a change in rhythm. The keepers are brave in the way workers become brave—not by lacking fear, but by giving fear a list of rules.

They live in the margins of the machinery. Their shelters are small and scattered: a corrugated room built against a transformer cage, a curtained alcove inside the hollow of an old root column, a sleeping platform above a pipe run where engine heat rises through the boards, a control shed with instruments glowing weakly through the damp. They do not build grand halls. The place discourages monumentality. Anything too rigid eventually cracks as roots thicken beneath it; anything too low eventually floods or is overgrown. Their homes are provisional, repairable, and partly borrowed. They hang lamps from hooks driven into dead wood, not living load-bearing tissue. They lay boards across soft peat to spread their weight. They keep spare coils, fuses, gaskets, and cutting blades in sealed drums. They know which floor panels are safe, which rails are rusted through, which stairs have become slick with algal film, which ladders must be tested before taking full weight.

Their food comes from the valley’s plant life, though it is not abundant or cheerful. They cultivate pale tubers in heated beds near engine outlets, where waste warmth encourages slow growth. They grow tough, grey-green mats of edible moss in shallow troughs under blue-green lamps. They harvest fungal shelves from the sides of old root cuts, dry them near ducts, and break them into flavorless cakes. They collect condensation in metal trays and boil it with resinous herbs that taste of sap and iron. In some basins there may be vats where algae thickens into a curd that can be pressed and salted. None of it is colorful. None of it smells like sunshine. It is food for endurance: dense, warm, faintly bitter, filling the body without delight. They eat standing up, or squatting beside a pump, with their lamps still on and their tools still within reach.

They sleep in fragments. The machinery does not keep human hours. Fans cycle. Pumps engage. Pressure valves release with distant thumps. Somewhere, always, something is starting or stopping. So the keepers learn to rest in shallow intervals, waking at the right change in vibration before they know why they are awake. Their dreams are full of sound. They dream of engines failing, of roots splitting, of red lamps multiplying in the dark, of sudden silence. Silence frightens them more than noise. In the basin, silence means that something essential has stopped. Air is not moving. Water is not draining. Heat is not circulating. The living architecture may begin to fail. To wake into perfect quiet is to wake into emergency.

Their relationship to the vegetation is not one of admiration in the ordinary sense. They do not look at the moss and think it beautiful, not usually. They look at it the way a sailor looks at weather. They know which green is stable and which is treacherous. A dark, tight mat may carry weight. A bright, glossy patch may be young, waterlogged, and ready to swallow a boot. A root column that looks solid may be hollowed by black rot behind its outer fibers. A hanging curtain of roots may be harmless, or it may be bearing a load that would shift if cut. They carry marking chalk, fiber ties, and small steel tags to indicate what has been tested, what must not be touched, and what is scheduled for pruning. They respect the plants not because they are gentle but because they are structural. The plants are the ceiling, the floor, the wall, the scaffold, and the slow danger.

There is also tenderness in them, though it is hard to see at first. They seal fresh cuts with resin. They wrap wounded root ends in damp sacking until growth closes the surface. They clear drainage channels by hand when machinery would tear too much. They place heated coils near young roots that need encouragement. They mark saplings or new root-shoots with red thread so no one will step on them. They speak of certain old root-columns with a kind of rough reverence, not as gods but as necessary elders. “That one holds the east gallery,” they might say, or, “Don’t lean your line there; she’s tired.” They do not mean the root is human. They mean it matters. They mean it has a place in the balance. They mean that if it fails, many other things will fail with it.

They age quickly in some ways and slowly in others. The cold enters their joints. The damp settles in their chests. Their hearing dulls to low sound and sharpens for irregularities. They can feel a pump falter through the soles of their boots. They can tell when a fan is changing pitch by the way the hair on their arms moves. They know the valley by pressure, smell, and vibration more than by sight. Their eyes are accustomed to dimness, to weak halos around lamps, to sudden contrasts between floodlight and black shadow. They squint in brightness if they ever encounter it. They dislike open, windless spaces. They prefer enclosed distances, where the dark has a ceiling and the sounds have sources.

When one of them dies, the body is not simply buried, because ordinary burial would be a waste and a disturbance. The keepers lay the dead into prepared peat beds, often near inactive shelves where the substrate is deep but stable. They fold the body into root-fiber mats, sometimes with tools, sometimes with a lamp, sometimes with a cutting from a plant the person tended. Then they allow the valley to take it back. The peat darkens. The moss spreads. The roots arrive in thin pale threads. In a few seasons, the place where a keeper lay becomes slightly raised, slightly greener, slightly firmer than the surrounding ground. No one walks there carelessly. No one speaks of it as sacred in a lofty way. It is simply known: that patch is someone. That root grew from someone’s ribs. That moss holds someone’s quiet heat. The dead become part of the architecture, not as ghosts, but as substance.

But the keepers are not the only large lives there.

There are other residents, older or stranger, depending on how one defines life. Because the valley is overwhelmingly plant, some of its inhabitants are plant in a way that approaches animation without becoming animal. They are not trees, though they may have the mass of trees. They are not beasts, though they may move. They are not machines, though they may be threaded with cable, pipe, and heat coils. They are the valley’s more deliberate growths: shaped, cultivated, perhaps partly awakened by the interplay of machinery and root-intelligence. They are the green slow ones, the root-formed bodies, the living supports that have become something more than supports.

Some appear as tall root-columns, thicker than ordinary pillars, their surfaces ridged and damp, their bases swollen where they drink from hidden moisture seams. These columns do not merely hold the ceiling. They seem to attend to it. Where stress gathers, they thicken. Where water weakens a joint, they send new fibers across. They respond slowly, over weeks and years, but they respond. The keepers know them by their shapes and positions: a twisted column near a collapsed gallery, a pair of fused pillars above a sluice, a black-green buttress with old drill scars along one side. They do not speak to them, exactly, but they talk around them. They place lamps at their bases. They clear debris from their roots. They avoid cutting them except under extreme necessity, and even then they brace first, apologize second, and seal the wound afterward.

Some of these plant-bodies are lower and more spreading. They form dense mats that creep forward by millimeters, binding loose peat and covering old machinery. They are not aggressive, but they are persistent. If a catwalk is left unused, they will rise around its edges. If a pipe leaks warmth, they will gather there, thickening into a living sleeve. If a control panel is abandoned, they may grow into its housing, not with malice but with the blind patience of tissue seeking moisture and minerals. In such cases, the machine does not die so much as change kingdoms
