What lives there has learned to exist in the pause before resolution. At first you would say nothing lives. The tableland offers only black stone, frost-heaved gravel, pale crusts of salt, shallow basins where ice keeps a gray memory of water. But after a time you begin to see the marks of habitation: hollows worn smooth by repeated kneeling, cairns leaning just enough to shed the wind, tracks that become less certain the harder you look.

The beings who live there are not mountain-born, not sky-born, but raised. Their bodies have been thinned by altitude in the way voices are thinned by distance. Close up, they are narrow and folded, with long limbs that move in careful economy. At a distance they seem taller than they are, as if the unresolved air has stretched them. Their chests are deep, ribbed like old boats, and along their sides are slits that open and close with the faintest click, equalizing pressure. They breathe not only through mouth and nose but through these slits, drawing in the thin air in long, patient sips. Their hearts are slow and large; their blood is dark and slightly sweet with cold.

They have little use for faces as lowlanders understand them. Their eyes are wide, matte, and set slightly back beneath a shelf of bone, made to gather dimness rather than detail. They blink sideways. Their ears are not ears but shallow concavities along the skull, membranes stretched over delicate lattices of bone. They hear pressure more than sound. A footstep is a small displacement. A coming frost is a bright line inside the jaw. Another person nearby is a soft fullness behind the eyes. Because of this, they can tell when the air is about to change long before any cloud appears, and they can hear the lowland storms crawling far below like animals trapped in a pit.
