What lives there does not first appear as a body. It appears as a darkening on stone.

A film no thicker than breath on glass, almost invisible until the light catches it at a low angle, living on sun, heat differences, and the faint ionic hunger of mineral surfaces. It is the first inhabitant of the country, the base on which everything else stands. It does not grow in leaves or stems. It spreads in patches, crusts, veins, and breath-like stains across sunlit rock, especially where thermal gradients meet conductive minerals. To the careless eye it is only patina. To those who live there, it is pasture, ancestor, battery, and omen. It is the sign that the stone is not merely stone.

From that quiet living skin come the larger lives.

On the open tablelands move the great grazing herds: broad, low-bodied creatures built like terraces given legs. Their shells are fused plates of mineralized tissue, worn smooth by dust and time. They do not resemble soft animals. They resemble moving kiln-work, boulders that have acquired joints, slow machines that have decided to persist. Their mouths are wide scraping plates, and they feed by drawing their faces across sun-warmed rock, lifting the electrochemical films without stripping the stone bare. Digestion in them is a long, dry process, more like slow chemistry than eating. A meal may sustain one of them for days. Their bodies hold heat well; their joints click faintly as they walk; dust settles in the seams of their shells and is not brushed away.

They are not hurried animals. Nothing in that country hurries. Their migrations follow invisible lines of charge, warmth, and mineral bloom, routes older than the towns, older than some of the rail lines that now cross the plains. When a herd moves near a settlement, the people do not chase it or panic. They observe. They adjust gates. They close certain low streets. They wait. Some towns are built deliberately beside habitual herd paths, using the creatures’ bulk as moving windbreaks and taking advantage of the plates they shed. A shed plate is not garbage there. It is material, blessing, architecture, memory. It may become a roof tile, a wall facing, a market tray, a shrine panel, or the outer skin of a child’s first ceremonial coat.

The young of these herds are born dry and articulated, small enough to hide beneath the broader bodies of adults. They learn the routes not by sound but by vibration and by the subtle electrical texture of the ground. They are not protected by softness. They are protected by placement, patience, and the mass of the herd. When a calf dies, it is not mourned as a soft thing would be mourned. It is left where it falls unless it lies in a path or near a settlement. Its shell may be harvested, its mineral body returned to the long economy of the land. Nothing there is sentimental about death, but nothing is careless either.

Above them live the gliders.

They are thin, membrane-bodied, almost impossible to see individually. They do not flap so much as adjust. They ride thermal columns with minute movements of their lattice-like bodies, filtering charged dust from the air. At certain hours, especially when heat rises from the plains and static tightens along the escarpments, they gather in immense loose veils overhead. They are not beautiful in the way of birds. They are beautiful the way a heat shimmer is beautiful: impersonal, vast, barely present. They distort the distance. They make the horizon tremble slightly. They do not call. The only sound they make is a faint rasping when many membranes shift at once, like dry cloth being remembered rather than touched.

The people of the place do not hunt them. They do not farm them. They read them. A veil of gliders means changing charge. A sudden thinning means a shift in thermal wind. A low, dense gathering above a mesa can mean that static will soon collect along the metal arrays. Children learn to watch for these patterns before they learn to speak in full sentences. In some towns, the first lesson a child receives is not a word but a gesture: be still, look up, notice what the air is doing.

In the settlements, life becomes denser and stranger.

The cities are not empty of fauna; they are full of it, but the fauna has adapted to walls, ducts, rails, and machines. There are polished beetles with carapaces like dark ceramic. They move through transformer yards and processing halls, cleaning dust from moving parts and feeding on residues that would otherwise clog fine mechanisms. They are tolerated, encouraged, sometimes bred for particular tasks. A city with healthy beetles is a city whose machines breathe more easily.

There are flat, coin-like creatures that live inside warm walls. They move in geometric patterns across heated surfaces, apparently feeding on leakage and helping to balance small electrical irregularities. No one removes them without reason. To strip a wall of its coin-life is to invite imbalance. Children are taught not to tap the walls where they gather. The patterns they make are not random; old wall-keepers can look at those patterns and tell whether a building is drying too fast, holding too much charge, or beginning to crack from thermal strain.

There are burrowers beneath rail lines and paved aprons. Their tunnels aerate compacted ground and prevent certain collapses. Engineers do not always welcome them, but they have learned to work with them. A rail route may be shifted by a few meters because a burrowing population has already stabilized a stretch of ground. Sometimes the animals are inconvenient. Sometimes their tunnels intersect foundations. But the people do not see this as war. They see it as negotiation. The land is already inhabited before a road is laid. The road must learn the land.

There are large sedentary filterers attached to exhaust ducts and thermal vents. They look almost like growths: broad, plated, still. They live on waste heat and airborne particulates. They are not decorative, but they are useful. They soften the output of industrial vents, trap coarse dust, and shed their outer layers in regular cycles. When they molt, workers collect the shed material and use it for insulation or composite brick. In some towns, a duct that has supported three generations of filterers is considered more valuable than a newly built one, because the living layer has become part of the machine’s function.

The boundary between animal, building, and machine is not firm there.

A wall may be made of shed animal plates. A vent may be kept clear by nesting creatures. A rail line may follow a migration route because the herd’s passage compacts the ground in useful ways. A factory may house beetle colonies in its lower chambers because they clean what hands cannot reach. A house may be considered healthy not only because its roof is sound but because its wall-life is steady, its burrowers below are not too numerous, and its heat-exchange surfaces are visited by the right kinds of dust-feeders. To live there is to live among many lives, most of them quiet, most of them useful, none of them ornamental in the ordinary sense.

And then there are the people themselves.

They are humanoid in basic form, but they have become something suited to that high, dry, warm, mineral world. They are not tall in a way that challenges the sky. Their bodies are low-centered, strong through the legs and shoulders, built for stability in wind and for long, deliberate movement across abrasive ground. Their feet are broad and careful, shaped to distribute weight without raising dust. Their hands are precise, patient, and often callused in patterns that reveal their work: film-tenders have smooth fingertips; rail-keepers have hardened palms; static-workers bear fine scars where charge has found them and been guided away.

Their skin is dry and dense, more like burnished clay or fine-grained stone than soft hide. It does not sweat. It does not weep. It is sealed against the air. Along the jaw, wrists, spine, and sometimes the outer edges of the face, fine conductive filaments rise when the static field changes. These filaments are not hair in any simple sense. They are sensory structures, delicate and strong, capable of reading charge in the air the way another body might read smell.
