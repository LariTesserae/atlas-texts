The basin is not possessed. It is listened to.

To describe what lives there, begin by giving up the idea of a single large tenant. The hollow is too wet and too old for mastery. It is lived in as a bell is lived in: by vibration, by moisture, by small motions that are amplified rather than owned.

The first lives one notices are the obvious ones: midges rising in loose columns, dippers flashing along the streams, frogs calling from black seeps, wagtails and pipits picking over wet stone, ravens crossing the rim, an ibex or mountain hare appearing and vanishing again. These are true inhabitants too, but they are not the whole answer. They are pulses, visitors, signals. If there is a being that belongs to the hollow in the same way the weir, the cairns, and the low bothy belong to it, that being is smaller, quieter, and more patient than any traveler expects.

Call them, if a name is needed, the Drip-Listeners. It is not a name they would use often. They would be more likely to answer to a particular click of water on stone, to a whistle thin as a pipit’s call, to the pause after an echo. But the name fits, because listening is their first work and their oldest sense.

They are not many. The basin could not hold many. Perhaps two or three are present in the warm wet months; perhaps no more than a handful over a whole year. In the hardest cold they may withdraw into deeper rock seams, or sink into a slow, insulated quiet beneath the bothy roof, waking only when the seeps begin to move again. Even when they are there, they seem occasional, because they have learned to live at the threshold of visibility. A person standing on the rim might look across the green hollow and see only stone, moss, water-shine, and cloud shadow. The watchers would already have seen the person.

In shape they are near human, but compressed and altered by the place. A grown one crouched low might seem no larger than a child, though its eyes would be too old for that impression. Their bodies are compact and round-shouldered, dense rather than heavy, with the look of things that have been kept low by wind and wet. Their skin is cool to the touch and mottled: peat-brown, stone-gray, and, in places where moisture lingers, faintly green, as if algae had learned to live on them as it lives on rock. It is not exactly scale or fur; it is more like a living dampness, a surface that drinks light. Their hair is dark and close, often beaded with water. Their eyes are large, black or deep amber, with a film that comes across them in bright sun or when they are listening hard. In dimness those eyes catch light like the surface of a pool.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. The palms are broad, the fingers long, the pads thick and sensitive enough to feel the difference between stone that will bear weight and moss that only pretends to. They can press a hand to the ground and seem to read the water table beneath it: where it is rising, where it is hesitating, where it will break through next. Their feet are wide and spreading, the toes long and practical, the soles tough but not numb. They move over wet rock with a certainty that looks like luck until you notice how carefully they place each step. They smell of rain, crushed saxifrage, cold peat, and the mineral sharpness of streams. If they stand still long enough, mist gathers on their shoulders as on boulders.

They wear little, because little can stay dry. A wrap of woven hair or cured hide, darkened with age and damp; a pouch for bone hooks, flakes of sharp stone, and cord; perhaps a hood or cloak stitched from shed skins and lined with moss where warmth is needed. Their possessions are few and repairable. They do not accumulate. What they keep has to be carried, buried, or left to the water without grief. The basin teaches that much.

The marks they leave are modest for a reason. The bothy is half sunk into the moraine not only for shelter but because a raised structure would be too visible to weather. The roof is sod and hide, low enough to be mistaken for a swell of ground. The windbreak is stacked stone, but not too straight; they leave gaps so gusts pass through rather than topple it. The cairns marking safe passage are capped with moss so they seem to have grown there. The weir is woven branches and weighted stones, bent by each season’s flow and repaired according to the new shape the water demands. They do not build as if they intend to stay forever in one fixed arrangement. They build as if they are negotiating with a powerful, patient neighbor.

Their days move with the weather’s pulse. They wake before full light, often before the birds, and listen. They can tell from the first sounds whether the night’s mist has thickened, whether the seeps are running faster, whether the central pool has risen. They test the air, then the ground. A staff of polished wood may be used, not for age but for reading. They go from stone to stone along the drier seams, sometimes alone, sometimes in pairs, moving with a low, unhurried economy. If a channel has choked with moss or debris, they clear only enough to let the water remember its course. If a path has become treacherous, they add a stone or shift a cairn by a finger’s width. If the weir has been bent, they study the damage before touching it, because the damage is also information.

They feed sparingly and with attention. The basin does not offer bulk, so they do not seek it. They gather tender shoots of sedge, glossy leaves near the pools, the swollen green mats that grow where spray is constant. They take insect larvae from cold seeps with fine bone hooks, and sometimes small amphibians, but not often; most creatures in the hollow are more useful alive than eaten. They harvest what has been exposed by flood or trapped in shrinking pools, treating such accidents as invitations rather than rights. They drink from moving films of water over rock, not from the still center of the deep pool, which they approach more carefully. What they cannot eat or use immediately they store in cold seeps or dry in thin air, though they do not hoard. A full store would make them careless, and carelessness here is a kind of noise.

Their senses are tuned to surfaces. The thin skin of moss, the film on water, the air just above the wet ground: these are their fields of knowledge. They hear the basin as a single instrument. Every drip has a character; every waterfall has a mood; every gust through sedges tells them which part of the rim the weather is entering. They feel the vibration of small movements through stone and water. A frog leaping, a beetle landing, a bird taking off, a crack of frost in the night—none of these are lost to them. Their hearing is so finely distributed that it can seem, to an outsider, like magic. It is not magic. It is attention carried to an extreme over many generations.

Their speech is quiet and strange. They do not talk often, and when they do it is usually in sounds that fit the basin: clicks made at the back of the throat, soft hisses, whistles that resemble wingbeats, vowels shaped so they carry without startling. They use echo the way other people use gesture. A call sent across the bowl may return from three directions, softened and delayed; they can read those returns as a map. To a stranger, a single voice might seem to become several, or a remembered sound might seem to answer before it has truly been made. They do not correct this misunderstanding. In their language, memory and presence are not always sharply divided.

Names, when they use them, are not fixed words but patterns of sound: the particular drip from an overhang after a long rain, the hollow note of a certain stone, the first creak of a snowfield in sun. A name may change with the season. An elder may have several names, each known only to one other. They do not introduce themselves casually. To hear your own sound reflected back from the cliffs, altered and delayed, is their way of saying that you have been noticed.

Children are few. The basin would not tolerate more. When one is born, it is usually after a period of warm rain, when insects are thick over the pools and the moss smells sweet. The infant is dark-eyed, quiet, carried in a sling of woven grass against an adult’s chest. It learns the hollow first by vibration: the beat of waterfalls, the hum of wind, the sudden silence before hail. Later it learns paths. A child is taught not to walk quickly but to walk correctly, finding firm ground by sound and pressure. One of their early lessons is to sit still through a shower and afterward name what was heard. The lesson is not about obedience. It is about becoming permeable.

They age slowly, or perhaps their aging is simply less visible. An elder may look worn down to essentials: face like wet stone, hands stained green, eyes so still they seem to be holding the sky in them. Elders become less mobile but more present. They sit near the bothy or beside the central pool and listen for long periods, sometimes through whole weather changes. When they die, there is no great monument. The body may be laid in a dry seam of peat with a smooth stone placed at the head, or carried to a snowfield and left to the slow water. The others do not speak much of death. They treat it as another kind of subsidence, a movement into the basin’s deeper quiet.

Their law, if they have one, is simple: water decides. It decides the path, the camp, the store, the season. They do not argue with it; they learn its moods. They know where the ground is treacherous, where the mist will gather first, where the pool will rise when the mountain’s breath quickens. They know that some sounds are warnings and some are merely weather. They know that the hollow is loud, and that loudness demands restraint. To shout here is not freedom but clumsiness. To build too much is to invite collapse. To take too much is to make the place poorer. Their intelligence is practical, but it is also ethical in the quietest sense: they have learned what the basin allows.

This is why their tools remain primitive by lowland standards. Not because they are incapable, but because they have no need of heavy mastery. A wooden spade hardened by fire is enough to cut a small channel. A bone hook is enough to lift what must be lifted. Cord of hair or sinew is enough to bind a weir without choking it. Sharp stone is enough. The tools fit the hand and can be repaired. More complex machinery would impose a rhythm foreign to the place: too fast, too loud, too hungry. They prefer small forces, understood completely.

There is strangeness in them, as there is in the basin. They know the pool that rises and falls too quickly for the weather, and they do not fear it. They have learned its breath. When mist seems to climb the cliffs before falling again, they move with it rather than against it. When echoes mislead, they use the misdirection to locate one another. At times they seem to be in more than one place: a flicker by the weir, a shape under the overhang, a low figure at the rim, all of them perhaps the same person moving by stages, or perhaps the basin remembering several at once. They are flesh, not ghosts; but flesh here has learned to live thinly, on surfaces, in intervals.

If you came there, you might not see them at all. You would arrive breathless, perhaps, from the broken stair of rock and wind-clipped grass, and the hollow would strike you with its loud emptiness. You would hear water everywhere. You would see green moss, dark cliffs, shallow channels, snow in shaded seams. You might notice the cairns and suppose they were old. You might see the bothy and suppose it abandoned. Then, after you had stopped moving and your own noise had fallen away, something would change. A pebble would tap once on firm ground where no pebble had been. A reed would bend against the wind. A pool would tremble without rain. You would look up and find, near the weir or beside a pool, a shape that had not been there before: low, still, wrapped in gray-green, face like a stone that had once been warm.

They would not approach quickly. They might not approach at all. If you were loud, hurried, grasping, they would remain hidden, and the basin would seem merely beautiful and indifferent. If you sat down, if you let your breath lengthen, if you did not try to drain or straighten or claim anything, they might show you the path. Not with words. A thrown stone might mark a dry step. A low whistle might indicate the side of a pool that would bear weight. A hand, perhaps, might rise once from mist and point toward the safer seam. If you followed, you would find yourself led without ever seeing a guide. If you tried to thank them too loudly, the echo would take your thanks and scatter it. If you left something—a smooth stone, a dry thread, a handful of clean moss placed neatly rather than scattered—you might later find it moved, not taken, but acknowledged.

One of them, if one must be singled out, is likely to be an old keeper of the watercourses. This one has fingers stained green to the joints and a cloak so patched with moss and hide that it seems grown rather than sewn. She—or he, or simply it, for the basin wears gender lightly—sits by the weir in the thin sun, untangling a cord of hair, listening to the little knocks of water against wood. When the central pool breathes, this elder places a palm flat on a nearby stone and waits. The waiting is not passive. It is a kind of asking. Then the elder moves a single branch, adjusts a stone, clears a clot of moss from a groove. The water accepts the correction, or rejects it. Either way, the elder nods, because both answers are useful.

This one remembers seasons not as calendars but as levels: the year the pool rose over the second flat stone, the year the frogs came late, the year the snowfield survived into harvest, the year a stranger shouted and the ravens answered for days. This one knows that the basin is not a treasure to be kept but a balance to be entered. She does not speak of ownership. If asked, she might make a sound like water slipping over cold rock, which could be laughter or warning. The basin has taught her that explanations are often heavier than necessary.

Whoever lives there, then, is not a single dominant presence. It is a listener with hands. It is a small, patient, amphibious people shaped by wetness, echo, and restraint. It is the one who mends the weir without mastering the stream, who marks the path without scarring the ground, who hears the mountain’s ordinary daylight and does not pretend to explain it. They live in the thin layer where water, sound, and attention meet. Their abundance is not in numbers or bulk but in motion, signal, and seasonal intensity: a flicker, a call, a repair, a footprint filling slowly with water.

The basin holds them as it holds everything: lightly, wetly, with room for silence between sounds. They are its tenants, its tenders, perhaps its senses. And if the hollow feels inhabited even when no figure can be seen, that is not because it is haunted. It is because someone there is listening back.
