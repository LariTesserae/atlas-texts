What lives in the Wet Throat is not a single kind of life, and not a stable population. It is a pressure of lives. It is a crowded, wet, mineral-fed animality that has filled every crack, shelf, pool, shaft, and dripping wall because the place keeps making openings faster than they can become finished. Nothing there belongs to a settled world. The inhabitants are creatures of pulse, leak, warmth, collapse, and sudden abundance. They live in a landscape that is partly geology and partly themselves.

The first and most fundamental inhabitants are not the larger animals at all, but the films.

They coat the warm stone in rust, bone-white, salt-grey, copper-dark, and shell-blue. They are bacterial and mineral skins, living crusts and scums, slick threads and matte patches, sometimes as fine as breath on glass, sometimes thick as spilled milk where a warm fissure has been leaking for days. They are not green. There is no leaf or root or moss-like softness in them. They smell of wet salt, ammonia, hot iron, and crowded water. They grow where moisture, heat, and mineral chemistry meet, and they are the base of everything else. The whole roaring, clicking, scuttling life of the Wet Throat begins with these thin living surfaces.

Everything else either scrapes them, strains them, eats the things that scrape them, or feeds on the dead that fall into them.

The walls are inhabited first by things that seem almost architectural.

There are flat, suckered forms pressed against the wet stone, some as small as coins, others as broad as shields. Their undersides are soft and muscular; their upper surfaces are often crusted with mineral deposits, so that they look like plates of salt-stained tile. They shift slowly, sucking their way across damp rock, rasping bacterial films with mouth-parts worn smooth by a lifetime of grazing. Where they rest for long, their secretions cement mineral grains around them, making little rims and shelves. When a drip strikes them, they ripple open. When the air dries, they seal themselves and wait.

Other wall-dwellers live stacked over one another. Armored bodies crowd into fissures, one atop another, their shells overlapping like rough slate. They are not truly colonial, but they are tolerant of pressure, and the advantage of the cluster is moisture: the lower ones stay wet longer, the upper ones catch drips first. In some places the wall appears to breathe because thousands of these stacked bodies rise and fall together, opening and closing breathing pores in rhythm with the humidity. In a beam of daylight, their wet shells flash faintly, and the wall seems to blink.

Still others are pale and thin, fitted for narrow cracks. They wedge themselves into fissures with bodies flattened like splinters, only their eyes and feeding limbs emerging when the air is safe. They are quick when touched by warmth. A shaft of sun can awaken a whole face of them, and suddenly the stone is full of tiny scraping sounds as thousands of mouths begin to work the blooming films.

The floor is denser, and stranger, because it is partly made of the creatures that move across it.

Across the mud flats and drying basins are low-backed crawlers in such numbers that the ground appears paved. They are broad, flat-bodied animals with shells roughened by mineral grit. Their secretions bind dust, shell fragments, and crushed skin to their backs, so each one carries a little piece of the landscape. They press shoulder to shoulder, moving slowly, feeding on detritus, dead membranes, bacterial scum, and the broken remains of smaller lives. They are not aggressive, but their mass gives them a kind of accidental safety. When startled, many lock into one another, forming a temporary crust. A predator can cross them, but it must cross a moving pavement of shell and muscle.

Beneath them, burrowers make the surface alive. They are soft-bodied, segmented, and strong-mouthed, more like living tools than elegant animals. They eat through shell-sand and compacted remains, digesting what can be digested and leaving behind dimples, ridges, and little collapse pits. Their movement rearranges the floor. In some places the ground seems to boil gently, as if water were rising through it, but the motion is bodies working just below the surface.

Between the crawlers and burrowers dart the quick things. Small, low, nervous predators and scavengers move in sudden runs. They have hard heads, forward-facing eyes, and limbs built for short bursts. They eat eggs, wounded crawlers, shed skins, dying filter-feeders, and anything stunned by a collapse or pulse of water. They are not rulers of the floor; they are its opportunists. When a plate of ceiling crust crashes into a pool and sends bodies sprawling over the bank, they arrive almost at once.

Egg masses lie in warm depressions wherever heat and moisture collect. Some are abandoned after laying: pale, bead-like clusters glued to stone or half-buried in shell grit. Others are guarded by flat-bodied parents who spread over them like living lids, keeping them moist and turning them slightly when the heat becomes too strong. The eggs are not soft in the way surface eggs might be. Many have mineralized shells or tough membranes, because drying, crushing, and flooding are constant dangers. When they hatch, the young often emerge into water so shallow it is little more than dampness, and they must feed immediately or be eaten.

The air above the basins is not empty. It is crossed by life that has learned to use the warm updrafts rising from the pools and fissures.

Some fliers are membranous, their wings or sails thin and wet-shiny, stretched over light frames of cartilage or chitin. They do not move with the leisurely grace of things born into open skies. They are darting, flickering, nervous. They ride heat columns, catch droplets, skim bacterial films from the air, and snatch smaller fliers in passing. Their shadows race over the ground in rapid bands, adding another layer of movement to the already restless floor.

Others are broad and slow, more like living sheets than birds or insects. Their membranes catch the updrafts; their bodies hang below, moist and heavy. Some have hooks or gripping pads along their edges, allowing them to settle briefly on walls or ceiling shelves. They feed on airborne microbes, shed skins, and fine organic particles lifted by the warmth. When a breeding swarm rises, these broad fliers become hunters, turning through the cloud with open mouths or sticky trailing limbs.

The strangest air-dwellers are the gas-bladdered drifters. They float on warm currents, their bodies suspended by translucent sacs that glow faintly shell-blue or salt-grey in the light shafts. Some trail fine tentacles. Others carry sticky threads or net-like membranes. They do not steer quickly; they adjust slowly, venting or swelling their bladders, rising and falling with heat and pressure. In a shaft of daylight they can look like pale lanterns, until one sees that each lantern is hunting.

Sometimes the air itself seems to molt. Clouds of shed skins rise from below, dry and pale, drifting through the beams like ash. They are not alive, but they are part of the living process: the discarded coverings of swarming creatures, caught in updrafts, carried until they tear apart or fall into pools where scavengers will finish them.

The pools are thicker with life than either floor or air.

Water in the Wet Throat is not a calm element. It arrives, stays, warms, changes, drains, or overflows. The creatures that live in it must be ready for all of these moods.

Long, soft-bodied swimmers coil through the shallows. They are muscular, flexible, and often nearly shapeless until they move. Some hunt by touch, tasting the water with their whole skins. Others have hard mouth-parts hidden inside soft lips, capable of crushing shell or gripping struggling prey. They are not fish, though they swim; they are closer to living ropes and sacks of muscle, sometimes translucent enough to show the dark shapes of swallowed bodies inside.

Filter-feeders anchor themselves where drips fall or moisture curtains hang. They spread fringed limbs, open mouth-fans, or extend sticky hairs into the moving water. When a steady drip creates a curtain of fine moisture, they become still and precise, catching microscopic life carried downward. When a sudden pulse of runoff enters a basin, they react at once, folding, opening, surging, or releasing reproductive clouds into the disturbed water.

Small armored swimmers dart along the edges of pools. They are hard-shelled, many-limbed, and quick to breed. They graze on films and dead matter, and they are the prey of nearly everything larger. Their shells crack under the feet of crawlers and the jaws of predators, adding to the mineral grit of the ground. In places where they die in great numbers, their broken carapaces form little ridges, pale and shell-blue at first, then ground down to grey powder.

In the deeper or more stable pools are larger predators: long, heavy-bodied forms with mineralized plates and heat-sensitive pits along their sides. They do not chase prey far. They wait, half-buried or folded against rock, then strike with sudden suction or a whip-like coil. Their movements are rare but important. When one shifts, smaller bodies scatter. When it feeds, scavengers gather. When it dies, its body becomes a temporary landscape, picked apart, dissolved, and pressed into the mud.

The pools also contain larval clouds. These are not single creatures but stages: millions of young forms rising from warm water, some still soft, some already armored, all vulnerable. They are food, movement, and future pressure. A pool that seems quiet can be full of them, waiting for heat or moisture to trigger the next burst of growth.

Among the largest lives in the Wet Throat are not predators, but disturbance-bodies.

There are huge, slow creatures that move through the shallows like living ridges. Some walk on thick limbs that might be mistaken for stone columns until they bend. Their backs are crusted with mineral growth and smaller clinging animals. They are not graceful. They do not hunt with speed. They feed by stirring, straining, or crushing, and their passage rearranges the basin floor. When one of them moves, water spills over its flanks, mud plumes upward, and smaller creatures scatter in all directions. Scavengers and cleaners follow them, picking at the life they expose.

Others are broad, low animals that rest in the warmest hollows, their bodies spread like wet hides over stone. They breathe slowly, their sides rising and falling with a heavy rhythm. They may remain motionless for long periods, filtering warm water or absorbing nutrients from mineral-rich seepage. Their presence creates microclimates: heat pools, sheltered shadows, damp refuges. Smaller lives gather near them, not out of affection but because their bodies make conditions easier.

Then there are the creatures that cause silence.

In a place so loud, silence is not peace. It is a warning.

Some predators have learned to move inside the gaps between sounds. They are broad, damp-skinned, and low-slung, their bodies absorbing vibration rather than making it. They do not roar or click. They wait until the general noise covers their approach, or they create stillness by their mere presence, as smaller animals sense them and fall quiet. A sudden hush in one basin can mean that one of these hunters has entered. The makers, and every other alert thing, know that silence may be more dangerous than any scream.

And among all these lives, there are the makers.

They are not numerous. The Wet Throat cannot support many tool-users, because it has no surplus that can be stored for long and no stable ground on which to build lasting settlements. But they are there. Their camps are small, temporary, and carefully placed. Their works are scattered marks in a much larger animal pressure. They are part of the fauna, not apart from it.

Their bodies are built for the place.

They stand low and move close to the ground. Their centers of gravity are deep in the hips and chest, because falls are common and surfaces shift. Their feet are broad, with thick gripping pads and small hook-like spurs that catch wet stone. Their hands are their greatest advantage: long-fingered, strong, sensitive, with palms rough enough to hold slick rock and thumbs capable of fine work. Between their fingers are loose membranes that help when they swim, but these fold away when they climb or handle tools. Their skin is smooth, pebbled slightly at the shoulders and back, dark as wet charcoal with salt-grey mottling. It secretes a thin protective film that guards against salt, ammonia, and constant damp. They are not feathered or furred; they are sleek, and when they emerge into a light shaft they gleam faintly with moisture.

Their heads are broad rather than long. Their mouths are wide, filled with replaceable scraping and gripping teeth suited to a mixed diet of scraped films, shell meat, eggs, dried flesh, and mineral-rich pastes. Their nostrils close in water. Their ears are protected by muscular flaps that can seal against grit and pressure. Their eyes are large, adapted to low light and sudden glare, with inner membranes that clear mist from them. Along the jaw and brow are heat-sensitive pits, useful for finding warm currents, unstable stone, and bodies hidden in the damp.

They do not look like masters of the Wet Throat. They look like creatures who have learned to pass through it carefully.

Their camps are built for pause, not permanence. A camp may be no more than a low ring of fitted stones used to anchor shelters, keep tools from being washed away, and mark a temporary claim. The shelters themselves are windbreaks and spray-breaks, framed with ribs, spines, or stiff pieces of chitin, covered with large shed carapaces. They are low enough that a flood surge may pass over them, light enough to be carried if the band moves, simple enough to abandon without grief.

Their tools are primitive, but they are effective because they are constantly repaired and constantly adjusted. They chip stone into sharp edges, using hard flakes for cutting and heavier cores for pounding. They shape bone points from the remains of larger animals, hardening them in warm air or rubbing them with mineral pastes until they become dense. They make scrapers from shell, choosing the densest layers and polishing them against stone. Their cords are twisted from sinew, gut, or drawn-out animal fibers, and their knots are simple but reliable. Their nets are loosely woven, meant to be repaired quickly and used in shallow flows rather than cast with elegance.

They carry water in bladders taken from larger creatures or in sealed clay vessels shaped by hand and hardened near heat vents. Their hooks are carved from dense bone or the hardest shell. Their spears are short, barbed, and meant for close work in pools or crowded crevices. Some carry weighted throwing stones, not because they hunt at a distance often, but because a thrown stone can stun a pool-dweller, break a crust, or signal another band across noise.

Everything they own can be carried. Everything they build can be left behind.

They feed by following water.

When a new drip appears, they notice it before most animals do. They listen with feet pressed to stone, with cheeks close to the air, with hands laid flat on ledges. They know the difference between a drip that will last an hour and one that will grow into a stream. They place shallow clay cups, woven mats, or shell basins where moisture will gather. They scrape bacterial films into vessels, letting heavier mineral particles settle before eating the softer layer. They set small weirs of stacked shell and clay to guide water creatures toward narrow pits, then harvest them with hooks, nets, or bare hands.

When a breeding swarm rises, they may wait at the edges with nets, catching falling bodies as the swarm collapses. When a pulse of runoff enters a basin, they move quickly to harvest the surge before it turns predatory. They collect eggs from warm depressions, taking only what they can carry and leaving enough to keep the pulse going. They pry wall-grazers from stone, turn over dead plates of crust, and follow large disturbance-bodies at a safe distance, waiting for the smaller lives they expose.

They do not farm in the sense of rooted fields or planted seeds. But they do tend.

A band may redirect a thin trickle toward a rock face where films grow well. They may clear dead crust from a shelf so that bacterial scum can bloom there again. They may scatter mineral-rich paste in damp hollows, knowing that warmth and moisture will turn it into food for smaller grazers. This is not agriculture as a surface world would understand it. It is more like listening to the place and helping it continue. Their husbandry is hydrological, chemical, and temporary.

They communicate less with voices than with struck surfaces.

Their throats can make sounds: low clicks, wet croaks, rattling exhalations, and short alarm calls. But in the roar of the Wet Throat, voice alone is often swallowed. So they use percussion. A spear-butt tapped on shell. A stone knocked against stone. A thrown pebble striking a pool. A rhythmic scrape along a ledge. These sounds carry through rock and water better than speech.

Each band has its own cadences. A particular pattern of taps may mean “safe ledge,” “moving,” “danger below,” “water coming,” or “harvest here.” They can send messages through the ground itself, and young children learn to feel meaning through their feet before they learn to produce it clearly. In a place where silence is dangerous, their communication is often a way of proving that they are still alive, still present, still moving together.

Their social life is shaped by flow.

They do not own land in any lasting sense. A mud flat may be gone by morning. A ledge may collapse under the weight of animals. A pool may drain through a newly opened crack. A light shaft may shift with the sun and lose its usefulness. So their claims are over access, not area. A band may hold the right to use a drip while it tends that drip. It may claim a pool while it is present, a weir while the weir stands, a warm shelf while it keeps the shelf clear. If it leaves, the claim weakens. If another group arrives and begins maintaining the flow, the old claim becomes memory.

Their markers are temporary: stacked shells, knot-cords tied to bone pegs, clay daubs on stone, arranged fragments of carapace. These are not monuments. They are reminders, more like a hand laid briefly on a moving thing. Disputes are often settled by displays of rhythm and presence rather than by battle. Two bands may face each other across a pool, tapping competing patterns, reading each other’s numbers, readiness, and resolve. If violence comes, it is close and sudden: thrown stones, short spears, grappling in the wet, then one side withdrawing before the noise draws predators.

They are not gentle. They cannot afford to be.

They kill when they must, scavenge when they can, and abandon what cannot be saved. But they are also careful. They know that excess can turn a rich place into a dead one. They know that if they take every egg from a warm hollow, there will be no next pulse. They know that if they poison a pool with too many bodies or too much waste, the moisture will turn sour. Their ethics, if one can call them that, are not based on kindness alone. They are based on continuity. The place gives, but only to those who do not break the giving.

Their young are born wet, dark, and clinging.

Infants have strong gripping hands almost from the beginning. They ride on the backs or chests of caregivers, holding on while the adult climbs, swims, or moves through crowds of animals. They are fed first with softened films and regurgitated pastes, then with small shell meats and dried strips of flesh. Children learn by imitation: where to place a foot so the stone will not shift, how to listen for a hollow space, how to tell a stable ledge from one built only of crust and shed skins, how to freeze when silence falls, how to move when a pulse arrives.

Elders are valuable not because they are strong, but because they remember. They remember which shafts bring light at certain hours. They remember which basins flooded last, which walls bloomed after heat, which passages closed after rockfall, which pools drained and never returned. In a landscape that keeps remaking itself, memory is one of the few portable treasures. An old maker may be slow, but if it knows the rhythm of the Wet Throat, it can keep a band alive.

They probably do not think of themselves as separate from the noise.

Their myths, if they have them, are likely rhythms rather than stories. Their sacred things are probably not objects but events: the first tap of a new drip, the moment a light shaft crosses a pool, the silence before a collapse, the shared harvest after a pulse. They would not build temples there. The Wet Throat would eat them. Instead, they might mark a moment by striking a stone three times and listening until the echo becomes indistinguishable from the roar.

They bury nothing.

The dead are not hidden in soil, because there is no soil in the surface sense. The ground is shell, bone, grit, membrane, crust, and compressed remains. A body left still will be cleaned by mouths, claws, water, heat, and time. The makers know this. They likely treat death as a transfer. A dead bandmate may be laid in a warm current, given to the pools, or placed where scavengers can work quickly. There may be a brief pattern of taps, a final rhythm, then movement. To remain too long with the dead is to invite stillness, and stillness is dangerous.

They are prey, too.

For all their tools, they are not safe. A hush-mouth can take one from the edge of a camp if the band grows careless. A pool predator can seize a child or an unwary adult. A collapse can bury several at once. A swarm pressed too tightly can crush the weak. A sudden flood can wash away tools, food, and bodies in the same moment. They survive by being alert, by moving in small groups, by keeping sound between them, and by knowing when to abandon everything.

This is why their camps are placed near moisture and light, but not in the heaviest flow. They need water, warmth, and visibility. They also need escape. A good camp has stable stone beneath it, a pool that will last at least a little while, a light shaft that can be used for drying and orientation, and at least two routes away. They do not build toward permanence. They build toward the next hour.

Their presence changes the place only slightly, but meaningfully.

A weir may redirect a stream long enough to carve a new channel. A ring of stones may protect a patch of film from being trampled. A drying rack may scent a wall with preserved flesh, drawing scavengers. A thrown stone may startle prey into a crevice, where another animal finds it later. A tap-pattern may echo through a basin and make other creatures pause. Even their smallest acts enter the larger turbulence.

They are not the throat’s masters. They are one of its voices.

If one asks, then, who lives in the Wet Throat, the answer must be layered.

The films live there, eating stone and making the first food. The wall-clingers live there, breathing in unison across damp rock. The floor-crawlers live there, making pavement of their own backs. The burrowers live there, turning the ground into motion. The quick-jawed scavengers live there, running between larger bodies. The membrane fliers and gas-bladdered drifters live there, hunting the light shafts and warm updrafts. The swarming lives live there, rising and falling in clouds. The pool-coilers, filter-feeders, armored darters, and ambush predators live there, thickening the water with hunger and reproduction. The great disturbance-bodies live there, moving like slow weather through the basins. The silent hunters live there, making quiet mean danger.

And the makers live there: low, careful, tool-bearing, percussive, temporary. They are flesh of the place. They drink its water, eat its films, wear its shed parts, build with its bones, and disappear into its noise when the pressure turns against them.

More truly, what lives in the Wet Throat is a condition: crowded, warm, wet, mineral, loud, and endlessly unfinished. Every creature there is both inhabitant and construction material. Every body leaves something behind: shell, skin, bone, secretion, dung, heat, movement, sound. The place is made of them as much as they are made by it.

When the light shafts align and the mist glows, the whole region seems to inhale. Wings flash. Pools ripple. Walls shimmer with feeding mouths. The floor shifts under invisible bodies. A camp appears for a moment as a small dark stitch against the mineral basin. Then a swarm rises, a pool overflows, something large moves through the shallows, and the sound becomes a single wet note.

The lives within it do not stop. They quicken. They scatter. They feed. They breed. They die. They become ground.

The Wet Throat is full of them. It has never been empty. It is not waiting to become a landscape. It is already alive, over and over, in every drop, every throat, every scraping shell, every tapping stone.
