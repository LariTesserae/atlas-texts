What lives there does not live by density. It lives by spacing.

There are no crowds in that high mineral city, no nesting places, no burrows, no herds, no swarms, no thickening of tracks around water or shelter. Life there, if one insists on calling it life, has accepted the conditions of the place: thin air, cold dryness, weak light, immense intervals, and a reality that has worn loose at the joints. Whatever resides there has become sparse enough to match the architecture and patient enough to be mistaken for it.

The first and most widespread inhabitants are not creatures in any simple sense. They are the felts, the crusts, the pale crystalline skins that line the shaded places and spread across the undersides of terraces. They are the city’s living surface, though “living surface” is already too solid a phrase for them. They have no centers, no limbs, no mouths, no obvious organs. They exist as organized chemistry, as delicate mineral webs that feed on temperature gradients, charge differentials, and the slow arrival of airborne dust. They grow not toward light but toward stability. Where a wall remains cool for long ages, they thicken. Where a thermal current passes regularly, they align themselves into fine parallel ridges. Where electrostatic charge gathers, they branch into pale, hairlike crystalline maps.

These felts may cover only a hand’s breadth of stone, or they may extend along an entire colonnade, so thinly distributed that they seem less like organisms and more like a discoloration of the rock. In some places they form white seams in black volcanic glass, trembling almost imperceptibly when the charge in the air shifts. In others they become crusts like frosted salt, so brittle that a passing vibration can make a crack run through them with a sound like a distant fingernail tapping glass. They do not decay as wet life decays. When they fail, they powder, flake, or become incorporated into the dust. When they succeed, they become part of the city’s skin, recording centuries of thermal habit in layers no thicker than breath.

They are the foundation of whatever passes for an ecosystem there. Not plants, not fungi, not animals, but the nearest thing the city has to pasture. Everything else depends on them, however remotely.

The smallest visible beings that move among them are the translucent mites. They are hand-sized, rarely larger, and so clear that one often sees them only as a distortion: a faint bending of the dim light, a subtle refraction along the edge of a wall, a momentary thickening of the air above a charged surface. Their bodies are not fleshy. They are more like articulated mineral films, thin plates and fine crystalline bristles arranged around a central lattice of static charge. They move with careful, deliberate taps, testing surfaces not for solidity but for potential. They prefer the places where dust has just settled or where a temperature difference has left a faint charge gradient across the stone.

They graze on the felts, if grazing is the right word. They do not bite or tear. They press their undersides against a crystalline mat and draw from it a minute transfer of structure: a little charge, a little organized mineral, a little pattern. Sometimes, after they have passed, one can see the faintest etched trails left behind, as if someone had drawn lines on glass with dry frost. The mites are solitary. One may cross kilometers of empty terrace and see none. Then, without warning, there may be three or four gathered along a charged seam, motionless except for the occasional lift of a bristle. They do not swarm. Even their gatherings are sparse, as if too many of them in one place would violate the city’s sense of proportion.

Above them, or rather along the thermal sheets that move through the broad avenues, are the mica gliders. They are flat, thin creatures, closer to flakes of polished stone than to insects. Their bodies are broad and lamellar, made of overlapping crystalline planes that can shift by fractions, altering their edges to catch rising or falling currents of heat. They do not fly in the familiar way. They do not beat wings. They glide. They adjust. They wait for a thermal tide to carry them from one terrace to another, sometimes hanging nearly motionless in the dim air for days while the city’s temperature shifts around them.

They feed, if they feed, by contact with charged dust and by resting on the felts long enough to draw a gradient from them. They are most often seen in the copper-gray light of the city’s day, when weak illumination makes their edges glow faintly as they pass between shadow and exposure. At rest, they lie against black glass or pale ashite and become almost invisible, appearing as nothing more than a seam, a scratch, a flake of mineral skin. When they move, it is usually in silence. One might see a glider cross a plaza in a long, shallow descent, then vanish against a wall as though the wall had accepted it.

In the cracks and under the stairways live the compressed worms. They are pale, segmented, and heavy-looking, as if made from powdered stone pressed into shape by slow force. They inhabit narrow fissures and emerge only when the thermal balance of the city changes: when a shadow lengthens across a sunward shelf, when a hollow tower exhales a different grade of dust, when a static bloom lifts fine grains from a plaza and leaves a new charge in the stone. Their movement is slow and deliberate. They do not burrow so much as negotiate. They enter a crack, expand slightly, and persuade the dust around them to rearrange. Over ages, they may widen a fissure by no more than a hair’s breadth, yet even that is significant in a place where nothing is freshly broken.

They are prey, though predation there is not dramatic. A mite may feed on a young worm, or a mica glider may settle upon one and remain there until some exchange has been completed. More often, the worms simply persist, moving through the city’s fractures like thoughts moving through old stone. They leave behind polished channels and tiny alignments of powder. Some stairways become more pronounced because of them. Some doorways appear, or disappear, because the worms have shifted the dust that gives a threshold its local definition.

Then there are the hunters, if hunters is not too active a word for beings whose chief act is waiting. They are the lithic patiences. They are almost impossible to distinguish from walls, lintels, floor slabs, or the broad faces of retaining stone. They may be creatures, or they may be regions of creatureliness spread across architectural surfaces. A stretch of pocked basalt may be one of them. A low parapet may be one. A broad, featureless panel beside a processional route may have been waiting there for centuries, gathering charge, becoming indistinguishable from the city until the moment a smaller organism passes within reach.

Their strike is not violent in the way a strike is understood in wetter, faster worlds. There is no lunge, no cry, no sudden burst of motion. Instead, a local relation changes. Distance contracts by a small, impossible amount. A wall becomes slightly nearer. A floor becomes more adhesive with charge. A doorway narrows without moving. The prey is held not by teeth but by geometry, by a temporary failure of the ordinary separation between one place and another. Then the predator settles again. It may spend a season, or several seasons, slowly dissolving what it has taken through electrostatic abrasion and mineral absorption. To witness such an event would be to see almost nothing: a faint rasp, a shift in dust, a shadow that afterward seems to belong to a different object.

Larger still are the terrace grazers, though calling them animals may be a mistake born of lowland habit. They are broad, low forms, room-sized in some cases, shaped like slabs or shelves of dull basalt. They move so slowly that they can be mistaken for platforms or collapsed walls. Their upper surfaces are often covered with felts and fine dust; their undersides are fields of charge and crystalline contact. They graze by apposition, pressing themselves against mineral crusts and drawing from them whatever gradient they need. They do not eat as one imagines eating. They acquire pattern. They take in organization. They incorporate stability.

One of these beings may remain at the edge of a stairway for an epoch, leaning slightly as if it has become part of the wind’s grammar. Another may cross a plaza so gradually that only the polishing of the stone beneath it reveals that it has moved at all. They do not gather. They do not call. They are separated by kilometers of vacant city, each one an island of slow life. If one stood beside such a being, one might feel nothing except a faint coolness and a sense that the stone nearby was paying attention to its own balance.

The hollow towers contain another order of residence. They are not alive individually, perhaps, but they are inhabited in a way that blurs the boundary between occupant and organ. Inside them, fine mineral particles are drawn upward, downward, sideways, depending on charge and thermal pressure. The dust forms thin silver threads, veils, and temporary columns. Sometimes these particles are merely dust. Sometimes they behave as colonial things: resonant fields of organized grain that exist only while the tower’s internal gradients maintain them. They make the faint ticking and hissing sounds one hears in the deeper districts. They exhale powder into adjacent streets. They draw in silence and release it altered.

These tower-colonies may be the closest thing the city has to voices. They do not speak, but they resonate. They keep ratios. They preserve intervals. When the light shifts and the thermal tide turns, a tower may change its exhalation, sending a different quality of dust across a plaza. Far away, a felt may respond by thickening. A mite may turn along a charged rail. A mica glider may adjust its course. In this way, the towers participate in the city’s slow circulation without commanding it. They are not machines. They are not instruments. They are more like hollow notes in a vast, dry chord.

Yet if one asks who lives there — not what, but who — the answer becomes stranger.
