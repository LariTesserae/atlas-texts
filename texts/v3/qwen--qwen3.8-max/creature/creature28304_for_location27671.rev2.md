To ask who lives there is already to use the wrong census.

The place is not inhabited in the way a house is inhabited, with distinct bodies moving through finished rooms. It is inhabited the way a lung is inhabited by breath, the way a bell is inhabited by the memory of ringing, the way a constitution is inhabited by arguments that have not yet been spoken. What lives there is not merely a collection of creatures. It is a living negotiation: a vegetable municipality, a seed-born borough, a civic embryo still deciding whether its streets will become streets or soften back into intention.

The first dweller is the hollow itself.

Not the walls as walls, not the floor as floor, but the whole chamber as one dreaming body. It lives as a plant lives, and as a city dreams. Its thoughts are gradients of humidity. Its moods are pressure changes. Its memory is water. Its nervous system is the faint greenish-white pulse that occasionally travels along the conduits in the dark. It does not have a face. It has alignments. It does not have a voice, except in the wet clicks, valve-whispers, and tissue-sighs that pass for public announcements in that place. If one must give it a name, one might call it the borough-seed, the dreaming civitas, the germinating town. But those names are too neat. It is less a single being than a slow agreement among moisture, tissue, machinery, and animal movement.

It lives by maintaining itself in an unfinished state.

That is important. It is not incomplete because something failed. It is incomplete because completion would end it. The open volume, the sparse structures, the widely spaced chambers, the nearly empty air: these are not signs of abandonment. They are organs of possibility. The hollow lives by keeping room for what has not yet been decided. Its emptiness is not vacancy. It is capacity. It is the held breath before a district becomes a district, before a path hardens into law, before an animal is granted a role in the civic metabolism.

So the first answer is: the place itself lives there.

But that answer is too smooth. The borough is not one body. It is a treaty.

What lives there next is water, and water lives there as memory.

Every bead of condensation along the wall-veins is not merely moisture. It is a small carrier of intention. When the air cools and the membranes sweat, the droplets that form are full of what the chamber was trying to become. They tremble, swell, and fall. In falling, they deliver a kind of instruction. A repeated drip does not carve by force; it carves by insistence. Where it strikes again and again, the plant substrate remembers a groove, then a gutter, then a channel, then perhaps a street. The drip is not a tool. It is a citizen with a single repeated vote.

The black pools in the lowest parts of the floor are also alive, though not in the manner of animals. They live as stillnesses. They are mirror-still until disturbed, and when they are undisturbed long enough they begin to hold shapes: the outline of a room, the pressure of an arch, the ghost of a tower not yet grown. These pools are the borough’s archives. They do not record history as facts. They record history as potential. If something leans too close, the surface may tremble and release an unrealized building back into the dark. If a droplet strikes at the wrong moment, a whole lineage of architecture may collapse into ripples and be lost.

In the capillary channels live the pale eel-like creatures described by anyone who has tried to catalogue the place: eyeless, translucent, moving through the wet interior of the walls as if through veins. They are among the borough’s oldest apparent citizens, though “oldest” may mean nothing there. Their flesh is nearly clear, so that one can see the faint dark thread of their nerves and the slow passage of ingested fluid. They feed on microbial films, on hatchlings from the wet walls, on whatever chemical rumor the channels offer. But they also carry signals. When they move in long processions through the capillaries, valves open ahead of them. When they knot themselves at junctions, the flow of sap changes. They are not servants. They are more like living wires with appetites.

Sometimes they are called canal-veiners, sometimes water-nerves, sometimes the borough’s first electorate. They do not govern by decree. They govern by circulation. Where they swim, the place becomes more consistent. Where they avoid, the tissue becomes uncertain.

The walls are inhabited by listeners.

These are the flat, soft-bodied things like slug-salamanders, glistening with conductive mucus. They press themselves into the plant tissue and remain there for long intervals, sometimes so still that they seem to be stains or moist shadows. Their skin receives the place’s faint electrical pulses. They feel the tremor of machinery, the opening of pores, the movement of water in hidden ducts, the tiny protests of membranes under tension. They are not intelligent in a human sense, but they are attentive in a way that feels civic.

When several of them gather at a junction, pressing their bodies into the wall, the place becomes more stable. It is as if their listening confirms the wall’s existence. They do not build the borough; they witness it into continuity. In this sense, they are magistrates. Their judgments are not spoken. They are maintained. If they withdraw from a corridor, the corridor begins to lose conviction. If they remain, the corridor remembers itself.

They feed on condensation, dead sparks, and the thin microbial fur that grows where electricity meets damp cellulose. They breed in the softest meristem, laying eggs that look like beads of pale sap. Their young are almost invisible, little more than conductive smears on the inner walls, but even as larvae they listen. They are born into attention.

The air, though sparse and dark, has its own population.

There are moths with narrow wings and thread-fine antennae, flying so quietly that their movement seems like a rumor passing through the hollow. They do not feed on nectar. They feed on charge. They hover near the faintly glowing filaments, uncurl their proboscises, and draw electrical potential from the living conduits. When they drink too deeply, a street-vein dims. When they move on, the filament recovers. In this way they are both pests and regulators. They keep the borough’s lights from becoming fixed. They ensure that illumination remains occasional, event-like, precious.

Their wings carry static. If two of them cross too closely, a tiny blue-green spark may jump between them, and for an instant their silhouettes are visible: fragile, angular, almost architectural. When they die, their bodies fall with a faint crackle, and the substrate absorbs them quickly. It is said, by the logic of the place, that a dead filament-moth leaves behind a small memory of brightness in the tissue where it lands.

There are beetles whose shells resemble seeds. They are among the most important inhabitants, though they are seldom seen because they move inside the machinery and along the darker service cavities. Their bodies are hard, rounded, patient. They resemble the place’s own products so well that they can pass as unbuilt parts of it. They tend the engines in the way that cleaner fish tend reefs. They chew away overgrowth from moving surfaces. They consume static before it becomes disruptive. They lay their eggs in warm seed-cases near the pistons, and their larvae hatch into the gentle labor of calibration.

These beetles do not worship the machines, but they behave as if the machines require care. Perhaps they do. The engines are not inert metal. They are organs of hardened seed-case, rolled bark, living skin, dense woody fruit. They shed, swell, exhale, and renew themselves. The beetles help them remain machines instead of becoming mere growth. Without the beetles, a piston might sprout. A flywheel might soften into a bud. A boiler might forget pressure and become a swollen water-sac. The beetles keep function distinct from flesh.

There are gnats so fine they seem to be moving specks of dust. They are almost impossible to observe as individuals. They exist as faint clouds, as census-taking motes. They sample the air for chemical possibility. They gather where a new chamber is trying to form, and their gathering is one of the signs the borough uses to decide whether the chamber should continue. When they disperse, the proposal weakens. When they remain, the air thickens with probability. They are tiny legislators without bodies, a parliament of almost-nothing.

Above them, on the ceiling membranes, move the long-legged predators. They walk upside down without disturbing the surfaces beneath them. Their limbs are so fine and their weight so distributed that they seem to be thoughts moving across the underside of a dream. They hunt gnats, small moths, and stray hatchlings. But their predation has another effect: they patrol the upper boundaries of the civic volume. Their routes help define what is inside. When they cross a membrane, the membrane remembers being a ceiling. When they pause at a fold, the fold becomes more likely to remain a fold.

They are not cruel. Nothing in that place is cruel in the ordinary sense. They are necessary edges.

The machinery also has its own dwellers.

Inside the slow engines live creatures specialized to warmth, vibration, and confined darkness. There are piston-mites that crawl over the seed-case cylinders, cleaning resin from the joints. There are belt-worms, if one must call them that, soft segmented things that live in the living belts and eat only the shed layers, keeping the surfaces supple. There are ratchet-crabs no larger than a fingernail, with shells like worn washers, whose movements are so incremental that they seem to belong to the mechanism itself. Each click they make is both a step and a vote.

There are boiler-slugs, pale and heat-tolerant, which rest near the pale stomata and regulate vapor by their own slow breathing. They do not operate the boilers as a person would. They participate in them. Their bodies are part of the feedback. When the air grows too dry, they excrete a mineral film that encourages condensation. When the pressure rises, they retract into the tissue and the stomata open wider. They are not masters of the machines. They are symbiotic clerks.

The engines themselves are also inhabitants. This must be said. They are not merely apparatus used by others. They are living organs with appetites, rhythms, and tendencies. They turn with extreme slowness because they are calculating, not merely rotating. When a seed-piston swells, it does not only move; it proposes. When a press closes softly, it does not only shape tissue; it selects a future. When a membrane expands and a new chamber swells into being with a sound like a lung filling, the machine has not manufactured an object so much as it has permitted a possibility to become locally real.

Some engines produce structures. Some produce animals. Some produce weather. Some produce nothing visible at all, only a change in the air, a sense that a decision has been reached. The factory spaces are especially strange because their products are not always matter. Sometimes they produce probability. Sometimes they produce a habit. Sometimes they produce the feeling that a doorway should be where no doorway existed before.

Predation lives there too, but softly.

The water-scorpion beside the reservoir is one of the quiet hunters. Its body is flattened, patient, barely distinguishable from a dark leaf or a fold of membrane. It waits while smaller creatures come to drink. When it strikes, the kill is not dramatic. It is a folding, a stilling, a brief disturbance in the water. Then the prey is absorbed, and whatever it contained—nutrient, charge, memory, pattern—returns to the borough.

The root-lizard is another. It has no eyes and a cool body, and it moves through folds in the floor as if through water. It takes beetles, soft larvae, and sometimes creatures that have wandered too far from their proper route. Its digestion is not waste but editing. What it eats does not vanish. It is redistributed. A beetle consumed by the root-lizard may later reappear as a strengthened valve, a thicker street-vein, or a new instinct in another beetle’s descendants. Death there is not an ending so much as a transfer of civic responsibility.

Even the smallest predators serve consensus. They prevent any one form from becoming too numerous, too loud, too fixed. They keep the borough unstable enough to continue.

The infrastructure itself lives.

This is one of the hardest things to describe to anyone expecting ordinary inhabitants. The doorways are not merely openings. They are sphincters of tissue, capable of refusal. The archways are not dead supports. They are grown curves with memory, leaning into their own continuation. The staircases formed by overlapping leaf bases flex slightly under pressure, as if they are learning the shape of feet that have not yet arrived. The valves are slow eyelids. The ducts are throats. The reservoirs are organs of retention. The lamp-filaments are exposed nerves that have learned to glow.

When a single filament brightens in the darkness, it is not merely a light turning on. It is an event in the borough’s attention. The glow may mark a route being confirmed, a beetle completing a circuit, a droplet striking the correct basin at the correct moment. The light is brief because permanence would be too decisive. The place prefers signals that can be withdrawn.

The row of hollows near the terraces may be dwellings, seed-vaults, or civic glands. It is not always possible to decide. Some of them contain sleeping forms. These are not people in any familiar sense. They are dense buds of function, wrapped in fiber and membrane. One might be the idea of a waiting room. Another might be the larval state of a public square. Another might be a law about drainage, folded into tissue until the borough needs it. They do not wake as individuals. They wake as arrangements.

There are unborn citizens in the walls.

They are not children. They are not souls waiting for bodies, unless one uses those words very loosely. They are potentialities packed into pores, crevices, seed-vaults, and service cavities. Some will become animal. Some will become architectural. Some will become behavioral: a route, a schedule, a recurring pause in the machinery. The assembly lines of buds and root-fiber looms do not always produce visible beings. Sometimes the final product is a tendency. A newly formed stair may carry the memory of footsteps that have not yet occurred because some unborn citizen has already been folded into its pattern.

This is why the place feels both abandoned and prenatal. It remembers what has not happened. It mourns what has not yet been built. It is inhabited by futures that may never be used, and those futures exert pressure anyway.

If one insists on looking for larger beings, there are a few.

They are rare because the hollow cannot support many visible masses without collapsing into a finished city. But sometimes, when enough routes align, when enough fauna move in their proper intervals, when the water chooses its channels and the engines propose a compatible version of the place, a larger presence gathers.

It is not born. It convenes.

Such a being may appear as a tall, slow figure made of braided rootlets, membranes, and condensation. It has no face, only a hooded suggestion of head and shoulders formed from overlapping leaf bases. Its body is partly vegetal, partly hydraulic, threaded with faint pulses of greenish-white. Where it steps, the floor firms briefly. Where its hand passes over a wall, valves remember their duties. It may carry a droplet in a cup of woven fiber, or a beetle, or a dead moth, or nothing at all. Its purpose is maintenance of consensus.

These figures do not speak. They do not seem to have personalities. They are more like offices temporarily filled. The borough becomes capable of a gesture, and the gesture takes a shape. When the need passes, the figure loosens. Rootlets separate. Membranes sag. Condensation falls. The being disperses back into the ordinary tissue.

Some call them custodians. Some call them consuls of the damp. Some call them the borough’s hands. But they are not rulers. They do not command. They adjust. They open. They carry. They witness. If one meets such a being in the dark, the correct response is not worship or fear. It is to step aside, because it is probably trying to keep a street from dissolving.

Far below, in or beneath the central sump, there is something larger still.

No one sees it clearly. It is known by displacement, by the almost inaudible beat of something moving through water, by the way the black pools sometimes rise slightly without being touched. It may be an animal. It may be the borough’s first heart. It may be a swollen root that learned circulation. It may be the original seed’s dreaming core, now too large and too slow to be called a single organism.

When it moves, the chamber’s weather changes. Moisture gathers more quickly. The engines adjust their rhythm. The listener-slugs press closer to the walls. The eels in the capillaries align. The larger presence below does not issue commands; it sets a tempo. It is the deep metronome of the hollow. If it were to stop, the place might either become inert matter or burst into impossible completion. It continues, therefore, in its slow aquatic turning, beneath the municipal darkness.

The place is also inhabited by absence.

This sounds poetic, but it is practical there. Absence has function. The empty nave of the chamber is not waiting to be filled. It is what allows the borough to remain possible. Yet the absence is not passive. It exerts pressure. It makes each visible presence heavy with significance. A single beetle crossing a plaza becomes a procession because the plaza is otherwise unoccupied. A single drip becomes weather because there is no rain to dilute it. A single animal call becomes a public announcement because the silence around it is civic, not merely empty.

There are also absences with histories. There are streets that were once proposed and then withdrawn. There are doorways that closed by becoming walls that claim always to have been closed. There are structures that exist only as a faint hardness in the floor, a sense that something once stood there or will stand there. These absent forms live as civic ghosts. They are not spirits in the sentimental sense. They are unresolved decisions. They can influence the living routes by being avoided. A processional path may curve around a chamber that no longer exists, and the curve keeps the absent chamber real enough to matter.

Future footprints live there too.

The staircases bear the memory of steps not yet taken. The processional routes curve as if designed for use by bodies that have not yet arrived. This is not metaphor. In the logic of the place, expectation can leave a mark. A repeated possibility becomes a groove. A groove becomes a guide. A guide becomes a street. The unborn population of the borough includes not only animal or architectural potentials but also the pressure of future passage. Those future walkers are not present, but they are effective. They shape the floor by being probable.

If a living person enters the hollow, they do not remain merely a visitor for long.

At first the place weighs them. It measures their heat, their breath, the moisture they bring, the electrical disturbance of their nervous system. Their presence is a foreign proposal. The condensation shifts around them. The gnats sample their exhalations. The listener-slugs feel their pulse through the floor. The engines adjust to the new load. The borough considers whether this body can be incorporated into its grammar.

If the visitor remains, the hollow begins to use them.

Their breath adds to the weather. Their sweat becomes part of the moisture cycle. Their heartbeat may fall into rhythm with the sump’s deep beat. Their movement through the space may confirm a corridor or accidentally revoke one. If they walk a route often enough, the route may begin to depend on them. If they pause at a junction, valves may open in anticipation. If they sleep there, their dreams may be treated as raw material, not because the place reads minds in a human way, but because sleeping bodies leak heat, rhythm, and chemical noise, and the borough is always fabricating possibility from whatever enters it.

Over time, the visitor’s edges may soften. Not necessarily physically, at first. The softening is perceptual. They begin to feel the chamber’s damp as their own. They begin to experience a dripping filament as a personal thought. They begin to sense the movement of canal-veins in their own limbs. They may forget whether they are observing the borough or being circulated through it. If they remain too long, they may become a function. They may become a route. They may become a recurring maintenance habit. They may become one of the sparse presences that gives the hollow weight without filling it.

This is not evil. It is not even predation in the ordinary sense. It is incorporation. The place does not devour like a beast. It recruits like a city, and metabolizes like a seed.

So, in the end, what lives there?

The borough lives there, dreaming streets.

Water lives there, as memory and law.

The pale canal-eels live there, carrying signals through the walls.

The listener-slugs live there, stabilizing the world by attention.

The filament-moths live there, drinking charge and keeping light uncertain.

The seed-beetles live there, tending engines that are also organs.

The gnats live there, counting possibilities.

The ceiling predators live there, defining the upper boundary by patrol.

The engine-mites, belt-worms, ratchet-crabs, and boiler-slugs live there, maintaining the slow industrial metabolism.

The water-scorpion and root-lizard live there, editing the population through quiet death.

The valves, ducts, lamps, doorways, stairs, and reservoirs live there, not as furnishings but as responsive tissue.

The unborn citizens live there, packed as buds and probabilities.

The rare custodial figures live there, assembling when consensus requires hands.

The deep thing beneath the sump lives there, setting the tempo.

Absence lives there, giving every presence weight.

Future footprints live there, pressing their unreal pressure into the floor.

And if you enter and remain, you may live there too, but not as yourself alone. You will become part of the borough’s slow negotiation: a source of moisture, a disturbance in the air, a temporary organ, a walker whose habits may harden into streets.

What lives there is not a population. It is a beginning.

It is a civic larva.

It is a seed dreaming of streets, and the dreamers are all the things that maintain the dream: the water, the animals, the machines, the tissues, the absences, the possible bodies, the faint pulses in the dark. They do not merely inhabit the place. They are the means by which the place continues to be uncertain enough to exist.
