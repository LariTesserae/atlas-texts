When a current shifts, the filter-lily answers. Its pale fan-leaves tilt by fractions, the pleats tightening or opening according to the pressure, and the microscopic cilia along their edges begin to beat in a fine, silvered rhythm. The water around it becomes visibly occupied: motes gather, scatter, are combed into lines, and vanish into the leaf-folds. That is the first clue. Nothing in the drowned chamber is merely scenery. Every stillness is a posture; every movement has a resident.

The smallest lives are almost indistinguishable from the light.

The suspended motes drifting through the green-gold water are not only dust, spore, or softened polymer. Some are eggs. Some are larval stages so tiny they seem like living grain in the illumination. Some are drifting colonies of seal-motes, organisms no larger than a fleck of paint, which exude thin films when they encounter a seam or hairline crack. They were made, or made themselves, to close things. They move in the currents with no apparent intention until they find a place where the chamber’s skin is tired, and then they settle, multiply, and become a living patch. Their work is invisible except in the long result: no leak persists, no edge frays beyond use. They are less like builders than like antibodies of the room.

On the thick plates of clouded glass beneath the dome, there are glass mites. They are transparent, flat-bodied, and slow, almost like moving lenses. They graze mineral film and old algae, leaving clean crescents where the light can pass more easily. They do not clear the glass to brightness; the chamber is too old for brightness. They only keep it from becoming blind. When they cluster near the highest curve of the dome, their bodies catch the yellow-green daylight and glitter faintly, as if the ceiling has shed a constellation into the water.

In the seams between enamel plates and along the riveted joints of the walls, threadworms live. They are slender as filament, coppery or pale depending on what they have eaten, and they inhabit the narrow spaces where metal meets metal. Their bodies can flatten to almost nothing. They clean corrosion, soften old sealant, and spin fine strands of polymer when disturbed. Those bright threads seen drifting in the water are sometimes shed by vegetation, but sometimes they are threadworm silk loosened from the joints. The chamber wears them like loose hair. They are part of its softness.

Where the water is warmer, life becomes more visible and more deliberate.

Along the pipe cluster where the tube-kelp clings, there are porcelain shrimp. They are small, clean-limbed, and curious, their carapaces green-black with a metallic sheen, as if they had been fired in the chamber’s own mineral heat. Their claws are not weapons but tools, worn smooth by generations of careful use. They rasp velvet algae from plates, pick spores from kelp nodules, and tap on hollow surfaces to communicate.
