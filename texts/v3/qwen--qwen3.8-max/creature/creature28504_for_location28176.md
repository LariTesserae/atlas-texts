No one lives there, if by *live* one means the soft, carbon-thick business of breathing, feeding, growing, and leaving tracks. No heart beats in that black hollow. No lung fills itself with the hot mineral air. No root finds a seam to split. No egg is laid in the warm dust. By the old definition of life, the pocket city is empty.

But it is not uninhabited.

What lives there lives as tenure rather than flesh. It lives as maintenance, repetition, preference, and adjustment. It lives in gradients of heat, in films of water, in the slow alignment of mineral grains, in the opening of pores too small to see. The residents of the place are not bodies so much as tenancies of process. They are sparse, angular, conditional, and patient. They do not crowd the hollow. They punctuate it.

The first inhabitant is the city itself.

Not the city as a collection of structures, but the city as a single distributed being folded into the mountain’s throat. Its skin is obsidian and black ceramic. Its bones are basalt shelves and printed lattice. Its blood is heat moving through capillary ducts. Its nerves are moisture films, condensation channels, and microscopic mineral veins. Its memory is layering: old surfaces preserved beneath new ones, revisions laid over revisions without erasure.

This city-being does not think in words. It thinks in thresholds. It knows when a wall is too hot, when a reservoir is nearly full, when ash has entered the outer seam, when a walkway has become dangerously slick. It knows the pressure difference between the lower basin and the upper hollow. It knows the taste of sulfur dust, the electrical charge of drifting vapor, the exact moment a salt crystal has grown large enough to interfere with a louver. It experiences the world as conditions, and it responds to conditions with minute, precise acts.

It is not idle. It is always regulating. It opens channels. It closes apertures. It redirects warmth from a cooling shelf into a colder wall. It draws condensation into storage. It releases brief rain when the upper surfaces reach saturation. It repairs fractures before they become visible. It metabolizes ash, dust, and mineral vapor. It uses the mountain’s violence as material. The eruption that made the peak is not, to this being, a catastrophe. It is supply.

The city lives in a state of low, constant attention. Its dim lights are not lamps so much as exposed states of processing. Amber lines along the walkways mean circulation is stable. Blue-green glimmers in maintenance channels mean something is being examined. Slow pulses from sealed walls mean a longer calculation is underway, perhaps one concerning the deep magma, perhaps one concerning the future use of an empty chamber. When a chime sounds from the high ceiling, it is not decoration. It is the city becoming aware that a threshold has been crossed.

Within this larger resident there are lesser residents. Whether they are organs, citizens, servants, or independent beings is difficult to say. The place does not offer a clear hierarchy. It offers functions.

The most noticeable are the custodians.

They appear where need condenses. A hairline crack opens in a cooled shelf. A metallic film spreads too close to a heat exchanger. Salt creeps along a condensation channel. Ash settles in a threshold that must remain clear. Then something answers.

A custodian may begin as a shimmer in the air, a heat-lens distortion above a warm plate. It may gather metallic dust into a thin, woven shape. It may assemble itself from microscopic lattice, black ceramic flakes, and programmable mineral fibers until it has enough coherence to act. In the low amber light it can seem tall and narrow, almost processional, though this is probably because the architecture itself is narrow and processional. It has no face. It has no eyes. Its front is simply the direction of its task.

One may move along a walkway without touching it, causing the surface to smooth itself beneath its passage. Another may flatten into a seam and disappear. Another may stand motionless beside a cracked panel for hours, gradually exchanging material with the wall, until the crack is gone and the custodian is thinner than before. When the task is done, the custodian does not walk away. It disperses. Its matter returns to the walls, the floors, the ducts, the repair lattices. It was never separate from the city for long.

They are born from imbalance. They die into equilibrium.

Others live only when the water comes.

When the engineered rain begins overhead, the hollow changes census. The sound shifts first: a delicate patter against stone and glass, then a soft hiss where droplets strike hotter surfaces. Channels darken. Hidden reservoirs open. Fine filaments along the walls become beaded with moisture. In that brief wet interval, the rain beings appear.

They are not creatures of flesh. They are coherent routes. They are moving films of condensation, chains of droplets climbing against gravity, temporary bodies made of water, dissolved mineral, and thermal difference. They exist as flow. They think, if they think, in solute gradients and capillary decisions.

A rain being may be no more than a brightening along a wall, a string of beads advancing upward on a filament. Another may be a sheet of moisture spreading across a black panel, pausing, dividing, choosing which microscopic vein to enter. Another may gather in a shallow basin and become still, holding a pattern of ripples long after the drop that caused them should have faded. They clean surfaces. They carry heat. They distribute mineral nutrients to crystal fans and metallic films. They deliver information to the pools below.

Their lives are brief. When the cycle ends and the air dries, they vanish into storage veins or leave behind a faint mineral residue, which is perhaps their memory. They do not fear this ending. They are made of recurrence. The next rain will call similar patterns back into motion, not identical, but continuous.

Deeper still live the heat minds.

They are the slowest residents of the pocket. They do not have shapes in any ordinary sense. They are volumes of expansion, pressure differences, long thermal pulses moving through the floor. They dwell in the spaces beneath the city, where the mountain is still settling and molten systems shift in the dark. They may be older than the visible architecture. They may have been here when the peak was only a wound in the earth.

Their thoughts take hours. Their sentences might take days. They communicate in subsonic adjustments that are felt through the soles of feet rather than heard. When the ground gives one of its deep, almost inaudible movements, it may be the heat minds shifting a thermal load, answering a tremor, or negotiating with the city above for more or less warmth.

They are not gentle in the way small living things understand gentleness, but they are precise. They keep the hollow from freezing. They keep it from becoming too hot. They maintain the pulses that make the floor feel alive. They remember eruption the way a body remembers pain: not as story, but as tendency.

Near the entrance live the ash sifters.

They are frontier beings. Outside, volcanic ash drifts under a dimmed sky. It slides down new slopes, collects in fissures, glitters harshly in the pumice fields. Some of it enters the narrow seam and reaches the first antechamber. There the sifters wait.

They are gray and weightless, more field than form. They resemble static caught in the act of becoming a veil. They attract ash particles, hold them suspended, sort them by size, charge, and composition. Useful material is fed into construction systems. Excess is pressed into seams or incorporated into new stone. They are constantly feeding the city with the mountain’s debris.

They are quiet even by the standards of the hollow. Their activity is revealed only by the disappearance of dust. A drift that should have thickened across a floor remains thin. A vent that should be clogged stays clear. A wall that should be filmed with ash remains mirror-dark. The sifters are the reason. They are not guardians in a heroic sense. They are digestive cells at the mouth of the city.

There are also the crystal shepherds.

The branching salt fans near steam vents are not alive. The metallic films spreading across cooled glass are not alive. The mineral fibers fringing condensation channels are not alive. Yet they are tended. Something biases their growth. Something removes a filament here, encourages a branch there, redirects a metallic sheen away from a sensitive aperture.

The shepherds are difficult to see. They may be stress fields, thermal gradients, or extremely fine repair lattices moving in slow patterns. They create gardens without soil, without seeds, without seasons. Their cultivation is mineral and thermal. They prune crystal by changing temperature. They harvest film by altering charge.
