The city, vast and mostly empty, is not dead. It is only inhabited in the way a machine is inhabited: by films, by slow creatures, by scattered caretakers, by habits that have become bodies, by lives that have learned to exist as surfaces.

What lives there first lives as skin.

The dark felts and crusts on the wet stone are not decoration. They are the city’s broadest living layer, if “living” can be used for something so thin and so patient. They spread in the pipe shadows, under stair lips, along the warm sides of drainage channels, and wherever condensation is regular but not violent. They are not green. They are black, brown, rust-orange, ash-gray, and sometimes a dull copper color that has nothing to do with leaves. They are fungal in part, but also something older and less nameable: filamentous, chemically fed, tolerant of heat and mineral poisons, able to grow on moisture that would corrode ordinary flesh. They cling to basalt, brick, ceramic seams, and iron ribs. They eat rust, salt, sulfur compounds, and the faint organic residues left by older ages of the city. They do not bloom. They do not flower. They thicken by fractions, by seasons, by decades. In places they form a nap like scorched velvet. In others they are only a darkening, a streak, a greasy stain that glistens when the lamps catch it.

These growths are important because they make the mineral world briefly edible. They convert the city’s heat and wetness into something that can be scraped, sifted, consumed, or used as a substrate. They are not crops in any familiar sense. No one plants them in rows. But they are tended, after a fashion. Where a channel carries particularly rich mineral water, where a vent gives off just enough warmth, where a ceramic panel remains slightly cooler than the air around it, the felts may be encouraged. A scrape here, a redirected drip there, a cleared seam to allow vapor to settle: these are the gestures of cultivation in a place without soil or sunlight. The city’s life begins not with planting but with guiding condensation.

Beneath this living skin, or within it, there are the small animals.

They are rare, and they do not announce themselves. In the warm shallow pools where mineral water settles long enough to become stable, one may find the crawlers: pale, eyeless things with thin shells or shell-like plates, their bodies almost translucent where the light passes through them. They move with a slow, deliberate patience, as if each motion must be negotiated with the heat. They graze on microbial films, scrape soft deposits from stone, or sift fine mineral particles through mouthparts made more for filtering than biting. They are not abundant. A pool that holds three or four of them is rich. A pool that holds one may be considered occupied for a long distance around.

Around the condenser fins and lamp brackets, where warmth gathers and moisture condenses into visible threads, there are occasional winged things. They are not beautiful in the way of butterflies or moths. Their wings are narrow, stiff, sometimes veined like cracked glass, sometimes folded close as if damp even in flight. They drift more than they dart. They do not swarm. One may hang near a fogged lamp for minutes, then vanish into the vapor as though it had been absorbed. They are drawn to warmth, to humidity, to the mineral taste of the air, perhaps to the films that grow on wet metal. If they reproduce, they do so in hidden seams, in warm wet cracks behind ceramic panels, in sediment at the bottom of cisterns where no one looks often.

In the darker galleries and drainage trunks, where the air is thicker and the sound of water is closer, there are worms and larvae: pale, soft-bodied, slow, often blind, living in wet mineral sludge. They break down what little organic matter exists, consume fungal threads, and in turn become prey when anything large enough can find them. They are not numerous enough to form a visible mass. They are encountered singly, by accident, when a grate is lifted or a channel is cleared. Their presence is known less by sight than by the faint disturbance they leave in wet sediment: a line, a curl, a smoothed trail.

Predators exist, but only as the system permits. Where prey is scarce, hunger must be patient. At the mouths of warm drains, beside condensation basins, or under the edges of weeping walls, one may find the waiting things: elongated, pale, nearly motionless. They have no need for speed. They have no surplus energy for pursuit. They rest with their bodies aligned to the flow of warm water or vapor, sensing tremors, heat differences, or chemical traces. When a crawler passes too close, when a winged thing settles too long, the predator moves in one short, economical action. Then it is still again. These creatures are among the most unsettling signs of life in the city because they are so clearly alive and yet so little like life as one might expect it. They are not fierce. They are not majestic. They are efficient, damp, and almost abstract.

But the question is not only what survives there. The question is who lives there.

There are inhabitants. Few. Scattered. Not a populace, not a society in the crowded sense, but a set of isolated presences that maintain the apparatus and have been shaped by it. They are not many. The city could not support many. It does not offer the ordinary wealth of green fields, herds, orchards, or fertile soil. It offers heat, water, mineral surfaces, and the slow yield of chemical gradients. Whatever intelligent beings remain there live at the edge of sufficiency, and they have become suited to that edge.

They may once have been human, or something close to human. If so, the city has edited them. They are tall in some cases, but not from vigor; rather, their bodies seem stretched by distance, by long walks between valves, by the need to cover empty spaces without urgency. Their limbs are often long and careful. Their movements are slow, deliberate, and heat-conserving despite the heat, as if they have learned that any wasted motion becomes sweat, fatigue, and thirst. Their skin is not leaf-bright or sun-darkened but mineral in tone: grayish, dusky, pale amber, ash-brown, sometimes with a faint sheen from humidity or a dusting of salt where evaporation has left its mark. It may be hairless, or nearly so, because hair would trap moisture and heat in the wrong ways. In some, the skin has a slick, almost ceramic quality, especially over shoulders, forearms, and cheeks, as though long exposure to mineral vapor has glazed it.

Their eyes are adapted to the dimness. Not large in a grotesque way, but wide-set, reflective, accustomed to diffusion. They do not search for sunlight; they search for changes in surface moisture, for the glint of a failing lamp, for the subtle difference between steam and vapor that carries heat danger. Their gaze is not sharp in the way of hunters under open skies. It is attentive to texture, sheen, drip lines, and small irregularities in vast repetitive structures. They may blink slowly. Their eyelids may be slightly heavy, protected by membranes or fine lashes that keep out mineral mist. In arc-light districts, they may squint or wear smoked glass discs set in copper or ceramic frames.

Their hands are their most human and most altered feature. The fingers are often long, but not delicate; the palms are textured, resistant to slick stone and warm metal. They can feel a vibration through a railing before it becomes audible. They can tell whether a pipe is running properly by touching its outer casing, not because of mysticism but because their whole attention has been trained toward such knowledge. Their hands bear calluses that are not like laborer’s calluses from rope or soil, but from ceramic edges, valve wheels, wet ladders, and the repeated handling of tools. Some wear finger guards of beaten metal or hardened lacquer. Some carry small scraping blades, oilers, sounding hammers, or wrenches suspended from their belts by cord or chain, so that no tool is dropped into a drain.

Their breathing is another sign of adaptation. The air is thick, hot, and sometimes sour with sulfur, ozone, or metallic vapor. They breathe slowly, with deep but unhurried rhythms. Their faces may carry masks, breath-plates, or porous filters made of ceramic fiber, charcoal, or treated felt, especially near vent stacks and transformer yards. In cleaner districts, they may bare their faces and let the humidity settle on their skin. Their clothing is designed not to keep cold out but to manage wetness and heat: loose where airflow is needed, close-fitting where loose cloth could catch on railings or machinery, treated to shed mineral water, often darkened by age and vapor. Fabrics resemble waxed canvas, woven fiber, or glazed textile more than soft cloth. They may wear shoulder coverings shaped to shed condensation away from the neck. Their boots have soles resistant to heat and slick mineral film, with deep channels for water to escape beneath each step.

They are not ornamental people, but they are not without adornment. What they wear is taken from the city itself: polished basalt beads, chips of glazed ceramic, rings of copper gone blue-green with patina, small valves worn as pendants, fragments of porcelain insulator, salt crystals preserved in sealed glass. Their ornaments are mineral, industrial, and earned. A piece of rare clear glass may be more valued than metal. A shard of old white ceramic, if smooth and unbroken, may be worn as a sign of careful hands. They do not wear flowers. They have no memory of flowers except perhaps as a strange word, an absence, a myth of softness that no longer applies.

They live in niches, not houses.

The city’s architecture is oversized for them, or perhaps built for processes they do not fully resemble. They do not fill the great halls. They occupy the margins within them. A dwelling may be a ceramic-lined alcove behind a pump-house, raised above the warmest floor level and ventilated by a slow draft from a condenser gallery. It may be a maintenance chamber inserted into the thickness of a wall, dry enough to sleep in, fitted with a narrow sleeping shelf, a water catcher, a lamp, and a rack of tools. It may be a suspended platform beneath a viaduct, reached by a ladder and enclosed by curtains of oiled cloth, where moving air keeps the worst humidity from settling. These places are small compared to the structures around them. They are not meant for families, gatherings, or storage of abundance. They are meant for rest, repair, and the conservation of moisture balance.

Their homes are valued for dryness, airflow, and the nearness of useful water. A good dwelling has a surface that sweats cleanly, a channel that does not overflow, a lamp that burns without fouling, and a route to a cistern or condenser fin without crossing an exposed plaza in the hottest part of the cycle. They do not decorate with softness. They arrange tools. They polish a curve of stone. They hang a string of ceramic beads where it will catch lamplight. They may mark the wall with notches, stains, or etched lines: records of repairs, water levels, dangerous heat spells, or the passage of other keepers. The marks are not writing in the ordinary sense, though they may be legible to those who know the city’s signs.

They eat thinly.

There is no agriculture of green things. There is no grain, no fruit, no leaf, no root in soil. What food exists comes from the city’s mineral ecology. The fungal felts are scraped, washed if necessary, pressed, dried near vents, or heated on ceramic plates until they become dense, dark cakes. These cakes are not fragrant. They smell of damp wool, chalk, iron, and heated stone. They are eaten slowly, in small portions, often softened in clean condensate. The pale crawlers, when taken, may be roasted or boiled in mineral water; their shells are brittle, their flesh sparse and faintly sweet in a way that has more to do with chemistry than fleshly richness. Larvae and worms are not despised, but they are not abundant enough to form a staple. Some districts cultivate particular films by adjusting the flow of warm, sulfurous water over prepared rock faces. Others harvest salt crusts, mineral grains, or crystalline deposits that can be dissolved into broths. Nothing is plentiful. Nothing is wasted.

Water is everywhere, but not all of it is safe or pleasant. Some water is too hot, too acidic, too metallic, too full of dissolved salts. The inhabitants know the city by its waters: which drip from cold ceramic is cleanest, which pool must be boiled or filtered, which channel carries industrial runoff, which cistern holds old water that has gone flat but harmless. They may carry small vessels of glazed ceramic or metal, always aware that water is both the city’s abundance and its danger. To be wet is normal; to be poisoned by the wrong wetness is a constant possibility. They teach by example: where to kneel, where to drink, where to let water run over the skin but not enter the mouth.

Their work is maintenance.

They do not govern the city so much as attend to it. They clear capillary channels when mineral crusts begin to block them. They scrape condenser fins when films grow too thick and reduce collection. They oil slow-moving fans, listen to pump valves, test rail joints, replace fogged lamps, tighten cables, and open or close gates according to pressures that shift with heat and rain. They know which stairways are safe, which catwalks have thinned under corrosion, which plazas become too hot after a long warm cycle, which galleries flood when the brief rains fall too fast. Their labor is not dramatic. It is repetitive, precise, and spaced across enormous distances. One person may be responsible for a terrace, a viaduct, a row of condenser towers, or a whole drainage sector, and may not encounter another person for a long time.

Because they are few, they cannot be everywhere. The city probably maintains itself in part through automatic systems: valves that open when cisterns reach certain levels, pumps that start on their own, fans that turn according to vapor pressure, rail-carts that move on old schedules, mechanisms so ancient that they seem half alive. The inhabitants may regard these mechanisms with a mixture of reliance and wariness. They do not worship them exactly, but they understand that a machine which has worked for centuries has its own temperament. A pump that coughs before starting must be listened to. A fan that slows in humid weather must not be forced. A valve that sticks at the same hour each cycle may be more reliable than one that moves too easily. The caretakers speak of these things, if they speak at all, in practical terms that shade into reverence.

Their social life is thin and long-distance.

They do not crowd. They do not gather in markets. They do not form dense neighborhoods. Their communities are networks of routes, signals, and obligations spread across the basin. One inhabitant may know another by the sound of a hammer on a pipe, by the pattern of a lamp blink, by a change in the rhythm of a nearby pump, or by a marker left at a junction. Communication is often indirect. A sequence of taps may mean: channel clear. Another may mean: heat rising, avoid lower gallery. Another: I am alive. Another: I require help, but not urgently. Another: do not enter; vapor bad. In some districts, they use the drainage system itself to send messages: a released float, a measured drip, a small change in water level. In others, they adjust lamps or steam vents so that signals can be seen or heard across mist-filled voids.

When they meet, it is rarely casual. They may meet at a pump-house during a repair that requires more than one pair of hands, at a reservoir when a valve must be turned by two people, at a rail junction when something must be moved, or at a place where two maintenance routes cross and the timing happens to align. Such meetings are quiet. They may begin by checking the other’s tools, skin, or breathing apparatus before exchanging words. They do not embrace often; touch in a hot, wet, mineral world can mean transferring heat, grit, or contamination.
