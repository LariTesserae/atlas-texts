To stand inside it, if one could, would be to feel that the chamber is not a container for life but a life composed of containment. What lives there is not merely a population of odd creatures rooted in odd stone. It is a chord that has acquired bodies. The Dry Choir is named rightly, if one names it at all: the place is inhabited by dry voices, by resonant organs, by small mineral persons and listening growths, all arranged around a single unending note.

The first thing one would recognize is not a body, but a pressure. The air, if it can be called air, feels tuned. It presses against the skin with a warmth that is not weather but intention. Every surface seems alert. Every crystal lip, every salt plain, every shard of black glass appears placed not by accident but by acoustic necessity. Nothing there is merely scenery. The room listens through its inhabitants, and its inhabitants live by listening back.

What lives there does not live by water. There is no sap, no blood, no moisture hidden in secret glands. The living are sealed. Their interiors move with oils, waxes, mineral solvents, warm gases, and slow threads of resin. Their surfaces are glossy, fibrous, ceramic, or furred with fine metallic filaments. They do not sweat. They do not weep. They do not soften. If one touched them, one would feel warmth and dryness, as if touching a sun-heated instrument that had just stopped being played.

The vegetation — if that word still belongs there — is the choir’s sustaining note. It does not grow upward toward light or spread outward in hunger.
