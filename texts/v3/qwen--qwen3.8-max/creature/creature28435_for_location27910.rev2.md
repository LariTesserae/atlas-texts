What lives there cannot be cleanly separated from the place that holds it. The underplain does not have residents in the way a city has citizens or a forest has trees. It has participants. Some are obvious, some are nearly invisible, and some are so large and slow that they seem like geography. The first answer to the question of who lives there is: the landscape itself.

The largest inhabitant is the plant body of the underplain: the root mat, the xylem walls, the bound ceiling stalks, the membrane fields, the buried chemical veins, the compressed older strata. This is not vegetation standing on land. It is land made of vegetation. It lives in sheets, layers, cables, membranes, and slow reflexes. It does not think as a brain thinks, but it decides. It decides where a wall thickens, where a cut should seal, where a machine may be tolerated, where a path should be lifted, where a hollow should be closed. Its decisions are expressed in tension, resin chemistry, fiber direction, and electrical gradient. A bolt placed badly will be rejected over months as growth shifts around it and loosens the bracket. A passage cut with understanding may be adopted, its edges thickened and polished until it becomes a permanent vein.

This body is not all equally awake. Some districts are metabolically quick: new growth, repair fronts, spore ridges, nursery plains. Others are dormant, old compressed layers that may remain unchanged for years until a pressure change or chemical signal calls them back into activity. The inhabitants know this. They do not treat a quiet wall as dead. They treat it as slow.

Smaller than this vast body, but still foundational, are the anhydrous micro-lives: ester-films, salt-crawlers, resin molds, dry fermenters, microscopic processors that live in surface skins and fiber interstices. They are not visible as individuals. They appear as sheen, powder, crust, faint discoloration, or a change in the way a surface accepts charge. They do the first work of the world. They condition raw fiber. They help resin cure. They break down dead matter into usable grains. They fix mineral salts into stable forms. They make the plant body’s chemistry possible. Without them, the underplain would be only dry structure; with them, it is a functioning metabolism.

Then come the visible fauna, and almost all of them have learned the same law: be useful, be quiet, waste nothing.

The husk-beetles are among the most common. They move through dead fiber and fallen plant debris in swarms that look like shifting grit. Their shells are dry, ridged, and pale, often the color of old stem interiors. They consume material that has already ceased to serve the living body and reduce it to finer particles. Their passing leaves behind granular deposits that air currents can sort into ripples. In places where harvesters have cut new faces into old strata, beetles arrive quickly, not as pests but as finishers, working the rough edges into future ground. Their sound is tiny: a soft ticking, like very distant fingernails on paper.

The flat-bodied grazers are larger and slower. They move over living surfaces in coordinated waves, rasping away excess growth without injuring the structural tissue beneath. They are broad, low, and patient, more like moving plates than animals. Their mouths are mineral-edged and self-sharpening. Their bodies are coated with a thin resinous film that reduces loss and keeps them from adhering to the surfaces they clean. Where they pass, the plant tissue is left smooth, slightly polished, and better able to bear loads. The caretakers do not herd them in any crude sense; they guide them with chemical markers, electrostatic cues, and the placement of buffer strips. A grazing wave can take days to cross a terrace, but when it is finished, the terrace has become more itself.

Inside hollow stems and collapsed tubes live the jointed dwellers: long, segmented, pale-bodied creatures that fold themselves into narrow channels and emerge when spore density rises. They feed on airborne reproductive material, but they also distribute it, carrying grains from one growth ridge to another on their bristled limbs. When a spore tide begins, they appear at the openings of hollow culms, testing the air with fine antennae. They are food for other creatures, and they are also indicators. If they remain hidden during a release, maintenance crews know that the air chemistry is wrong. If they emerge too early, the growth-control districts take note.

The aerial forms are quieter still. They do not fly so much as negotiate with the air. Some have broad membranes and ride thermal drafts from engine halls and growth masses. Others use charged filaments that interact with the underplain’s electrostatic field, allowing them to rise, hover, and drift without much effort. They feed on spores, dust-bound nutrients, and thin resin films. Their motion is almost ceremonial. They turn in the pale air above the plains, barely disturbing anything, and when a spore release crosses an open basin they gather in loose bands, their bodies becoming part of the weather. They roost on ceiling ribs and reflector galleries, folding themselves into shapes that resemble detached leaf-plates. Only a careful eye can tell the difference.

Predation exists, but it has adopted the same restraint. A seam-stalker may lie flattened beside a ventilation outlet for days, its body the color and texture of old root fiber, waiting for smaller feeders to pass. When it moves, it does not lunge; it folds around its prey with a soft, resinous adhesion. Rib-gliders drop from high supports on silent membranes and intercept drifting spore-sailors in mid-air, then return to their perches without a sound. Some hunters are almost indistinguishable from growth nodes: rounded, still, seemingly inert until a small creature comes close enough to be drawn into an internal chamber. These acts are not violent in the way wet-world predation is violent. They are efficient, enclosed, and brief. No cry follows them. The underplain absorbs the event and continues.

There are also creatures that live near power. Static-feeders cluster around warm cables, transformer housings, and electrostatic gradients. They are small, often moth-like or louse-like, with fine conductive filaments extending from their bodies. They take energy from charge differentials and are usually harmless, but their presence tells the caretakers where insulation is wearing thin or where a field has become unbalanced. In some districts they are tolerated as living instruments. A line that attracts too many static-feeders is checked before it fails. A line with none at all may be too cold, too shielded, or disconnected from the living circulation around it.

Deeper in the older layers live the slow borers: large, mineral-plated, root-colored animals that tunnel through compressed plant strata. They are rare, and they are respected. Their movement is measured in seasons rather than hours. They eat dead or dormant material, create passages, redistribute salts, and leave behind stable channels that later become cable routes, ventilation seams, or service corridors. The caretakers do not chase them. They plan around them. A borer path discovered during construction is often incorporated into the design rather than blocked. In this way, even the fauna participate in urban planning.

High above the ground, on the ceiling lattice and reflector vanes, live the cleaners: thin, translucent organisms that graze dust and old films from the light surfaces. Some resemble limpets made of pale glass; others are soft-bodied and move with a slow crawling motion that barely disturbs the air. Their work is essential. If they fail, the ordinary light of the underplain dims. Maintenance crews monitor their populations and leave undisturbed zones near the ceiling so they can recover after heavy spore tides. The light that feels so normal is, in part, a product of their labor.

And then there are the people.

They are few. They are quiet. They are not the owners of this place but its careful negotiators.

Their bodies have become suited to the underplain’s rules. They are dry-bodied and slender, with light frames and broad, soft-soled feet that distribute their weight over fibrous ground. Their skin is not wet or glandular; it is layered, matte, and faintly fibrous, more like cured leaf or fine woven material than animal hide. It sheds fine dust rather than perspiration. A thin ester film protects their joints and reduces friction, but it is not moisture in the old sense. Their internal chemistry is sealed, conservative, and efficient. They do not pant, flush, or exude. Heat is managed slowly, through rest, shade, conductive contact with cool plant walls, and regulated activity. Their waste is dry and is returned to processing beds where it can be re-mineralized and reused.

Their faces are built for dust and silence. They have filtering fringes or internal baffles around their breathing passages, and many wear half-masks of woven fiber when working in harvest zones. Their eyes are adapted to the ordinary, diffused light of the underplain, but they are protected by clear inner membranes or close-fitting goggles of polished leaf-plate when the air is thick with spores. Their hands are their most important instruments. The fingers are long, precise, and sensitive, with pads that can read texture and tension and fine conductive hairs that notice electrostatic change. A caretaker can place a palm against a wall and learn a great deal: whether the tissue is stressed, whether a channel is carrying charge, whether a repair is taking hold, whether the growth beyond is ready to be cut.

They dress in layers of woven fiber, resin-treated cloth, and living seams that can repair small tears. Their garments are practical, quiet, and often slightly older than their wearers, because durability is valued over novelty. The colors are muted: pale straw, resin amber, salt-gray, dull copper, the blue-white of mineral crusts. They carry pouches of nutrient resin, ceramic blades, insulated clamps, grounding cords, marker threads, and small sensor plates. Nothing about them is designed to make noise. Even their fasteners are chosen for silence.

Their speech, when they use it, is low and rustling. Much of it is signed. Much is transmitted through touch: taps on living walls, coded pressures along cables, patterns pressed into conductive panels. They do not shout. Shouting is not only rude; it is wasteful, startling, and potentially dangerous in a world of fine dust and dry fiber. Their public spaces are designed to keep sound local. Their machines are muffled. Their trains hum rather than roar. When urgency appears, it expresses itself as precision, not speed. A worker moving quickly still moves smoothly, placing each footfall with care, because running would raise dust and disturb the substrate.

Their work is divided by skill rather than spectacle. There are listeners who monitor the plant body for stress. There are cutters trained to open new passages without triggering collapse or rejection. There are weavers who guide young fiber over frames until it becomes load-bearing. There are resin-smiths who refine exudates into sealants, insulation, and structural lacquers. There are spore-herders who manage releases, drift patterns, and collection screens. There are cable-tenders who maintain electric gradients and prevent dangerous static accumulation. There are dust-wardens who walk the districts after spore tides, checking filters, grounding straps, and vulnerable deposits. There are growth-shapers who spend years coaxing a single wall, roof, or terrace into the right form. And there are memory-keepers who record changes in the terrain, because the place is always becoming and a map that is not updated becomes a lie.

Before any major cut, they listen. A small team will stand at the intended site and place instruments against the fiber. They may wait hours or days. They are not asking permission in a sentimental sense; they are reading the living system’s readiness. If the tissue responds with unstable charge, if nearby growth is already stressed, if a dormant layer shows signs of slow rejection, the cut is delayed or moved. When cutting begins, the tools are ceramic, cold, and carefully grounded. The exposed face does not bleed liquid. It releases pale dust, resin granules, and sometimes a faint static crackle. The wound is immediately treated: sealed with resin powder, bound with fiber mesh, or fitted with a temporary liner until the surrounding growth can take over. The goal is not to conquer the wall but to invite it into a new function.

They eat without water. Their food is dry, concentrated, and carefully sealed until the moment of use: pressed pollen cakes, spore flour, mineral salts, cultivated starch nodes, resin-rich nutrient bars, and slow-release chemical packets. They do not cook with open heat. Their kitchens use enclosed electric plates, warm resin baths, and dry curing racks. Meals are quiet affairs. There is no steam, no boiling, no smell of wet cooking. Instead there is the faint odor of warm cellulose, toasted fiber, and mineral salt. They eat slowly, because their bodies are efficient and because haste creates crumbs, dust, and waste. Leftovers are not discarded; they are returned to processing beds or offered to beetle colonies in controlled amounts.

Their homes are grown as much as built. Residential tiers are carved or cultivated into root bulges and thick growth walls. The interior surfaces remain faintly alive, able to repair small damage, filter dust, and stabilize temperature. Furniture is guided rather than assembled: a shelf coaxed from a woody ridge, a seat thickened by repeated growth cycles, a sleeping alcove lined with soft compressed fiber. Where industrial reinforcement is needed, it is inserted with care: steel struts wrapped in insulating fiber, ceramic plates set into living tissue, cables run through hollow culms so that the plant body can accept them as organs. A house here breathes, but quietly. Its vents are membrane valves. Its lamps are sealed electric panels. Its warmth comes from stored heat, chemical packs, or contact with controlled engine loops. No open flame is allowed. Fire is not merely forbidden; it is treated as a kind of insanity.

Their cities are sparse because the living body requires space. They do not fill every interval. They leave blank plains, empty buffers, and unfinished zones. These gaps are not failures of planning; they are allowances for change. A plaza may be a membrane field reserved for future growth. A road may end where a bridge will exist only after the far side has thickened enough to support it. A residential block may have a blank facade because the wall beyond is still deciding what it wants to become. The inhabitants are comfortable with this. They do not demand that a place be finished before they use it. They use it lightly, adjust it gradually, and let completion remain provisional.

Their culture is shaped by the same restraint. They value quiet not as emptiness but as attention. Greetings are often tactile: a hand pressed to another’s forearm, a shared touch against a wall, a brief exchange of coded vibration through a floor panel. Names may be patterns rather than sounds—a rhythm tapped into a root wall, a static signature, a particular sequence of pressure. Music, where it exists, is low and physical: sub-audible pulses in a meeting hall, stretched fiber strings plucked inside enclosed chambers, resin bells wrapped in cloth so that their strike becomes a soft throb. Dance, if one can call it that, is slow and precise, more about balance and path than display. Their art often consists of arranging the landscape itself: guiding ripples into pleasing curves, polishing a fiber plain until it catches the light, training a growth shelf into an elegant arc, or leaving a fresh cut exposed long enough for its raw stripes to be seen before healing begins.

Fire is their oldest prohibition. In a world of dry fiber, fine dust, and static gradients, flame is not a tool but a catastrophe waiting for a mistake. Their industry is therefore sealed, electric, chemical, and slow. Where heat is necessary, it is contained in ceramic housings and monitored by multiple safeguards. Sparks are treated as near-criminal accidents. Every district has grounding paths, inert suppressant caches, mineral dust barriers, and crews trained to isolate a burning section before it can spread. Old char scars, if any exist from earlier mistakes, are preserved in resin blocks as warnings. Children are taught the danger before they are taught many other things. They learn that warmth must be earned without flame, that light must be borrowed without burning, and that the underplain’s abundance is conditional.

Their relationship to the fauna is neither pastoral nor industrial in the usual sense. It is cooperative, cautious, and often indirect. They do not keep animals as pets, but they maintain populations as processes. Husk-beetles are encouraged near harvest fronts. Grazers are guided toward surfaces that need pruning. Drifters are monitored during spore tides because they both assist and indicate the movement of reproductive material. Static-feeders are watched as living diagnostics. Predators are tolerated in controlled numbers because they prevent smaller feeders from becoming unbalanced. The inhabitants do not think of these creatures as possessions. They think of them as colleagues with different bodies and different understandings.

Some animals have learned to work near the people. Certain beetles will follow a chemical trail laid by a maintenance crew to a pile of waste fiber. Certain grazers respond to electrostatic markers placed along a terrace edge. Some drifters gather near collection screens where spore harvest is underway, taking what spills and leaving the main deposit undisturbed. The people do not command these behaviors so much as invite them. The underplain is too complex for absolute control. Its inhabitants have accepted that the best work is work done with the current of the place, not against it.

Their children are taught stillness before skill. A child in this place learns to sit against a living wall and feel its rhythms. It learns to place a hand on a floor and distinguish the vibration of a distant tug from the deeper pulse of ventilation. It learns to watch beetle swarms without disturbing them, to recognize the signs of an unstable dust bank, to ground itself before touching a cable, and to move without scuffing. It learns maps that are never final: woven charts with knots and resin beads that are constantly revised. It learns that a blank space on a map is not ignorance but possibility. It learns that the world is under construction and that the proper role of a person is not to finish it but to keep it from hurting itself.

Their dead are returned to the body of the place. The details vary by district, but the principle is consistent: nothing useful should be lost, and no one should be made into waste. The body is dried, separated, and incorporated into growth beds, fiber walls, or memorial structures. A name may be tapped into a root panel. A personal tool may be embedded in a new terrace. In this way, the dead remain participants. They become part of the insulation, the architecture, the slow memory of the underplain. This is not considered grim. It is considered accurate.

There are older inhabitants too, ones that do not move at all in any ordinary sense. Deep root masses, mineralized growth shelves, dormant strata, and sealed chemical reservoirs may be alive in ways that take decades to express. They are not gods, but they are treated with a kind of practical reverence. Before a major expansion, caretakers will study their responses over long periods. If an ancient mass begins to activate, work stops. The underplain may be built by many hands, but some hands are older than the city, and the city knows it must make room for them.

Because the terrain is still in its first draft, the inhabitants themselves have become provisional. They do not expect permanence. They expect adjustment. Their buildings can be moved, their rails re-aligned, their walls thickened, their passages closed if the substrate demands it. They are not nomads, but they are not fixed either. They live in a place that is still inventing its own geography, and they have invented a way of life that can keep pace with that slow invention. They are patient without being passive, careful without being fearful, industrial without being loud, and reverent without pretending that the world belongs to them.

If one had to name the dominant life of the underplain, it would not be the people. It would be the living body that contains them. If one had to name its dominant temperament, it would be quiet attention. Everything that lives there has learned to conserve solvent, conserve sound, conserve heat, and conserve force. Everything has learned that in a dry world, excess is danger, and waste is failure.

Above, the salt crust remains white and empty, scoured by wind and forgetting rivers. Below, the underplain is full of lives that do not need rivers: plant bodies that think in layers, micro-films that build chemistry grain by grain, beetles and grazers and drifters that perform their small offices, predators that move without noise, and people who have made restraint into a civilization. They live in the ordinary light, in the cool air, in the unfinished terrain. They live inside a world that is mostly plant and mostly patient. And because the world is still becoming, they too are still becoming: careful, sparse, and quietly alive.
