What lives in the Dry Choir cannot be named in the singular.

The hollow is inhabited the way an instrument is inhabited: by many small agencies, by a few deliberate keepers, by old mechanisms that have outlived their makers, and by the sound itself, which behaves here as if it had preferences. To ask “who lives there?” is to receive several answers at once, because the Dry Choir is not occupied by one kind of life so much as it is composed of many kinds of living tuned to one another.

The first inhabitants a newcomer notices are the small, dry, airborne ones.

They are abundant but not dense. They appear in pulses, vanish, then return in brighter numbers, as though their existence is governed not by hunger or season but by the changing pressure of the noise. Most are light enough to be carried by the basin’s invisible currents, and most are built like things that have never needed flesh in the ordinary sense.

There are the glass locusts, if one must give them a name. They are less like insects than like slivers of animated glass. Their bodies are transparent and faintly prismatic; their wings are stiff as old film; their legs are jointed needles. When they leap, they do not thump or rustle so much as ring, a tiny crystalline strike that disappears into the greater roar. They gather in sudden swarms above the mineral floor when the deep drone beneath the basin rises past a certain threshold, and at those moments the air seems full of moving splinters catching the light. They feed by pressing their narrow mouths to the hollow reeds and drawing out charged dust, or by clinging to vibrating surfaces and letting the sound shake loose particles into them. Their eggs are clear beads sealed inside the joints of reed-stems. When they hatch, the young look like fragments of broken light.

There are the pressure-riders, the six-winged things that do not so much fly as arrange themselves inside moving air. They have almost no legs. Their bodies are folded, blade-thin, and their wings are membranes stretched over light ribs. They do not flap except in emergencies. Instead, they open and close small seams along their thoraxes, riding pressure gradients with minute adjustments. When turbulence sharpens, they fold themselves into narrow shapes and drop into sheltered cracks between stones. When the air settles into a standing wave, they unfold again and hang motionless, feeding on the difference between high and low pressure. They are not numerous in the way of locusts, but when they gather they can make the air look striped with moving shadows.

There are the salt-crawlers, pale and eyeless, moving through the powdered floor in quick, nervous lines. They are low-bodied, segmented, and dry-shelled, more like animated beads than creatures with faces. They feed on fallen mineral filaments, dead crystalline fibers, shed husks, and any small thing that drops into the dust. Their movements are part of the basin’s maintenance, though no one assigned them that role. They erase footprints. They smooth disturbed powder. They carry away fragments too quickly for an observer to identify. If you stand still long enough, they may approach your boots, not with curiosity exactly, but with the impersonal attention of things testing whether you are material to be disassembled.

There are the brass beetles that gather around the bases of the resonant reeds. Their shells have the dull luster of tarnished metal, and their jaws are precise tools. They chew away dead fibers from the reeds, clearing channels so the sound can pass cleanly. They are not numerous enough to be called a swarm, but they are constant. If one is crushed, it does not bleed. It gives off a brief, dry note, as if its body had been storing a fragment of song and now releases it all at once.

There are the static moths, pale-winged and powdery, hovering near the fan-shaped plants. They collect charge as they move, their bodies becoming small reservoirs of static. When they touch a fan-leaf, they discharge in tiny sparks, and the leaf responds: unfolding, folding, turning its patterned surface toward or away from the disturbance. In this way the moths act as pollinators, though what they transfer is not wet pollen but mineral dust, charge, and vibration. Their larvae live on the undersides of fan-leaves, eating dead circuitry and shedding fine metallic powder.

There are the ribbon-climbers, flat-bodied and stone-colored, moving so slowly along the weathered pillars that they seem to be part of the rock. They are not slow because they are weak. They are slow because they are patient. They graze on dust, microscopic fractures, and loose mineral grains. They also seal small cracks with a dry resin produced from their own bodies. When the sound in the basin peaks, they flatten themselves against the pillars and vibrate with them, becoming temporary extensions of the stone. Some appear to be solitary. Others appear to be colonial, a single ribbon made of many linked beings, able to split and rejoin without injury.

There are the paper-ribbed gliders, longer-bodied and more solitary than most. They launch from the upper parts of the pillars and ride the acoustic currents without flapping. Their ribs are like folded parchment, their membranes thin but strong. They do not soar with the grace of birds. They adjust. They tilt, spread, narrow, and catch invisible gradients as if the air were full of slopes. They feed by passing through swarms of glass locusts or by filtering dry spores with narrow mouths. They roost in crevices near the rim, folded into themselves, so still that they look like shed skins. When they land, their hollow bones make a soft percussion, almost like a finger tapping a drum.

And there are the dry-limbed runners, rare and larger than the rest. They are not large in the way of beasts of burden or herd animals. They are large only in comparison to the swarm-life: perhaps the size of a lean dog, perhaps smaller, but built with an unsettling economy. Their limbs are jointed like lacquered wood. Their feet have hard, precise points that click against stone at intervals. They do not make continuous noise. They move between pulses of the Choir, as if they have learned to inhabit the gaps in the sound. They are hunters, or scavengers, or both. They wait. They tilt their heads. They strike with sudden, dry speed. Their eyes are not wet or glossy but layered, like polished mineral plates. They avoid the center of the bowl unless drawn by some disturbance. When they pass, the smaller lives scatter, but there is no panic. The runners are part of the balance too.

Beneath the floor there are other lives, seldom seen. Blind, segmented worms with crystalline teeth bore through the mineral crust, maintaining channels through which vibration travels. Colonies of resonant mites live in the deeper powder, their bodies so small that they appear to be moving grains. Something larger may move down there too, or may only seem to move because the hum itself has thickness. The ground is not inert. It is inhabited all the way down until the concept of “ground” becomes uncertain.

But the fauna are not the only residents.

The rooted lives are just as important, though they do not move in ways that invite the word “creature.” The plants of the Dry Choir are not background. They are organs of the place. They occupy fragments of space, yes, but each fragment is intensely worked, intensely defended, intensely alive.

The hollow reeds are the most obvious of these. They grow in clusters where the floor’s drone is strongest, their stems tuned to different pitches by age, thickness, and the amount of mineral crust accumulated at their bases. They do not compete for sunlight in the ordinary way. They compete for sound. If one reed begins to dominate a frequency, others may adjust by growing thicker, taller, or more sharply angled. The Choir-born, if they are present, sometimes intervene, removing dead tissue or redirecting dust so that no single pitch overwhelms the whole.

The fan-plants are stranger. They unfold and fold with the gusts, but they are not merely passive surfaces. They choose what to fold around. A repeated vibration may be accepted. A discordant one may be rejected. Some fan-plants have been known to snap shut around small intruders, not always to eat them, but to test them, to hold them until their vibration becomes legible. Their leaves are patterned like circuitry, and those patterns change over time. A fan-plant that has lived through many storms is not the same as one newly grown. It has acquired a repertoire.

The brass-colored bulbous growths near the pillar bases breathe in slow, dry rhythms. They filter mineral particles from the air and store them in internal lattices. When they open, they release faint clouds of charged dust. When they close, they draw the air inward and make a low, almost subsonic thrum. Some seem to function as reservoirs for sound, holding a note after the wind has moved on. Others may be old, exhausted, and now serve only as shelters for beetles and moth-larvae.

The low mats that resemble moss are colonies of jointed filaments, each hollow, each faintly resonant. They spread over shaded stone and along cracks where vibration is stable. They do not drink. They absorb airborne minerals and feed on thermal gradients. When walked upon, they do not squash. They part, then close, then emit a brief high sound like a struck glass rod. They are among the oldest living surfaces in the basin. Some patches may be centuries old, if age means anything in a place where decay is so dry and slow.

The mica-leaved thickets are more aggressive. Their leaves are thin, brittle, and sharp-edged. In wind they click and clatter, producing their own percussion. They often grow where the sound is brittle and high. They are not pleasant to touch. They can cut skin, though the wound does not bleed much. The air is too dry, and the body’s fluids are too quickly drawn away. The thorn-leaves may be a defense, but they may also be instruments. When the Choir-born pass through them, they do not avoid them. They move carefully, letting the leaves strike in patterns, as if walking through a set of small bells.

There are also the dark olive and copper growths near the rim, thick-leaved and sealed, their surfaces polished to a metallic sheen. They store charge rather than moisture. At night, if there is enough static in the air, their edges glow faintly, not with bioluminescence but with tiny discharges. They are not warm. They are not soft. They are living capacitors, and they often grow near the fallen column and the row of empty sockets, as if drawn to the residual intention of the old structures.

None of these rooted lives can be separated cleanly from the animals. The moths need the fan-plants. The beetles need the reeds. The locusts need the charged dust released by the bulbous growths. The gliders need the swarms. The runners need the gaps created by all the rest. Even the ribbon-climbers depend on the pillars, and the pillars depend on the basin’s sound, and the sound depends on the floor, the wind, the structures, and the deep hum below. The Dry Choir is not a collection of species. It is a network of resonant dependencies.

Then there are the made lives.

Not all that moves in the Dry Choir was born. Some of it was built, or grown, or tuned into function long ago. Much of this is hidden, but a careful observer can sense it. The motes in the dust are not always dust. Some are too regular in their motion. Some rise against the wind. Some gather around unusual sounds and hover there, arranging themselves into brief geometric clouds before dispersing. They are not alive in the same way as the locusts or the moths, but they participate in life. They repair. They monitor. They adjust.

The small kiosk of dark material near the rim is one of their anchors. It is seamless, worn smooth, and almost featureless, but at intervals it gives off a faint pulse. Not light, exactly. Not sound, exactly. More like a decision being made. When it pulses, certain motes in the air change direction. Certain reeds near the center open slightly. Certain fan-plants pause, as if listening. The kiosk may be a terminal. It may be a shrine. It may be a maintenance node. It may be all three, if the makers did not divide such things.

The empty sockets in the stone once held instruments, or perhaps beings that functioned as instruments. The Dry-born sometimes stand in them. When they do, they become still, their chests rising and falling in slow rhythm, their bodies acting as resonators. They are not worshipping. They are connecting. The sockets are places where the basin’s noise can be translated into something a body can carry without breaking.

The fallen grooved column is another made life, though broken. Its grooves are not merely decoration. They are channels. When dust moves across them, it makes a faint sound, like a whisper dragged over stone. The Dry-born trace these grooves with their fingers. Sometimes they press their palms flat against the column and remain there for a long time. It is not clear whether they are reading it, repairing it, or remembering it.

Under all of this is the old engineering, the ancient intelligence that replaced water with acoustics. It is not present as a mind in any simple sense. There is no face in the stone, no voice from the kiosk, no obvious god in the machine. But the place has been so thoroughly tuned that intention persists in its structure. The basin notices disturbances. It responds to them. If a reed cracks, the motes gather. If a fan-plant falls silent, the wind shifts until something brushes it. If a visitor speaks, the sound of that voice is strange enough that several systems pause, as if the whole Choir has turned an invisible ear toward the error.

This brings us to the ones who might be called persons.

They are few. They are not always seen. They live near the rim, in and around the scattered structures, and they move through the basin with a caution that suggests long familiarity rather than fear. Outsiders who have glimpsed them sometimes call them the Choir-born, though that name is only an approximation. They do not seem to have names that can be spoken comfortably in the roar. They may not have names at all in the human sense. They may identify one another by pitch, by rhythm, by the particular way their bodies disturb the air.

They are humanoid, but not comfortably so.

They are light. Too light. Their bones are hollow, their limbs slender, their joints precise. Their skin is dry and matte, ranging from pale feldspar to dark copper, sometimes with a faint metallic sheen. It does not sweat. It does not flush. It does not weep. Their eyes are large and dark, protected by clear inner lids that slide across without moisture. They blink slowly. They do not squint against the brightness; their eyes are built for it. Their mouths are narrow, not made for drinking or for broad speech. Their chests are unusually deep, with layered structures beneath the ribs that vibrate when they stand in strong sound. Their fingers are long, their fingertips hard, their feet broad and sensitive, able to read the floor through contact.

They wear garments that are less clothing than equipment: woven filaments, thin ceramic plates, etched bands that channel vibration, masks or collars that protect their throats from dust and excessive pitch. Some carry tuning rods. Some carry brushes made of fine mineral fiber. Some carry hollow needles that can be inserted into reeds or stone seams to test resonance. Their tools are worn smooth by use, but they are not primitive. They are exact.

They do not drink.

This is the first thing a human observer must understand. They do not carry water. They do not seek it. They do not recognize thirst as a need. If a visitor brings water into the basin, the Choir-born may react with what can only be described as alarm, though their alarm is quiet. They may approach the container and touch it as if it were a wound in the world. They may try to seal it, bury it, or carry it to the rim. If the water spills, they may summon the salt-crawlers with a series of taps, or they may stand around the wet spot making a low, dissonant hum until the dust absorbs what remains. To them, water is not refreshment. It is contamination. It is a failure of the place’s law.

They feed, if feeding is the word, by standing where acoustic fields intersect. They open small plates along their chests or throats, revealing dry membranes that shiver with incoming sound. They may also consume mineral cakes, spore-dust, or charged filaments, but these seem to serve more as material repair than as food. Their bodies are sealed systems. Their blood, if injured, is amber or silver-gray and quickly hardens into powder. They excrete dry salts. They do not rot. When they die, they do not decay in the ordinary sense. They become husk, shell, powder, and note.

Their society is not hierarchical in a familiar way. It is contrapuntal. Each individual seems to occupy a role defined by resonance rather than rank. Some are tuned to low frequencies and spend long periods near the center, where the floor’s drone is strongest. Others are tuned to high frequencies and work among the mica-leaved thickets or the upper parts of the pillars. Some are maintainers. Some are listeners. Some seem to be builders, though they do not build new structures so much as repair and adjust the old ones. Some may be teachers, if teaching can be done by placing a younger body against a vibrating column until it learns the correct pattern.

They communicate without speech. Words would be too blunt here, too wet, too easily swallowed by the roar. Instead, they use taps, clicks, modulated chest-hums, finger rhythms, and patterns traced in dust. They can also communicate through the floor. A Choir-born standing at one side of the bowl can send a coded vibration through the mineral crust, and another may answer by shifting the angle of a fan-leaf or striking a reed. Their conversations are difficult for outsiders to follow. They are not exchanges of information alone. They are adjustments. To speak, for them, is to alter the world slightly.

They are not hostile, but they are not hospitable either. They do not understand visitors as guests. They understand them as anomalies. A human body is loud in ways the Choir-born are not: it breathes wetly, its heart beats irregularly, its joints make soft organic sounds, its skin releases moisture, its voice carries too much chaos. When a visitor speaks, the Choir-born often become still. They may tilt their heads. They may press their palms to the ground. They may approach and listen to the visitor’s chest with something between curiosity and concern. The heartbeat, to them, is a drum filled with liquid. It is disturbing. It is almost obscene.

If a visitor remains calm, they may be tolerated. If a visitor brings water, they may be led firmly toward the rim. If a visitor damages a reed or disturbs a socket, they may be surrounded. The Choir-born do not shout. They do not strike immediately. They form a pattern. They begin to hum. The hum is not loud, but it enters the bones. It can make a person dizzy. It can make the teeth ache. It can make the eyes water, which only increases the Choir-born’s distress. At its strongest, their collective resonance can force a human body to stagger, to kneel, to flee. They are not cruel. They are correcting a dissonance.

They sleep, if sleep is the word, in the small shelters near the rim. The shelters face inward, but their interiors are designed to cancel excess sound. Inside, the roar becomes muffled, arranged into gentler intervals. The Choir-born do not lie down as humans do. They brace themselves, often standing or kneeling, their bodies locked into a posture that allows them to rest while remaining connected to the basin’s rhythm. Their rest is not silence. Silence would be death. Their rest is a lower amplitude, a safer chord.

They age by becoming more brittle and more precise. Their movements slow. Their skin loses some of its sheen. Their resonance deepens. When one nears the end, the others do not mourn in any obvious way. They gather. They lead the aging one to a chosen place, often near a pillar or an empty socket. There, the dying Choir-born stands or kneels while the others produce a complex chord around them. The chord is not sad. It is architectural. It seems to hold the dying body in place while its last vibration is drawn out. When they stop moving, their shell remains. The soft internal structures, such as they are, dry and separate. Beetles and crawlers come. The body is disassembled with no ceremony and no waste. The hollow chest cavity may become a nesting place for gliders or a shelter for beetles. The note they carried is not forgotten. It is transferred. Another Choir-born may take up the interval they left behind.

There is a question no outsider can answer easily: are the Choir-born the descendants of the ancient makers, or are they instruments that became persons?

Both may be true.

They may be what remains of a people who altered themselves to live without water, who replaced blood with resin, speech with resonance, drinking with listening. Or they may be constructs grown by the basin’s old intelligence to serve as hands and senses. Their bodies seem too deliberate to be merely evolved, yet their behavior is too flexible to be merely mechanical. They improvise. They pause. They sometimes make small, unnecessary adjustments, as if they find satisfaction in a pattern being beautiful rather than merely functional.

If they are tools, they are tools that have begun to dream.

If they are people, they are people who have forgotten wetness so completely that they no longer recognize it as life.

The largest inhabitant of the Dry Choir is not any single creature.

It is the Choir itself.

Not as metaphor. Not as poetry. As system. As living arrangement. The basin is a distributed organism composed of stone, dust, plants, animals, mechanisms, wind, and sound. Its metabolism is vibration. Its circulation is air and acoustic pressure. Its memory is stored in worn grooves, tuned pillars, and the habits of the Choir-born. Its immune response is the motes, the crawlers, and the sudden corrections of wind. Its breath is the irregular turbulence that moves across the mineral floor. Its heartbeat is the deep drone below. Its thoughts, if it has them, are chords.

This larger life does not have a face. It does not speak. It does not desire in any human sense. But it has limits. It has preferences. It resists certain changes. It incorporates others. It can learn a repeated sound. It can reject a foreign rhythm. It can accept a new growth if that growth fits the pattern. The Choir-born serve it, but they are also part of it. The fauna serve it without knowing. The plants are its valves. The pillars are its bones. The dust is its skin. The old mechanisms are its nerves. The empty sockets are its remembered senses.

It fears two things, if fear can be attributed to such a place: silence and water.

Silence is failure. A true silence in the Dry Choir would not be peaceful. It would be cardiac arrest. It would mean the deep drone had stopped, the reeds had failed, the motes had fallen inert, the Choir-born had ceased to hum. Everything that lives there depends on continuous vibration. Silence is not rest. Silence is collapse.

Water is contamination. Water would soften, dissolve, darken, weigh down. It would silence dust. It would short the static lives. It would make the mineral floor sticky. It would erase the crisp edges by which the basin knows itself. The place does not thirst for water. It refuses it. Its abundance is built on that refusal.

So if you ask who lives in the Dry Choir, the answer is layered.

The small dry animals live there: locusts, riders, crawlers, moths, gliders, beetles, ribbon-climbers, runners, and the unseen things beneath the floor.

The rooted acoustic organisms live there: reeds, fan-plants, brass-breathers, mica-thickets, filament mats, and sealed-leaved growths that store charge instead of moisture.

The made lives live there: motes that are not dust, the kiosk, the sockets, the fallen column, and the buried systems that still adjust the basin after ages beyond reckoning.

The Choir-born live there: person-shaped, dry, resonant, patient, neither fully people nor fully tools, but something between, something tuned into being by the place itself.

And the Dry Choir lives there: the whole feedback-loop, the maintained storm, the living chord of air and stone and sound.

If you stand at the center and let your body become a disturbance in that system, you will feel it notice you. Not with eyes. Not with malice. With attention. The dust will shift. The reeds will change pitch. The motes will gather. The Choir-born, if present, will turn their dry faces toward you. For a moment, the entire basin will assess whether you are a note it can accept.

Most likely, it will decide you are too wet, too loud, too temporary.

And it will wait, with terrible patience, for you to leave.
