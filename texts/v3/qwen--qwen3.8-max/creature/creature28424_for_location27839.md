## What Lives in the Dripping Stacks

Life in the Dripping Stacks does not arrive as greenness, flocks, or soft abundance. It arrives as a change in texture: a slickness on brick, a faint blue-green glow in a vat, a tremor in a black pool that has no visible cause. The inhabitants are few by number but intense by attention. Everything that lives there is tuned to the pulse of water, the pressure of darkness, and the grammar of sound.

The first residents are not animals in the ordinary sense. They are films, threads, crusts, and slow colonial lives that coat the manufactured surfaces and make them habitable.

### The Living Skin

The lowest life is the living skin of the place.

Bacterial films spread across troughs, jar-lips, drain walls, and the inner faces of cisterns. Some are rust-colored and brittle when dry; others are bone-pale, grey, or faintly shimmering with mineral iridescence. When moisture returns, they swell and become slick, digesting dust, dissolved salts, dead insect powder, and the fine organic waste that drifts down from the canopy. In some districts they are cultivated in shallow trays, fed with careful washes of mineral slurry, and scraped away only when thick enough to be pressed into cakes.

These films are not beautiful in a pastoral way. They are functional, saline, and strange. They smell of wet ash, cold clay, and old metal. Yet they are the base on which much of the rest depends. Without them, the water would turn foul. Without them, the fungal threads would have less to bind. Without them, the beetles, larvae, and crawlers would have little to graze.

More important still are the fungal threads.

They seam the cracks between bricks, stitch broken ceramic, and bind loose ash into firmer crusts. In some places they are so fine they look only like a pale bloom on wet stone. In others they gather into ropes, felted mats, or curtain-like nets stretched across vents and drip-lines. When the air is dry, they contract and become brittle. When moisture returns, they soften, stretch, and resume their work.

The threads are not decorative. They are part architecture. They hold surfaces together while mineral water passes over them, encouraging new crust to form. They filter sediment. They trap tiny drifting organisms. Their tougher strands are harvested, twisted, and used as cordage. In some vats they glow faintly blue-green, giving the only light that does not come from oil or flame.

The fungal network is perhaps the truest native of the Stacks. It does not think as a single animal thinks, but it chooses. It thickens where water is reliable. It abandons a wall that has become too dry. It sends pale filaments into a crack before a keeper’s hand can pack it. The people who work the basin do not so much command the threads as bargain with them, feeding them slurry, shading them from harsh lamp heat, cutting them back when they threaten to choke a drain.

Mineral blooms also live in a border sense. White, rust-colored, and bone-pale growths spread across wet surfaces, not purely alive but not wholly dead either. They form where bacterial activity, dissolved salts, and dripping water meet. Some are scraped away because they weaken walls. Others are left because they seal micro-cracks or make a surface easier to clean. A keeper learns to read these blooms the way a sailor reads wind: by color, by texture, by the way they change the sound of a surface when tapped.

### The Small Animals of the Wet Dark

Animal life is rare, localized, and precious. It does not roam freely across the basin. It appears in patches where moisture, warmth, shelter, and food align. A cistern may seem empty for hours, then reveal a pale shape turning under the surface, a ripple, a sudden strike.

The most common visible creatures are the saltback beetles.

They are pale, flat-bodied, and slow, their shells crusted with mineral powder until they look like moving chips of the wall itself. They crawl over fungal mats and dry terraces, grazing on bacterial films, dead moth dust, and the thin organic skin that forms where water retreats. They are not abundant enough to be a pest, except in the sense that any life is dangerous when it disturbs the water system.

Saltbacks know the cycles. When the air begins to bead and the first fat drops form overhead, they climb. They ascend scaffold poles, rope curtains, jar-necks, and retaining walls with a patient, mechanical persistence. If water catches them, they lock their legs, flatten themselves, and become almost indistinguishable from mineral crust. Keepers sometimes knock them into collection pits when they gather too thickly near walkways, because too many beetles can scrape a new crust before it hardens. Yet they are also protected in certain districts, because their grazing keeps drain-lips clean.

In the still waters hang the larval knots.

They look like tiny ropes or tangled threads, pale and faintly translucent, drifting or adhering to submerged ceramic. They are filter-feeders, drawing in microscopic life and suspended waste. When touched, they contract into tight knots, becoming dense and hard. When released, they slowly uncoil and resume their feeding.

The knots are useful in ways that are not immediately obvious. Their bodies produce a fine, tough secretion that can bind mineral particles together. Keepers sometimes transfer them into leaking seams or new clay patches, letting them work briefly before the next pulse of water. Too many knots can clog a narrow pipe, so their numbers are watched. Their presence is a sign of still water; their sudden absence may mean a coming flush.
