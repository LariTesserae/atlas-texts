**What Lives in the Dry Nave**

Whatever lives in the Dry Nave is, at first, only a fault in the echo.

A grain of grit slips from the vault. It falls. The fall returns. But the return is not clean. It arrives wearing a small shadow, as though the sound has passed through something alive before coming back to the center. There is no footstep there, no wingbeat, no drip of water. Only a sense that the hollow has listened before answering.

If it must be given a shape, the shape is low, not tall. It does not rise in defiance of the dark. It has learned the dark’s humility.

There are several of them—or perhaps one thing divided into several postures—arranged along the broken terraces of the floor. They are not statues, though they have borrowed stone’s patience. They are pale, the color of the plain above: bone-white, salt-gray, the faint rust-stain of old iron carried in veins that no longer need blood in any ordinary sense. Their skin, if it is skin, is a cracked mineral film, flexible only enough to prove they are alive. Their backs are vaulted. Their ribs rise beneath them in delicate arches, so that sound can pass through and around them without being broken.

They have no eyes.

Where a face might be expected, there is instead a shallow bowl, a broad listening-shape, a horizontal mouth like a fissure in chalk. Their heads tilt not toward light, which does not exist there, but toward the nearest return. They hear with more than ears. Their palms, pressed flat to the terraces, feel the floor’s answers. The hollows of their chests collect resonance. Their throats are dry and narrow, made for producing sounds so brief they seem accidental: a click of the palate, a breath drawn backward through the mouth-crack, a knuckle tapped once against stone.

Then stillness.

In that stillness, they live.

They do not walk in procession across the nave. No line of them moves from entrance to altar, because there is no entrance and no altar, and because they have replaced pilgrimage with placement. Each one occupies a position according to the acoustics of its body. One crouches where the roof’s curve sends sound downward. Another rests along a terrace where a whisper can be made to multiply. A third clings near the wall where echoes thicken and slow. They are not arranged by rank or devotion, but by resonance. When one shifts, the others adjust, not in alarm, but as a room adjusts when a piece of furniture has been moved.

They leave the center empty.

This is not fear. It is reverence. The center is where all returns can be compared. It is the place from which the edges of the nave become known. To stand there would be to become the thing measured. To stand there would be to interrupt the listening. So they circle it in their slow, low movements, never crossing it, never filling it, preserving it as an open ear.

Their language, if language is what it is, does not consist of words moving from one speaker to another. It consists of sounds sent into the room and then interpreted as they come back altered. A click may return sharpened by the wall, meaning: *the stone is tense tonight*. A hum may return softened by dust, meaning: *the roof has settled, there is no new crack*. A long dry exhalation may return in layers, first from the nearest terrace, then from the curving walls, then from the vault, then from some hidden seam too fine to see, meaning something like: *we are still sealed, we are still whole, we are still here*.

They know the boundaries of the Dry Nave because they are always asking them.

They eat what dryness can still give. There is no green life there, no moisture fat enough to breed a garden. They feed on the slow bloom of minerals: salts that sweat from stone over centuries, films of calcium and iron too thin for any eye to see, the powdered remains of older rock that has forgotten being water-borne. Their mouth-fissures graze the terraces without hurry. A single meal may last a generation. They do not drink. Their bodies are closed wells. Whatever trace of water remains in them is held so deeply that it cannot be called liquid; it is a memory inside their cells, a ghost-weight of wetness in the architecture of their joints.

They do not smell like rot. They do not smell like musk. They smell like dust warmed by a hand, like chalk after it has been held too long, like the air inside a vessel that was sealed before anyone learned what thirst meant.

Some of the terraces are not terraces.

They are the settled ones.

When one of the listeners grows very old, its movements become shorter. Its echoes grow denser. It chooses a place along the floor, folds its vaulted body tighter, and lets the mineral world enter its joints. First the limbs still. Then the skin stiffens into crust. Then the inner hollows fill with crystal so fine it is almost silence. The others do not remove it. They do not mourn in any way that resembles grief. Instead, they use the new ledge as a sounding shelf. They rest their palms upon the backs of ancestors and feel how the old bodies have changed the room.

The floor of the Dry Nave is broken into low terraces because it is full of finished listeners.

The young are not born so much as they are begun. A flake of living mineral skin is chipped from an elder’s side and laid in a crack where the echo is weak. For years—decades, perhaps longer—the elders hum over it. They do not feed it. They tune it. They teach it to answer. One day, in a sound so small it is almost imagined, the flake returns its first echo. Then it is alive. Then it is a listener. Then it is given a place somewhere along the terraces, where its small body can help complete the room.

Perhaps they were once something else.

Perhaps, when the pale plain above was not yet so dry, people came down into the hollow to escape the vanishing rivers. Perhaps they brought with them the shapes of worship but not the gods, and when the surface became crust and crack, they sealed themselves below rather than carry the memory of water into a world that had forgotten it. Over long ages, their bodies changed to suit the sealed room. Eyes became unnecessary. Skin learned to conserve every particle of moisture. Bones thinned into sounding-ribs. Faith, having nothing left to ask of the sky, turned inward and became acoustics.

Or perhaps they did not descend at all.

Perhaps a room of that size, shaped like a nave and sealed in the earth’s palm, could not remain unattended. Perhaps the hollow needed a witness. Perhaps the mineral plain, cracked and riverless above, pressed its silence down until the silence gathered itself into bodies. Perhaps what lives there is the Dry Nave’s own way of knowing itself.

They do not pray for water.

That would be too human, too hungry, too full of longing directed outward. Their holiest act is maintenance. They keep the room audible. They keep the echoes honest. They preserve the exactness of the boundaries. In their click-and-hush tongue, the nearest thing to a sacred phrase might translate, if translation were possible, as: *the shape remains*. Or: *we are the edges hearing one another*. Or simply: *dry is not empty*.

At intervals longer than seasons, all of them make a sound together.

It is not loud. It is a dry chord: clicks, breath, palms striking stone, the faint ringing of mineral throats. The sound goes out from the living bodies and enters the nave. The nave answers. The vault returns it rounded. The walls return it split into ribbons. The terraces return it weighted with the density of the settled ones.
