It is an all-liquid expanse over raw geology that is still coming into place, and for that reason what lives there is not yet wholly separate from where it lives.

At first, if you are submerged there and looking for life in the ordinary sense—fins, fronds, shells, eyes, mouths, the quick dart of appetite—you will find nothing that satisfies the search. There are no fish. There is no kelp. There are no crawling things, no bones, no eggs, no tracks, no burrows, no reefs, no colonies arranged in the patient architecture of survival. Nothing has made a home there because there is nothing yet to make a home against. No shore offers a margin. No bottom offers a settled floor except the still-cooling, still-shifting crust itself, a foundation not fully convinced of its own permanence.

And yet the water is not empty.

It is more correct to say that the High Birthwater is not inhabited so much as it is beginning to inhabit itself.

The first living fact is near the surface.

Under the bright hammered ceiling, just below the skin of the ocean, there exists a vast and almost invisible population of light-facing motes. They are not plants, not animals, not quite anything that would later become either. They are flat, clear, buoyant things, each one barely wider than a grain of sand or the edge of a fingernail, depending on how near you are when you see them. They have no color. They cast no strange glow. They do not shimmer with meaning. They simply exist in such numbers that the upper water is thickened by them, a living film so fine that it seems at first to be only a trick of refraction.

They orient themselves toward the white-gold sun.

They do this without brains, without nerves, without apparent intention, and yet the whole upper ocean is arranged by their response. Their bodies bend the daylight. Their shadows fall in tiny, honest coins through the blue below. When the surface moves, they move with it; when the light changes, they adjust in a slow, waveless turning. The hammered look of the ceiling is not caused by them alone—wind and water and the high thin air have their say—but they sharpen it, multiply it, give it a kind of minute metallic order. They make the surface seem worked. They make it seem almost pressed into shape by countless small touches, though nothing there has hands.

These motes feed on light.

They also feed, in a lesser way, on the mineral breath rising from the young crust below. They take in the sun and the dissolved newness of the planet and they persist. They release small bubbles that rise and break at the surface, adding nothing dramatic to the world, no scent, no omen, only a faint physical fact. They are the first grazers of daylight in a high ocean with no shores, and they are so ordinary in their function that they become unsettling. They do not look like a miracle. They look like a condition.

Below them live the things that come closest to being called creatures.

They are not animals, but they have the manner of animals in one respect: they move through the water as if movement matters to them. They are transparent, mostly, and are seen more by what they do to light than by any substance of their own. They appear as elongated swellings, as slow distortions, as clear bodies with slightly different densities than the water around them. Sometimes they resemble long gloves filled with nothing. Sometimes they resemble sleeves, veils, or sacs drawn out by a current that is not present. Sometimes they seem like the memory of a shape that has not yet decided which organ it wants to become.

They have no faces.

That is one of the first things the mind protests. They have no fronts, no backs, no obvious heads, no mouths, no eyes. They are not blind because they lack eyes; they are blind because sight has not yet become necessary. They sense pressure. They sense gradients of heat and chemistry. They know the difference between the sun-warmed upper water and the mineral-cool depths. They know, in some dim physical way, when another body is near.

They drift, but not helplessly.

Their motion is a slow contraction, a tightening and releasing of their transparent substance, as if they were breathing water through themselves rather than moving through it. They do not swim so much as permit themselves to become elsewhere. When one of them changes direction, it does not dart. It thickens on one side, thins on another, and the whole surrounding current accepts the change. Their shadows are soft but real. They fall through the blue with the same honest neutrality as the shadows of the light-motes above. Nothing about them is theatrical. Nothing about them asks to be interpreted.

They feed on what descends.

The dead or spent motes from the upper water sink in invisible numbers. Mineral particles loosen from the young crust and rise in delicate suspensions. Chemical differences bloom in cool plumes
