What lives there is not soft, and it is not green. Life in that high, hot mineral place has arrived by pressure, thrift, and repetition. It has learned to exist without the comfort of soil, shade, or abundant water. It is life made attentive to small signs: a darkening seam in stone, a bead of condensation on iron, a change in the temperature of a wall, a faint hiss from a valve, the almost imperceptible movement of dust in heated air.

The people are the most obvious answer, but they are not the whole one.

They are a quiet, careful people, shaped by altitude and heat into bodies and habits that waste nothing. They tend to move with economy, not laziness. Their steps are placed. Their hands know rails, stone lips, valve wheels, ladder rungs, and the polished edges of doorways. They are often lean without being fragile, with deep chests and slow breathing, adapted by generations to thin air. Their skin carries the marks of the place: fine mineral dust worked into the creases, faint salt bloom along the jaw and wrists, coppery stains near the fingertips, small pale scars from hot metal or sharp glass. Their eyes are accustomed to glare. Many wear smoked lenses, mica visors, or veils of woven mineral fiber when they must cross bright terraces at midday. Even without protection, they squint less than a newcomer would; their faces have learned to accept severe light without surprise.

Their clothing is severe too, and practical. They wear layered garments made from woven glass fiber, fine metal mesh, imported cloth where trade allows it, and stiff treated hides when heatwork demands it. Sleeves are often rolled or tied back with cords. Collars are high against dust. Pouches are common, but the most valued pouches are those made to hold containers: a small cup, a sealed vial, a folded sheet of thin metal that can be angled to catch a drip. A person without a cup is considered unprepared in the same way that a person without footwear might be considered unprepared elsewhere. The wealthy are not those who possess many ornaments, but those who possess reliable vessels, fine seals, and rights to steady moisture.

They speak softly. This is not merely custom; it is sense. Loud sound can stress brittle crusts, disturb settled dust, and in certain shafts create dangerous resonance. But over time the practical reason has become a moral texture. Their voices are low, intimate, and measured. They stand close when they speak. They touch a wall, a railing, or another person’s sleeve to gain attention rather than shout. They have many gestures, some of them tiny: a finger drawn across condensation, a hand turned palm-up beneath a pipe, a knuckle tapped twice against stone. In the louder industrial levels, where engines breathe behind ceramic casings, they communicate more by lamp, flag, moving chain, or colored disc than by speech. Their language has not vanished, but it has been made careful. A single struck bell is enough to mark a change in shift, a warning, or a death.

They know the city by heat more than by name. A resident does not simply say, “I am going to the market hall.” The body understands that the route means descending through a warm passage, crossing a shaded cut where the stone stays cool until late morning, climbing a stair that becomes dangerous after noon, and pausing beneath a condensation gallery where the air tastes metallic. They navigate by thermal gradient. They know which handrail will be too hot to touch, which wall will be damp before dawn, which courtyard holds a pocket of cooler air, which threshold has begun to rise and now requires a shorter step. Children learn these things early. A child in this city is taught not to run, not because running is joyful but because running jars loose dust, risks cracked crusts, and wastes moisture in the body. Children learn to drink slowly. They learn to hold a cup with both hands. They learn to listen for the faintest tick of water finding its way through stone.

Water is the first wealth.

At dawn, when cold iron sweats and ceramic catchers bead with condensation, the city becomes briefly ceremonial without becoming loud. Fin-tenders move along the upper arrays, adjusting angled surfaces so that moisture slides into troughs rather than evaporating uselessly. Households open sealed basins. Workers check bead-lines along walls. In certain districts, people press their palms to rock and wait, judging whether the coldness comes from depth or merely shadow. A good seam-listener can tell where brine is weeping before it becomes visible. Such people are valued, and their knowledge is passed carefully from hand to hand, teacher pressing pupil’s palm against the same stone at the same hour for many days.

Water rights are serious. The right to hang a condensation fin, to repair a cistern, to divert a weep, to drain a heat-pool, or to claim the first measure from a public basin is not casual. Disputes over drips can last longer than disputes over money. Waste is offensive not only because it is impractical but because it insults the labor of collection. A leak is reported at once. A cracked vessel is repaired, not discarded if repair is possible. A household known to lose water through carelessness is pitied more than envied, because such a household has failed in the basic etiquette of the place. The people here do not pray for rain in the way lowland peoples might. They prepare surfaces. They clean catchers. They angle fins. They listen. If a cloud bank climbs the escarpment and presses through the streets like a warm gray tide, they do not cheer. They become quicker, more focused. Walls slick over. Cables drip. Cloths are hung. Children stand with mouths open only if the elders permit it, and even then the gesture is brief.

Heat is not their enemy either. It is a current to be routed.

The city’s residents have learned to live inside heat as river people live inside water. They know its levels, its eddies, its sudden pressure. The lower terraces near the vents are worked in shifts. Workers descend into zones where the air has a sour mineral taste and the stone is too warm to sit on without insulation. They wear thick-soled boots, layered sleeves, and masks over the nose when sulfur grows too strong. They do not linger. Rest chambers are cut into cooler rock nearby, sometimes with damp cloths hanging over frames, sometimes with shallow basins whose evaporation lowers the air by a small but precious degree. A worker’s body becomes literate in these differences. A person can tell when a vent has changed because the heat against the cheek is no longer even. A person can tell when a furnace level is running long because the stairwell exhales too slowly.

They do not fight temperature so much as move it. Heat from kilns and rock seams is led into drying floors, warming cells, distillation trays, and pressure engines. Shade is built deliberately: awnings of mineral fiber, slate shutters, recessed doorways, narrow alleys angled so that direct sun reaches them only for a short time. Certain streets are passable only in early morning or after dark unless one is protected. The body learns the city not as a map of distances but as a map of exposures. A short route at noon may be a long route at dusk. A high terrace that feels cool in wind may be dangerous after several hours of glare. The people are not heroic about this. They adjust. They change their clothing, their hours, their tools. They know that heat can kill quietly, by drying thought before it dries skin.

Their work is industrial, but it is not frantic.

The city makes things slowly. Wire is drawn in long patient intervals. Glass is cooled in thick imperfect panes. Pressure seals are fitted, tested, refitted. Ceramic tiles are fired in kilns that breathe for days. Salt is scraped, refined, stored. Insulated tubing is wound by hands that know not to rush. Batteries are charged in humming sheds where the air tastes faintly of metal. Replacement parts are made constantly because the city is always resisting heat, uplift, dryness, and slow strain. Nothing is assumed to remain fixed. Repair is not an interruption of life; it is one of life’s main occupations.
