What lives there is plural before it is singular.

The first thing one would have to admit, if one tried to describe the inhabitants of that dry country under stone, is that there is no single master of the place. No one species stands above the rest as humanity might stand above a landscape, holding it conceptually apart from itself. The life there is woven into the architecture so completely that the city and the citizens are difficult to separate. The towers are not merely occupied. They are made by living bodies, maintained by living bodies, and eventually composed of living bodies that have ceased to move. The roads are polished not by wheels or hammers, but by the passage of countless dry limbs. The light is not kindled; it is metabolized. The sound is not produced as an effect or entertainment; it is the medium through which the inhabitants recognize, regulate, and continue themselves.

If one insists on naming the people of that place, the most honest name would be something like the Builders. But that word is misleading if it suggests conscious construction in the manner of engineers. They do not design with instruments. They do not lift stone with devices. They do not cut, fasten, or assemble in the way a tool-using creature would. They add themselves. They secrete, bind, graze, deposit, wear down, and renew. Their city is an extension of their bodies and instincts over immense time.

If one wishes to understand them more accurately, one might call them the Choir, because sound is what holds them together.

The Builders are not beautiful in any easy or familiar sense. They are pale, dry, low-slung creatures, flattened from top to bottom so that they can move through narrow cracks and over broad dust plains without wasting moisture or height. Their bodies are segmented and waxen, coated with a fine mineral bloom that makes them look as though they have been rolled in powdered bone. They do not shine. They do not gleam. They absorb the weak living light around them and seem made of the same dim substance as the towers. In some angles they resemble large, flattened grubs; in others, like slivers of chalk given joints and intention.

They have no true eyes. At most, they possess scattered light-sensitive patches along the sides of their bodies, faint milky spots that can tell the difference between the blue-green glow of fungal growth and the deeper absence beyond it. They do not need sight as surface creatures understand it. Instead, they live through vibration, scent, touch, and the taste of dust. Their antennae move almost constantly, reading the air for chemical traces and for the minute tremors carried through stone and compacted powder. Their forelimbs are sensitive enough to detect the resonance of a hollow tower from a distance, the thickness of a wall, the presence of larvae working within it, the subtle wrongness of a cracked support.

Their mouths are complicated organs. They can rasp fungal tissue, scrape mineral crusts, grind old shed skins into usable powder, and press fibers into place. More importantly, they possess glands that produce binding substances: dry silk, wax, cement-like secretions that harden when exposed to the cool air. These are not tools. They are parts of the body’s own economy, turned outward into the world.

They do not drink. The idea of drinking, of seeking liquid, would be alien to them. Water for them is not a substance one approaches. It is an interior fact. It exists inside tissues, in the bodies of fungi, in the eggs of insects, in the metabolic byproducts of slow digestion. They conserve it with extraordinary efficiency. Their outer surfaces are sealed. Their breathing passages are narrow and tortuous, minimizing loss. Their waste is dry. When they feed, they feed on tissues and dusts that contain just enough bound moisture to sustain them. If one of them were cut open, it would not bleed in the way a surface animal bleeds. It would show pale, dense tissue, almost waxy, with fluids held under tight biological control.

They are not large. An adult Builder might be as long as a hand and broad across as a folded palm, though some of the older forms, especially those that remain near the deep nurseries, grow larger and slower. Their size is not impressive in itself. What gives them presence is number, patience, and coordination. A single Builder crossing a dust plain may look like a strange pale seed moving on six legs. A column of them moving along an avenue becomes a river of soft clicking. A district of them at work becomes a weather system.

Their young are more important to the city than the adults.

The larvae are the true masons. They are soft-bodied, white or faintly amber, blind, and almost entirely confined to the interiors of walls, towers, and bridges. They live in galleries so narrow that an adult could not enter them. They feed on dust, fungal cords, dead skin, mineral grains, and the remains of older generations. As they eat, they excrete and secrete material that hardens into structural substance. They do not choose a blueprint in any individual sense. Their instinct is architectural, but it is not conscious planning. It responds to vibration, to chemical gradients, to the texture of existing surfaces, and especially to the songs of adults.

A larva working alone is only a small soft body in the dark. Thousands of larvae responding to the same resonant pattern become a wall, a buttress, a fluted column, a ceiling. Over years, their incremental additions create shapes that appear deliberate. The hollow towers are hollow because generations of larvae were drawn by sound to reinforce certain curves and leave others open. The vents exist because air moving through passages produces frequencies that guide further building. The bridges form where the chemical scent of two growing colonies meets and stimulates larvae to spin across the gap. Nothing is sudden. Nothing is imposed. The city accumulates the way coral accumulates, except that here the coral is dry, insect-born, and responsive to song.

Adults emerge from sealed cells after long development. They are harder, drier, and more mobile than the larvae. Their role is not primarily to build new mass, though they can repair and refine surfaces. Their role is to maintain, coordinate, feed, sing, and move the patterns of the city forward. They walk the avenues constantly, keeping them clear with their bodies and mouthparts. They crop fungal growths before they become too dense. They carry spores on their legs and backs, transferring them to new districts without intending to. They feed larvae by regurgitating concentrated fungal paste, or by leading them to rich patches of mycelium. They remove the dead, not by burial but by incorporation. A corpse dried to paper-lightness may be ground into dust and worked into a wall. A shed skin may become part of the next layer of a tower.

The adults are also the singers.

Their bodies carry ridges, plates, and rasping surfaces that they rub together to produce sound. Some sounds are high and thin, made by scraping narrow joints. Some are deep and hollow, produced by tapping mandibles against resonant walls or vibrating the thorax against a tower surface. Some are almost below hearing, felt more as pressure than as tone. They do not sing as birds sing, for display alone. They sing to coordinate. They sing to mark place. They sing to regulate the behavior of larvae, to warn of predators, to maintain roads, to signal reproductive readiness, to stabilize the acoustic identity of a district.

The deafening noise of the country under stone is, in large part, their civic life.

It is not chaos. It is layered. The high rasping of smaller Builders may indicate feeding activity or surface maintenance. The slow grinding chords from hollow towers may signal that larvae are building within. A sudden thinning of sound can be more alarming than any scream, because it means that something has interrupted the pattern. Predators moving in large numbers, collapse of a fungal stem, or the presence of an unfamiliar chemical trace can cause a district to fall quiet in patches. Then the sound returns, changed. The whole city listens to itself through its own noise.

Their intelligence is not absent in individuals, but it becomes most itself in groups. A lone Builder can navigate, feed, avoid danger, and respond to simple signals. It can remember textures and chemical trails. But its mind is narrow, shaped for immediate tasks. The collective is larger than any one mind. When many Builders sing together in a resonant district, their sounds interact with the architecture they have made. The towers amplify certain frequencies and suppress others. The result is a slow, distributed cognition: not a single consciousness, but a living system capable of adjusting itself over hours, seasons, and generations.

They do not think quickly. A decision may take days. A shift in building pattern may take years. They have no need for rapid response except in the presence of predators or structural failure, and even then their actions are coordinated through sound and scent rather than command. They do not have leaders in the ordinary sense. There are no rulers giving orders from high places. There are fertile individuals hidden in deep chambers, there are older bodies whose chemical signatures influence those around them, and there are resonant patterns so old that they function almost like ancestral voices. But authority, if it exists, is acoustic and architectural. It lives in the shape of the city and the repetition of inherited song.

They have no language of words as surface peoples would recognize. Their communication is closer to a dense grammar of vibrations, textures, and chemical states. A particular rasp against a particular wall can mean something like: this passage is open. A changing drone can mean: this wall must thicken. A burst of short clicks can mean: predators nearby. A slow pulse can mean: larvae active here. A chemical trace laid along a road can mean: follow, feed, carry, return. There is no abstraction detached from place. Their signs are always tied to surfaces, bodies, and immediate functions. Yet within that limitation there is great subtlety. They can communicate degree, urgency, duration, and location. They can distinguish old song from new, safe dust from toxic dust, living fungal tissue from dead.

They do not own things. They have no possessions in the sense of portable objects kept apart from the body. There are no tools, no ornaments, no weapons carried for later use. What they have, they have by being part of it. A road belongs to them because they maintain it. A tower belongs to them because their bodies made it and continue to shape it. A fungal grove belongs to them because they prune it, feed on it, spread it, and depend on it. Their relationship to the world is not ownership but participation.

They are not gentle, exactly. Gentleness implies a choice made from softness, and they are too dry, too patient, too shaped by necessity for such a word. But they are not cruel either. They do not waste. They do not kill for excitement. They kill when they must, or when their biology demands it, and they are killed in turn. Predation is part of the city’s balance. Some Builders become food for other creatures. Some creatures are culled by them. The system has no sentiment about this. Death is not a tragedy to be overcome. It is material returning to use.

The other fauna of the country under stone lives in close relation to them, though not always under their control.

The pale beetles that move in slow columns along the avenues are not mere scavengers. They are part of the city’s circulation. They consume shed skins, dried fragments, waste pellets, and the powdery remains of old structures. Their clicking shells keep the roads clean by constant movement. They may not understand the Builders, but they respond to the same acoustic and chemical cues. When the Builders’ song changes, the beetle columns alter their paths. When a district falls silent, the beetles slow or gather in knots, as if waiting for the pattern to resume.

The wingless moths, thick-bodied and ash-colored, are carriers. They rest in overlapping multitudes on fungal caps, their bodies dusted with spores. When they move, they carry fertility from grove to grove. They are not tended in the manner of domesticated animals, but they are tolerated, encouraged, and sometimes guided by scent trails and light patterns. Their presence indicates a healthy fungal district. Their absence is a warning. When they die, their dry bodies become part of the dust that larvae will later bind into walls.

The crickets and other stridulating creatures are not Builders in the strict sense, but their sound enters the Choir. Some of them are distant relatives of the Builders; others are separate lineages that have fallen into resonance with the city. Their harsh rasps and grinding notes overlap with the Builders’ songs, producing complex chords. This is not accidental harmony. The acoustics of the towers favor certain frequencies, and creatures that produce useful or compatible sounds persist in the districts where they can be heard. Over long ages, the city has become an instrument that selects its players.

The tiny fliers that rise and fall in the dim air are almost too small to see individually. Their wings make a sound like falling sand. They disperse microbes, spores, and luminous particles. They may be responsible for some of the faint phosphorescence that clings to cracks and ceilings. They are short-lived, abundant, and essential. Without them, the slow exchange of living dust between districts would fail.

The predators are another matter. They are blind, quick, and dry like everything else, but their bodies are made for seizure rather than construction. They move through cracks and under mats, hunting smaller creatures, larvae, and sometimes young Builders. They are not enemies of the city. They are checks. They prevent populations from exceeding what the fungal groves and mineral plains can support. Some districts tolerate them more than others. In places where predator scent is strong, the Builders move in denser groups and their songs become shorter, more vigilant. The predators do not destroy the pattern. They sharpen it.

Even the luminous larvae on the ceiling belong to this living order. Some are the immature forms of creatures that never fully descend to the basin floor. Others are colonies that have existed in high places for so long that their weak light has become part of the city’s orientation. The Builders do not farm them, but they respond to them. Certain songs are performed beneath glowing patches, as if the cold light were a marker for reproductive or architectural work. The glow is too weak to illuminate much, but it is enough to give the dimness a direction. It is a memory of dawn without ever having seen dawn.

The Builders’ food is not agriculture in the human sense. They do not plow, irrigate, or sow. But they do cultivate. They graze fungal mats in ways that encourage regrowth. They trim old mycelial cords, allowing new threads to spread. They move spores on their bodies. They clear dust from surfaces where lichen needs to establish. They maintain waste heaps that become nutrient beds for mineral-loving growths. Their cultivation is embodied and continuous. It is not separate from their ordinary movement. To live is to tend. To feed is to prune. To travel is to disperse.

Their fungal forests are therefore not wilderness. They are also not farms. They are something older than that distinction: living systems shaped by mutual dependence. The great pale columns that stand like trees are partly natural growths and partly the result of generations of Builders moving, feeding, singing, and dying around them. Some fungal caps are kept low by constant cropping. Others are allowed to rise because their spore production benefits nearby districts. Some hollow stems become resonating chambers for adult song. Some dead stems are left standing because their dry fibers provide scaffolding for new growth. Nothing is purely wild, and nothing is purely made.

They have no concept of water as surface beings know it. They do not dream of rain. They have no memory of rivers, no myth of pools, no instinctive longing for clouds. If they possess a word for water at all, it would be a word meaning hidden, inside, bound. Water is not outside. It is life’s interior condition. A creature that leaks water would seem broken to them. A place where water stood open and free would seem monstrous, if they could imagine it at all.

This is why they are so perfectly fitted to their country. They have not merely survived the absence of free water; they have become an expression of it. Their bodies, their city, their sounds, their slowness—all of it is shaped by conservation. They do not wait for rain because rain is not part of their world. They do not expect renewal from outside because outside is not part of their world. They have turned limitation into form.

Their relationship to death follows the same logic.

When a Builder dies, it is not removed to a place apart. It is dried, broken down, and reused. The soft parts may be consumed by larvae or pressed into nutrient mats. The harder plates may be ground into dust and incorporated into new wall layers. Chemical traces from the dead body may stimulate building behavior in nearby larvae. In this way, the ancestors remain physically present in the city. Not as spirits, not as remembered names, but as structure. A tower may contain the mineralized remains of ten thousand generations. An avenue may be smoothed by bodies that became dust that became wall that now shapes the song of living adults.

There is no burial. There is no cremation. Fire is impossible and unthinkable. There is only continuation through material. To the Builders, this is not grim. It is ordinary. It is the only kind of immortality their world allows: to become part of the persistence of the place.

Their settlements are sparse because they must remain balanced with the larger country. The city is not a continuous carpet of towers. It is clusters, districts, islands of life-light separated by broad dust plains, silent stretches, and low fungal thickets. This sparseness is not emptiness in the sense of absence. It is interval. The Builders need open ground for sound to travel, for air to move, for predators to range, for fungal groves to expand slowly without being overgrazed. They also seem to need emptiness aesthetically, if one can use such a word for creatures without human art. Their towers are placed so that resonance can pass between them. Their avenues open into plains where the noise of the city can thin into a low hum before gathering again.

To stand in one of those empty stretches is to understand the scale of their civilization. One may see only a few pale towers in the distance, faintly glowing, and believe the land is deserted. Then the eye adjusts. Movement appears everywhere: a beetle column crossing the dust, a moth shifting on a fungal cap, a line of Builders repairing a ridge, a shadow of predators under a mat. The emptiness is full of paths, signals, memories, and uses. It is not uninhabited. It is unhurried.

Their time is long. They do not experience history as a sequence of dramatic events. They experience it as accretion. A tower grows by fractions. A song changes by small variations. A district expands over generations and then contracts when fungal food sources shift. They have no chronicles, no written kings, no dated wars. Their history is stored in layers of wall, in altered resonances, in the chemical memory of trails, and in the inherited patterns of song. If a new tower rises, it is not recorded as an achievement. It simply becomes part of the environment that future larvae will respond to. If a district dies, its remains are absorbed into the next phase of building.

They are capable of conflict, but not in the manner of nations. Conflicts among them are usually local: competition between adjacent colonies for fungal tissue, disputes over resonant space, clashes when predator pressure disrupts normal patterns. These conflicts do not produce weapons. They produce changes in sound, scent marking, and building behavior. One district may thicken its walls against another. One group may alter its song to drown out a rival frequency. Occasionally, bodies die. The city absorbs the disturbance. There is no total war because there is no surplus to fuel it, no technology to escalate it, and no imagination of annihilation.

They are not immune to catastrophe, but their catastrophes are slow. A fungal blight can empty a district over decades. A shift in air currents through the stone can silence a set of towers and force the Builders to abandon them. A predator lineage can increase too rapidly and strip a grove. A mineral crust can become unstable and collapse under its own ancient weight. Even then, the response is not panic. It is adjustment. Songs change. Larvae are guided elsewhere. Roads are abandoned. New patterns emerge. The country does not reset. It continues.

If an outside creature were somehow to enter that place, it would be sensed before it was seen. The Builders would know it by the wrongness of its moisture, its heat, its smell, its movement. Anything carrying free water would be almost incomprehensible to them. It would seem unsealed, leaking, dangerously excessive. They would not attack it out of malice. They might avoid it, wall it off, or attempt to cover it with dust and binding secretions if it came too near a nursery. They would treat it as one treats a breach in the world. Fire would be worse than impossible; it would be a violation of every rule by which they exist. Light without coldness, heat without life, sudden transformation without growth—such things have no place in their order.

A human, if one could stand there, would feel the pressure of their attention without ever being seen. The sound would change around him. The towers would seem to listen. The dust would become too still. The Builders would not gather in a threatening swarm unless disturbed. They would simply adjust their patterns around the alien presence, trying to classify it, failing, and then perhaps beginning the long work of sealing it away from the living city.

Yet for all their strangeness, they feel normal in that place. That is the unsettling truth. They do not seem like monsters imposed upon a landscape. They seem like the landscape’s own completed thought. Their bodies fit the dust. Their voices fit the towers. Their patience fits the ancient stone overhead. They are not a rebellion against the dryness under stone. They are what the dryness became when life had enough time to answer it.

They are the country’s living tissue made mobile. They are its memory made sound. They are its architecture made temporary, walking, and then returned to permanence again.

Who lives there?

The Builders live there. The Choir lives there. The larvae in the walls, the adults on the avenues, the beetles on the roads, the moths on the caps, the predators under the mats, the fliers in the dim air, the glow-worms on the ceiling, the fungal columns themselves, the lichen, the dust, the resonant towers, and the vast balanced emptiness between them—all of it lives there.

But if one must choose a single answer, the truest answer is that the place is inhabited by a collective being: a dry, blind, musical, city-making life that exists in many bodies at once. It has no face, but it has presence. It has no king, but it has order. It has no tools, but it has works. It has no writing, but it has memory. It has no word for rain, no longing for the sun, no fear of silence, because silence is only a pause inside its enormous song.

It lives as persistence. It lives as accretion. It lives as sound moving through dust and stone. It has finished becoming what it is, and now it simply continues, loud and patient, in the cool dark under the world.
