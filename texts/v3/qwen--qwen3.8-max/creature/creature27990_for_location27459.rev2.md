## What Lives in the Root-Town Under the High Tarn

The town is not empty because it lacks hearths, doors, or beds of dry wood. It is inhabited, but not in the way a land-town is inhabited. Nothing there claims a room by shutting a door behind itself. Life in the Root-Town is proved by participation in the slow circulation of the tarn: by filtering, binding, grazing, sounding, pruning, listening, and being listened to.

To ask who lives there is to receive many answers at once.

The moss lives there.  
The fish live there.  
The birds that fall through the surface and vanish into the green streets live there.  
The few human tenders live there, though their living is more attendance than occupation.  
The sounds live there, too, if one grants that a sound can have a home, a habit, a territory, and a season.  
Above all, the town itself lives there: the whole assembled balance of root, water, animal, and patient craft.

It is not one population but a commonwealth.

---

## The Green Majority

The largest inhabitant is vegetal.

If the Root-Town has a people, its first people are plants. They are not scenery, nor merely material for construction. They are the living substance of the place: floor, wall, fence, roof, gate, instrument, and memory. They make the town possible by becoming it.

The mosses are the most numerous. They form close lawns over the terraces and spill in soft green edges along the causeways. They are not one moss but many: fine-threaded mosses that knit into smooth mats, cushiony mosses that rise in low hummocks around stone collars, dark slick mosses in the shadier channels, and pale liverworts pressed like old coins against the sides of boulders. Together they make the town’s skin. They catch silt. They soften current. They give small creatures a place to rest, graze, and hide. They also suffer injury slowly. A careless kick from a diver’s foot may leave a bare patch for a season; a torn mat may take years to close again. For this reason the Rootbound treat moss as one treats a grazing animal or a sleeping child: with care, restraint, and a sense that it must not be disturbed without need.

Below the moss are the cord-roots. These are the town’s buried timbers, except that they are alive. They run thick and sinuous beneath the channels, crossing under lanes, anchoring posts, binding the woven walls of fish courts, and holding the basin together where sediment would otherwise shift. They are not dead wood set into place; they are growing things, trained over generations. Some have been bent around stone vanes and made to serve as living hinges. Some have been split by patient pressure, not blade, so that they widen into arches. Others have been braided with their neighbors until separate plants became a single architectural mass. When a cord-root is old enough, its surface becomes rounded, dark, and polished by current. It may be furred with moss, patched with tiny liverworts, or threaded with younger rootlets seeking purchase. The Rootbound do not cut such roots casually. An old cord-root is not a beam to be replaced. It is an elder of the structure.

From retaining walls and old woven terraces hang curtains of fibrous root-hair. These are among the most animated features of the town. They move constantly, drifting and lifting in the current like combed wool, like hair in wind, like slow banners in a procession no one can see. They filter particles from the water. They catch drifting seeds, motes, eggs, and insect cases. They provide shelter for young fish and crayfish. They soften the edges of doorways and make the interior chambers feel furred and hushed. When a current changes, the curtains change with it, leaning one way, then another, as though the walls themselves were thinking.

Then there are the jointed water-stems: pale green, hollow, upright, rising in sparse columns from the deeper corners. They are among the strangest and most important living residents. Their interiors are full of ascending bubbles, and their joints give them a segmented, almost artificial look, as if they had been turned on a lathe. But they are plants, not carved posts. They grow toward the light in deliberate slowness, and they are used by the town as both structure and voice. Some have been encouraged to grow through stone collars until they form gates. Some have been trained into rows to make living fences. Some have been selected, hollowed only by their own nature, and set upright as sounding pipes. When water passes through them, they whistle, knock, and hum. They are the town’s throats.

The plants do not live in silence. They creak under pressure. They flex when sluices open. They release bubbles that rise in bright threads. They shed fibers, leaves, and loosened threads that drift into the current and are caught elsewhere, becoming part of another wall, another floor, another baffle. In the Root-Town, growth is not separate from architecture. Growth is architecture. To live there as a plant is to be continually recruited into the shape of the place.

## The Swimming Commons

Animal life is abundant, though not crowded in the way a market is crowded. It is distributed according to light, current, shelter, and sound. The town has rooms for different lives.

The most visible residents are the fish.

Small fish live in the upper channels and lit plazas. They move in glittering shoals, turning as one body when the light shifts or a shadow passes overhead. They are quick, silver-green, and restless, always feeding on tiny larvae, algae, and drifting organic motes. Their movement is one of the town’s daily weathers. When they rise, the water seems to flicker. When they scatter, the town seems to flinch. The Rootbound read their behavior. A sudden dart means a bird has entered. A slow spiral may mean a current has shifted. A tight balling near a root curtain may mean a larger fish has passed through a doorway.

Larger fish hold in the deeper doorways, under arches, and along the shaded sides of the central hall. They are not always easy to see at first. They lie with fins just moving, bodies aligned to the flow, patient as old guardians. Some are broad and dull-colored, their scales muted by mossy light. Some are sleeker, darker, and inclined to hang motionless before vanishing into a root-shadow with one economical stroke. These larger fish are not hunted often. Some are known individually by the divers, in the way land-dwellers might know old trees or familiar dogs: by a torn fin, a pale patch, a habit of returning to the same post, a manner of turning when the sluice tones change.

In the breeding courts, at certain seasons, fish become audible. They drum, scrape, and pulse against the woven floors and root walls. The sound carries far underwater. It is not a beautiful sound in any simple sense. It is a bodily sound, a communal sound, a sound of life pressing against the shape of the town. The Rootbound listen to it with attention, because it tells them whether the courts are healthy, whether the currents are right, whether too much silt has settled where eggs should lie.

Mussels are another ancient population.

They rest in shallow troughs of woven matting, along filtration lanes, and in beds arranged where current brings them food without bringing too much sediment. Their shells are dark, green-black, sometimes furred with fine growth, sometimes polished smooth by water and time. They open and close with tremendous slowness, as though each decision were a civic act. They are the town’s filters. They draw water in, take what they need, and send it back clearer. They do not move, but they are not passive. Their closing is a signal. If a bed closes tightly and remains closed, the Rootbound know something is wrong: silt, disturbance, a change in water quality, or some injury to the current.

The oldest mussel beds are treated with something like reverence. Not because they are sacred in an ornamental way, but because they are infrastructure and ancestry at once. Their growth rings hold years. Their shells accumulate the memory of seasons. To damage them would be to wound the town’s patience.

Crayfish live in the root-shadow.

They are armored, wary, and most active when the light dims. Their antennae sweep constantly, reading the water. They click in the dark corners, a dry, sharp sound that becomes stranger underwater because it seems to come from everywhere at once. They eat what has loosened: dead plant matter, fallen larvae, small animals weakened by season or injury. They are scavengers, but not dirty ones. They keep the margins clean. They move through the root-knots with a sideways deliberation that never seems hurried, even when they flee. Children among the Rootbound are taught not to chase crayfish, because chasing them stirs sediment and disturbs the acoustic quiet of the root-shadow.

Insects are everywhere, though often small enough to be noticed only by their works.

Caddisfly larvae build portable cases from moss grains, seed fragments, and tiny mineral particles. They move through the town like living pebbles, slow and deliberate, each one carrying its own house. To a diver with time and stillness, they are astonishing: masons no bigger than a fingernail, assembling armor from the town’s own dust. Water beetles climb the stems, carry bubbles of air like silver buttons, and dart through root curtains in sudden bursts. Backswimmers and surface-skaters belong more to the upper skin of the tarn, but their larvae and young are part of the submerged economy. Snails and limpets graze the stone vanes, posts, and old wooden collars, keeping them free of excessive film. Their rasping is faint, but in the quiet lanes it can be heard if one is still enough: a tiny, patient scraping, like the town cleaning its own teeth.

Amphibians belong to the margins and the shallower terraces.

Frogs rest on moss platforms, their throats pulsing before they push away in languid kicks. Newts move through the root-hair curtains with soft, serpentine grace. In breeding season, the edges of the town become loud with them. Their calls blur through water and air at once, resonant and thick, sometimes so layered that the whole shallow margin seems to throb. Their eggs are laid among root tufts, and their larvae graze in the calmer channels before transforming and moving between water and rim. They are seasonal citizens, but powerful ones. Their presence marks the town as alive in two realms: below and above, submerged and emergent.

## The Feathered Edge

Birds belong to the upper edge, but their influence reaches down.

Dippers and ouzels are perhaps the most intimate avian residents. They walk the submerged walls, gripping root tufts with surprising strength, pecking larvae and small invertebrates from the weave. They do not tear the town open. They work within it. Their gray-brown bodies move with brisk purpose, and their calls—sharp, clicking, water-cut sounds—carry along the surface and into the shallows. The Rootbound tolerate them gladly, because they help keep insect life from overrunning certain channels and because they do not uproot what they touch.

Diving ducks and other plunge-feeders are more sudden. They fall from the surface like stones, vanish downward, and appear again in another street with a burst of bubbles. Their passage can send cascades of air through the canopy. Their shadows alarm the small fish, causing quick silver scatter through the architecture. They are not residents in the same steady sense as dippers, but they are regular visitors, and the town is shaped with them in mind. Some courts are partially roofed with woven rootwork to prevent too many diving birds from taking fish in one place. Some open lanes are left deliberately exposed, because birds are part of the town’s larger life. They bring nutrients from outside. They carry seeds. Their droppings enrich the margins. Their wings stir the surface and help breathe the upper water.

Even birds that do not dive are felt below. A shadow crossing the light changes fish behavior. A splash changes the acoustic field. A feather sinking slowly through a shaft of green-white light can draw the eye of a diver as surely as a spoken word. In the Root-Town, the sky is not absent. It is translated downward through water, sound, and movement.

## The Human Tenders

The people are the fewest of the town’s recognized inhabitants, and they know it.

They are sometimes called the Rootbound, though that is less a grand name than a description of their obligation. They live mostly on the rim and in hidden inlet shelters: low huts woven from root-fiber, turf, and wind-shaped shrubs, tucked under overhangs or screened by cushion plants. From above, their dwellings might be mistaken for natural mounds or wind-thrown thickets. They are not numerous. The town cannot support many, and they do not wish to multiply beyond what the tarn can bear. Their presence is sparse by intention.

They descend by braided root ropes or slip under through hidden inlets among the cushion plants. They wear hooded skins oiled against the cold. They carry pouches of shell tools, coils of line, bone awls, antler picks, slate blades, and small stone knives. No metal touches the submerged town. No iron nail, no forged bracket, no machinery of alloy or screw belongs there. Their craft is tying, grafting, weaving, stacking, abrading, and encouraging growth. Their tools are simple, but their knowledge is not. It is accumulated, precise, and conservative in the best sense: it remembers what worked, what failed, what healed, and what was harmed.

Their bodies have adapted less by becoming monstrous than by becoming disciplined. They are not gilled, not finned, not made into water-creatures by legend. They are human, but humans trained for cold, slowness, and listening. Their hands are strong and callused, especially at the fingertips, from knotwork and root-fiber. Their lungs are practiced. Their pulses are slow. They can hold still in the chill long enough for the town to resume its ordinary rhythm around them. Their eyes are accustomed to green-white light, to motes, to the way shadows thicken under root arches. They do not speak loudly, even above water, because they have learned that sharp sound is not private. Sound in the tarn belongs to everyone.

They move slowly because haste disturbs the water, and the water carries disturbance everywhere. A sudden kick sends silt rising. A hasty stroke startles fish. A dropped tool strikes the root-floor and travels through the acoustic channels like a wound. Therefore the Rootbound move with economical strokes, turning their bodies along current-lines rather than fighting them. They do not swim through the town as conquerors. They pass through it as careful workers pass through a sleeping house.

They have different offices, though none are rigidly separated.

There are sluice-listeners, who tend the hollow reed pipes and the water-gates. They know each pipe’s voice: its dry note, its flooded note, its warning note, its breeding-call note. They can tell by sound whether a gate is partly blocked, whether silt has gathered at a collar, whether a current is running faster than it should. They open and close sluices not by brute force but by sequence, listening as the tones change.

There are mossbinders, who repair terraces, press loosened sediment into place, weave baffles, and encourage moss lawns to heal. Their work is quiet and almost invisible. When it is done well, no one notices. The terrace simply remains. The lawn simply closes. The silt simply stays where it should.

There are root-guides, who train living stems over years. They bend rather than cut. They tie rather than nail. They divide by pressure rather than blade. If a stem blocks a channel, they persuade it into a new direction. If a root threatens a gate, they alter the pressure around it until it grows away. Their art is long. A single correction may take a generation to complete. A root-guide may begin a bend in youth and see it become architecture in age.

There are air-well keepers, who maintain the inverted basket-domes sealed with pitch and plant resin. They clear valves, test trapped air, replace worn reed fittings, and listen for leaks. Air-wells are few, simple, and vital. They allow divers to remain below long enough to work without exhausting themselves. They are also meeting points, signal points, and sometimes places of refuge when a current turns unexpectedly strong.

There are knot-keepers, who remember. They make no written books. They record in cords: knots, spacing, fiber type, shell beads, toggles, and dyed threads. A knot-record may hold a season’s fish counts, a repair made to a gate, a storm damage, a change in water level, or the acoustic signature of a particular channel. The knot-keepers can read these cords by touch in dim water. Their memory is not abstract. It is stored in the same materials that build the town.

The Rootbound have rules, though they are rules of practice rather than text.

Nothing living is cut if it can be guided instead.  
A stem that blocks a channel is bent, tied, and trained over years.  
A root that threatens a gate is divided by pressure, not blade.  
If a living anchor must be severed, another must be planted and bound in its place before the old one is removed.  
No fire is made below the surface.  
No heavy stone is dropped where it might bruise the root-floor.  
Nets are temporary and loosely woven.  
Harvesting is done by pinching, pruning, and gathering what has already loosened.  
Quiet lanes are kept quiet.  
Sound is not wasted.

Their law might be summed up in a phrase the town itself seems to repeat:  
what falls is gathered; what gathers is bound; what binds becomes ground.

They eat from the town, but they do not mine it. They take fish in small numbers, mussels when the beds are strong, tubers and water-stem shoots where growth is abundant, insect larvae where they can be gathered without disturbing nurseries. They dry and store food on the rim, where fire is permitted. Below, there is no cooking, no heat, no smoke. Heat belongs to the rim. The town belongs to coolness.

Their children learn stillness before they learn speed. First they are taught to float without panic. Then to breathe calmly in cold water. Then to listen. Then to move without stirring sediment. Then to tie knots. Then to recognize the sounds of gates, pipes, frogs, fish drums, crayfish clicks, and root-creak. Only later are they taught to prune. Cutting is taught last, and sparingly. Many Rootbound go years without making a true cut. To cut is to admit that guidance failed, or that necessity overtook patience. It is not forbidden, but it is serious.

They have no single ruler. Decisions are made in listening circles, often at air-wells or on the rim at dusk. The eldest are not necessarily those with the most years, but those with the slowest judgment, the finest ear, and the longest memory of repairs. A person who speaks too quickly is not trusted with a gate. A person who moves too fast is not trusted with a root. Authority among the Rootbound resembles stewardship more than command.

They are not joyless. Their joy is simply quiet. It appears in the satisfaction of a healed moss lawn, in the first true tone of a newly trained pipe, in the return of fish to a court after winter, in the perfect stillness of a dive where nothing is disturbed. They laugh softly. They play with knot puzzles. They mimic bird calls and fish drums, sometimes to tease one another, sometimes to teach children the differences. They do not tell loud stories underwater. They tell stories by touch and sign, or save them for the rim.

To outsiders, they may seem strange: cold-handed, slow-spoken, too attentive to noises other people ignore. But to the town, they are useful organs. They are the part that can carry intention without violence.

## The Sound-Lives

If the Rootbound were pressed, they would admit that sounds also live there.

Not as flesh, perhaps, but as presences with habits, territories, seasons, and consequences.

The wind dragging across the surface sends a low rolling hum down through the water column. That hum has no fixed shape, but it is not formless. It thickens in the central channel, thins in the side lanes, and vibrates differently against root walls than against stone vanes. Rain becomes a thousand tapping knuckles on the ceiling of the world. In heavy rain, the town sounds as though it is being drummed upon from above by impatient fingers. In light rain, it is a soft patter, almost like grazing.

Water moving through root-gates makes hollow wooden notes. The hollow stems whistle and knock. The upright reed pipes in the central channel produce sequence after sequence of low tones when the sluices are opened. These tones are not decoration. They are living infrastructure. They mark gates. They warn of changing currents. They call fish into certain courts. They tell divers where they are. A diver caught in drifting motes or behind a root curtain may know the west gate not by seeing it but by hearing its particular tone under the general green hush.

Fish drums are seasonal residents. Frog choruses are seasonal residents. Crayfish clicks are nightly residents. Bird strikes are sudden visitors. The creak and throb of living plant architecture adjusting to pressure is the town’s deepest and most constant sound. The Rootbound sometimes call this the Under-Voice. It is never absent. It changes with water level, temperature, current, and growth. In spring, when the tarn fills, it becomes more taut. In late summer, when levels fall, it becomes more intermittent, with pauses where certain channels grow shallow and bird-noise enters from above. In winter, if ice forms, the sounds deepen and slow; the town becomes a resonant vessel under a lid, and every shift carries like a struck bowl.

Sound in the Root-Town is treated as a creature that must be fed, housed, and respected. It is fed by water flow. It is housed in pipes and hollow stems. It is released through sluices. It is protected in quiet lanes. Some channels are kept deliberately silent, not because they are naturally soundless, but by custom: no one makes deliberate noise there. Those lanes are preserves for listening. They are places where a diver can hear the root-floor settle, where a knot-keeper can touch a cord without distraction, where the Under-Voice can be judged without interference.

Other places are allowed to ring. The fish courts, the gate-channels, the reed rows, the breeding margins: these are loud in their proper seasons. The town has acoustic zones as clearly as a land city might have market quarters or temple courts. To make a careless noise in the wrong place is not merely rude. It is an injury to orientation, to breeding, to repair, and to memory.

Thus the sounds are among the town’s inhabitants because they are not accidental. They are cultivated. They are named, in a sense. The Rootbound speak of the “three-knot tone,” the “hollow throat,” the “fish-call,” the “rain-ceiling,” the “old bowl note,” the “crack-warning.” They listen for absences as much as presences. A missing frog chorus can mean cold. A changed pipe tone can mean blockage. A new sharp report can mean stone shifted or root torn. In the Root-Town, listening is not passive. It is a way of inhabiting.

## The Shape of a Day Among the Inhabitants

At dawn, the surface brightens. The moving ceiling of light turns silver where wind touches it and blue-white where sun enters directly. Small fish rise in the upper channels, their shoals flickering as they feed. Mussels open by slow degrees. Snails rasp at posts. Caddis cases drift a little, carried by current, then catch on root-hair.

A Rootbound diver descends from the rim by braided rope, hooded skin darkening in the water. The descent is slow. At the first air-well, the diver pauses, breathes through the polished reed valve, and listens. The pipe tones are checked. One is slightly lower than it should be. Silt, perhaps, at the collar. The diver taps a shell twice against stone: a signal to a partner above or nearby. No shout. No urgency. The water takes the tap and distributes it.

Mossbinders work on a terrace where sediment has drifted. They do not sweep. They press, gather, weave, and encourage. Their movements are so careful they seem almost ceremonial, but they are practical. A terrace left bare would erode. A baffle left loose would choke a channel. Their labor is small, repeated, and essential.

A dipper walks a submerged wall nearby, gripping root tufts, pecking at larvae. It does not flee the diver. It has learned that these slow beings are not predators of its kind. A shadow passes above. The small fish scatter. A duck has entered. It glides through a street with wings half spread, trailing bubbles, then vanishes upward with a muffled report that arrives below as a sharp slap.

In the fish courts, a sluice-listener opens a woven gate just enough to let water pass through the upright reed pipes. The tones sound: low, resonant, spaced. Fish move toward them by habit and instinct. The listener watches, not to command them, but to confirm that the court is receiving its proper flow. Later, the gate will be closed again. The sound will change. The town will adjust.

By evening, the light dims. The water becomes blue-gray. Frogs begin at the margins. Crayfish click in the root-shadow. The Under-Voice remains: creak, throb, pressure, patience. The divers return to the rim. Their skins are hung. Their tools are cleaned. Fire is made above, never below. They eat sparingly. They speak softly. They listen to the tarn even after they have left it, because the tarn never fully leaves them.

## The True Resident

Who lives in the Root-Town Under the High Tarn?

The moss lives there, making ground out of patience.  
The cord-roots live there, holding the town together as living beams.  
The root curtains live there, filtering, sheltering, swaying.  
The water-stems live there, rising as columns and voices.  
The fish live there, quick and glittering in the light, slow and shadowed in the doorways.  
The mussels live there, opening and closing like valves of the town’s own body.  
The crayfish live there, clicking in the dark margins.  
The insects live there, building cases, carrying air, grazing, hunting, becoming food.  
The amphibians live there, loud at the edges, bridging water and rim.  
The birds live there, even when they belong mostly to the surface, because their passage shapes the streets below.  
The Rootbound live there, though they sleep on the rim and work below, because their attention is a form of dwelling.  
The sounds live there, housed in pipes, released by sluices, protected by quiet lanes, interpreted by listeners.  
The town itself lives there, not as a single creature but as a balance.

It is not a ruin full of ghosts. It is not a drowned accident. It is a converted habitation, a place where life has accepted the terms of water, cold, slowness, and sound. Its inhabitants are not numerous in the way of land-cities, but they are deeply present. They live by restraint. They live by repair. They live by listening.

And perhaps that is the deepest answer: the Root-Town is lived in by everything that has learned not to force the water, but to belong to it.
