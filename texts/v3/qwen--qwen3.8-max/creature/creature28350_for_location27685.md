What lives there does not live as separate bodies moving through a landscape. It lives as the landscape. The first and most difficult thing to understand is that the inhabitants are not “in” this place in the ordinary sense. They are its wetness, its pressures, its slow decisions, its mineralizing edges. To ask who lives there is almost to ask the wrong question, because the world you described has not divided itself into scenery and creatures, environment and organism. It is all organism, or all process, or all becoming. The terrain itself is the closest thing to a body. The weather is closer to breath than to atmosphere. The sound is not made by throats, but by a vast living mass thinking hydraulically.

Still, if one insists on naming what lives there, forms begin to emerge—not animals, not persons with faces, but agencies. Some are ancient in mass though young in substance. Some are local and fleeting. Some are so large that they can only be recognized by what they do: make fog, harden tissue, redirect water, deepen a resonance, begin stone.

The primary inhabitant is the green continental body itself.

It is not one organism in the way a single plant is one organism. It is too fused, too layered, too internally weathered for that. It is more like a civilization of tissues that have grown into a single planetary fact. Its “self” is not seated anywhere. It is distributed through capillary tension, chemical gradients, mineral hunger, light-seeking, and the slow memory of strain in fibers. It does not think in quick images or desires. It thinks in wetness and pressure, in the opening of pores, in the thickening of cell walls, in the decision of water where to go. Its thoughts may take hours. Its intentions may take seasons. Its memories may become veins of silica or crusts of calcium, laid down as living mineral and preserved in the body of the world.

This body is neither kind nor cruel. It is total. It absorbs, grows, filters, adjusts. It has no need for eyes because light is not something it watches; light is something it distributes, harvests, shades, steals, shares. It has no need for mouths because nourishment is not seized and swallowed; it is negotiated through surfaces, drawn upward by tension, dissolved into films, exchanged through membranes. It has no limbs because motion here is not locomotion but reconfiguration. A whole province can turn toward a faint brightening in the mist over the course of many days. A saturated hollow can close into a denser knot as water tables shift. A ridge can exhale enough vapor to seed a storm in another region. These are the gestures of the place.

Within that immense body, there are what one might call local selves, though they are not individuals with boundaries. They are persistent patterns of activity, living offices of the world.

One of the oldest of these is the waterwarden tissue. It is not a being with a shape so much as a vast capillary intelligence: root-lace, stem-pith, fibrous channels, spongy reservoirs, and microscopic passages tuned so finely that water can be moved upward, sideways, even apparently against expectation. This is the part of the world that knows thirst before thirst becomes visible. It senses mineral charge, temperature differentials, the weight of moisture in upper layers, the dryness latent in a thin draft. When a shallow basin begins to lose its mirror, it is this tissue that drinks. When a high ledge needs firmness, it is this tissue that sends water into its fibers until they swell and hold. It is always deciding, though no decision is spoken. It redirects flow. It closes paths. It opens others. It can starve one region to stabilize another. In a world without roads or pipes, this waterwarden lattice is both infrastructure and instinct.

Higher up, where the air is cooler and mist gathers into fringes on every surface, live the fog-combers. These are the upper fronds, the hair-fine leaf structures, the waxy ridges and weeping edges that catch vapor and persuade it to become liquid. They are among the most delicate inhabitants, if delicacy can be spoken of in a place so dense. Their existence is a patient combing of air. They draw moisture from passing clouds, from internal exhalations, from the slow drift of saturated vapor through upper tiers. They do not wait for rain. They make rain possible by collecting what is already near. In their presence, the air seems to thicken and lean inward. Droplets form along them in ordered lines, tremble, then fall into lower layers, beginning the long descent through living filters. If the world has gardeners, they are not figures with tools; they are these surfaces, endlessly condensing, endlessly passing water downward.

Then there are the stomatal choirs.

They are among the loudest inhabitants, though they make no voice. They are fields of breathing pores spread across broad membranes, high slopes, and vast internal chambers. When they open, the world inhales. When they close, pressure changes ripple through nearby tissues. They can synchronize in ways that seem almost liturgical: thousands upon thousands of pores widening together, drawing cool air down, sending vapor upward, altering the internal weather of whole regions. Their “song” is the hiss and drone of moving air and water under tension. It is their coordination that produces the strange storms inside the landscape. A choir may begin by merely breathing more deeply. Then humidity rises. Then condensation thickens in upper folds. Then a local rain begins—not falling from a sky, but falling from one vegetative tier to another, as if the ceiling of the world has become a cloud and the cloud has become a floor. These choirs are not musical in any gentle sense. Their sound is immense, physical, sometimes oppressive. But they are among the clearest signs of distributed agency in the place. When they act in unison, the world seems to choose something.

There are also the light-tenders.

Because the world is dim, light is precious and strangely managed. The light-tenders are not plants in the simple sense of leaves turned upward toward a sun. They are tissues specialized for capturing, diffusing, and redistributing faint illumination. Some are translucent veins that glow faintly when internal chemistry shifts. Some are broad, olive-dark films that absorb what little brightness penetrates the gloom. Some are pale, almost mineralized surfaces that reflect light downward into deeper layers. They do not compete only by shading one another; they also negotiate brightness through layers, passing it inward, storing it, redirecting it. Their movement is subtle: a slight brightening along a ridge, a blue-green pulse in a mist bank, a slow darkening where tissue thickens. If there is beauty in this place, it is often theirs: cold glows in wet green-gray air, luminous threads trembling with chemical change, the sense that the world is quietly illuminating itself from within.

Among the strangest inhabitants are the resonance bodies.

They are the parts of the world most deeply shaped by sound. The place is deafening, and that deafening noise is not meaningless. Certain tissues have grown into acoustic instruments: hollow stems, taut membranes, water-filled chambers, fibrous ledges that vibrate when pressure changes pass through them. They do not make sound for expression. They make sound because sound is a physical force here. Some resonances encourage stiffening. Others trigger softening and expansion. A low continuous drone can help mineral lattices settle into place. A sharp percussive pulse from a collapsing droplet can cause nearby pores to close. The resonance bodies learn the world through vibration. They also tune it. They may repeat pressure waves until a region’s growth aligns with a particular rhythm. They may dampen excessive noise by thickening their surfaces. They may amplify certain frequencies until mist itself seems to move in patterns. If the world has language, it is not words but recurring pressure motifs, hydraulic chords, vibrations felt through saturated matter. It is a language without grammar and without speakers, but not without meaning.

Then there are the stone-makers.

They are among the most solemn inhabitants because they are the ones turning life into geology. They are not builders in the sense of constructing objects. They are precipitators, binders, mineral guides. Through their living filters, water moves carrying calcium, silica, iron, and other cold crystalline possibilities. As it passes through their tissues, minerals begin to deposit: first as films, then as crusts, then as faint veins, then as something harder and more lasting. These inhabitants are still alive even as they begin to become stone. That is the eerie dignity of them. They are not dead matter being buried. They are living matter becoming world. Some of them may eventually harden beyond all flexibility, leaving behind pale mineral structures that future ages might mistake for ancient rock. But now they are new. They are soft in places. They are still deciding. A ledge that looks like a cliff may be only a first draft. A shimmering grain-bound surface may be the beginning of a mountain, if mountains here ever become mountains in the old sense. The stone-makers do not know that they are making permanence. They only continue the work of binding, layer by layer.

There are also the reclaimers.

No animal decay exists here, so death cannot be imagined as carrion, bones, or scavenging. Instead, when tissue fails, it is returned through saturation, enzymatic softening, and slow dissolution into the living mass. The reclaimers are the regions where this happens most intensely: dense knots of digesting pulp, wet membranes, chemical gradients, and absorptive filaments. They are not grim. They are thorough. They do not tear or devour in the manner of hunger with jaws. They unmake by immersion, by chemistry, by patient envelopment. A weakened growth front may be folded into another. A collapsed chamber may be softened until its stored nutrients seep into surrounding tissue. A failed canopy may be overgrown, dissolved, and reincorporated so completely that no trace of separate identity remains. This is one of the harder aspects of the place for an outside mind to accept. Nothing is wasted, but nothing is spared as an individual either. The reclaimers ensure that all matter remains inside the living system. In their presence, one understands that this world has no outside, no refuse, no abandoned dead. Everything returns.

There are also more fleeting inhabitants: the spore-tides, the mist-blooms, the pressure fronts.

These are not permanent beings. They arise, intensify, and vanish. A spore-tide can thicken the air until the whole region becomes a single cloudy event. While it lasts, it changes humidity, mutes sound, alters chemical exchange between tissues, and may trigger growth responses far away. It is almost like a temporary mood of the world. A mist-bloom may appear when several waterwarden regions release vapor at once, creating a soft luminous bank that drifts slowly through upper folds. A pressure front can move like an invisible wave, making leaves shiver and droplets leap into new paths. These fleeting events are perhaps the nearest thing the place has to quick life. They are born from thresholds. They live for minutes or days. They leave consequences behind: a newly seeded fog, a shifted water route, a hardened surface, a changed resonance.

If one looks for conflict among them, it is there, but without pursuit, flight, or fear.

The drama is botanical and physical. Canopies compete for dim light by rising, folding, broadening, shading. Root-lattices compete for moisture and mineral charge by merging, splitting, diverting flow. Some plant-bodies envelop others and dissolve their boundaries in long enzymatic sieges. There are winners and losers, but the losers are not killed in the animal sense. They are incorporated. Their tissues become part of the victor’s mass. Their stored water is drunk from within. Their fibers are redirected. Their mineral patterns may be overwritten. A region that seems calm may be locked in a slow war of absorption, a war fought in chemistry and pressure over seasons. Yet there is no malice in it, no cruelty. It is simply the logic of a world where all matter must remain alive and where advantage is taken by growth.

Alliances exist too, though not as friendships. Tissues graft. Channels merge. Water is shared when gradients permit. Resonances align. A new mineral vein may stabilize several competing fronts, turning conflict into architecture. Sometimes an entire slope will cease behaving as separate growth and begin acting as a single breathing unit. These are the moments when the world feels most inhabited: not because a face appears, but because an arrangement becomes intentional. A mist bank thins in just the right place. A chord deepens. Water climbs where it is needed. A soft ledge stiffens into a ramp. Something has been coordinated.

If an outsider tried to meet the inhabitants, the encounter would be disorienting.

There would be no one to face. No form would step forward from the green-gray density. No eyes would open in the dark. Instead, the meeting would happen as pressure. The mist would thicken slightly. The sound would change pitch, perhaps dropping into a deeper drone. Surfaces near the intruder might become more attentive—not moving visibly, but altering their texture, their wetness, their temperature. The world would notice in the only way it can: by sensing a disturbance in humidity, heat, mineral balance, or vibration. A warm body would be strange there. It would introduce heat into a realm of sap-cold equilibrium. It would shed salts, gases, microscopic particles. The place would not respond with curiosity as an animal would. It would respond with gradients. Moisture would move toward or away from the heat. Tissues would adjust. If the outsider remained long enough, the world might begin to incorporate them—not with attack, but with deposition, encrustation, soft envelopment. First a film of moisture. Then a faint mineral bloom. Then root-hairs or filamentous threads exploring the foreign surface. Not hatred. Not hunger exactly. Only the world doing what it does: turning everything into more world.

In that sense, the inhabitants may be less like people than like conditions with persistence.

They are the waterwardens who keep the high slopes saturated.  
They are the fog-combers who make rain from near-nothing.  
They are the stomatal choirs whose breathing becomes weather.  
They are the light-tenders who distribute dusk and glow.  
They are the resonance bodies who tune growth with sound.  
They are the stone-makers who begin geology without knowing it.  
They are the reclaimers who allow nothing to be lost.  
They are the spore-tides and pressure fronts that arrive, alter everything, and pass away.  
They are the green continental body itself, dreaming in capillary tension and mineral precipitation.

Yet even that list is too tidy. The place resists being divided into roles. The waterwardens are also part of the stone-makers’ work, because mineral deposition depends on water movement. The stomatal choirs feed the fog-combers by changing humidity. The light-tenders affect the resonance bodies by shifting chemical states. The reclaimers make room for new growth fronts. Everything leans into everything else. There are no clean boundaries, no isolated actors. If one tried to point to a single inhabitant, the finger would sink into living tissue and the tissue would already be part of something else.

Perhaps the truest answer is this: what lives there is a world learning to have a body.

It is not yet finished. Its stone is not fully stone. Its ground is not fully ground. Its weather is not separate from its flesh. Its inhabitants are not separate from its weather. The whole place is in the first age of itself, when biology has not yet become background and geology has not yet become fate. Everything is still negotiable. A surface may remain soft or become crystal. A mist may dissipate or become rain. A tissue may grow, harden, dissolve, or become a new slope. The beings of this place are those negotiations. They are the living arguments by which the world decides what it will become.

And because of that, they do not live in the landscape.

They are the landscape living.
