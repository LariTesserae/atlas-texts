What lives there has been shaped by the tableland’s central fact: there is enough to continue, but not enough to be lavish. As a result, life has become precise, spaced, and functional. It does not fill the landscape so much as time itself to it. The beings of the plateau are less like residents than like operating parts of a vast, cold, slow factory that manufactures only conditions.

The most visible inhabitants are the great grazers, the broad-bodied animals that move across the benches in herds so widely dispersed that a traveler may see only three or four at first and mistake them for scattered boulders. They are low, long, and heavily insulated, built not for speed but for endurance. Their backs are broad enough that fog settles along them in thin ribbons, and their shoulders rise in rounded humps under fur thick with underwool and dusted with mineral powder. Their coats are dark in most lights, but close up they are banded and flecked with gray, rust, and pale stone-colors, so that when they stand still they almost disappear into the ground.

Their feet are wide and padded, with short blunt claws that rarely scrape unless they break crust. They place these feet with a care that seems deliberate beyond animal habit, because the surface is often thin, sharp, or brittle with frost. A healthy adult can stand on a mineral skin that would crack under a narrower weight. When they walk together, the sound is not a stampede but a soft, intermittent pressure: a muted brushing, a faint clicking, a rhythmic settling, like sediment moving in slow water.

They feed with terrible patience. Their mouths are broad and blunt, fitted with rasping plates rather than teeth suited for biting. They crop microbial films, black lichen, algal crusts, and the thin organic felts that form in damp hollows. They do not strip a place clean. They take a little from each patch and move on, because scarcity has trained them into restraint. A herd can pass over a basin and leave it apparently unchanged, though every mouthful has altered the chemical balance of the surface. In this way they function as a kind of living harvest, spreading their pressure across immense distances so that no single patch is destroyed.

They are quiet animals. Their throats are not built for frequent calls. Most of their communication happens through the ground: footfalls, shifts in weight, low pressure pulses that travel through stone and crust. A change in direction often begins as a subtle turn in one part of the herd, then spreads without sound. In charged weather, when the air prickles and the fine hairs along their flanks stand apart, they avoid exposed ridges and gather in shallow depressions where their bodies can ground the static. Some of them carry short, blunt keratin points along the shoulders or spine; in tense air these points gather a faint corona, a dull glow that makes the herd look as though it is slowly burning without heat.

Their young are born near vents or in fog-sheltered basins, wherever the air is a little less harsh and the ground a little less brittle. Calves are coated at first with a waxy film that sheds moisture, and they stand sooner than their size seems to allow. The herd does not fuss over them. It simply arranges itself around them, slow bodies becoming windbreaks, heat sources, and barriers. Predators rarely test a healthy herd, not because the adults fight in dramatic ways but because the herd is too large, too patient, and too difficult to disturb. If a calf is lost, the loss enters the plateau’s wider economy. Nothing is wasted there for long.

Smaller lives depend on the same conditions. The damp hollows and seep-fed pools are nurseries, chemical baths, and feeding stations. Around them live the insects of the plateau, pale and fine-boned, their life cycles tuned not to day and night but to temperature, humidity, pressure, and electrical charge. Their larvae develop under microbial skins and in briny films, processing dead matter, salts, and microscopic growth. Some are translucent worms with frilled gills; some are flat, slow crawlers that cling to the underside of mineral crusts; some live in such crowded layers that the pool bottom seems to breathe.

When emergence comes, it comes in masses. The surface of a pool may tremble, then release a cloud of winged bodies so fine that they look like smoke lifted by no fire. The adults live briefly. They mate in the dim air, lay eggs in the same pools or in moist salt crusts, and die in drifts along the basin edges. Their wings make a sound like distant fingertips rubbing together. During these hatches, the plateau’s silence is not broken but thickened; the air itself seems to acquire a soft, living texture. Other creatures arrive to feed: the grazers open their broad mouths into the drifting clouds, fog-feeders extend filaments into them, small predators wait at the margins, and scavengers gather afterward for what falls.

The fog itself is inhabited. In passes and wind lanes there are drift-feeders: creatures so light and so nearly transparent that they appear to be part of the weather. Some are anchored in colonies, fine filaments rooted in lichen or fissured rock, spreading nets no larger than a hand. Others are loose membranes, thin as meltwater skin, drifting on gradients of temperature and static charge. Others still are trailing bundles of fibers that hang from high points and tremble when the fog moves through them. They collect moisture, spores, charged dust, and microscopic organic particles. They do not sing, buzz, or cry. They only shiver faintly when the air passes through them.

In certain fog events, these drift-feeders become numerous enough to alter the appearance of the landscape. The fog seems woven with pale shapes, as if the air had become a textile. A traveler walking through them may feel a cold touch on the face and find that the air has left a faint mineral film on the skin. The drift-feeders are not aggressive. They are barely distinct from the dampness they inhabit. When they die, their bodies fall as fine gray flakes and become part of the nutrient dust that slowly accumulates in basins.

The cold vents support their own small populations. These vents are not dramatic geysers but slow exhalations of compressed gas and mineral vapor, breathing through cracks in the stone with a rhythm that can be felt if one stands still long enough. Around them live vent limpets with shells crusted by carbonate, pale worms in wet channels, beetles plated with mineral deposits, and small crawling things that feed on chemosynthetic films. Some of them scrape the openings clear, others secrete mucus that traps dust, others build thin shelves by accretion around the edges of the vent. Their activity looks like maintenance, though it is not guided by intention in the human sense. It is simply the living shape of flow.

The vents also draw larger animals. Herds stand near them in deep cold, not for warmth exactly but for the damp air, the mineral smell, and the softening of crust. Scavengers patrol the edges of vent fields, because weakness sometimes finds animals there. Predators may wait downwind, where the vent’s breath carries scent without revealing their position. The vents are meeting points without being centers. They are places where the plateau’s hidden pressure becomes briefly available to living bodies.

Predators are few, but they are exquisitely adapted. The commonest are low, long-bodied hunters, built for stillness rather than speed. They lie beside pools, along fog lanes, or at the edges of grazing routes, their fur mottled with lichen-colors and mineral dust. Their eyes are small and set forward, sensitive less to detail than to movement and to the faint electrical shimmer of living bodies. They can remain motionless for long periods, their breathing shallow, their temperature lowered, their outlines broken by frost and dust. When they strike, the strike is short and efficient. There is no prolonged chase, no drama of pursuit. The plateau does not have energy to waste on spectacle.

Scavengers are more numerous than predators, and more important to the long-term order. They follow the herds at intervals, sometimes visible, sometimes not. There are flat, many-legged crawlers that lift the edges of carcasses and enter from below. There are tiny mites that work hide and tendon into fibers. There are flies whose larvae enter bone cracks and dissolve what larger animals cannot reach. There are dull, slow beetles that feed on dried remnants and carry fragments away into dust. A body that falls is not mourned; it is distributed. Over time, the remains become pale objects arranged by wind and frost, looking almost deliberate, as though the plateau had sorted them into inventory.

The vegetation, though sparse, is also a kind of habitation. The black lichen colonies are among the oldest residents. They grow by fractions, spreading over exposed rock in slow stains. They hold dust, begin soil, and provide the first surface on which other life can lean. Moss gathers in places where wind and moisture reach a compromise, forming small, dense mats that are dry above and coldly damp beneath. Cushion plants press into sheltered cracks, pale and tight, surviving by refusing to rise. Microbial films coat pools, seeps, and vent margins, forming the edible skins that support much of the plateau’s animal life. These films are not beautiful in any ordinary sense. They are dull, slick, sometimes faintly slippery, and often smell of wet mineral and cold decay. Yet they are essential. Without them, the great herds could not exist.

If one asks who lives there in the fullest sense, the answer may include people, though they leave no sign that people usually leave. They are few, and they are not builders. They have no villages, no roads, no stored goods, no permanent shelters. If they are called anything by outsiders, it might be the Interval People, because they live in the intervals between the plateau’s pulses: between fog and clearing, between hatch and silence, between one herd movement and the next.

They are shaped by the same conditions as everything else. Their bodies are compact and patient, with broad feet sensitive to crust and stone, long arms that can rest on the ground without chilling, and hands made for placing rather than grasping. Their faces are sheltered: deep-set eyes under bony ridges, broad noses with internal passages that warm and humidify the thin air, small mouths that lose little heat. Their skin is dark gray-brown or shadow-colored, covered with fine insulating hair that lies flat in dry cold and lifts slightly when the air is charged. They are not easy to see. They do not shine. They absorb the dimness.

They do not build because building is not merely difficult on the plateau; it is wrong to the place. Anything fixed would be worn, buried, corroded, or made irrelevant. Instead, they practice a life of temporary adjustments. They may shift a stone to let condensation run toward a pool. They may break a thin crust to release trapped gas. They may open a passage for fog to settle in a hollow. They may use a sharp flake of frost-cracked rock to cut into a carcass and then leave it where it falls. Their tools, if they can be called tools, are momentary: a bone probe used to test a seep, a stomach membrane used once to carry water, a cord of twisted fiber tied and then untied, a mantle of shed herd underfur worn until it falls apart. They do not accumulate objects. To accumulate would be to create a burden and a mark.

Their knowledge is not stored in things but in timing. They know when a basin is likely to fog, when a pool will skin over with ice, when a vent’s breath will strengthen, when insects are likely to emerge, when a storm cell will split around a ridge. They navigate by pressure, scent, ground texture, and memory. Their calendars are bodily and seasonal rather than written. They remember routes as sequences of sensations: the taste of mineral air before a vent, the feel of charged dust on the skin, the sound of crust under a certain wind, the way fog thins at a particular slope.

Their language is quiet. Much of it is below easy hearing: foot-taps, heel rhythms, pressure pulses through stone, hand signs made at close range in fog. When they vocalize, it is rarely more than a low hum felt in the chest. They do not waste breath on distance. Names among them are not permanent in the way lowland names are permanent. A person may be known by the condition in which they are met: Fog-Under-Left-Foot, Salt-on-Breath, Stillness-at-the-Pool, Charge-in-the-Shoulder. Such names are not possessions so much as events.

They live in small bands when food is scarce and gather briefly when the plateau produces one of its surges. An insect hatch, a vent opening, a rare season of stable fog, a herd movement toward a basin—any of these can bring them together. Their gatherings are not loud. They touch palms to discharge static, press foreheads to stone to share vibration, and make patterns with their feet that are erased by the next weather. They do not dance for display. They coordinate. They remember. They decide where pressure should go next.

They are not kind in a simple sense, nor cruel. They are careful. If a traveler enters the plateau, the Interval People may watch for a long time without revealing themselves. They will notice how the traveler steps, whether they break crust unnecessarily, whether they drink from a pool without disturbing its skin, whether they make noise that wastes heat and attention. If the traveler is clumsy, they vanish. If the traveler is patient, they may appear at the edge of visibility, standing so still that they could be mistaken for stones.

They do not offer permanent gifts. They may offer a cupped handful of clean fog-water, a warm pebble from near a vent, a pinch of salt, or a gesture indicating where to stand. They may walk beside a stranger for a day and leave no footprints that matter. They do not expect gratitude. Gratitude would imply a debt that lasts, and they do not make lasting debts. They expect only that the visitor learns not to disturb the balance more than necessary.

Their relation to the animals is not ownership. They do not herd the terracebacks in the way herders elsewhere herd livestock. They attend them. They may move near a herd to discourage a predator from taking a weak animal too soon, or they may open a path toward a fog-fed basin when the herd’s own memory is uncertain. They may eat insects during hatches, take marrow from scavenged bones, gather microbial films in careful strips, or lick mineral condensation from stone. They take what can be used immediately or carried in the body. They do not store surplus beyond the needs of the next interval.

In this way, they are the plateau’s most conscious expression, but not its masters. They understand themselves as part of its processes. They do not say, “This land belongs to us.” They act as though they belong to the land’s schedule. Their highest skill is to have passed without evidence. Their deepest error would be to make a mark that outlasts its need.

Yet the plateau is also inhabited by things that are not people, not animals, and not plants in any simple sense. The microbial films are living. The lichen colonies are living. The cold bacteria in the seeps are living. The chemosynthetic mats around vents are living. They are the smallest citizens and in many ways the most important. They convert mineral moisture and faint energies into edible surfaces. They respond to charge, temperature, and chemical shifts. During electrical episodes, some release spores in fine clouds, feeding drift-feeders and adding to the plateau’s slow circulation of organic matter. Without them, the larger life would be impossible.

Even the absence of life is a kind of habitation. The empty stretches are not dead. They are intervals. They allow the herds to spread their pressure. They allow predators to remain hidden until the moment is right. They allow fog to move without obstruction. They allow sound to dissipate instead of accumulating. The emptiness is part of the system’s spacing. A crowded plateau would collapse under its own demand. A sparse plateau can continue.

To encounter the life there is to understand that it does not perform for observers. A herd will not turn to look with curiosity. A predator will not announce itself. A fog lane will not part dramatically. An insect hatch will not arrive with bright wings and song. Instead, the traveler becomes aware gradually: first of a shape that may be animal, then of breath, then of a slow shift in the ground, then of the fact that the stillness is populated. The more one looks, the more one sees that the landscape is full of function. Every patch of lichen is a territory of slow growth. Every pool is an appointment. Every vent is a valve. Every fog bank is a feeding corridor. Every carcass is a factory floor for decomposition.

The beings that live there are not opposed to the industrial character of the place; they are its living machinery. The herds are grazing pressure. The insects are batch production. The fog-feeders are atmospheric filters. The vent creatures are maintenance staff. The predators are regulators. The scavengers are cleanup crews. The microbes are chemical processors. The Interval People, if they choose to be understood at all, are the intelligence that participates without possessing.

Nothing there is built, but everything is done. The plateau manufactures conditions: dampness, charge, nutrient films, mineral crusts, silence, spacing, patience. Its inhabitants manufacture nothing that lasts except continuity. They are the workforce of an unmanned industry, the staff of a factory without tools, the citizens of a country without roads.

Whoever lives there lives by restraint. Whatever lives there lives by timing. The place is not empty. It is spaced. It is not silent because nothing speaks. It is silent because speech, heat, and motion are too valuable to waste. Its life is immense, dim, cold, and overwhelmingly distributed: a high-altitude ecology operating with the regularity of machinery and the patience of stone.
