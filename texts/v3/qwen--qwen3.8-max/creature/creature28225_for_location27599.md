What lives there is plural first.

If you ask for the inhabitant of that high marsh, the place does not answer with a single face. It answers with films, fibers, pulses, breaths, pressures, and slow moving bodies. It answers with the countless lives that have become continuous with the wetness. Nothing there lives as a tenant in a landscape. Everything lives as part of the landscape’s own working flesh.

The smallest residents are the first citizens. They are not easily seen as individuals because they exist as skins, mats, threads, and living varnishes. Microbial films coat the stones, the submerged stems, the inner walls of bubble-chambers, and the upper surfaces of peat where water holds a glossy stillness. These films are thin, often darker than the surfaces they cover, sometimes black-green, sometimes rust-brown, sometimes milky where mineral water seeps through them. They are the oldest workers of the plateau. They taste the water, bind fine sediment, release gases, fix minerals, and begin the long digestion of dead matter. They are not conscious in any way a dry-land creature would recognize, but they are responsive.
