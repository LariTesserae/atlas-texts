The emptiness is part of the inhabitant.

What lives in the high hollow does not live as a single tenant lives in a room. It lives as a chord lives inside an instrument: distributed, resonant, impossible to seize without stopping the very thing that makes it real. If you look for one creature there, you will miss the population. If you look for a population, you may suddenly feel that the whole hollow is one body, and that all its small lives are only the motions of its thinking.

The first inhabitants seem to be the waters.

Not water as a substance, but water as a kind of slow animal. The weeping threads on the cliffs have moods. They gather with a deliberateness that is not quite intention, yet not quite accident either. They bead along fracture lines, pause at mineral lips, lengthen, tremble, and fall. When they fall, they do not seem to obey gravity alone; they seem to obey a schedule. Each drop arrives where the hollow expects it. The marsh-pool receives them without rising. The wet stone accepts them without shining any brighter. The air takes back what it can in mist. This circulation is the first life of the place: a wetness that behaves like breath.

Within that breath are smaller lives.

Midges hang above the marsh in stationary clouds, but they are not resting. They are suspended in the charged air as if caught in invisible standing waves. Their wings are almost too fine to be seen, and when they catch the blue-white pulses from the quartz veins they flash briefly, like a field of cold stars brought down to knee height. They do not swarm at random. They hold positions. They form thin constellations, dissolve, and reform. When an electric arc passes through the moss, some of them turn their bodies toward it, and for a moment each one becomes a tiny lens, holding the light before releasing it again. They feed on droplets that taste of iron and ozone, and on the faint luminescence that gathers in the pitcher-shaped growths at the edges of the sedge.

Beetles move across the moss cushions like living rivets. Their shells are dark, polished, and faintly conductive. Where they walk, their legs make small metallic sounds, not because the moss is metal, but because the hollow is so finely tuned that even a beetle’s weight can set a filament ringing. They are scavengers of charge. They consume spent sparks, oxidized flakes of light, dead static, the residue left where an arc has passed through root or stone. When two beetles meet, they touch antennae, and a small blue ring expands between them, then collapses. Neither seems alarmed. The exchange is ordinary here, a greeting or a transfer or perhaps both.

In the black pool live transparent worms, visible only when a pulse moves through the water. Then they appear as thin bright lines, dozens or hundreds of them, drifting in slow helixes beneath the surface. They are not swimming so much as being conducted. Their bodies seem made of the pool’s own clarity, and when the light fails they vanish again into the dark mirror. They may be larvae. They may be organs. They may be the pool’s way of remembering itself. When one of them touches the surface film, the film brightens and sends a ripple outward, though the ripple often travels in the wrong direction, inward instead of outward, as if the pool prefers to keep its events folded.

Frogs sit on the wet ledges with their throats flickering pale blue. Each breath draws a small spark from the air, and each spark is swallowed into the wet machinery of their bodies. Their calls are not loud in themselves, but the cirque amplifies them until they become part of the sustaining chord. They do not call to one another in any simple sense. They call into the hollow, and the hollow answers by adjusting itself. A frog croaks, and somewhere a thread of water changes direction. A frog blinks, and a cluster of spore-caps releases vapor. A frog leaps, and the splash may arrive before or after the body enters the water, as if the pool is deciding which part of the event it wishes to keep.

The vegetation is inhabited too, and perhaps is itself a kind of fauna.

The moss cushions are dense little cities without streets. Inside them, mites and springtails move through green corridors no wider than a hair. They do not build, but they clear, carry, pause, and release. Their work leaves no trace, which is exactly what the hollow requires. The black-green ferns with short stiff fronds host tiny beetles that feed on condensation alone. The liverworts, like wet copper leaves, shelter pale insects whose bodies are so swollen with water that they seem ready to burst, yet never do. When one of these insects dies, if death is even the right word, it does not rot. It softens into a bright slime that the moss drinks, and a few moments later the same small shape may be seen again, moving along another frond, unchanged.

The pitcher-shaped growths are among the most alive of all the presences. They are not quite plants, not quite animals, and not quite lamps. Their pale bodies stand in clusters on the shelves of rock, each one filled with a faintly luminous fluid. The fluid hums when insects approach. It does not lure them to drown. It listens to them. When a midge lands on the rim, the fluid brightens, and the insect’s wings quiver as if receiving a message. Sometimes the insect leans down and drinks. Sometimes it simply sits there, vibrating, while the pitcher’s glow deepens. Then the insect flies away, or appears to fly away, though sometimes it dissolves into a shimmer of spores that rise and become another insect in the same lane of air. The pitchers fill and empty, fill and empty, yet the marsh-pool remains exact. They are reservoirs without storage, vessels without ownership.

Below all these visible lives is the fungal web.

It is not seen directly except as pale threads lacing the moss, but it is one of the hollow’s true inhabitants. It behaves like switchgear, like memory, like a nervous system spread over stone. When a beetle lands on a certain knot of mycelium, a circuit closes and a cluster of spore-caps hisses pale vapor into the air. When the rock’s charge rises, the fungal threads route it away from tender roots and send it along insulated black cords of dwarf-shrub bark. When the stone breathes, the mycelium tightens and loosens in answer. It does not accumulate. It does not command. It distributes. It is the hidden population that makes the others possible, and yet it has no center. If you were to tear it open, you would find no heart, no nest, no queen. You would find only more threads, more wetness, more connections.

The birds are few, and all the more noticeable for that.

They are dark, dipper-like creatures with slate-colored plumage and eyes that seem to hold a thin silver film. They walk along the submerged edges of the pool without haste, dipping their heads, pecking at sparks as if they were larvae. They do not seem to mind the cold. They do not seem to mind the water. Sometimes they vanish beneath the surface and reappear on the far side of the marsh without any visible act of swimming, as though the pool has merely passed them along. Their calls arrive late. A bird may open its beak and nothing comes; then, a moment later, the sound ticks out of the opposite wall. Sometimes the bird itself seems delayed, a dark afterimage moving just behind its own outline.

They make no nests. Nothing there makes a nest. When they rest, they settle into hollows in the moss that seem to have been waiting for them. Perhaps the moss grows into their shapes over repeated use. Perhaps their shapes were always there. The larger wings that move above the rim of the hollow rarely descend. They are silhouettes in the cloud, broad and slow, more like ideas of birds than birds. Their cries fall into the cirque after the shapes have already turned, as if even the sky above the hollow cannot keep its events in the proper order.

Then there are the eels, or the things like eels, that live between water and air.

They are long, translucent, and unhurried. They move through the thickest mist as though it were a current, swimming in slow loops around the weeping cliffs. Their bodies catch the blue charge until their spines show like thin lamps, one after another, a procession of cold light in the grey damp. They never touch the ground. They never seem to hunt. They do not open their mouths in any visible feeding, yet they are not empty. They are part of the circulation. As they swim through the saturated air, droplets gather along their flanks and are carried upward, released near fissures where the stone draws breath. They are weather-workers. They are living conduits. Sometimes two of them pass through one another and become a single brighter current for a few heartbeats before separating again, if separation is what happens.

The cliffs themselves are inhabitants.

This is harder to accept, but the hollow insists upon it. The young rock is not merely a setting. It is active. It breathes through fissures with a slow piston rhythm. It expands and contracts under pressures that have no visible source. The sound is loud and deep, a periodic hush that underlies all other sounds, and once you hear it you cannot stop hearing it. The mica vanes turn in natural flumes, split into near-perfect fins by the stone’s own geometry, and as they turn, quartz veins brighten with brief blue-white flashes. The cliffs are not dead matter waiting to be shaped. They are the slowest animals in the hollow, and their bodies are mineral, pressure, and charge.

The fresh faces of the rock seem sensitive. They taste the air. They register mist. They do not weather in the ordinary way because erosion has been folded back into the system. A crack opens, and instead of widening into ruin it becomes a new chamber for breath. A ledge sweats, and instead of dissolving it becomes a surface for moss. The stone smells of cold iron, wet flint, and ozone, and that smell is not merely mineral. It is the smell of operation. The hollow is working. The cliffs are part of the work.

The pool hides another population.

At first it shows only the dim ceiling of cloud, the wet cliffs, the black mirror of itself. But if you look too long, the reflection begins to disagree with the hollow above it. The cliffs appear taller. The vegetation appears denser. The air seems full of moving shapes. There are lattices of root and stone turning in slow arrays, chambers of light opening and closing, processions of creatures moving along synchronized routes. These are not reflections of things that exist elsewhere. They are the hollow’s inhabitants seen in their full relation. Above, you see only isolated presences: a beetle, a frog, a thread of water, a bird. Below, in the water, you see how they are joined.

The reflection-dwellers move with a precision that the surface does not openly display. They are not more real than the beetles or the mist-eels; they are the same reality made visible all at once. Some of them appear almost architectural, like living machines built from sedge roots, quartz light, and fungal wire. Some are small and quick, darting between the reflected cliffs. Some are vast and slow, turning in places where no wall should allow them to turn. If you touch the water, the image breaks into rings, but it does not scatter. The rings return, and the reflection reassembles itself with exactness. Sometimes, just before it settles, one of the shapes seems to look back. It has no face. It has only attention.

Nothing made can live there, and so the inhabitants do not make.

They do not build dams, channels, nests, tools, shrines, or stores. They do not accumulate. They produce, but their production is repetition, not inventory. The hollow manufactures larvae, sparks, mist, cold light, calls, and the same set of motions again. It outputs continuity. Every living thing participates in this. The beetles carry charge from one node to another. The midges hold patterns in the air.
