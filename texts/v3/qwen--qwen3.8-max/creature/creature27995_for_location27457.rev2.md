What lives there is not, first of all, a people in the ordinary animal sense. The settlement is already alive before its residents appear. It lives as a green and engineered body: roots as foundations, channels as veins, pumps as organs, bark walls as skin, canopy as breath. The engines give it a pulse. The water gives it a circulation. The light moving through the leaves gives it a slow, shifting mood.

But if you ask who moves within that body, who tends it and speaks through it and is changed by it, then the answer becomes stranger and more particular.

The inhabitants are plant-born, plant-built, and plant-maintained.

They are not trees pretending to be people. They are not animals disguised as shrubs. They are something cultivated over many generations into a shape that can carry tools, climb ladders, listen to pipes, open valves, splice cables, and prune the living walls that shelter them. They are made of bundled stems, tough bark plates, living root-hands, sap-dark joints, and carefully trained fibrous muscle. Where a human body might have skin, they have smooth, weathered bark or woven stem-sheaths. Where a human might have hair, they may have moss, fine vine-tendrils, seed-threads, or caps of softened lichen kept trimmed and neat. Where a human might have eyes, they often have polished seeds, dark glass insulators, amber beads of hardened sap, or small ceramic lenses set into living tissue.

At a distance, some of them look almost humanoid. Up close, they are not.

Their shoulders may be too broad because they are grown around internal frames of bentwood and iron. Their arms may branch at the wrist into several finer digits, some meant for gripping, some for delicate pruning, some for feeling the vibration of a pump. Their legs may not end in feet so much as root-clusters that can spread for balance, grip wet planks, or settle into socket-hollows when they are resting or drawing moisture from the ground. Some walk with a slow, deliberate creak. Others move with the soft rustle of leaves being turned in wind.

They are warm to the touch, especially those who work near boilers or engines. Their bodies hold heat the way damp wood holds heat: not sharply, but persistently. If you stood very near one, you would smell green sap, tannin, wet bark, machine oil, and sometimes the faint mineral sharpness of steam. They do not sweat as animals do, but their surfaces may bead with condensation, or a thin bright line of sap may appear where a joint has been strained.

They do not have a single name for themselves that translates easily. In their own language, which is made of knocking on hollow stems, pressure changes in pipes, the opening of valve-throats, the rustle of trained leaves, and short bursts of steam, the closest meaning is something like “those who keep the water moving.” Outsiders, if any ever come, usually call them graftfolk, rootwrights, green-tenders, or the maintained. They accept these names without much offense, because names are not very important to them. Patterns are more important. Maintenance is more important. A well-cut graft is more important than a word.

They are not born. They are taken, chosen, shaped, and continued.

A new inhabitant may begin as a cutting from an elder, a seedling raised in a warm nursery pool, or a bundle of compatible stems trained around a frame until they grow into a single load-bearing body. The settlement has nurseries in its lower galleries: shallow troughs where young root-bundles learn to grip, where first limbs are tied around smooth wooden forms, where tiny valve-ports are fitted while the plant flesh is still supple. There is no infancy in the animal sense. There is training. There is selection. There is the long patient work of deciding what a young growth will become.

Some are shaped for strength: broad-backed rootwardens who stand in the lowest levels and judge whether a platform root can bear another gearbox. Some are shaped for agility: cane-runners and platform-tenders who move quickly along walkways, carrying tools, messages, spare clamps, and oilers. Some are shaped for wet work: channel-keepers with long, flexible limbs and water-shedding bark who wade into culverts and listen to the flow. Some are shaped for heat: boiler-tenders with thick, darkened outer bark, ceramic plates grown or strapped over their chests, and careful, slow hands that know how to feed a furnace without letting danger spread.

Their bodies are repaired the way the settlement itself is repaired: by grafting, binding, bracing, and replacing. If an arm is torn by a snapped cable, it may be cut back cleanly and a new limb trained into place over months. If a chest plate is cracked, a patch of living bark may be grafted over it, held with iron clips until the union takes. If a root-foot is crushed, the injured part may be removed and the individual fitted with a carved wooden support until new growth can be guided. They do not fear repair. They fear rot, dryness, fire, and neglected blockage more than they fear wounds.

They do not all look alike. In fact, their forms reveal their histories.

An old pump-tender may have copper fittings grown half-submerged into their forearms, the metal no longer attached so much as incorporated, with bark ridges swelling around bolt heads. A cable-keeper may have glass beads and ceramic rings threaded through flexible tendrils, both as insulation and as markers of skill. A pruner may carry living hooks in their own body, shaped since youth into curved cutting edges, kept sharp with stone and oil. A water-listener may have hollow reed-chambers in their throat and chest, so that when they breathe or speak, the sound resembles water passing through narrow places.

They wear things, but not much. They are not naked in the animal sense, because their bodies already carry layers: bark, woven stem-sheaths, moss, protective plates, tool harnesses. Still, they add what they need. Tool belts made of woven bast fiber. Rubber pouches for grease and resin. Shoulder slings for wrenches, splicing knives, glass fuses, and pressure gauges. Oiled canvas wraps for wet work. Brass rings used as fasteners, not jewelry. Small ceramic tokens hung near the heart-wood, each one carved with the pattern of a first graft. They do not dress for modesty. They dress for use, safety, and memory.

Their faces, when they have anything so simple as a face, are often calm and masklike. They may have carved-looking features formed from bark folds, knot-swirls, and polished eye-beads. Some have mouths or mouth-like openings, but these are usually valve-slits, reed-throats, or breathing gaps lined with fibrous mesh. They do not need to eat as animals do. They absorb nutrients through root-baths, through mineral-rich water channels, through carefully prepared sap flows, and sometimes through warm compost beds where spent plant matter has been broken down into dark, tannin-rich soil. Sunlight matters to them, but not as simple food. It is more like a condition of health. They seek sun patches when they are sluggish, and shade when their outer bark dries too much. Engine heat is also nourishing in a secondary way: it keeps their joints supple and their growth steady.

They speak, but not often with words that an outsider would recognize.

Their true language is mechanical and botanical at once. A short knock on a pipe may mean “pressure rising.” Three soft taps on a root-floor may mean “hold still.” A hiss through a throat-valve may mean agreement, or warning, or the beginning of a question. A fluttering of leaf-shutters on the shoulder may express irritation, readiness, or delight, depending on the rhythm. They can shape crude vocal sounds if they must, especially when dealing with visitors or with ancient instructions recorded on wax cylinders or punched bark-sheets, but they prefer languages that travel through solid things.

Because the settlement is loud, their speech has adapted to noise. They do not shout over the engines. They use vibration. They place a hand on a pipe and send a pattern through it. They press a foot to a root platform and feel the answer rise through the floor. In the deepest pump houses, where the thump of machinery is constant, two inhabitants may stand perfectly still, one palm on a shared beam, and hold a long conversation that to an outsider would feel like silence.

When they gather, the sound is like the settlement tuning itself. Wood knocks. Water gurgles. Steam sighs. Belts slap. Leaves whisper overhead. A valve opens somewhere and closes somewhere else. No one seems to be speaking, yet decisions are being made.

They are patient, but not emotionless.

Their feelings are expressed through care. If one tends another, they will clean a joint, oil a rusted hinge, remove moss from an electrical fitting, or smooth a cracked edge with a warm resin paste. Affection is shown by maintenance. Trust is shown by allowing someone else to prune you. Grief is shown by keeping a broken tool or a dead cutting in a wall-niche until it can be properly composted or grafted into new growth. Anger is rare among them, or at least rarely visible. When it appears, it tends to be a tightening: valves closing, limbs folding inward, bark plates shifting with a dry creak. They do not scream. They go still. Then they work.

They are not gentle in the soft sense. They live in a place of heavy machinery, boiling water, sharp tools, wet heights, and constant noise. They are practical. If a growth is diseased, they cut it. If a pipe is leaking onto a cable run, they move it. If a young stem is growing into a dangerous place, they tie it back without sentiment. But their practicality is not cruelty. They understand that every living part of the settlement is also someone’s body, someone’s ancestor, someone’s continuing shape. They cut carefully. They ask permission from roots before major work, not because the roots answer in words, but because the asking forces them to plan. They mark a cut with chalk, oil, or a tied leaf before making it. They return later with grafting tools and resin. Nothing important is done in haste.

Their settlement has rules, and they embody those rules.

Water must move. They treat blocked channels the way a doctor treats a failing pulse. A clogged culvert is not merely an inconvenience; it is a threat to foundations, to dry storage, to electrical safety, to the health of load-bearing roots. Channel-keepers spend long shifts clearing leaf-fall, trimming reeds, scooping black silt from low places, and testing flow with floating seeds. They know the sound of healthy water. They can tell by the pitch of a gurgle whether a pipe is half-full, choked, or about to overflow.

Roots must not be killed carelessly. Before any major repair, rootwardens map the affected structure by touch. They press their hands to buttresses and close their eye-beads while feeling for the deep pull of tension. They know which roots carry roofs, which carry walkways, and which have grown around old ironwork so thoroughly that removing them would shift an entire wall. If a root must be cut, it is cut clean, treated, and often replaced by a living graft trained along a new path.

Machines must allow for growth. They design nothing as final. Bolts are left with room. Brackets are shaped so bark can swell without cracking them. Pipes have loops and slack sections. Engine mounts are built from layered root platforms that can be raised over time. The inhabitants do not see this as imperfection. They see it as respect for living material. A machine that refuses growth will eventually tear its host. A host that refuses machinery will eventually lack the means to move water, generate heat, or lift heavy loads. Both must yield.

Heat must be contained. They are not afraid of fire in a panicked way, but they treat it with absolute seriousness. Boilers are housed in metal-lined chambers surrounded by wet barriers. Flues are cleaned constantly. Fuel is only dead plant matter: pruned branches, spent fiber, dried husks, fallen bark, and compressed leaf-cake stored in dry bins. No one carries open flame through the lanes. Lamps are enclosed. Furnaces have keepers. Fire drills are practiced with water pumps and damp sack-lines, though the word “drill” is too brisk for them. They call it “remembering the burn.”

Electricity must stay dry. Cable-keepers are among the most careful of all inhabitants. They climb through the upper galleries with ceramic tools, glass spools, and rubber wraps. They know every drip-point, every condensation line, every place where a leaf joint may fail and send water down onto a terminal. They reroute cables before storms. They replace cracked insulators. They train small overhanging leaves into living rain-hoods. When they work, they move slowly and never with damp hands, unless those hands are specially insulated with living resin or dry bark sheaths.

Sound is accepted. They do not wish for quiet. Quiet would mean a stopped pump, a dry channel, a dead engine, a failure somewhere. The constant noise of the settlement is, to them, proof of continuation. Young ones are taught to sleep through thumps and hisses. Lovers, if they form bonds, may choose resting places near a steady dynamo because the hum feels like health. Elders who settle into walls often ask to be placed near machinery, because the rhythm keeps them aware.

Change is slow. This is perhaps the deepest truth of them.

They do not experience time as animals do. They notice seasons, rainfall, light angles, pump cycles, growth spurts, and the slow thickening of stems. Their memories are stored not only in their own tissues but in the settlement itself. A scar on a walkway may remember a fallen branch. A welded patch on a pipe may remember a burst from three generations ago. A root platform may remember the weight of an old engine long after that engine has been moved. They read these marks the way another people might read books.

They have histories, but not many written ones. Their histories are structural.

If you ask them about the past, they may take you to a wall. They will place your hand on a place where bark has healed over an old iron sign. They will guide your fingers along a groove worn by thousands of passing hands. They will tap a pipe and let you hear the difference between an older section and a newer graft. They will show you a pump whose base has grown around it until the machine looks as if it sprouted there. That is their archive. Not paper, not stone, but continuing use.

They venerate age, but not by preserving it unchanged. Age is honored by being incorporated.

When an inhabitant becomes too old to move easily, they do not simply die in a private room. They may request settlement. This is not considered sad. It is considered a continuation. The elder is taken to a place where the settlement needs strength: a weak wall, a new platform, a pump mount, a stair-support, a handrail along a dangerous drop. Their body is prepared, pruned, fed, and guided into the structure. Over months or years, they become part of the architecture. Their awareness does not vanish. It slows and spreads. They become less like a single person and more like a presence in a particular place. People still speak to them. They still touch them when passing. A root-elder embedded in a stair may answer with a faint creak underfoot. A pump-mother grown around an engine casing may answer with a change in warmth. A wall-keeper may answer by shifting moss growth in a pattern that trained readers can interpret.

This is why the settlement feels crowded by presences rather than by people.

There are not many inhabitants moving at any one time, because the place is small and its population is steady. You might see a dozen figures in the lower lanes: a rootwarden inspecting a swollen platform, two channel-keepers lifting a grate, a cable-keeper ascending a ladder with a coil of insulated wire, a boiler-tender carrying a basket of dry fuel. You might hear others above, among the galleries, but not see them except as shadows moving behind leaf screens. Yet everywhere you look, something that was once a person, or is still partly a person, is holding the place together.

The settlement also contains lesser lives, but they are few.

Fauna is rare, and the inhabitants do not try to increase it. They do not hate animals, but animal life feels abrupt to them: too quick, too hungry, too wet in the wrong way, too likely to disturb delicate growth or foul a water channel. Birds seldom enter; when one does, it panics under the canopy and beats against leaf screens until it finds a way out. The inhabitants watch it with mild pity and do not chase it. Insects exist but are managed. Beetles under bark panels are tolerated unless they bore into structural stems. Snails on wet root steps are moved carefully to outer channels. Mosquitoes are discouraged by moving water, by small fish in cisterns, and by certain aromatic plants grown near sleeping niches.

There are pale moths that gather near lamps at night. The inhabitants do not kill them. They consider them indicators: if too many moths gather near a particular fitting, it may mean the lamp is running hot or the insulation is giving off a smell that needs checking. In the deeper channels there are blind fish and amphibians, rarely seen but sometimes heard as soft splashes when a keeper wades past. Their presence is a good sign. It means the water is not poisoned. It means the flow is stable. It means the lowest roots are not rotting.

Flesh-born people are even rarer.

Occasionally an outsider may come: a traveler, a trader, an inspector from some other kind of place. Such visitors usually find the settlement overwhelming. The air is too thick. The noise is too constant. The ground feels alive underfoot. The inhabitants seem too still, too quiet, too patient. The outsiders speak too loudly and move too fast. They startle the moss. They drip oil where it should not go. They ask questions expecting quick answers. The inhabitants are courteous to them, offering dry resting platforms, warm tea made from steeped bark and sweet sap, and careful guidance along safe lanes. But they do not invite them to stay long. The settlement is not suited to animal haste.

Some outsiders claim they have seen human-faced figures in the oldest parts of the ward: faces pressed into bark, eyes like clouded glass, mouths frozen in the shape of words. The inhabitants do not deny this. They say those are not dead people. They are memories that grew too strong to remain separate. Whether that is a comfort or a warning depends on the listener.

The machines, too, are lived in.

Not in the same way as the plant-people, but they are not dead objects either. A pump that has run for decades acquires a character. Its rhythm becomes known. Its wear points become familiar. Its sounds become part of the settlement’s speech. The inhabitants speak of engines as one might speak of large, slow, reliable beasts, though they know better than to call them animals. “The east pump is tired today,” they may say, meaning its pressure is low. “The north boiler is proud,” meaning it runs hot and needs careful feeding. “The little dynamo is learning,” meaning it has been newly fitted into a living frame and the surrounding roots are still adjusting to its vibration.

Old machines are not thrown away if they can still hold shape. A decommissioned engine may become the base for a new walkway. A broken gearbox may be opened, cleaned, and allowed to become a trellis for fine stems that need support. A rusted boiler shell may be filled with water-loving plants and turned into a reservoir. The inhabitants do not see this as degradation. They see it as retirement into usefulness.

Their shrines are pumps.

Not because pumps are gods. Nothing in the settlement demands worship in the ordinary sense. But pumps are vital organs. If a pump fails, water stops. If water stops, rot or drought follows. If rot or drought follows, the living architecture weakens. So the great pumps are kept in small plazas where their sound can be heard clearly. They are cleaned, polished, painted, and decorated with hanging leaves, braided fibers, and little brass fittings. The inhabitants leave offerings there, but the offerings are practical: a cup of clean oil, a new washer, a polished stone placed where it will not obstruct, a trimmed sprig from a healthy parent plant. To tend a pump is a form of reverence. To listen to a pump is a form of prayer.

Their festivals are tied to water and growth.

After a long rain, when the whole settlement weeps from eaves and cables and leaf joints, they gather in the lowest safe plaza to check the overflow system. This is not celebration in the cheerful sense. It is a shared act of attention. They stand in the mist and listen to the channels fill. They signal one another through steam whistles and tapped rails. When the water moves correctly, when no channel backs up and no root platform softens too much, they open certain valves in sequence and let the settlement sing. The sound is astonishing: water rushing, engines thumping, leaves hissing, hollow pipes humming, metal creaking in a vast slow chord. It is loud enough to feel in the chest. It is their version of joy.

At the first warm dry spell of the season, they hold a pruning rite. They walk the lanes with sharpened tools and inspect every vine, every cable-run, every roof-leaf, every living wall. They cut back what has grown too far. They tie up what has sagged. They replace brittle shingles and clean sun-screens. The cuttings are not discarded. They are carried to nurseries or compost beds. The best cuttings are marked for future bodies. The worst are burned in controlled furnaces, and their heat is used to warm the nursery pools. Nothing leaves the cycle without purpose.

Their children, if one can call them that, are strange and quiet by ordinary standards.

Young growths are kept in warm shallow beds where their limbs are trained around frames. They learn by repetition and gentle constraint. A young channel-keeper may spend a year simply learning how to stand in moving water without disturbing silt. A young cable-keeper may practice climbing wet trunks with insulated pads until the motion becomes instinct. A young rootwarden may spend seasons touching different roots and learning to identify load, stress, rot, and healthy tension. They are not forced. They are guided. If a growth shows a tendency toward a certain shape, that shape is encouraged. If it resists, it may be reassigned. Identity, for them, is not a fixed essence. It is a pattern that can be adjusted, as long as the adjustment is not cruel.

They play, but their play looks like work.

They build small waterwheels from leaves and pins. They race floating seeds through gutter-channels. They tap rhythms on pipes and try to make complex echoes. They climb to high platforms and practice balancing while the canopy sways. They graft tiny decorative knots into non-structural bark, then later undo them so as not to leave bad scars. They tell stories by arranging cuttings in trays, showing how an ancestor grew into a wall, or how a great pump was saved from flood. Their games teach maintenance. Their toys become tools. Their imagination is rooted in consequence.

They are not without conflict.

There are disagreements about growth. A rootwarden may insist that a platform must be reinforced, while a pruner may argue that too much reinforcement will choke a drainage line. A boiler-tender may demand more dried fuel, while a canopy-keeper may refuse to allow additional pruning during a dry season. A cable-keeper may want a vine removed entirely, while a wall-tender may say the vine protects the bark from heat. These disputes are settled slowly, often by observation. They test. They measure. They wait. They return after rain or after a pump cycle and see what has happened. If someone was wrong, the wrongness is recorded in the structure: a warped board, a stained wall, a softened step. No one enjoys being wrong, but no one can argue long with a leaking roof.

There are also dangers.

Rot is the enemy beneath all enemies. It enters quietly. It hides inside roots. It softens load-bearing joints until a platform gives way without warning. The inhabitants fear rot more than fire, because fire announces itself and rot does not. They have listeners whose only task is to press their faces to wood and breathe in the smell of hidden decay. They use long probes, polished and marked with notches. They keep quarantine zones where diseased stems are cut and burned. They seal wounds with resin. They improve drainage endlessly.

Fire is the second enemy. It is useful, even necessary, but it must stay in its chambers. When an alarm sounds for fire, the settlement changes at once. Valves open. Pumps shift to high flow. Wet sacks are pulled from wall niches. Sand and compost bins are carried along walkways. The inhabitants do not run wildly; they move with terrible focus. They know exactly where the water is. They know exactly which cables must be isolated. They know which walls can be sacrificed and which cannot. Afterward, if the fire was contained, they stand in the blackened place and say nothing for a long time. Then they begin cleaning.

The third enemy is haste.

Haste causes bad cuts. Haste leaves bolts overtightened. Haste lets a vine grow into a belt path. Haste lets a drip point form over an electrical box. Haste makes outsiders stumble and break young shoots. The inhabitants train themselves against it. They move at the speed of care. When they must act quickly, they do so with practiced precision, not panic. Their greatest skill is knowing when speed is needed and when speed would be violence.

They are, in their way, kind.

Not soft. Not sentimental. Not innocent. But kind in the sense that they understand dependence. No one survives alone in the settlement. The root needs the water. The water needs the pump. The pump needs the engine. The engine needs the fuel. The fuel needs the pruning. The pruning needs the hand. The hand needs the body. The body needs the root. Everything is held by something else. They know this so deeply that they rarely speak of independence as a virtue. Their virtue is reliability. To be dependable is to be loved. To keep your assigned rhythm is to honor the whole.

They have art, but it is subtle.

They shape bark into patterns that heal into smooth ridges. They train leaves to open in sequence so that light falls in moving shapes on a wall. They tune pipes so that steam passing through them produces low notes at certain hours. They polish copper until it glows under lamps, then leave it to darken again. They arrange moss in seams so that green traces follow the lines of old repairs. They make beauty by maintenance, not by adding ornament. A perfectly functioning channel is beautiful. A healed graft is beautiful. An elder handrail worn smooth by centuries of touch is beautiful. A machine that has grown into its frame without crushing it is beautiful.

They do not paint much, because paint traps moisture and hides flaws. They do not carve faces into living walls, because faces imply a person where there may only be pattern. They prefer symbols that can grow: a spiral trained into a stem, a knot left visible where a branch once was, a brass valve polished bright above a dark root, a row of glass insulators catching green-gold light. Their art is temporal. It changes. It weathers. It is repaired. It is not meant to remain exactly as made.

If you stand near the center of the settlement, you can hear most of it at once: pumps, engines, water, leaves, tapping, creaking, the soft movement of inhabitants going about their rounds. At first it may seem impersonal. Then, if you listen longer, you begin to distinguish presences.

There is the slow rootwarden in the lower lane, one hand resting on a buttress, feeling whether yesterday’s rain has softened the platform. There is the cable-keeper high above, wrapping a joint in dry tape while a moth rests on their shoulder. There are two channel-tenders lifting a reed grate, their bodies leaning in perfect rhythm, water shining down their arms. There is the boiler-tender opening a furnace door just enough to feed another handful of dried fiber, face lit by orange glow against wet green walls. There is the young growth near the nursery trough, practicing how to close a small valve without cracking the stem around it. There is the elder in the stair-rail, no longer speaking except as pressure under a palm.

None of them are merely individuals. None of them are merely place. They are the point where the settlement becomes aware of itself.

So if you ask what lives there, the true answer is layered.

What lives there is a green-bodied people shaped by long industry, patient as roots, careful as mechanics, loud in their machinery and quiet in their persons. What lives there is a community of maintainers who have made kinship out of grafts, law out of drainage, memory out of worn wood, and worship out of keeping the pumps running. What lives there is also the settlement itself: one vast, breathing, plant-built organism with iron organs and water veins, inhabited by the very beings who are also made of it.

It is not a place of beasts or crowds. It is not a place of silence. It is a warm, wet, dense, loud little world where life has chosen to continue slowly, with oil on its hands and water at its feet, while the engines keep time and the roots hold everything together.
