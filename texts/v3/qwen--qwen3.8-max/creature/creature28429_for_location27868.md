What lives there does not announce itself. It leaks in. It condenses. It begins as a skin on a warm stone, a film over a pool, a faint thickening in the wet air where heat and mineral water have learned to feed on one another.

The first inhabitants are not bodies so much as surfaces. Bacterial felts spread over basalt shelves and broken concrete, rust-colored, cream, bruised violet, the dull orange of old blood diluted in brine. They are the pasture and the membrane of the flats. They eat iron and sulfur, leaked oil, salt, ash, and the chemical residue of whatever vanished world left its debris behind. They move so slowly that only absence reveals their motion: a bare patch on Monday, a velvet stain on Friday; a valve wheel softened with growth where yesterday it had been naked metal. Where a foot presses the ground, the felt often darkens hours later in a broad warm bloom, as if remembering the step. In some places the felts are so thick they breathe, lifting and settling with the faint pulses of heat from below.

On the felts feed the pale thread-fungi, climbing in lace-like nets over pump housings, rail sleepers, and the legs of dead machinery. They are sweet-smelling in a way that is not pleasant but intimate, like warm milk left in a closed room. Their threads enter cracks in ceramic insulators, stitch through rust scabs, and make soft white whiskers along the undersides of catwalks. They fruit in small caps the color of bone, then collapse into milky films that amphibian things lick with slow, rasping mouths.

There is also something that behaves like moss but is not moss: a rubbery, tar-dark mat that clings to warm slopes and the sheltered interiors of sheds. It yields under pressure and then slowly rises again. It smells of bitumen, fish, and hot pennies. The warm-dark people, if they can be called that, use it for caulking, bedding, and the lining of nests. When it is torn, it bleeds a black syrup that seals itself within a day.

The smallest animals are everywhere, and they are not small in importance. Insects drift in slow columns above vents and leaking pipes, not darting, not buzzing, but turning in patient spirals as though caught in a dance too large to finish. Their wings, when they have wings, are like oiled paper, translucent and faintly crinkled. Some are born in engine housings, their larvae feeding on mineral scum and grease, pupating in the warm hollows of dead gauges, emerging as heavy, soft-bodied adults that navigate by heat and static. Others hatch in flooded switchboxes, where their bodies acquire a faint charge; when they touch metal, they make tiny blueless sparks that are felt more than seen. They mate by exchanging these charges. They die by folding their wings over themselves and sinking into the water, where crabs and worms take them apart with ceremonial thoroughness.

There are beetles, or things that occupy the beetle-shaped niche, with shells bright as tarnished brass and mandibles fine enough to strip insulation from cable without biting the copper within. They gather in clusters around oil seeps, chewing the iridescent surface films. Their droppings are tiny glassy beads that collect in the joints of machinery. When enough of them move together, they sound like a handful of sand thrown against tin.

The blind crabs are among the most obvious citizens. They move through the shallows in dense congregations, their shells pitted, warm, often encrusted with shell fragments, rust flakes, fungal threads, and the old mineral signatures of several pools. They are not merely animals moving through the landscape; they are a census, a slow parliament, a moving reef in embryo. A single crab is simple. A hundred crabs become a decision. A thousand become a pressure on the future. They cluster in turbine housings, under sheds, inside the ribs of broken engines, and in the dry upper chambers of drowned control cabinets. They carry their eggs in warm corners where water circulates faintly. When the eggs hatch, the shallow pools cloud with minute pale lives, and for a time every surface seems to breathe.

Old crabs are often more architecture than animal. Their carapaces become homes for worms, for fungal spores, for small eels that slip between legs while the crab is molting. Some crabs have shells so thickened with mineral growth and stuck debris that they resemble walking stones. When they molt, the abandoned shell remains as a perfect, hollow self, soon occupied by smaller creatures. Children of the warm-dark people sometimes wear these molted shells as helmets or chest plates, not because they are protective in any useful sense, but because the shell still holds the shape of patience.

Eels are the moving veins of the flats. They thread through flooded machinery, along pipe runs, under ridges of new basalt, through valve wheels and around transformer cores. Their skin is slick with mucus and fine black sediment, their muscles slow and sure. They follow current, heat, and electrical gradients. Some are ordinary in size, thick as a wrist; others are long and heavy enough that when they pass through a channel, the water rises along the banks as though the channel itself has drawn breath. They sleep coiled inside dead housings, their bodies making loops that resemble wiring. In places where live cables leak, eels gather and remain motionless, drinking the hum. The warm-dark people believe that eels know the true map of the flats, because they move through the submerged interiors of things and do not trouble themselves with distances above the water.

Worms crowd the edges of pipes and the lips of gullies, building tubes from rust flakes, shell fragments, grains of pumice, and tiny chips of glassy stone. Their colonies make low ridges that slow the water and trap sediment. These ridges are not accidental. Over time, worm-work changes the shape of channels, diverting flow, creating still pools where larvae and amphibians can gather. Some worms are red, some gray, some nearly transparent, their digestive tracts visible as dark threads. The people of the flats do not usually eat them, but they respect them. A channel thick with worm tubes is a channel that will hold memory.

The amphibians are quieter than the crabs and stranger. They rest on wet stone with their translucent skins pressed flat, throats moving almost imperceptibly. Their bodies show the chemistry of the water: a violet flush when sulfur is high, a pallor when salt crust thickens, a faint rust bloom where iron seeps. They do not croak in any ordinary sense. They make subsonic calls, pulses that travel through water and stone and settle in the chest of anyone nearby. These calls are not heard so much as endured: a sudden pressure, a feeling of being recognized, a memory that does not belong to you. Their eggs are laid in warm shallows, glued to valve stems, rail spikes, and the lower rungs of broken ladders. The tadpoles are flat, pale, and patient. They feed on bacterial films and the soft rot of old rope.

There are heron-like forms too, though they are difficult to place in any simple category. They stand in the water without moving, necks curved, eyeless faces tilted slightly downward. Their legs are thin and jointed like old iron, their feathers—or whatever resembles feathers—are stiff, oil-dark, and faintly metallic. They may be birds grown wrong, or artifacts grown alive, or both. They feed on small disturbances: a fish shifting in the silt, a bubble rising from a buried tank, the heat-bloom left by a passing crab. When they strike, it is not fast. It is precise. The water closes over their beaks as if it had always contained them. Their droppings are white mineral stains that harden into crystalline shelves, and on these shelves insects lay eggs, worms build tubes, and new films of life begin again.

The rat-like things are hairless, warm, and clever. They nest in the dry interiors of dead control cabinets, behind panels, under switchboards, inside the hollow spaces of machine housings where geothermal warmth still gathers. Their skin is pink-gray or rust-brown, folded at the neck and shoulders. Their fingers are delicate and strong. They collect objects: bolts, gauge glass, shell fragments, bits of wire, teeth, buttons, old tools, the polished bones of fish. They are not afraid of the machines because they have never known machines as separate from shelter. They chew insulation but avoid live cables, having learned through generations what bites. They are thieves, but not malicious ones. They move objects from one district of the flats to another, creating unintended exchanges. A wrench lost in a pool may later appear on a catwalk three ridges away, placed there by a rat-thing that liked its weight. In this way, they are also cartographers of a kind.

Among all these lives, and not apart from them, live the people of the warm dark.

They are not human in the sunlit sense, if they were ever human at all. Some stories might say they are descendants of workers who remained after the machinery became strange; others might say the flats made them out of mud, heat, and old habits. Both may be true. They are low-bodied, broad-footed, and patient. Their skin is often pale, mottled with rust or gray, sometimes translucent enough at the wrists and throat to show the slow flush of heat beneath. Many have eyes, but the eyes are secondary, protected by heavy lids or milky membranes, used only for reading close patterns of heat and movement. Some keep their eyes mostly closed, navigating instead by pressure, taste, touch, and the faint electromagnetic tremor that runs through the flats.
