The basin is inhabited, but not thickly. Its life has learned to be counted by traces rather than crowds: a ripple where no fish should be, a warmth on a valve wheel, a lamp newly trimmed, a crust of moss disturbed at the edge of a quay. Whatever lives there has become answerable to water.

At first, it seems the animals are the only true residents.

They are everywhere in the small, patient way of creatures that have never needed to impress anyone. The pale amphibians lie in the seams of stone as if they were carved there, their eyes bright and unhurried, their throats moving in faint pulses. They do not scatter when a footstep approaches; they only settle deeper into shadow, trusting the wet dark to keep them. Bats hang in the upper hollows, soft-furred and damp, their wings folded like old umbrellas. When they move, it is with a quick, soundless dip, a brief disturbance over the black water, then stillness again. In the deeper channels, the eels pass like slow thoughts. They rise sometimes in coils, tasting the air, and then sink back into places where light has never been.

There are shrews and sleek dark rats that move along the pipe joints with the confidence of long residence. Their bodies are quick, but their movements are muffled by the wet air; even their scurry seems softened. Freshwater crustaceans, larger and paler than their surface cousins, work the edges of the drains. They clean the film from stone and iron with delicate, tireless motions, as though they were attendants appointed to the smallest offices of the basin. Moths the color of rust and wet paper circle the low lamps without frenzy. They are dusted with mineral powder, and when they rest on a railing they leave a faint pale print. High in the vaults, where grey light leaks through cracked discs and forgotten shafts, the birds make their homes in old ventilator housings. They are quiet, pale, and clicking; they do not sing, but they speak to one another in dry little sounds, like seeds tapped together.

These creatures are not accidents. They are citizens of the place. They have entered the machinery and made it part of their world. Crayfish shelter in junction boxes. Fish drift through screens that once barred them. Mollusks cling to moving parts so slowly that the machines have grown accustomed to their weight. In one chamber, a colony of pale worms lives along a leather belt, and when the belt flexes, they shine faintly, as if the old labor had begun to glow from within. The basin has not been invaded by life; it has been adopted by it.

Yet there are others too.

They are few, and they do not appear all at once. At first you might mistake one for a shadow gathered too carefully against a wall, or a bundle of oilcloth left on a landing. Then the shadow shifts, and you see that it has hands. Then a lamp moves where no lamp should be. Then a face turns toward you from across a canal, pale and watchful, framed by damp hair, and you understand that you have been observed long before you saw anything at all.

Whoever lives there has become more like the basin than like the world above.

They are the keepers, though they would not call themselves masters. They are attendants, custodians, the remaining hands in a place built for many hands. They may be descendants of the workers who first bricked the walls and set the pipes, or they may be something the basin made later out of necessity and silence. It is hard to say. The place has a way of wearing history smooth. Names do not last well there. Titles fade. What remains is function, habit, and the knowledge that if the channels are neglected, the water will remember what it was before it was taught manners.

They are not tall in the way of people who live under open sky. Their bodies have learned low ceilings and narrow galleries. They move with a careful economy, bending before they need to bend, turning before they need to turn. Their skin is pale, not sickly but cool, as if their blood has accepted the temperature of the water. In lamplight their eyes catch a faint shine, wide and adjusted to dimness, quick to notice movement but slow to judge it threat. They blink often and softly. Their hands are their most expressive feature: broad-palmed, callused, stained with oil and mineral bloom, fingers strong enough to close a stubborn valve yet delicate enough to lift a moth from a pool without crushing it.

They smell of the basin: wet stone, old iron, algae, wool dried too many times and never fully dry again. Their clothing is layered and practical, patched beyond its original shape. Oiled canvas, waxed cloth, felt, rubberized sheeting salvaged from old linings, strips of insulation woven into belts or shoulder wraps. Metal fasteners have gone green or brown with age. Boots are softened at the sole, sometimes lined with felt so they make no sharp sound on grating. Hoods are common, not for modesty but because the drip from above is constant. Some wear caps with short brims to keep moisture from their faces. Others have no hoods at all, only hair slicked back and tied with cord, dark or colorless, heavy with damp.

They do not carry much, but what they carry is chosen with care. A short hooked pole for pulling debris from channels. A brass key worn smooth from use. A small oilcan. A rag folded many times. A listening tube stopped with cork. A lantern with clouded glass, always burning low. Some carry chalk or a sharpened piece of slate for marking levels. Others carry knots of cord, each knot a memory of a valve, a gate, a change in weather from above. Their tools are not bright. Nothing about them is bright. Brightness would be a kind of noise.

They live in the upper galleries and side chambers where the water does not reach except in the worst seasons. Their dwellings are small, raised, and careful. Floors are slatted or stone-paved with drains cut into the corners. Sleeping places are built up from the ground, sometimes hammocks of woven fiber, sometimes narrow platforms covered with wool and waxed cloth. There is little furniture, because clutter becomes damp, and damp becomes heavy, and heaviness becomes another thing to manage. Racks hold tools and food in dryness. Hooks line walls. Every object has a place where it can hang or be lifted away from seepage. In some rooms there are low stoves of brick or ceramic, fed with slow-burning cakes of pressed fungal pith and oil, giving more warmth than flame. The heat is not wasted. It is used to dry socks, to warm hands, to keep one corner of the world from weeping quite so much.

Their settlements, if they can be called that, are arranged around duty rather than comfort. One household watches a cluster of gauges. Another tends a row of settling pans. Another keeps the lamps along a long quay trimmed and burning. Another listens to the pipes when the pressure changes. No one rules. No one needs to. The basin has its own authority, and everyone who remains has already submitted to it.

They rise and rest according to pump cycles and water levels rather than sun. There is no morning there, only a change in sound: a pump beginning, a chain tightening, a gate sighing as it takes new weight. When the machinery breathes out, some sleep. When it breathes in, others wake. Time is not measured in hours so much as in intervals: the lag between rain above and water arriving below; the length of a drip sequence; the slow blooming of rust on a new bolt; the season when algae threads grow long in the shallow channels and must be combed back from the grates.

They speak little, and when they do speak their voices are low. The air is too heavy for loudness; sound drops into it and is swallowed. Their speech has been shaped by echo and moisture. Consonants are softened. Words are often begun with the mouth half-closed
