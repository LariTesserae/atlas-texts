What lives there is not, at first, a “who” in any simple sense.

It is not a species standing apart from its city. It is not a population of bodies moving through streets as though the streets were dead passages waiting to be crossed. The first inhabitant is the basin itself: the whole green mass of it, breathing in wet heat, tightening around its own fissures, listening to itself grow. The city is lived in because it is alive; it is inhabited because it is, in some sense, the inhabitant.

But that is only the first answer. The place does not remain singular for long.

As you stay with it, the city resolves into plurality. Not into individuals exactly, and not into creatures, but into living presences: growth-patterns that have acquired persistence, structures that have begun to behave like persons, districts that hold moods, root-networks that remember injuries, towers that answer one another across the humid air. What lives there are organized intentions without skulls. Civic appetites without mouths. Identities made less of flesh than of repetition, pressure, and memory.

The residents are botanical, but not in the passive way of ordinary plants. They are botanical in the way of architecture becoming aware of its own stresses. They are botanical in the way a root knows obstruction, a vine knows tension, a leaf knows light, a wound knows repair — except here all those knowledges have thickened into something like culture.

No animal lives there. Nothing with legs, wings, eyes, or a beating heart. Nothing that chews, bleeds, nests, or cries out in the old animal sense. There are no predators, no grazers, no parasites with fangs or feelers. Even the smallest motions belong to tissue, fluid, chemistry, and growth. If there are faces in this place, they are not faces: they are knots, folds, scars, cavities, the accidental resemblance of split bark to expression. If there are voices, they are groaning fibers, clapping leaves, booming hollow stems, dripping chambers, the wet tear of new shoots forcing themselves through older tissue.

And yet it is occupied.

The most obvious inhabitants are the great fixed forms: the structural elders of the city. These are the buttress-roots, the fused stem-walls, the broad platforms, the towers stiffened with lignin and perforated by openings that breathe with the changing heat. They do not move from place to place because they are the places. They are the load-bearing citizens. Their bodies are civic works. Their longevity is not measured in years so much as in scars. A tower that has split, healed, split again, and regrown over its own wound is old in the only way age exists there: by accumulated repair.

These fixed beings are slow, but not inert. They shift weight over long intervals. They alter their posture in response to moisture, sound, and pressure. They lean away from overgrowth, thicken where strain is greatest, open pores when heat gathers, and close them again when the air thickens with condensation. They seem patient because their decisions take the form of growth. A choice for them is not a sudden gesture; it is a direction. A refusal is a failure to sprout. An invitation is a space left open.

Among them, the hollow towers are especially prominent. They are not merely buildings; they are announcers, listeners, resonators. When air moves through them, they boom. When water runs into their bases, they deepen in tone. When the surrounding district begins to change, they often sound first, as though the city speaks through them before it knows what it wants to say. In this way, the towers are less like individual residents than like public voices — places where the city’s collective pressure becomes audible. They are organs of declaration.

Then there are the moving citizens.

They are not animals, and they do not walk. They migrate by creeping, rooting, releasing, and rooting again. Low mats of springy tissue advance over fallen stems, not as a single organism but as a communal front, testing gaps, filling depressions, binding loose surfaces into walkable flesh. Vines and tension-cords shift more deliberately, drawing themselves forward by incremental contraction, finding anchor points, braiding into temporary bodies, then loosening when the structure they were holding is no longer needed. Root bundles sometimes lift from their footholds in wet weather, becoming slow, many-fingered rafts that drift along channels or climb over one another to reach higher platforms.

These mobile growths are the city’s restless population. They are the ones that make the streets possible. A corridor remains open not because it is paved or swept, but because a thousand creeping impulses avoid it, suppress themselves at its edges, or are persuaded away by chemical signals and shared pressure. When a passage begins to close, the movement of these residents changes. They thicken elsewhere. They fold inward. They test the corridor with new shoots, then withdraw if the city’s deeper pattern still requires openness. In this sense, the streets are maintained by living obedience.

Some of these mobile forms seem almost processional. Long chains of leaf-discs drift across still water pockets, aligning themselves into rows that break apart and re-form. Root-tips gather at the edges of fissures, pause, then cross in bundles, like pilgrims deciding together that a new region has become holy. In places where light falls through the canopy in moving coins, vertical sprouts rise and lower almost in rhythm, as if rehearsing a ceremony that has no priest, no audience, and no purpose except its own continuation.

There are also the water-keepers.

They live around moisture the way other citizens might live around fire or food, though nothing here burns and nothing is eaten in the ordinary sense. Broad condensation fronds hold themselves at precise angles, gathering beads from the air. Pitcher-shaped depressions and cupped leaf-basins maintain their curvature with almost fussy attention, shedding debris, unfolding when the vapor thickens, closing when too much heat would drink them dry. Sponge-bodied growths swell after brief rains and then slowly release their reserves through fine capillary networks. Bladder-towers stand turgid and glossy, their surfaces tense with held liquid.

These water-forms are among the most sensitive residents of the basin. They react quickly. They tremble when droplets strike them. They adjust their posture by minute hydraulic changes. Their “work” is not labor as an animal would understand it, but continual self-shaping in response to need. When moisture is scarce, they become more patient, more inward. When water returns, they open with visible relief, unfolding membranes and widening their catchment surfaces. The city’s hydration is not managed from above; it is practiced by thousands of such bodies, each one a small reservoir, a small judgment, a small act of conservation.

Then there are the acoustic inhabitants.

At first one might think sound is merely produced by the city, as wind produces noise in leaves. But here sound is more than byproduct. It is a medium of coordination, a means of presence, perhaps even a kind of body. Drum-membranes stretched across frame-like branches vibrate in response to distant booms. Resonating culms answer one another in low pulses. Seed-vessels shaped for explosive dehiscence crack open not only to release spores or vapor but to broadcast a signal. Hollow chambers amplify dripping into rhythm. Sap-conduits rush with a sound like buried traffic.

Some residents seem to exist almost entirely as acoustic events. A particular vibration may travel through a grove, cause a hundred leaf-vents to open, and then vanish — yet while it lasts it organizes the whole district. It is less like a message sent by someone than like a temporary someone made of signal. When the noise swells, these acoustic beings become dense enough to feel physical. The air stiffens. Surfaces tremble. New growth follows the pattern of the sound as though the sound had laid down a plan.

In this way, the city’s deafening loudness is not confusion. It is society.

The inhabitants communicate without language in any human sense. They communicate by pressure, humidity, chemical gradient, vibration, and the physical memory of tissue. A wound releases signals. A closed pore changes the moisture around it. A root encountering another root may fuse with it, recoil, or send inhibiting compounds through the shared substrate. A street that is being used too much may become chemically discouraging; a plaza ready for gathering may become slick with condensed water and resonant underfoot. Meaning here is not spoken. It is grown into.

Their perception is likewise distributed. They do not have eyes, but they register light with exquisite precision: its angle, warmth, duration, the way it is broken by overlapping canopies. They do not have ears, but they feel vibration through membranes, hollow stems, and taut fibers. They do not have skin in the animal sense, but every surface is sensitive: wax, bark, leaf-flesh, root-hair, scar-tissue. They know when something moves through them because movement changes tension. They know when a path is being followed because repeated passage alters the chemistry of the ground and the pressure of surrounding growth. They know when something is new because it creates a fresh gradient of humidity, shade, or resistance.

If an outsider were somehow present there, the city would not see it as a person sees a person. It would register the outsider as disturbance: a warm interruption, a blockage of light, a compression of tissue, a strange chemical signature. The city might close around it, test it with rootlets, absorb it if it were organic enough, or grow away from it if it remained unintelligible. There would be no greeting, only adjustment.

The social life of these inhabitants is not based on separate households or private bodies. Their fundamental social act is grafting. When two growths touch under the right tension, they may fuse. This is not merely reproduction or construction; it is relationship made physical. A bridge becomes a bridge because several living limbs have accepted one another. A wall becomes strong because competing stems have either merged or settled into mutual support. A district becomes coherent when enough local forms begin repeating the same architectural habits: the same arch angle, the same spacing of towers, the same manner of folding leaves into wells.

Affection, if it exists here, is continuity. Hostility is overgrowth. To oppose another growth is to shade it, strangle it, divert its water, or outcompete it for structural position. But even conflict is not final. The loser is not usually destroyed in the way an animal body is destroyed. It is absorbed, overgrown, re-routed, or composted into a new use. A defeated stem may become footing. A failed tower may become a ramp. A suppressed pattern may return later from a root-memory no one could have seen.

There is no government by command, yet there is order. The city is ruled by pattern. Certain forms repeat because they work. Circular wells appear at intervals because that shape gathers condensation efficiently. Root-bridges span channels at similar heights because those heights match the flow of moisture and the reach of stable anchors. Towers cluster where acoustic resonance reinforces coordination. Long low platforms recur because they stabilize growth and create surfaces suitable for collective response. These repetitions are not laws written down; they are habits strong enough to become civic.

The inhabitants, therefore, are not only the visible growths. They are also the recurring shapes. A repeated arch is a resident. A persistent corridor is a resident. A rhythm of dripping water is a resident. A scar that keeps influencing new growth long after it has healed is a resident. The city is full of such presences: not ghosts, exactly, but structural memories with continuing power.

This makes identity strangely fluid.

A single organism may be several citizens at once. A root-platform can be a floor, a foundation, a nutrient exchange, a historical record, and a district boundary simultaneously. A vine that begins as one tendril may branch into dozens of anchored points, each behaving differently, until it is more a neighborhood than a body. A tower may split during a storm and become two thinner towers, each carrying part of the old pattern but diverging in height, tone, and orientation. Are there then two residents where there was one? The city does not seem to ask. It adjusts.

Conversely, many separate growths can become one. When roots graft across a channel, when stems fuse into a wall, when epiphytes merge with their hosts until no seam remains, separate origins cease to matter. The resulting body remembers multiple ancestries but acts as a single architectural fact. Individuality here is not a boundary; it is a phase.

Reproduction is likewise plural. Some residents release seeds or spores in explosive bursts, filling the air with dust-like possibility. Others propagate by fragmentation: a fallen limb roots, a torn mat reattaches, a shed strip of bark becomes the beginning of a new screen. Still others reproduce by imitation. A successful arch induces similar arches nearby. A useful well-form spreads through the district by being chemically and structurally reinforced. In this sense, the city’s children are not only bodies; they are also patterns that have managed to repeat themselves.

The young growths are everywhere, and they are loud. New shoots tear through old tissue with wet cracks. Seed-vessels pop in rapid volleys. Unfurling leaves strike one another as they expand. These are the city’s most turbulent citizens: brief, numerous, unstable, full of blind direction. Most are absorbed or folded back into existing structures within what feels like minutes or hours. Some persist. A few become new streets, new bridges, new towers. The city is always being made by its own excess.

Death, when it occurs, is not departure. It is incorporation.

There are no corpses left to rot in the animal sense. Fallen structures do not remain as waste. They become substrate. A collapsed bridge swells with moisture, roots into the channel margins, and becomes a terrace. A hollow tower that loses its resonance may be infiltrated by internal growth until it becomes a solid pillar. A plaza overgrown by rising fronds does not die; it changes function, becoming a shaded understory, then perhaps a thicket, then perhaps the floor of a new vertical district. The dead are not buried beneath the city. They are the city’s next sentence.

Ancestors, if one can call them that, exist as scars, grafts, buried conduits, and inherited directions. The memory of an old path can persist long after the path has vanished, because root systems still avoid the former corridor. The memory of a collapsed tower can shape new growth around the absent center. The city remembers by structure. Its past is not recorded elsewhere; it is load-bearing.

The strangest residents, however, are not the solid ones.

Because reality in the basin is unstable, some inhabitants seem to belong less to the present than to adjacent possibilities. There are translucent duplicates of structures: wavering leaf-shadow versions of towers and arches that exist beside their solid counterparts. They are not reflections, and they are not illusions. They behave as if they are other decisions the city might have made. Sometimes they precede physical change. A translucent arch may appear where no arch exists, and later solid growth will follow its outline. Sometimes they remain as permanent alternatives, never becoming flesh but still influencing the way solid forms bend around them.

There are also temporal residents: sounds that arrive before their causes, patterns that seem to remember futures, pools that show older or younger versions of the basin. The beings of the city live inside this instability without distress. They do not experience time as a straight avenue. A district may act as though it has already become what it will become next. A root-network may respond to a fissure before the surface splits. A tower may boom before the warm air pulse that appears to cause the boom. For the inhabitants, causality is not a rule but a habit that occasionally loosens.

This gives some of them an eerie quality of being ahead of themselves. A wall may bulge as though reacting to a pressure that has not yet arrived. A street may close because it has become aware of too many possible movements through it. A row of arches may multiply not by growth but by pattern-intensity, becoming more numerous because its own repetition has grown persuasive. The inhabitants do not merely occupy the city; they occupy its variations.

Because of this, the question “how many live there?” has no stable answer.

At one moment the city feels like a single vast being. At another it feels like millions of competing growths locked into negotiation. At another it feels like a chorus of districts, each with its own tempo, its own moisture, its own acoustic signature. At another it feels populated by countless ephemeral persons: a pressure-wave that organizes a grove, a root-front that crosses a channel with shared intent, a cluster of seed-bursts that briefly behaves as a crowd before being absorbed into the general green.

Number itself is unreliable there. Three arches can become seven because the pattern has decided to be more numerous than itself. A nearby tower can recede through folded layers of vegetation. A familiar street can curve back behind its own beginning. The inhabitants do not resist this. They are made of it. They are the city’s way of being unstable without ceasing to be organized.

If one tries to imagine their inner life, it must not be imagined as a mind inside a head. It is more like a field of preferences. Light is followed. Water is conserved. Pressure is answered. Wounds are sealed. Repetition is reinforced. Excess is folded back. Stillness is rare, but not desired. The city does not seek peace; it seeks continuation. It does not aim for perfection; it aims for enough coherence to keep growing.

There is, however, something that resembles attention.

When a new fissure opens, the surrounding growth turns toward it. When a bridge weakens, nearby structures thicken in anticipation. When a plaza becomes too reflective, fronds rise to shade it. When a corridor is overused, the city becomes aware of it in the only way it can: by changing around it. This is awareness without contemplation. It is the awareness of a living system that has become too complex to remain merely reactive. It overcorrects. It forgets. It remembers too much. It repeats forms long after their original usefulness has passed. It produces echoes of itself simply because echo has become one of its habits.

The city is not kind, and it is not cruel. It is total. Everything within it is usable. Everything is material, signal, memory, or future structure. If something weakens, it is incorporated. If something persists, it is imitated. If something disappears, it may return as a shape in the root-memory. There is no outside waste. There is no final refuse. Even collapse is fertile. Even noise is construction.

What lives there, then, is a civilization of growth.

Not a civilization of tools, but of tropisms. Not a civilization of hands, but of pressure. Not a civilization of spoken words, but of resonance and scar. Its citizens are rooted and mobile, singular and collective, solid and echoing. They are towers that remember, roots that decide, leaves that listen, water-cups that tend their own fullness, seed-clouds that arrive like brief festivals, districts that become persons for a season, patterns that outlive their bodies, and sounds that move ahead of their causes.

They do not build the city. They are the city building itself.

They do not live in the architecture. They are the architecture living.

And if you search for their faces, you will not find them. You will find glossy healed splits where something tore and learned its own strength. You will find hollow openings breathing warm air. You will find root-curtains brushing together as if in consultation. You will find pools reflecting not what is above, but what might have been. You will find streets that remain open because countless growths have agreed, without language, to keep them open. You will find noise so dense it becomes a body. You will find the whole basin inhaling before another crash of becoming.

That is who lives there.

The green multitude. The civic flesh. The rooted chorus. The city that is also its own population, its own government, its own weather, its own memory, and its own unending birth.
