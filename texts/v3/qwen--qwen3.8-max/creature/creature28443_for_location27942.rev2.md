What lives there does not arrive in the ordinary way.

It does not come with green leaves, open water, birdsong spilling over a sunlit slope. It comes as pressure, as warmth in stone, as a fine powder disturbed in a deliberate line. If you looked only for the signs you were taught to trust—grass, damp soil, soft wood, running water—you would call the pocket dead. Then you would stand still long enough to see the dust lean toward a warm pipe, and you would understand that life had been there all along, wearing a different grammar.

The first answer is the people.

They are the pocket-born, though not all of them began there. Some bloodlines came from lower places, from passes and mines and high caravans, drawn by work, refuge, debt, curiosity, or the simple gravity of a hidden room in the mountains. Over generations, the place has trimmed them to its needs. They tend to be deep-chested and careful in their breathing, their bodies thriftier with moisture than lowland bodies. Their skin is dry and close-grained, their lips often sealed by habit, their eyes patient in dim amber light. They do not have the startled look of people used to wide green distances. Their gaze is tuned to edges, seams, hairline cracks, the slight lift of dust that warns of a fall or a change in air pressure.

They wear the pocket on them. Dust cloaks with high collars and hoods. Masks fitted with ceramic filters or woven mineral cloth, sometimes left hanging at the jaw, sometimes pulled over the mouth when the air thickens. Boots with felted or layered soles, quiet on metal and stone. Gloves that are thin at the fingertips so they can feel a vibrating cable or the faint prickle of charge on a rail. Many carry small grounded tags, braided wires, or smooth discharge stones, touching them before they touch sensitive machinery. Their clothing is patched, re-oiled, re-woven, tightened, and retied until it has the same assembled look as the buildings. Nothing is thrown away while it can still serve.

Water has made them ceremonious without making them priests.

No one pours openly there. No cup is passed carelessly. Drinking is a private act, done in sealed alcoves or behind curtains, from vessels that close again at once. A full water flask is not a casual gift; it is a serious one. Children learn early that sweat is wealth leaving the body, that breath is not free, that tears are not merely feeling but loss. They are not taught to suppress grief, exactly, but to keep it inside the hood. In the driest seasons, or when the stills run poorly, even the moisture from exhalation is caught in mask-filters and returned. The people have a practical reverence for the body’s hidden reserves. To spill liquid is not just waste. It is a breach of the pocket’s deepest law.

Their voices are low.

Not because they are emotionless, but because the architecture has taught them scale. A shout feels too large for the chamber; it bounces badly, startles, wastes breath, and can set dust moving. They have developed a language of hands, taps, and body turns. A touch on the shoulder moves someone through a crowded passage. Two knuckles against iron mean wait. A scraped line in salt means a claim is disputed. A short click of tongue against teeth may call a child down from a dangerous rail. In the markets, bargaining is often done with fingers over felt mats, with small nods, with the presentation of tags. When they laugh, it is usually close and stifled, a warmth shared between bodies rather than broadcast into the air.

Children are quick, light, and vertical.

They learn the pocket before they learn its history. They know which ladder is safe in cold weather, which rail can be slid down without snagging, which wall should not be leaned against after the afternoon chill. They can read a dust devil by the way it bends around a strut, and they know the smell of overheated insulation before they know the names of the machines that use it. They play in the gaps: racing beetles in shallow pans, drawing in pale powder, sliding down cables with loops of leather, trading moth-wing dust, pressing their ears to warm ducts to hear the centipedes move inside. They are not reckless in the way lowland children are reckless. They are precise. A fall here is not softened by grass. A loose stone is not an accident but a signal.

The old become listeners.

Their lungs may carry dust, their joints may stiffen from cold and narrow spaces, but their attention sharpens. They can tell from a change in hum whether a turbine is about to strain. They know the sound of a crack before it fully opens. They sit in recesses near warm pipes, wrapped in layered cloth, tapping walls with short hammers, advising where braces should go, which loads should be moved, which seams are breathing wrong. No one discards them quickly. In a place where every structure is in negotiation with collapse, memory is a kind of engineering.

They are not one people in origin, but they have become one by constraint.

They call themselves many things depending on terrace, guild, and family line, but if they need a common word, they often choose something simple: the Dry, the Pocket-born, the Salt-handled. They are workers, refiners, chemists, mechanics, weavers of wire, cutters of mineral crust, tenders of fungal beds, collectors of bird dung, keepers of lamps. Their society is dense and overregulated because it has to be. Rights are vertical. A family may own a sleeping shelf, the air above it, the hook below it, and a share in a cable route three terraces away. Disputes flare fast and are settled fast, because a blocked passage or a severed wire does not inconvenience one household; it endangers the pocket.

Yet there is tenderness in them.

It appears in small acts. Someone cleaning another’s mask without being asked. A worker saving the last warmth from a machine casing for a sleeping elder. A trader covering a child’s cough with a folded cloth so the dust will not stir. Lovers pressing foreheads together rather than kissing openly, conserving breath, conserving moisture. A repair crew sharing a sealed flask after a dangerous shift, each taking only the smallest sip. Affection there is often maintenance. To love someone is to keep their footing safe, their filters clean, their grounding strap sound, their water count unbroken.

Below the people, and in many ways before them, are the mats.

These are the true base of the pocket’s life, though the word “base” may be misleading. They are not foundations in the sense of being passive. They are leathery, slow, and strangely alert to gradients of heat and charge. In the darker corners, behind brick housings, under cable bundles, along the backs of furnaces, they spread in scales and brittle ripples. Some are ash-gray. Some are the color of dried bone. Others darken to black varnish where they have fed on mineral-rich seepage from old iron. They do not leaf, do not flower, do not turn toward the sky. They cling, crust, and thicken.

The people call them by practical names: ash-leather, bone-scale, static bloom, kiln-crust, breath-mat. The names change from terrace to terrace. No one argues too hard about what they are. They are not plants. They may not be fungi in the old sense. They seem to live on mineral films, chemical differences, warmth, faint electrical leakage, and whatever trace vapors the machines and bodies surrender. Where they grow, the air feels less sterile. They do not make the pocket soft, but they make it possible.

Some mats are wild. Others are cultivated in shallow troughs of crushed salt and powdered rock, placed where waste heat can feed them without cooking them. Mat-tenders move among these beds with soft brushes, scrapers, and sealed hoods. They know which crust is healthy by its sheen, its brittleness, the way it lifts at the edge when touched. Harvesting is careful work. The mats are cut in dry sheets, then handled inside tents or closed cabinets so that any bound moisture or volatile chemistry is not lost to the air. In lean times, certain processed mats are pressed into dense cakes and used as feed for insects, or, after further treatment in sealed retorts, as emergency ration for people. More often they are insulation, indicator, filter medium, wound-packing, or substrate for the smaller lives.

At night, some of them glow.

It is not a bright glow. It is a faint greenish or bluish sheen, visible only when the lamps have been lowered and the eyes have adjusted. It gathers in seams and under ledges where charge has been accumulating. The people do not romanticize it, but they watch it. A mat that glows too strongly may be near a fault. One that dims may be starving, poisoned, or dying. Children sometimes whisper to the glow as if it were a sleeping animal. In a way, they are not wrong.

Where the mats live, the small animals gather.

The first of these are the moths.

They are pale, powder-winged things, not like fat lowland moths but narrow and dry, their bodies slim enough to slip between cables. Their wings look salted, dusted with mineral powder so fine that it seems to move when they are still. They cluster around lamp cages not because they worship light but because the lamps gather heat and charge, and because the faint currents around them carry the smells of dead mats and shed insect skins. Their larvae feed on the dried remains of the pocket’s lower life: dead crust, shed wing-dust, old fungal matter, the occasional desiccated bird. The people tolerate them, and more than tolerate them. Moth larvae are a reliable source of dense protein when managed properly. Moth eggs are counted on tags. Wing-dust is collected for use in testing air currents, because it moves with the slightest breath and reveals leaks in ducts and seals.

The beetles come next.

They are blunt, coppery, and surprisingly sturdy. Their shells have a dull metallic sheen, and when they walk over iron gratings they make a tiny ticking sound, like seeds dropped on a plate. They patrol lower corners, under platforms, along the backs of storage cages, eating fungal crust, dead insects, and whatever organic residue the settlement produces. Some workers keep beetle lines the way others keep filters: a managed population moving through a designated seam, cleaning as it goes. Children race them, though the races are quiet affairs. A beetle will not hurry for shouting. It must be coaxed with warmth or the smell of old crust. Beetle shells are saved when they die, ground into pigment, used as tiny toggles, or traded as counters. Nothing in the pocket is too small to have a second use.

Then there are the mites.

In some places they move in lines so dense and slow that they look like red thread pulled through a wall seam. They are not fast. They do not need to be. They consume dust, dead filaments, flaked skin, microscopic residues that would otherwise choke moving parts. The people cultivate them in certain passages, guiding them with warmed needles or copper strips. A good mite line can keep a duct cleaner than any brush. A bad one can clog a filter or get into food stores, so they are watched. When a child crouches by a crack with a heated wire, tongue caught in concentration, she is not merely playing. She is learning one of the pocket’s oldest trades: persuading small lives to work where hands cannot reach.

Blind centipedes live deeper in the ductwork.

They are pale, long-bodied, and flattened where they need to be, with heads shaped for prying into narrow spaces. They do not see. They feel their way through vibration and texture, hunting smaller insects, mites, and the strange switch-life that gathers in warm housings. Their many legs make a sound like fine rain on metal, though almost no one born in the pocket has heard rain. People are not fond of them, but they do not kill them without reason. A centipede in the wrong place can bite or startle, and their bodies can jam small mechanisms if they die inside. But a centipede in the right duct keeps populations from tipping too far. The workers who maintain airflow call them duct-brooms with teeth.

The lizards are stranger and gentler.

They have no scales in the ordinary sense. Their skin is dry, pale, and fibrous, like papyrus or tightly pressed paper. They do not bask, because there is no sun broad enough to trust. Instead they press themselves against warm machine casings, pipes, and heated stone, flattening their bodies to take in as much warmth as possible. They move in sudden, soft darts, catching moths, beetle larvae, and stray mites. Their feet are soft and sure, made for salt crust and iron alike. They are often found near sleeping shelves, where human warmth gathers. No one officially keeps them, but no one chases them away. A paper lizard on a warm wall is considered a sign that the wall is sound and the heat is flowing well. Some children leave dead moths near favored lizards, not as feeding exactly, but as a kind of friendship.

The birds belong partly to the pocket and partly to the void outside.

They are narrow-winged, high-flying things, built for thin air and knife-edged currents. They enter through the rim fissure at certain hours, dropping into the pocket on folded wings, then rising again before the light changes too much. Their calls are muted clicks and thin whistles, not loud songs. There is no space for loud songs. They nest in the upper gantries, in cages and shelters built for them by human hands, because the people have learned that birds are useful. They eat insects. Their droppings are collected, dried, and traded into chemical processes or fungal beds. Their eggs are harvested under strict count, never so many that the line weakens.

Some birds become resident. A pair may nest so long in the same iron corner that their chicks hatch knowing the pocket as the only world. These birds are watched with particular interest. They seem less eager for the rim. Their wings still want height, but their habits bend around the lamps and platforms. If one escapes upward through the fissure, the people sometimes wait for its return. If it does not return, no one speaks of it much. The sky beyond the rim is vast and indifferent. The pocket, at least, is warm.

Then come the lives that are harder to name.

The people do not speak of them with superstition, because superstition is too wasteful. They speak of them as workers speak of difficult tools: with caution, respect, and a readiness to adjust.

There are things in the switch housings.

Tiny, pale, almost threadlike, they nest in old insulation and in the dust that gathers around charged parts. Workers claim they feed on damaged insulation, or on the chemical changes current leaves behind, or on the faint static film itself. They are too small to see clearly unless one opens a panel with care, and even then they scatter. Their presence is known by effects: a slight chewing sound, a black grain left on a contact plate, a small surge smoothed out, a fault that never happens. Some engineers hate them. Some rely on them. A switchbox with a healthy population of these lives is often stable, because they clean away what would otherwise become a path for dangerous discharge. But if they grow too numerous, they can bridge a gap or shed debris where none is wanted. So they are managed. Panels are opened with ceramic tools. Grounding plates are offered. The small lives are encouraged to move, not destroyed.

There are filamentous grazers on charged surfaces.

They appear only when conditions are right: dry air, strong potential, dust held in a fine enough suspension. They move across metal and salt crust in patterns too regular to be accidental and too shifting to be drawn. Sometimes they look like frost writing itself. Sometimes like threads of greenish light pulled into loops. They are not a single creature, perhaps, but a colony, a behavior, a living discharge. The people call them charge-lace, circuit-bloom, quiet-thread. When they gather in rings around a busbar, engineers lower the load. When they vanish suddenly from a panel, someone goes to check the grounding. They are not pets. They are omens with practical value.

At night, in the fungal mats, there are pulse-shapes.

They are small, translucent, no larger than a hand or sometimes no larger than a thumbnail. They move slowly through the crusts with a rhythmic brightening and dimming, as if they are breathing light rather than air. No one has ever examined one for long. When touched, they often collapse into powder or slip into seams too fine for fingers. Children call them lamp-sisters, ember-knots, night-lice. Adults pretend not to hear. But when a new baby is born in the pocket, some families leave a tiny scrap of warmed metal near the wall, not as an offering, exactly, but as an acknowledgment. The pocket has many kinds of warmth. Some of them answer.

Below the lowest terraces, in a chamber no map fully agrees on, something large turns slowly.

This is the inhabitant people mention least and rely on most. Some say it is a machine from the pocket’s first making, an old sealed drum or turbine that has never been fully understood. Some say it is a growth: mats, mineral crust, beetle remains, chemical deposits, and old iron fused into a living mass. Some say it began as one and became the other. The few who descend to maintain it wear heavy masks and return with dust on their shoulders and a distant look in their eyes. They speak of heat, of a smell like hot stone and old lightning, of a sound that is less heard than felt in the chest.

They call it the Undermill, the Slow Lung, the Turn, the Low Heart.

It processes waste, salt powder, dead mats, rejected chemical sludge, and perhaps other things no one speaks of. It gives back heat, charge gradients, and a slow circulation of air that helps the pocket breathe. Whether it turns because of pressure, chemistry, or something like intention is a dispute no one settles. In the pocket, such disputes are less important than maintenance. The Undermill is fed. The Undermill is listened to. When its pitch changes, work pauses. When it slows too much, warning lights bloom along the lower levels. It is not worshipped. It is kept.

It may be alive in the way a reef is alive: not one body, but a negotiated city of bodies. It may have chambers where beetles move in darkness. It may have veins where static crawls like slow fire. It may have memories stored in mineral layers. It may dream in clicks. The people do not need to know its exact nature to understand that their lives are braided with it. If it stops, they stop. So they keep it fed with sorted powders and careful attention, and they teach their children not to mock the sound below, because the sound below is the sound of continuing.

All these lives press together into something larger than any one of them.

The pocket itself becomes a kind of inhabitant.

Not a single animal, not a mind with a face, but a distributed attention made of human habit, animal movement, fungal patience, machine hum, and mineral unrest. The city breathes through the fissure. It warms through its engines. It sheds dust in slow veils. It feels damage as a change in sound, a change in charge, a change in the way mites move through a seam. It repairs itself not because it has a soul in the simple sense, but because every part of it is in constant negotiation with every other part. A crack appears, and the pocket responds with braces, shifted loads, rerouted cables, altered market lanes. A lamp fails, and shadows reorganize. A bird returns, and someone notes the hour. A child laughs too loudly, and the city hushes her, not from cruelty, but from scale.

The people sense this. They say the pocket is “settled” or “hungry,” “listening” or “in a bad temper.” They do not mean poetry. They mean that a wall is drawing moisture from the air, that a particular dust drift has changed direction, that the static is lifting hair on their arms before a surge, that the mats are glowing too early, that the Undermill has shifted its note. Their language is technical and animistic at once, because in such a place those words have never fully separated.

What lives there is crowded but not loud.

It is abundant but not soft.

It is full of small hunger, small warmth, small precise motions. Bodies pass close on walkways. Hands touch shoulders. Goods move over rails. Filters are tapped clean. Lamps are caged. Birds turn in the high arc of sky. Moths powder themselves against wire. Beetles tick through corners. Mites flow in red lines. Mats thicken over warm stone. Engineers argue in whispers over diagrams scratched into soot. Children learn to love the pocket not by imagining escape, but by learning its signs.

If you ask who lives there, the answer is not one figure.

It is a salt-stained child sliding down a rail with a leather loop under her arm.
It is a mat-tender scraping bone-scale into a sealed pan.
It is an old wall-tapper pressing his ear to a cold surface, waiting for the next crack to announce itself.
It is a pale moth folding its wings against a lamp cage.
It is a paper lizard flattened against a turbine housing.
It is a bird dropping through the rim fissure with the void still on its wings.
It is a red line of mites moving through a seam.
It is charge-lace writing temporary frost across a grounded plate.
It is a pulse-shape glowing faintly in the crust while the lamps are low.
It is the slow pressure-sound below, turning in the dark.
It is the city itself, adjusting, tightening, repairing, surviving.

They do not live there in spite of the dryness. They live there by becoming dry.

Not dead-dry. Not empty-dry. But precise-dry. Mineral-dry. The dryness of salt kept clean, of metal kept grounded, of breath kept close, of warmth kept where it is needed. They have made a home out of withholding. They have built a dense, vertical, breathing world inside a wound in the mountain, and they have filled it with lives that ask for very little and give back careful attention.

When evening comes and the high slit of sky darkens, the pocket glows from within. Lamps bloom one by one behind wire cages. The salt walls take the amber light and hold it in layers. Dust settles on sleeping shelves, on folded moth wings, on beetle shells, on iron struts, on the shoulders of people who have stopped moving long enough to be still. Somewhere below, the slow thing turns. Somewhere above, a bird tucks its head into the ironwork. Somewhere in the wall, a mat brightens faintly, and a small shape pulses through it like a thought the pocket is having but does not need to speak.

Then a stone ticks loose. A cable shifts. A surge passes. A child is hushed. A lamp flickers. The dust rises in a thin column, hangs, and begins again.

The lives there move with it.

They are not waiting for rain. They are not longing for a greener world. They are already complete in their own severe way: quiet, crowded, watchful, industrious, and endlessly repairing themselves around the next small break. What lives there has learned the deepest secret of the pocket—that permanence is not made by standing still, but by changing quickly enough, carefully enough, and together enough to remain.
