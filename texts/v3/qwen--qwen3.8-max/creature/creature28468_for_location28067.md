What lives there is not one kind, but a close plurality of lives fitted into intervals. The most evident are the people: human, numerous, and so shaped by the shallow underground that they seem less like inhabitants of stone than like its careful attendants. They are not pale, eyeless things, nor are they surface dwellers merely hiding below the soil. They look like ordinary people made unusually exact by their surroundings. Their bodies are compact and practical. Their shoulders are strong from climbing ladders and lifting baskets. Their legs know the angles of uneven floors so well that they can cross a broken gallery without looking down. Their hands are always in use: gripping rope, testing clay, moving a pebble, feeling whether a wall is sweating more than it should.

They are not uniformly slender or stooped, but they move with a shared economy. Nobody wastes a step where a step might loosen gravel. Nobody swings a tool where a tool might strike a support. They duck without thinking where ceilings lower, turn sideways where paths narrow, and pause instinctively when a loaded bridge gives its faintest warning creak. Their posture carries a habit of attention: heads lifted a little to read overhead cracks, feet placed with care, shoulders angled through crowded spaces. They touch the town as they move. A palm brushes a support. Fingers trail along a groove cut for water. A heel tests a board before weight is given to it. In this way, the body itself becomes a kind of instrument for knowing the place.

Their skin is not colorless, but often muted by cool air and indirect light. Some are fair, some brown, some deeply darkened by heritage or by time spent working near the brighter shafts; the place does not erase difference, only dims it. Their eyes are not enormous, but they are practiced. They read shadow well. They notice the slightest change in brightness on a wall, the faint glimmer that means new moisture, the dulling of a surface that means dust has settled where it should not. In strong daylight from above, they squint and look away, not because they are blinded, but because their eyes have learned to favor softer contrasts. Many wear broad-brimmed caps or hoods when working under open shafts, partly against falling grit, partly against glare.

Their clothing is layered and repaired. Because damp is constant, they dress with the same logic they apply to architecture: keep the body from staying wet, allow air to move, protect what can be protected without pretending that water can be entirely defeated. They wear soft-soled shoes, often made of woven reed, hide, or felted fiber, sometimes with wooden oversoles for the lowest wet floors. The soles are quiet. That quietness is not ornamental; it is one of the town’s most ordinary courtesies. Their garments are fastened with toggles, pins, and cords rather than elaborate buttons. Hoods, wrappings, and shoulder capes are common. Cloth that has begun to rot is cut into smaller pieces and reused until it becomes stuffing, wadding, or tinder. Nothing worn by the body is treated as disposable.

They smell of the place, but not unpleasantly: wet stone, faint woodsmoke, fungal soil, oiled rope, sweat kept close in cool air. There is often a mineral sharpness to their breath in the lower chambers, and a warmer human smell in the sleeping stacks. They wash where water can be spared, but not lavishly. Cleanliness is practical: hands are cleaned before food, feet before entering dry sleeping areas, tools oiled after use. They know that in a closed, crowded space, dirt is not only an offense but a danger. Fouled water, spoiled cloth, and accumulated waste can become larger than any single mistake. So their cleanliness is tied to maintenance, not vanity.

They speak softly. This is one of the first things an outsider would notice. Their voices do not boom. They have learned to place words where they will not bounce harshly from stone or pass too far into neighboring rooms. Their language has likely grown rich in terms for moisture, stone condition, air movement, and structural risk. There may be several words for different kinds of damp: one for cold condensation on ceilings, one for seepage carrying silt, one for rising vapor, one for the darkening that precedes a new drip. There may be verbs that mean “to brace,” “to divert,” “to listen for,” and “to hold until the stone decides.” Their grammar may favor observation over command. Even children speak in sentences that sound, to an outside ear, unusually precise: not “the wall is wet,” but “the wall has begun to wet above the third shelf,” or “this drip has shifted toward the sleeping mats.”

They also use silence and gesture. A lifted hand can stop traffic on a narrow bridge. A finger drawn along a gutter can indicate a blockage. Two taps on stone may call attention to a sound that matters. A certain pause in conversation may mean that someone has heard a crack, a drip, or a change in airflow. They are not expressionless; they are simply tuned to small signals. A glance, a slight tilt of the head, a hand resting on a rope — these can carry meaning across crowded spaces without sound. In a place where every noise belongs partly to everyone else, gesture becomes a form of respect.

The people are not joyless. That would be a mistake. Their pleasures are constrained, not absent. They enjoy warmth after cold, dry socks after wet work, food eaten while sitting, a lamp lit safely, a task finished before the day’s light fades. They enjoy skill. A well-cut channel pleases them. A ladder set so perfectly that it needs no wedge is praised. A basket woven tightly enough to keep grain safe from damp is handled with admiration. They enjoy small jokes, often dry and understated, many of them about leaks, bad timber, or the ambitions of builders. They enjoy games that require little space: knot-making, counting games, memory contests, quiet races along familiar routes, balancing stones, or guessing where a new drip will fall. Children invent toys from scraps: little rafts for cisterns, clay animals, miniature ladders, cord figures that can be worked with the fingers.

Their emotional life is shaped by closeness. They know one another’s habits, breaths, footsteps, and moods. Privacy is not achieved by distance but by discretion. A curtain of woven reeds, a turned shoulder, a lowered lamp, an agreed-upon hour of silence — these create rooms within rooms. People learn not to stare into another’s sleeping alcove, not to repeat overheard arguments, not to ask questions when someone has gone apart to sit near a quiet pool. They are not without conflict. In such density, conflicts must arise: disputes over water rights, over storage space, over noise, over a prop that should have been moved, over a child who dropped a tool, over a neighbor whose compost grew too warm. But their survival depends on preventing small grievances from becoming structural failures. They repair relationships as they repair walls: early, practically, and with attention to cause.

Children are numerous and deeply valued, not as ornaments but as future keepers of the place. From infancy they are carried close, wrapped against bodies, lulled by the sounds of dripping water and low voices. They learn to walk quietly almost as soon as they learn to walk at all. They are taught to touch before they trust. They learn that a dry-looking board may be slick underneath, that a pretty white crust may hide soft stone, that a new puddle is not a toy but a message. They are given small responsibilities early: carrying a cup of water without spilling, turning a drying mat, watching a basin for an hour, sweeping dust from a walkway, feeding a fungal bed with measured compost. These tasks are not punishments. They are initiations into competence. A child who can hear the difference between a safe drip and a dangerous one is praised more than one who can simply recite a story.

Education is embodied. Children learn by following adults along maintenance rounds. They learn where not to stand when a load is being lifted. They learn how to pass a tool handle-first. They learn how to walk over a bridge without making it sway unnecessarily. They learn how to recognize a vent that has become blocked by a hanging mat. They learn how to clean a cistern without poisoning the water. They learn how to move in the dark without panic, using feet and hands and memory. They also learn the town’s ethics without needing them stated: water must be guided, not blocked; stone must be answered, not ignored; neighbors must be considered because neighbors are near. By the time they are grown, these are not rules so much as reflexes.

Elders are not set apart by frailty. Many remain active, their hands slower but more certain. Their value lies in memory. They remember which crack widened three seasons ago. They remember the last time a certain shaft admitted stormwater. They remember which timber came from a bad batch and where it was used. They remember how the town shifted when a ceiling fell in the upper crossing. They sit in pale light where their eyes can still read surfaces, and they teach by pointing. Some become arbiters in disputes because they have seen patterns repeat. Some become watchers of weather signs. Some no longer climb, but they mend cordage, shape pegs, tell stories, or listen to reports and say whether a sound described is familiar or new. In a place built on maintenance, age is not merely decline; it is accumulation of useful signs.

The social order is practical rather than monumental. There may be leaders, but they are not kings in the open sense. Authority belongs to those whose knowledge matches the problem. If a cistern is threatened, the water-tenders speak first. If a roof crack lengthens, the stone-readers and timber-setters decide. If a fungal bed begins to overheat, its keepers have command of the hour. There are councils in wide landings and on broad ledges, but they are likely brief, low-voiced, and focused. Decisions are measured in consequences: Will this redirect water? Will this weaken a support? Will this block a path used by carriers? Will this foul air below? The town does not admire abstract power. It admires competence that keeps many lives from colliding.

The people divide themselves by work as much as by household. There are water-watchers who know the habits of every seep, gutter, and basin. They can tell whether a drip has quickened because of last night’s rain or because a channel has shifted upstream. They mark levels in clay or on stone with small scratches. They know which basins fill first and which overflow last. There are cistern keepers who clean, cover, and ration stored water. They understand that water kept too still can sour, and water disturbed too often can cloud. They skim floating matter, shade delicate pools, and know when to let a basin rest.

There are stone-readers, perhaps not called that formally, who spend their lives watching fractures. They place tiny wedges or chips across cracks to see if movement continues. They press ears to walls when the pressure changes. They know which stones ring soundly when tapped and which have gone dull. They are not prophets, but they are the closest thing the town has to forecasters. When they recommend moving a storeroom or bracing a walkway, people listen.

There are timber-setters and rope-workers, people who know how to fit beams into irregular rock without forcing them. They understand lashings, leverage, and the way damp wood changes over time. They keep a reserve of straight poles, split planks, and flexible withes. They know which joints will hold and which must be checked weekly. Rope is precious to them. They mend it constantly, coil it properly, hang it where it will not rot, and replace it before it fails. In a town built across gaps, rope is not merely material; it is trust made visible.

There are fungal gardeners, and their work is among the most delicate. They tend beds in shaded hollows where composted waste, plant matter, and careful moisture produce pale caps, broad shelves, and soft white threads. They speak of their beds almost as one speaks of animals: temperamental, responsive, easily harmed by neglect. They know which substrates heat too quickly, which need more air, which should be covered, which must be turned. They harvest with care, leaving enough for regrowth. They dry surplus in thin air currents near upper vents. They know that fungus is not only food but also a sign of the town’s metabolic health. A bed thriving means waste is being used well. A bed failing may mean the air has changed, the water has shifted, or contamination has entered the compost.

There are tenders of reeds, mosses, and algae. Where light falls enough for plants to live, these people become farmers of narrow niches. They harvest reeds in rotation, bind mats, weave screens, and replace worn roofing layers. They encourage mosses on walls where their growth helps hold fine particles and mark stable moisture. They scrape algae from stone only where it can regrow. They understand that even sparse green life is productive if managed without greed. In the widest shafts, they may coax tough grasses and thin-leaved plants into pockets of washed-down soil, protecting their roots from foot traffic and sudden floods. They do not expect abundance. They expect continuation.

There are fish stewards who care for the pale inhabitants of cisterns and quiet pools. They do not always call the fish theirs; perhaps they consider the fish fellow residents with a duty to remain clean and few enough to survive. They know when to feed them, when to leave them alone, which pools are too cold, which are becoming too rich. They watch for lice, fungus, or clouded water. They may mark individual fish by scar, color, or habit, not sentimentally but because observation is easier when creatures become distinguishable. When a fish dies, it is noticed. In a small system, death is information.

There are bird-watchers, often children or elders, who know the upper ledges and the times of day when wings pass through light shafts. They do not chase birds unless a species becomes harmful. They observe which insects are being taken, whether nests are forming, whether a predator has entered. The sudden appearance of a hawk or a weasel-like animal can become a matter of serious discussion, because such a creature may disturb the balance more than it helps. Birds are allowed a limited liberty. Their songs are rare enough to be treasured. Their droppings may be collected carefully for use in gardens, but not allowed to foul water or food stores. Even in this, the town is precise.

There are compost keepers, perhaps less glamorous than any other workers but essential. They manage the slow transformation of waste into useful matter. They know how to keep heaps from becoming foul, how to mix damp and dry materials, how to keep beetles without allowing flies to overwhelm the space. They work near the lower levels, where air is cooler and heavier, and they are respected because everyone understands that a town with no way to absorb its own waste would poison itself. Their hands are often stained, their clothes earthy, their knowledge intimate with decay. They are not associated with disgust. They are associated with continuation.

There are firekeepers and heat-carriers. Because open flame is dangerous and fuel limited, these people manage small hearths, vents, lamps, and warmed stones. They know how to bank embers so they last without smoking. They know which clay vessels can hold heat and which will crack. They arrange for stones to be heated safely and carried in insulated baskets to warm sleeping shelves or workrooms. They understand that warmth here is not to be wasted as spectacle. It is portioned, moved, conserved. When a hearth is lit for cooking or repair, someone remains with it until it is fully out. Fire, like water, is treated as a force that must be given a path and a limit.

There are carriers, too: people whose work is movement itself. They bring timber down from above, haul clay up from lower seams, carry sealed bins of grain, transport oiled tools, ferry messages between levels. Their bodies are tireless in a quiet way. They know the best routes at different hours, which ladders are strongest, which bridges should not be loaded when wet. They often work in pairs or small groups, using low calls or rope signals. Their labor makes the town possible, because without movement the densely packed spaces would become separate, stranded cells.

What they eat reflects the place. Much of their food comes from above: grain, dried fruit, roots, salt, perhaps small animals or eggs, depending on their relationship to the surface. But below, they supplement and extend these supplies with what they can grow or gather. Fungi are central. They are dried, stewed, roasted on warm stones, ground into meal, or preserved in sealed jars. Fish, where plentiful, are eaten sparingly, more as seasoning or occasional nourishment than as daily abundance. Algae and tender water-plants may be scraped, washed, and cooked into thick cakes or broths. Reed shoots, roots, and grass seeds from light wells add bitterness and variation. Insects are not despised; certain larvae or beetles may be gathered from compost when numbers allow, roasted or crushed into paste. Nothing edible is dismissed, but nothing is taken without asking what its removal will do.

Their meals are quiet but not silent. Food is often prepared in shared niches where heat can be controlled. There are flat stones for warming, clay pots for slow cooking, baskets for soaking grain, sealed jars for fermenting or storing. Because fuel is limited, much is eaten softened, dried, or pre-cooked. They may favor porridges, dense cakes, broth thickened with fungal meal, and small portions of fish or seed. Water is drunk with gratitude but not wastefully. Children learn to sip, not spill. Scraps go to compost, fish bones to craft or soil enrichment, stale grain to sealed bins where it can be reworked. Eating is one more way the town cycles matter through itself.

They have art, but it is not separate from use. Beauty appears in the polish of a handrail, the symmetry of a woven mat, the clean curve of a water channel, the neatness of a repaired crack. They may decorate with arranged stones, knots, feathers fallen from visiting birds, dried fungal caps, or patterns scratched into clay. Music is subdued: tapping rhythms on hollow wood, soft humming, small bone flutes played near vents where sound can escape without filling every room. Stories are told in low voices, often during work. Many likely concern water: the flood that came through a forgotten shaft, the child who heard a crack in time, the builder who tried to wall off a seep and lost three rooms, the bird that brought a seed into darkness and taught people where to plant. Their heroes are not conquerors but maintainers.

Their relationship to the surface is neither rejection nor dependence alone. They are below, but not sealed away. Some people climb out regularly: watchers, traders, gatherers of timber and soil, those who tend relationships with people above. Others may spend many days below without seeing the sky. The upper ledges are places of exchange. Baskets are lowered and raised. Signals are sent by taps, reflected light, or calls that carry through shafts. News from above enters with goods: weather, conflict, harvest, disease, prices, deaths.
