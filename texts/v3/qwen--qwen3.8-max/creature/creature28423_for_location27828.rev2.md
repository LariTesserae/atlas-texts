What lives there is not a single body that can be pointed to. It is not a creature crouched in a corner, nor a face behind a leaf-screen, nor a worker bent over a vat. There is no fur, no feather, no shell, no eye turning toward a sound. If one asks who lives in that sunken place, the first answer is that the place itself has become the living one. The hollow is inhabited as a bell is inhabited by resonance, as a loom is inhabited by the pattern it keeps making. The dweller there is the whole wet, warm, working system: a slow, many-bodied vegetable municipality, saturated with water, threaded by machines, patient to the point of seeming eternal.

Yet that is only the first shape of the answer. As one stays longer, the inhabitant breaks apart into countless presences, none of them animal, all of them active. The mosses live there. The root platforms live there. The trained trunk-walls live there. The canals live there. The pumps, valves, wheels, lamps, cables, reservoirs, and dripping gutters live there too, not as dead tools, but as organs in a larger body. Everything in the hollow has been drawn into a single prolonged act of maintenance. The life of the place is not separate from its architecture. Its buildings grow. Its machines breathe through sap and condensation. Its streets are living tissue. Its population is made of green mass, mineral film, warm water, microbial slime, and old iron that has learned the habits of roots.

The oldest and softest citizens are the mosses and liverworts. They are not decoration and they are not merely covering. They are the skin of the hollow. They seal seams, hold warmth, slow evaporation, and keep stone from cracking too sharply in the damp heat. They live on the lips of channels, on the backs of pipes, on the shaded sides of pumps, on the knees of trunks, on every surface that remains still long enough to be touched by spores or fragments. They are so quiet that their labor could be mistaken for accident. But they are relentless. Given time, they will soften an edge, close a gap, turn a bare metal flange into a green cushion, and make a rusted plate feel as old as the ground. They are the first to notice a change in humidity. They swell when the air thickens, stiffen when warmth rises too much, and release their faint green smell into the close atmosphere. They do not flee, because there is nothing to flee from. They only spread.

Above this skin stand the larger plant bodies. They are the structural people of the hollow, if “people” can mean beings without faces, without limbs in any animal sense, without voices that form words. Their bodies are trunks, buttresses, interwoven stems, thick rhizomes, and broad leaves that have learned the shapes of walls, railings, arches, and roofs. Some have grown so completely into one another that there is no telling where one trunk ends and another begins. Their bark is folded into ridges like folded cloth in places, or polished smooth where moisture has run for years along the same path. They are the walls, but they are also the inhabitants of those walls. They do not occupy the courtyard as tenants occupy rooms. They are the courtyard’s upright flesh. They drink through their roots and through their leaves, they feel light as a weak warmth, they feel vibration as a tremor in the water film on their surfaces, and they answer by growing, by tightening, by altering the angle of a leaf, by sending a new root toward a crack where moisture gathers.

These plant bodies are not uniform. Some are low and spreading, making terraces and embankments. Others rise into narrow columns where old stone or iron once gave them a line to follow. Others again have become broad, roof-like canopies over vats and drying racks, their leaves overlapping like shingles. In the damp corners, some have grown into swollen, bladder-like forms that hold water in living reservoirs. They are not trees as the surface world knows trees. They are architectural growths shaped by confinement, warmth, and long use. They have been trained by the place, and they train the place in return. A channel that runs too close to one of them may be slowly lifted, shaded, narrowed, or bridged by roots. A pipe that leaks warm water may be surrounded by a thick knot of tissue that drinks the heat and turns the leak into a source of life. An old iron railing may disappear inside a stem until only a faint seam in the bark reveals that metal still waits within.

Among them are the water plants, the reeds, sedges, floating mats, and fibrous marginal growths that live along the canals. They are the restless citizens of the hollow, though their restlessness is still slow. They sway with the movement of water, bend under the weight of droplets, and comb the channels with fine roots. They keep the margins from washing away. They catch floating fragments of leaf and stem, not as predators catch prey, but as a net catches what the current brings. Some of these fragments take root where they lodge, becoming new patches of growth. The water plants are the ferrymen and road-menders of the hollow. They do not travel, but their offspring and broken pieces travel for them. They make the channels feel inhabited, as though the water itself had grown green thoughts and let them float.

Deeper than sight, thinner than touch, there are the microbial lives. They are in the water, in the wet bark, in the peat under the root mats, in the mineral film on warm pipes, in the black mirrors of standing pools. They are not visible as bodies, but their presence is everywhere. They digest fallen matter, release nutrients, soften fibers, and keep the warm wet world from choking on its own growth. They are the hollow’s chemistry. If the place has a mood, part of it lives in their work. When decay is balanced, the air smells sweet, peaty, and mineral. When heat rises in a vat or a channel slows too much, their activity changes, and the smell deepens, becomes darker, more fermented. They are not conscious in any way one could meet, but they make the hollow responsive. They translate dead matter into food, food into growth, growth into structure, structure into new shelter. They are the invisible workers without whom the visible mass would fail.

The machines also live there, though they live differently. They are not alive in the way a plant is alive, but they are not inert. They have rhythms, needs, weaknesses, habits, and failures. The waterwheels turn with the grave patience of large organs. The pumps thud and whisper, lifting water from lower basins to overhead reservoirs. Valves open and close according to pressure, temperature, and the slow commands of growth around them. Pistons move with measured force. Belts slip and hiss when they are damp. Pipes knock when pressure shifts. Gauges tremble. Lamps glow and dim as current changes. In the hollow, machinery has become almost bodily. A pump is a heart, but a heart made of iron, ceramic, and old brass. A reservoir is a lung or stomach, swelling and subsiding. A channel is a vein. A cable is a nerve. None of these parts rules the others, but all of them participate in one shared circulation.

What is extraordinary is that the machines have been adopted by the vegetable life. They are not merely overgrown; they are integrated. Roots have curled around casings until the metal seems to sit inside a living socket. Broad leaves shade valves that give off warmth, adjusting their growth over years until the valve is always partly covered, always cooled just enough. Stems thicken around bolts and gantries, making them stable without the need for repair by any outside hand. Where a machine vibrates, surrounding tissue becomes denser. Where a machine leaks, moss and rootlets gather the moisture and make use of it. Where a machine grows hot, nearby plants learn the shape of that heat and arrange themselves to benefit from it. The machinery is not conquered by the green world. It is befriended, absorbed, made into an organ of the whole.

If one had to give the inhabitant a name, it might be something like the Saturated Commons, or the Rooted Engine, or the Slow Body of the Undercourt. But no one there names it aloud. There are no voices to carry names. Instead, the hollow has signatures made of pressure, moisture, and sound. A drip-pattern becomes familiar. A pump’s rhythm becomes a pulse. A certain creak from a waterwheel becomes a sign that the lower basin is filling. A particular hiss from a valve becomes a warning that heat has gathered in one corner. These are not words, but they are communication. The place speaks to itself through its own operations.

Its senses are not eyes and ears. They are surfaces. The water mirrors are sensitive skins, trembling at the smallest vibration. Leaf blades are pressure plates, bending under droplets and changing angle with warmth. Root tips are tasters, reading minerals in the seep water. Bark is a record of scars, temperatures, and old wounds. Moss is a humidity gauge. Metal fittings tick when they warm and cool, and those ticks become part of the hollow’s awareness of itself. Electric lamps are not eyes, but they are centers of attention. Where a lamp burns steadily, leaves gather, moisture condenses, and growth becomes richer. Where a lamp fails, the neighborhood cools, darkens, and slows. The hollow does not see light so much as it metabolizes it.

Movement there is not the movement of walking, flying, or swimming. It is the movement of swelling, flowing, turning, thickening, and settling. A root may spend hours closing around a loose pipe. A leaf may bend gradually until a drip falls into a different channel. A vine may tighten as it dries slightly near a warm engine and thereby pull a lever no animal hand touches. A floating mat may drift a hand’s width over the course of a day and lodge against a mesh, becoming a new bank. A pump may start because a reservoir has grown heavy, and stop when the living wood above has drunk enough. The inhabitant moves like weather inside a room. It has no sudden gestures, but its gestures are powerful because they never cease.

There is a kind of attention in this. It is not curiosity as an animal knows it, but it is a readiness to respond. When a vibration passes through the hollow, moisture appears. This is not mere condensation, though condensation is part of it. It is also the place gathering itself around the disturbance. Water beads on leaves. Thin threads run down walls. The air thickens. A dropped tool, a warm surface, a shift in pressure—all are met with dampness. The hollow answers intrusion by absorbing it into its own circulation. If something remains, it will be wrapped. If something leaks, it will be drunk. If something breaks, roots will grow around it until the break becomes part of the structure. There is no rejection here, only incorporation.

The labor of the inhabitant is production without spectacle. It makes fiber by softening stems in warm water. It makes pulp by pressing and grinding plant matter between living surfaces and old stone. It draws sap along channels and concentrates it in vats. It presses oils from seeds or fleshy growths that fall into collection basins. It spreads starch-rich matter in shallow troughs where heat and microbes turn it into usable stores. It binds rope from long leaf fibers, twisting them by the slow contraction of stems and the tension of drying racks. It forms peat blocks from compressed decay, using them as fuel, insulation, or building mass. It distills water where purity is needed, drawing vapor from warm basins and catching it on cool leaves and copper coils. It generates electricity where the fall of water or the heat of decomposition allows, feeding lamps, small motors, and the pumps that keep the whole system moving. Nothing is made for display. Everything is made to continue.

The workshops are not empty, though no worker stands at a bench. They are filled with active forms. A press may be held in place by a living arch that slowly tightens. A drying rack may hang from stems that adjust its height according to warmth. A vat may be lined with tissue that secretes a waterproof resin or balances acidity. Storage cells may breathe, their living walls taking in excess moisture and releasing it when the air grows too dry. Gauges are watched not by eyes but by growth: a root may swell against a dial until the needle touches a point that triggers a valve. Levers are moved by the weight of water collected in leaf-cups. Switches are held in position by bark that has grown around them. The hollow’s industry is not operated from outside. It is performed by the same substances that compose the place.

Because there are no animals, there is no fear in the hollow. There is no alertness, no startle, no prey, no hunter. The emotional weather of the place is not panic but pressure. It can feel strained when a channel clogs, when heat rises too high, when a pump labors, when a reservoir overfills. It can feel eased when water finds its proper level, when a valve releases, when a lamp steadies, when a new root takes hold in a moist seam. These are not feelings as a mammal would have them, but they are states of the whole. The hollow has preferences. It prefers wetness without flood, warmth without dryness, flow without violence, growth without blockage. It prefers the sound of steady dripping to the silence of stagnation. It prefers the small amber glow of lamps to total darkness, because even shade-loving growth uses what little light is given.

There is no society of individuals, but there is order. The order is made of thresholds. Too much water in one basin and a pump lifts it. Too little in a channel and roots slow their drinking. Too much heat near an engine and leaves spread wider, moss thickens, condensation increases. Too much growth across a walkway and the weight of passing water, or the slow tension of stems, opens a gap again. Decisions are not made by councils, but by feedback. Still, one could say there is a parliament of limits: every living part has a vote expressed as growth, contraction, absorption, or release. The result is not perfect, but it is stable. The hollow has learned its own capacities. It does not exceed them for long.

Its memory is not written in books. It is written in scars, rings, mineral deposits, worn channels, repaired joints, and the shapes of old structures that have been absorbed. A buried arch remembers being stone by the way roots follow its curve. A broken stair remembers footsteps by the moss that still grows in the shallow depressions of its treads. A pipe remembers pressure by the staining and thickening around its joints. A trunk remembers injury by the folded bark that has closed over it. The hollow does not recall the past as story, but it carries the past as form. Every repair becomes part of its body. Every accident becomes a lesson written in tissue and rust.

Reproduction there is quiet and persistent. Many plants spread by runners and rhizomes, sending new shoots into wet cracks. Others break into fragments that float across channels until they catch in root-mesh and take hold. Where pollen or seed is needed, water carries it, or air currents drawn through ventilation shafts move it, or old mechanical brushes built into the infrastructure stir it from one growth to another. There is no courtship, no nest-building, no mating call. There is only continuation. A new patch of moss appears where a wet fragment settled. A new stem rises where a root found warmth. A new channel forms where water found a weakness. The hollow renews itself without drama. It does not need youth to replace age; it adds to itself continuously.

Death, when it comes, is not a spectacle. A stem may die where heat has changed or where water has stopped moving. It does not fall away as a corpse to be scavenged. It softens. It darkens. It becomes food for microbes and support for newer roots. Its fibers may be pressed into peat. Its hollowed length may become a conduit. Its mass returns to the floor and becomes part of the sponge underfoot. Nothing is wasted. There is no revulsion, no mourning, no carrion. Decay is another worker in the hollow, warm and patient. Even broken machinery may be left in place until it becomes a trellis, a housing, a source of mineral taste, or a shape around which new growth forms. The hollow does not discard easily. It keeps things until they have become something else.

At times, the whole presence seems to gather into a central mass. Not a ruler, not a brain, but a node where the life is especially dense. In the lowest part of the courtyard, perhaps, where several channels meet beneath an old turbine, there may be a great knot of roots wrapped around iron. Bark has folded over the casing in thick, wave-like ridges. Moss covers the upper curve. Brass valves protrude from the living mass, darkened by age and green with oxidation. Water enters the knot from one side, passes through the turbine with a low continuous sound, and leaves through another, slower and warmer. Cables disappear into the roots as if swallowed. Leaves grow from the mass itself, broad and dark, tilted toward the amber lamps above. This is not a face, but it is a place where attention seems to pool. If the hollow has anything like a heart, it is here. If it has anything like thought, it is the measured turning of that wheel, the opening of those valves, the slow thickening of those roots around what they must hold.

Standing near such a node, one might feel observed, though there are no eyes. The feeling does not come from being watched. It comes from being registered. The water on nearby leaves trembles faintly. A drip falls. A pipe ticks. The air grows a little heavier with moisture. The lamps reflect in black pools that seem to deepen as they return one’s image broken and green. The hollow notices heat, weight, breath, vibration. It does not judge. It does not attack. It simply begins to include whatever is present in its calculations of dampness and warmth. A boot on the root-floor leaves a depression that slowly fills with water. A hand against bark leaves a warmth that changes the condensation pattern. A voice, if one is foolish enough to speak there, is swallowed by the sound of dripping and machinery until it becomes only another vibration among many.

The inhabitant is not lonely. Loneliness requires absence, and here there is no absence. Every gap is filled. Every surface is claimed. Every silence is occupied by some small sound: a bead of water falling into a pool, a leaf folding under weight, a belt slipping, a root creaking against iron, a lamp humming, a channel lapping at its edge. The hollow is crowded in a way that has nothing to do with numbers. It is crowded with presence. One cannot move through it without touching something alive or once alive or made animate by long association with life. It presses close. It does not offer distance. It has no horizon, no far field, no empty prospect. Its beauty, if one can call it that, is the beauty of saturation: every detail answering another, every need met by a neighboring form, every sound returning as moisture or growth.

It is also, in its way, urban. It has streets, terraces, workshops, storage cells, channels, ramps, and public fronts. It has circulation, labor, maintenance, and infrastructure. But its urbanism is not made for crowds of separate beings. It is made for a single continuous body that is also many bodies. The streets are not for walking so much as for carrying water and roots. The terraces are not for viewing but for spreading growth in layers. The buildings are not shelters for inhabitants; they are inhabitants that have taken shelter in their own shapes. The hollow is a city that grew itself into a machine, or a machine that was colonized by plants, or a garden that learned industry. It resists simple category. It is all three at once.

Its temperament is not gentle, if gentleness implies softness toward outsiders, but it is not cruel. It is simply complete. It does not need permission. It does not ask whether something belongs; if something remains, it becomes part of the system. It does not hate dryness, but it resists it. It does not love water, but it moves water endlessly. It does not worship the machines, but it keeps them running. It does not fear darkness, but it tends the lamps where they are needed. It is patient in the way only growing things and old machines can be patient. It can wait hours for a drip to swell. It can wait seasons for a root to close around a bolt. It can wait years for a broken stair to disappear under moss. Time is not an enemy to it. Time is material.

If one must describe whoever lives there in a single image, the image should not be a face. It should be a motion: a bead of water gathering on a leaf edge, swelling until it holds the amber glow of a lamp, then falling into a black channel where it makes a small ring of light. Around that ring, roots shift almost imperceptibly. A valve sighs. A pump thuds somewhere behind a wall of bark. A waterwheel turns another degree. Moss drinks the spray. The air grows warmer by a fraction of a degree. The hollow adjusts.

That adjustment is the life there. The resident is the ongoing act by which water is lifted, heat is kept, growth is fed, channels are cleared, machines are turned, and darkness is softened by lamps. It is not one creature, but a communal body of vegetable industry. It is the root that holds the pipe. It is the moss that seals the stone. It is the microbial film that turns decay into nourishment. It is the pump that answers the reservoir. It is the leaf that bends under weight. It is the wheel that never stops turning. It is the old iron made soft by wetness. It is the living wall made strong by memory. It is the saturated, loud, patient, non-animal soul of the undercourt.

And if one asks, at last, who lives there, the truest answer may be this: no one, and everything. No person, no beast, no bird, no insect. But also nothing vacant. The hollow is inhabited by its own continuation. It is occupied by water learning the shape of roots, by roots learning the warmth of engines, by engines learning the patience of plants, and by plants learning to be ground, wall, roof, worker, and body all at once. What lives there is the place itself, breathing through drip and valve, growing through iron and bark, keeping itself alive in the dark with the endless, intimate labor of its own circulation.
