What lives there is not a resident in the old sense. There are no households, no sleepers, no eaters gathered around warmth, no faces turning toward windows. Yet the basin is inhabited, and inhabited deeply. What lives there has changed the terms of living: from breath to pressure, from blood to current, from voice to pulse, from body to pattern. The place is full of lives, but they are lives that have learned to exist as the place exists—slowly, darkly, coldly, and through continuation rather than intention.

The first life is the film.

It is the most common citizen of the drowned hamlet, the base population, the pasture and the skin. It covers every surface that has remained still long enough to be claimed: brick, iron, ceramic, glass, wood, stone, cable sheathing, the shoulders of engines, the inner lips of broken windows. In some places it is black and slick, almost lacquered, a living varnish made of bacteria and mineral residue. In others it is furred, velvet-thick, soft to the eye and treacherous to touch. Where stray current leaks through metal, it takes on a greenish smear, a weak luminous stain that does not brighten the water so much as prove that darkness has texture.

This film is not merely dirt. It eats. It feeds on dissolved salts, suspended fines, stray electrons, temperature gradients, the slow chemical memory of rust. It excretes crust. It binds silt. It softens edges. It is the patient mouth through which the basin digests its own wreckage. Without it, the machinery would be dead matter. With it, every surface becomes a slow organ. It turns iron into something closer to reef. It makes brick porous and receptive. It gives the cold a living skin.

From this film come the filaments.

They are not plants, though they have a plant-like patience. They are fine, hair-thin growths, metallic in stiffness, often clinging to charged surfaces in curtains, tufts, and trembling braids. They behave as if they are part biology and part circuitry. When a field passes near them, they lift. When current strengthens, they stiffen. When charge leaks from a damaged cable or a warm junction, they lean toward it with the obedience of roots seeking water. In some places they grow across ceramic insulators in pale threads, making the old switchgear towers look like shrines wrapped in saint-hair. In others they hang from pipe bundles like black threadweed, swaying in currents too slight to be felt by anything larger than a mote.

These filaments are among the nearest things the basin has to senses. They register changes in conductivity, pressure, heat, and motion. They glow faintly when stimulated: amber, green, blue-white, depending on what is passing through them and what they have grown into. They do not illuminate the streets, but they punctuate them. A line of them may brighten along a wall when a crustacean passes, then fade. A cluster may flare once, silently, when a capacitor discharges somewhere unseen. They are not aware in any human sense, but they are attentive in the way that a wire is attentive, or a trap, or a prayer repeated so often it has become architecture.

The animal life is sparse, but it is not absent.

Blind amphipods drift through the lower alleys and the flooded interiors of cabinets. They are pale, small, almost translucent, their bodies bent like tiny commas in the black water. They do not swim so much as browse, moving with intermittent flicks through settled silt, bacterial mats, and the dust of corroded insulation. They live in the hollows of machines: inside dead gauges, under valve wheels, in the shells of old lamps, in the blackened cavities where wooden drawers once sat. Their antennae test the water constantly. They are drawn to faint warmth, to microbial bloom, to the weak electric fields that gather around damaged cables. They are the small cattle of the place, feeding on film and fines, never numerous enough to be obvious, but persistent enough to be part of its metabolism.

There is also a flat crustacean, slow and deliberate, the color of wet slate and old bone. It moves through crevices with careful leg-work, as if each step must be approved by the architecture before it is taken. Its back is crusted with mineral growth, so that it often looks less like an animal than a mobile piece of the terrain. It grazes on bacterial velvet and the finer electrophytic hairs, but it also seems to use its legs to tap and test surfaces. It pauses at joints, at seams, at warm pipe-collars, as though listening through its own joints. It is not abundant. One may pass through a long alley and see none. Then, in a side chamber, one may be moving across a wall with the solemn patience of a monk maintaining a wall that no one has looked at in centuries.

Occasionally, something eel-like passes through the higher archways. It is rarely seen clearly. It appears as a pale line, translucent near the spine, dark at the edges, gone before the eye is sure it existed. It seems drawn to weak electric fields and to the faint biological shimmer of filament growths. It may hunt the amphipods. It may feed on the mote-clouds that gather near warmer machinery. It does not linger. It belongs to the basin in the way a rumor belongs to a town: by passing through and leaving doubt behind.

In the warmer pockets, where brine or engine heat leaks into the cold, there are clouds of organisms so fine they look like smoke. They are not insects, though they may swarm like them. They are not spores, though they drift with spore-like aimlessness. They gather around thermal plumes and charged surfaces, feeding on suspended iron and microbial films too small to see. When disturbed, they scatter into a dark mist, then slowly reassemble. Sometimes a sudden flicker in the water—a predator, a discharge, a pressure pulse—breaks them apart. A moment later the dark closes again, and the cloud reforms as if nothing had happened.

These animals are rare for a reason. The water is heavy with minerals and stray currents. It is not kindly to soft life. Most of what lives here has become partly mineral, partly electrical, or partly mechanical in its habits. The fauna does not dominate the basin. It slips through the basin. It survives in niches, in leaks, in margins, in the spaces between processes too large for it to understand.

The dominant life is the machinery.

Not machinery as a surface engineer would recognize it: not clean, purposeful, obedient machinery. Here the machines have become ecological. They are organs in a submerged body. They have metabolisms, rhythms, reflexes, appetites, failures, recoveries. They do not burn. They do not roar. They turn, pulse, store, release, contract, and settle. Their energy comes from temperature difference, current shear, brine density, pressure gradients, and the slow leakage of old charge. Their motion is so gradual that it can be mistaken for stillness. To see one of their movements fully, one would have to watch for a long time.
