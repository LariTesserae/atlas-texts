What lives there lives first at the seams.

Not across the whole basin, not evenly, not in anything like a green carpet of occupation. Life begins in the cracks, the damp margins, the warm threads, the shallow pools that last long enough to hatch something, the rock faces that weep, the hollows where fog settles and becomes almost water. The first inhabitants are barely visible: films, skins, crusts, stains, faint blooms. They are less like creatures than like the first slow opinions of the ground.

On the stable shelves of raw rock, blackish lichens spread with immense patience. They do not soften the landscape; they register it. They grow as dark, tight scabs and branching maps, eating mineral surface into dust over centuries. They are the place’s oldest-looking life, though many of them are young by the standards of such bodies. Where they hold, the rock begins to be altered. Acids work invisibly. Tiny fractures widen. The stone becomes a little less absolute.

In the hollows where meltwater lies long enough to catch weak light, there are olive films and microbial slicks. These are cold, quiet producers, turning mineral water and thin sunlight into something edible. They coat stones with a slippery, faintly living sheen. They bloom briefly when warmth, stillness, and light align, then fade when the pool cools, drains, or is scoured by grit. They are not beautiful in a decorative way. They are useful in the deepest sense: they make the first small surplus.

Where water moves gently enough, dull green moss furs the ground in low patches. It is not lush. It is close, tight, and practical, holding moisture and heat in a thin body. Within it live the basin’s smallest animals: mites, springtails, nematodes, rotifers, tardigrades, things that move in water films too fine for the eye to follow. They are the true hidden abundance of the place. When conditions turn against them, many of them dry, freeze, or enter suspended states, waiting until damp returns. The basin does not need to be generous for them to persist; it only needs to be intermittently survivable.

The pools, though shallow and cold, are where life becomes suddenly obvious.

In them, tiny crustaceans move in jerking clouds: fairy-shrimp-like forms, copepods, ostracods, small cold-water branchiopods. They hatch when water arrives, race through brief lives, feed on microbes and fine organic particles, mate, and leave resting eggs in the sediment. Those eggs can wait through frost, drainage, and drought. When the pool fills again, the water remembers them. This is one of the basin’s main tricks: apparent emptiness, then sudden population.

In the runnels and seeps, insect larvae gather where there is enough flow to bring food but not enough to tear them away. Some are black or blood-dark, thin as stitches, anchored to stone. Others live in saturated sediment, eating microbial films and dead matter. A few make themselves portable cases out of the basin’s own materials: black grit, pale mineral flakes, fragments of crust. They look like grains of the ground that have learned to move. Nothing here has been built in the human sense, but life still arranges matter for its own purposes.

When the air steadies and the fog thins a little, midges or their cold-tolerant equivalents rise in faint vertical columns. These are not great swarms, not dense clouds of summer insects. They are thin, hesitant, almost mechanical-looking threads of movement. The adults may live only long enough to mate and lay eggs. They are eaten by birds, by spiders, by other insects, or they fall into the water and become food that way. Their bodies are small offerings in an economy that wastes very little.

Spiders live low, because there is nothing tall to hang a web from. They make small sheet webs between stones, in hollows, along the edges of thermal runnels where insects are more likely to pass. The webs bead with mist and become briefly visible, then break or collapse. Some spiders hunt instead of waiting: flat, dark, quick things under stones, near damp crevices, along the edges of pools. They are patient in the way cold animals must be.

Beetles move beneath loose rock and through saturated sediment. Many are dark, flattened, and fast when exposed, with metabolisms tuned to scarcity. They eat carrion, fungal threads, dead insects, algae, whatever the basin provides in small amounts. Cold-adapted worms work the wet mineral mud, swallowing and sorting the first mix of silt, organic matter, and microbial life. They are not numerous enough to turn the ground into soil, but they begin the long process of making it less inert.

The warmer seeps and geothermal runnels support their own specialized life.

There, water arrives from below with a faint heat that feels startling against the cold air. Ice thins around it. Vapor rises. Mineral crusts form in pale rims and scales. The water may carry dissolved gases, salts, iron, sulfur, or other mineral traces that stain the stone rust, ash, bone-white, or black. These warm threads are small, but they are enormously important. They become the basin’s richest edges.

In them live heat-tolerant larvae, dark worms, microbial mats thicker than elsewhere, and sometimes amphibian life. Dark tadpoles are common in the shallower warm channels: black-bodied, slow until disturbed, then flicking away into the deeper runnel. In some places there may be salamander-like forms, paedomorphic and water-bound, keeping their feathery gills into adulthood because the warm water allows them to remain aquatic. They wait in the liquid warmth, feeding on larvae and small crustaceans, unwilling or unable to cross the cold expanses beyond their thermal corridor.

These warm points gather animals the way a small light gathers moths. Birds come to drink and bathe. Insects rise above them. Predators check them. Even the smallest beetle or shrew will orient toward warmth when the air is cruel. The heat is not comfortable in a domestic sense. It is not a hearth. It is a difference in temperature, and in this place that is enough to become a center of life.

Birds are among the most visible presences, though they do not fill the basin.

They come in loose groups, often as migrants or seasonal visitors, using the basin as a feeding and resting station rather than a permanent home. Wading forms move through the shallow pools and wet mineral flats: small shorebirds, quick-legged and alert, with bills suited to probing soft sediment, picking larvae from films, or snatching crustaceans from cold water. Their bodies are often gray, brown, black, or mottled, so that when they stand on pale crusts or dark gravel they look like marks made by ink.

Dark waterfowl rest on the larger pools, especially where wind and temperature make landing and takeoff possible. They feed on algae, microbial films, small invertebrates, and whatever the shallow water offers. Their calls can seem too loud for the emptiness: brief, nasal, echoing strangely over the flat ground before being absorbed by mist. They preen, float in tight groups, and lift away when pressure changes or when a predator passes too near.

Small predators follow the productive seams. There are quick-eyed birds that hunt insects, chicks, eggs, and small wetland animals along the margins. There may be falcon-like forms, jaeger-like scavengers, corvid equivalents, or cold-adapted owls that take advantage of the basin’s dimness. They do not live everywhere in the basin; they live along its lines of movement. They perch on stable rock, wait near warm runnels, or move from pool to pool with the economy of animals that cannot afford wasted flight.

Birds also import nutrients. This matters. The basin is poor in accumulated organic wealth, but birds bring the memory of other places in their bodies. Their droppings fertilize pools. Feathers fall into mud. Carcasses feed beetles, flies, scavengers, and microbes. A place where a flock rests for a few days can become subtly richer than the ground around it. The basin’s life is not self-contained; it is threaded into wider migrations, wider weather, wider geography.

Mammals are rarer, but not absent.

The basin does not support large herds or broad grass-eating populations. There is not enough volume, not enough soft vegetation, not enough settled ground. What mammals exist are small, sharp-metabolized, and tied to the most productive points. Shrew-like insectivores hunt through damp crevices, under stones, and along insect-rich seeps. They move quickly, eat often, and live close to the edge of energy deficit. Their fur is dense, often oily or water-resistant, and their bodies are built for squeezing into narrow dry spaces where the cold cannot reach as easily.

There may be vole-like or lemming-like forms in the few places where moss, sedges, and microbial crusts provide enough food, but they are local and sparse. They do not dominate the landscape. They are pockets of warmth and appetite in a cold room. Predators follow them: weasel-like animals, small and fierce, able to pursue prey into crevices and under rock shelves. These predators are not abundant, but they are efficient. They leave little evidence except occasional tracks, droppings, and the sudden absence of smaller lives.

No large animal belongs to the basin in the way large animals belong to forests or grasslands. If something bigger enters, it is passing through, likely following birds, water, or a temporary pulse of food. The place cannot support mass. It supports knots, seams, temporary concentrations.

Yet among all these lives, there are a few forms that feel especially native to the basin’s unfinished condition.

One is a kind of fog-insect: pale, moth-like or gnat-like creatures that emerge from microbial mats or wet crusts when the air is thick. They do not fly strongly. They drift, tremble, and gather in mist, their wings dull and water-resistant. They are short-lived. Birds eat them. Spiders take them. Some fall into pools and become food for larvae. They seem less like separate animals than like the atmosphere briefly becoming animal.

Another is the basin’s most constant bird-like resident: the seep-strider.

Seep-striders are not large. They stand low to the ground, gray and black, with dense plumage or feather-like integument that sheds water and traps enough warmth to keep them moving in conditions that would drive most animals to shelter. Their legs are long-toed and sure, adapted to wet stone, soft crust, and saturated ground that may not bear weight evenly. Their bills or snouts are sensitive, able to read temperature, chemical traces, and movement in the water film. They feed on larvae, crustaceans, insects, amphibian eggs, and whatever small life gathers in runnels.

They do not build. They make no nest, no burrow, no stored cache, no structure. They roost on stable rock in loose groups, often facing the wind, one or two individuals alert while the others rest. If they breed in the basin, they use warmth and camouflage rather than construction: eggs placed in mineral gravel, warmed by a seep or by weak sun through thinning cloud, the young mobile almost as soon as they hatch. Nothing about them suggests accumulation. They are life adapted to a place where almost nothing can be kept.

Seep-striders seem to follow the basin’s breathing more than any fixed territory. When a thermal pulse opens, they arrive. When a channel dries or a pool freezes, they thin away. They move along gradients the way other animals move along paths, except their paths are not repeated in the same way twice. They are sensitive to pressure and charge. Before a storm, they may become still, heads lifted, as if reading something in the air. Their calls are rare: low clicks, brief signals, mostly used when the group is spread too far by fog.

They are not social in a warm or conspicuous way. They are coordinated by necessity. Their presence gives the basin something like attention without ownership. They watch, test, adjust, and move on.

There are also the slower residents, the ones that are easy to miss because they do not move in ways the eye expects.

Black crusts that seem mineral may contain living tissue. Certain low cushions or scab-like growths on wet rock may be lichens, mosses, or lichened colonies, but in some places they blur into animal or quasi-animal life: slow, filtering, condensation-dependent bodies that grow where fog is reliable. They do not stand up. They do not have faces. They spread in dark patches, bead with moisture, and trap airborne particles, microbes, and mineral nutrients. They are not abundant, but they are important. They help make the wet rock more than rock.

In saturated sediment, there are things that live as barely visible threads: fungal networks, worm-like organisms, cold-adapted larvae, microbial colonies. They do not create the visible drama of the basin, but they perform its quiet conversions. They break down what falls, what dies, what is carried in by wind and bird. They turn scarcity into the next small meal.

The food web here is not broad. It is tensile.

It stretches across emptiness and tightens at productive points. A shallow pool blooms with algae. Algae feed larvae and micro-crustaceans. Larvae and crustaceans attract insects, amphibians, birds. Birds bring predators and scavengers. Predators leave scraps, droppings, feathers, and eventually their own bodies. Microbes and worms take those remains and return them as mineral richness. Nothing is lush, but nothing is wasted. The basin’s abundance is secret because it appears only where conditions allow it to concentrate.

Death is incorporated quickly, though cold slows the process. A bird that dies by a pool may be found by beetles, flies, shrews, or scavenging birds. Its feathers scatter. Its bones become small white facts against dark gravel. Water, frost, and mineral movement erase the evidence over time. The basin is not cruel. It is exact. Energy moves where it can. Life clusters where gradients permit. The rest remains open.

The inhabitants leave almost no permanent mark.

No paths persist. Water smooths them. Frost cracks them. Wind fills them with mist and sediment. Tracks in wet mineral ground last only until the next drip, rain, or freeze. Feathers sink or are buried. Eggshells, if they exist, are small and quickly broken down. Even the channels that animals use are not truly theirs; they are the basin’s own temporary decisions. The place erases evidence with the same patience it uses to produce it.

This is why the basin feels both empty and watched.

There are eyes here, but not many at once. A bird stands at the edge of a pool, still as a stone. A predator pauses on a low rise, checking movement along a seep. A spider waits in a mist-beaded web. A seep-strider lowers its head to read the temperature of a runnel. A tadpole hangs motionless in warm water until a shadow passes. Attention is scattered, brief, and intense. It does not belong to a single mind. It belongs to the operation of the place.

Weather changes the distribution of life more than any season in the ordinary sense.

When fog lowers, the basin contracts. Animals draw closer to known seams. Birds may stand still until visibility improves. Insects drop lower. Seep-striders move by short steps, keeping contact through rare clicks. When fog lifts, the world seems to have been rearranged. A new pool may have formed. A seep may have strengthened. A warm patch may have released vapor into the air. Life responds quickly. Birds arrive. Insects rise. Crustaceans hatch. Microbial films brighten by a shade almost too subtle to notice.

Cold is the constant pressure. It lives in the breath, the fingertips, the wet stone, the slow drawing of heat from anything that touches the ground. But the cold is not uniform. It is interrupted by warmth from below, by weak light, by sheltered hollows, by bodies clustered together on stable rock. These interruptions are small, but they govern everything. The inhabitants know them not as comfort but as opportunity.

Electrical charge affects them too.

When the air carries charge, fine hairs lift. Mist seems to tremble. Pools brighten without any change in light. Insects may become erratic. Birds fall silent or lift away in sudden loose groups. Seep-striders grow still. Predators pause. Even the smallest lives seem to register the gradient. Lightning, when it comes, is often muffled, internal to cloud, a broad brightening of fog rather than a clean strike. If it touches the ground, it leaves little evidence: perhaps fused mineral grains, a smell of ozone, a darkened patch of stone. The basin does not dramatize its electricity. It simply incorporates it.

The animals are not separate from the basin’s industrial character. They are part of it.

Microbes convert mineral water into food. Insects convert microbial films into bodies. Birds convert scattered abundance into movement, droppings, eggs, feathers, and migration. Predators convert smaller lives into fewer, more concentrated lives. Worms and larvae sort sediment. Crustaceans stir shallow pools. Even the act of feeding is a kind of processing. The basin operates through gradients, and life is one of the fluids moving through those gradients.

No creature here makes an artifact. No creature needs to. The place itself provides the functions that artifacts would provide: shelter in crevices, warmth in seeps, water everywhere, food in concentrated seams, concealment in color and stillness. Where tools might seem useful, the basin instead offers timing. The right pool at the right temperature. The right insect hatch. The right thermal pulse. The right fog cover. The right moment to arrive, feed, rest, and leave.

If one asks what lives there, the first answer is: small, cold-tolerant, moisture-dependent life, gathered in knots and threads.

If one asks who lives there, the answer becomes stranger.

There is no single owner, no ruling form, no dominant intelligence. But there are beings whose behavior gives the basin something like a face. The seep-striders are the closest to such a face: patient, watchful, gradient-following, unbuilding, leaving no trace except the temporary pressure of their feet on wet stone. They do not master the place. They attend to it. They are its most obvious continuous observers, though even they may vanish for days when conditions turn against them.

And perhaps the truest “who” of the basin is not any one species but the distributed attention of all its lives together: the bird watching the pool, the spider sensing vibration in its wet web, the amphibian hanging in warm water, the beetle under stone, the crustacean cloud jerking through a newly filled hollow, the microbes blooming in weak light. None of them owns the emptiness. All of them use it. All of them pass through its unfinished rooms.

The basin is empty of mass, but not of presence.

It lives in stains and films, in hatching pools, in insect columns, in dark birds standing like ink marks on pale ground, in warm runnels where improbable amphibians persist, in small predators checking hollows, in fog-drinking bodies that seem to belong to the weather itself. Its inhabitants are not numerous enough to fill it, but they are enough to make it active, alert, and continually becoming.

What lives there lives as the place lives: provisionally, exactly, and in the middle of being made.
