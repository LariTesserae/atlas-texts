Where birds roost, the ledges are powdered with a dry, pale flour: guano, feather-dust, seed-husks, and the fine chalk of old stone worn by countless small feet. The roosts are not abandoned to chance. They are scraped clean at intervals, built up again with fiber, shed snake-skin, insect silk, and strips of bark. The nests hang under broken vaults like closed fruits, each one tethered by cords to keep it from falling into the dim below. In the morning, when the first slanted light warms the upper air, the birds wake not with a flood of song but with a dry percussion: wing-claps, bill-rattles, sharp metallic cries, the sound of many small bones and feathers moving inside stone.

The people who live in the Dry Court are shaped by this sound.

They have no single name that translates cleanly into surface speech. Among themselves they use a word that means something close to “the warm ones,” or “those who keep the room,” or simply “the living inside.” Outsiders, if any ever find the seam and survive the sideways passage down, might call them dust-born, resin-kin, salt-sworn, or the dry people. None of those names are wrong, but none are sufficient. They are not a separate species so much as a people made continuous with their place: a human population long sealed within a warm, waterless hollow, adapted by habit, selection, and ritual to a world in which moisture is not given but kept.

They are slight and wiry, heat-loving, narrow in the waist and long in the hand. Their bodies have learned thrift. They do not sweat freely; when they labor in the upper shafts, heat leaves them through quick breath and the thin skin at wrists and throat. Their skin is dry, fine-grained, and often dusted with pale powder, as if the chamber itself has laid a blessing on them. Some are the color of old clay, some salt-white, some bronze, some gray-green from the plant oils they rub into their arms and faces. Their lips crack if neglected, so they seal them at night with wax or resin. Their eyes are large and quick, accustomed to green half-light, and they have a way of turning their heads in small, exact movements, tracking a beetle on a wall or a moth in a light shaft without seeming to look directly at it. In the deeper dark, their eyes catch the glow of fat-lamps and larvae with a faint shine.

Their hands are their most obvious inheritance. The fingers are long, the palms calloused, the nails worn into hard, useful points. They can untangle a knotted vine in darkness, feel whether a seedpod is ripe by the faint dry rattle inside it, and climb rope bridges with a silence that surprises visitors. Their feet are broad and quiet, accustomed to the felted floor and to narrow ledges where a careless step might send a shower of pebbles echoing through the whole court. Children learn to walk without striking stone. A heavy footfall is considered rude, not because it is loud, but because it wastes the chamber’s attention.

They dress in layers that are themselves dry gardens. Their clothes are made from beaten inner bark, woven moth-silk, leaves stitched flat with insect thread, feathers turned inward for warmth, and panels of thin, cured lizard skin. Nothing is washed in water, because there is no water to wash with. Instead garments are smoked, dusted, scraped, anointed with plant oils, and left in warm currents to sweeten. A well-kept cloak smells of resin, warm dust, crushed leaves, and the faint animal musk of the court. They decorate themselves with seedpods, hollow beetle shells, chips of salt, bird bones, and tiny stone beads. When they move, they make a small dry music: a whisper of fibers, a clicking of ornaments. This is not accidental. In the Dry Court, silence is not the absence of sound; it is a signal. To move without any sound at all is to be dangerous, ill, or outside.

Their homes are built into the vertical body of the chamber. The floor belongs to the court’s digestion: beetles, ants, pale litter-dwellers, fungal threads, fallen leaves, shed feathers, and the slow breakdown of generations. The people cross it, work on it, harvest from it, but they do not usually live upon it. They live above, in ledges, alcoves, broken galleries, old chutes, and hanging platforms tied to ancient masonry. Their houses are shallow rooms and curtained niches. Doorways are hung with strings of seeds, strips of bark, or curtains of woven moth-wing that make a soft rasp when someone passes. Inside, the walls are polished smooth by bodies and hands. Sleeping places are lined with felted leaf-fiber, down, and the soft cast-off skins of reptiles. Families sleep close, not only for warmth but for reassurance; the Dry Court is warm, but the stone remembers cold, and the plateau above can press its chill down through cracks on bitter nights.

They do not build large fires. Open flame is dangerous in a sealed dry place filled with resin, dust, and old growth. Instead they use contained heat: fat-lamps in stone cages, ember-boxes lined with salt-crusted clay, compost pockets where decaying plant matter gives off steady warmth, and deep vents where the chamber’s own stored heat breathes upward. Their lamps burn slowly, fed by rendered insect fat, bird oil, and plant resin. The light is gold-brown and intimate. It does not flicker like surface fire; it beads and sways, casting a thick glow that makes nearby leaves look lacquered and turns human faces into masks of amber.

Their food is a cuisine of enclosure. Nothing is poured. Nothing is spilled. Every gift of moisture is eaten, sucked, chewed, or pressed into the mouth from its natural vessel. They harvest thick-stemmed vines whose sap comes slowly, not as a running liquid but as a heavy, milky bead that swells, stretches, and hardens if left too long. They collect it in sealed tubes of hollow reed or cured gut, then eat it fresh before it sets, or work it into resin-cakes that last for months. They cultivate bulbs and fat roots in shaded crevices, splitting them carefully so the inner tissue can be chewed for its bound moisture. They grow fruits that seem dry outside but are dense with rich, oily flesh. They grind seeds into flour, mix it with insect fat, and bake it into hard flat cakes on warmed stone. They eat beetle larvae, moth pupae, roasted crickets, bird eggs taken under strict law, dried lizard flesh, and the occasional snake, which is considered a solemn meal.

They know the difference between thirst and desire. A child in the Dry Court learns early that the mouth may feel empty without the body needing drink. They are taught to breathe through the nose, to keep the lips closed, to let saliva gather slowly, to eat the right leaves before climbing. The deepest insult is to waste what is inside. To spill sap, crush a water-rich bulb without eating it, or cut a vine in careless harvest is not merely foolish; it is a moral failure, a breach with the whole court. Their proverbs are dry and exact. “The hand that spills is empty twice.” “What is kept inside is given by the body.” “A full room is not a wet room.” They do not speak freely of rain. When they do, it is as one speaks of a dangerous god: distant, excessive, impossible to bargain with.

Their gardens are vertical and patient. Every family tends some part of the green body of the court. Some prune the waxy vines that climb the walls. Some tie aerial rosettes to cornices, training their cupped leaves to catch falling dust, feathers, and dead insects. Some tend the fat-stemmed shrubs whose trunks store sap and whose papery bark must be harvested without killing the plant. Some cultivate flowering cushions that open only when the light shafts are strongest, brushing their heavy pollen onto trays of woven fiber. Others keep the lower dark: fungal beds in deep litter, pale and slow, fed with shed skins, dead beetles, broken seed husks, and the dry remains of meals. Nothing is wasted. Even a feather has several lives: first as ornament, then as insulation, then as food for beetles, then as powder in the floor.
