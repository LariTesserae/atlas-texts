What lives there is not first a shape. It is a pressure, a listening, a hunger distributed across wet surfaces and hollow volumes. If you demand a census, the basin will give you one in the wrong order: it will begin with the insects, then the mosses, then the mineral films, then the towers themselves, and only lastly with anything you could call a person.

The most obvious inhabitants are the animals, and they are abundant enough to seem like the city’s true population. They live not in rooms but in acoustic niches, not behind doors but inside intervals of echo. The smallest of them are almost invisible: midges and flying motes so fine that they appear as moving glints in the humid air, or as a slight roughening of the light when they gather in clouds above the terraces. These tiny creatures are born in the standing pools and in the wet crusts where algae glow, and they rise in swarms whenever the air pulses with warmth or the hidden machinery of the basin shifts into a new hydration cycle. Their wings do not merely beat; they tune. A cloud of them can produce a high, glassy shimmer that seems to hang in one place, and when a predator passes below, the swarm changes pitch all at once, like a breath drawn sharply through teeth.

Larger insects cling to the damp faces of the causeways and the inner walls of the hollow towers. Some have carapaces polished like dark resin; others are furred with spore-dust and filamentous growths. Many possess resonating chambers in their thoraxes or abdomens, and they use the architecture the way a musician uses a sounding board. A single beetle, no bigger than a thumb, can fasten itself to a mineral ledge and produce a tone that rolls outward through three adjacent chambers, returning to it altered by each surface. These beetles do not defend territories by distance alone. They defend frequencies. Two rivals may remain motionless for a long time, each sending a patterned call through the same hollow, waiting for the other’s rhythm to falter. When one finally yields, it often does not flee; it changes its note and becomes part of the background chorus again.

The amphibious creatures are subtler and stranger. They live in the tanks, the leaf-cups, the narrow canals, and the thin sheets of water that slide down glossy walls. Their skins are slick and patterned to imitate algal crusts, so that they seem less like animals than like animated patches of green-gold film. Some are broad and flat, pressing themselves against wet stone until they appear to be mineral deposits. Others are long and ribbon-bodied, capable of moving through mere moisture rather than true water, riding capillary films upward along vertical surfaces. Their calls are rarely simple croaks. Most are sequences: a first sound, then a pause timed to the basin’s echoes, then a second sound that only becomes meaningful when the architecture answers. A hidden amphibian can make it seem as though three or four creatures are calling in coordination, though the listener is hearing one body distributed through time by the city’s shape.

The eel-like things in the deeper tanks and flooded channels are not always aquatic. Some climb wet walls with slow, muscular grace, their bodies secreting a faint sheen that keeps them from drying out. They feed on microbial mats, on larvae, on the soft organic films that collect in corners, and in doing so they clean the city’s finest conduits. Their movement is not random. They follow grooves and capillary tracks as though reading veins beneath skin. Where they pass, the surfaces behind them become more reflective, more alive, more ready to receive condensation.

Birds, or what pass for birds in this basin, occupy the upper openings and the broad hollow crowns of the towers. Many have no true feathers. Some are covered in fine filaments that collect moisture, so that when they perch in a shaft of dim amber light they appear edged with trembling droplets. Others fly on stiff membranes that make almost no sound themselves but stir the air enough to set nearby mosses shivering. Their nests are not cozy things; they are acoustic devices. Built from root-fibers, fungal husks, shed membranes, and mineral flakes, they are placed in the mouths of towers where rising warm air can carry their calls downward or outward. Some nests amplify the parent’s call. Others filter it, making the bird’s presence known only in certain chambers. The young are fed not only on insects but on regurgitated mineral slurry and luminous fungal paste, so that from the beginning their bodies are incorporated into the basin’s chemistry.

Predators live here too, and they are shaped by the same deafening abundance. The small ones move along ledges with low, fluid bodies, their limbs set wide for stability, their skins adapted to damp rather than speed. They hunt by counter-rhythm: entering the pattern of a chorus with almost no disturbance, then striking when the surrounding sound briefly reorganizes around them. The larger predators are often stillness itself. They wait inside hollow arches, in unfurnished chambers, or beneath fungal shelves wide enough to shelter a crouching body. Some can produce a sudden resonant pulse, a pressure burst that stuns smaller animals or sends insect clouds into disarray. Others simply open their mouths and let the architecture drive prey toward them, having learned which corridors turn panic into confusion. Their eyes catch the faint biological light in scattered points, and when they move, the noise of the basin often changes before their bodies are seen: insects fall silent, frogs alter their timing, and the whole auditory texture tightens.

Yet if you stop at the animals, you have only described the loudest citizens.

The second population is the living surface. Mosses coat the stable planes in black-green velvet, but they are not mere ground cover. They are acoustic skins. Each kind thickens where certain tones repeat, damping some frequencies and allowing others to travel. In places where the basin’s engineered hum is strongest, the moss grows in dense whorls, soft and deep, as if the stone itself were trying to breathe through it. When rain begins, the moss brightens and gives off a smell of crushed green sweetness; when dryness persists too long, it contracts and turns brittle, and the channels beneath it become quieter, slower, more mineral.

Root-mats crawl across vertical walls in branching networks, pale and sinuous, more like exposed nerves than ordinary roots. They cling to fractured basalt, to calcium crusts, to the white porous material that may be rock or grown infrastructure. These mats drink from condensation, bind loose grains, and carry nutrients across distances that would otherwise be separated by empty air. When cut, they do not bleed sap so much as a faintly luminous fluid that seals the wound and leaves a scar that later becomes a new growth line. They are not conscious in any simple sense, but they remember. A wall repeatedly wetted by a particular sequence of droplets will develop a root-map suited to that sequence. A terrace that has hosted a certain chorus will keep a thicker mat there, oriented toward the source of the sound.

Fungal shelves extend from cracks like pale balconies, and they are among the most active of the basin’s living structures. Small ones tremble with every passing vibration. Large ones stand broad enough to shelter animals beneath, their undersides gilled or porous, their edges faintly luminous. Some expand rapidly when moisture arrives, opening like slow hands. Others release spores in timed pulses, and those spores carry light, scent, and perhaps information. In certain districts, the fungal shelves flash in slow patterns that seem to coordinate insect swarms or signal the beginning of a water cycle. Whether they command these events or merely respond to them is difficult to say. In this place, response and command have blurred.

Algal skins shimmer inside thin water channels, giving the conduits a green-gold glow. They are living filters, converting weak light and dissolved minerals into sugars, films, and oxygen. They also register changes in vibration. When the basin’s tone shifts, the algae alter their color slightly, becoming brighter, duller, more amber, more blue-green. A visitor might mistake this for mood. It is closer to diagnosis. The algae show what the water is doing, what the air is carrying, and whether the mineral surfaces are about to repair, erode, or release stored moisture.

The broad-leafed climbers and translucent fronds that unfurl into the hollow air are not merely decorative. They are catchers and directors. Their leaves are shaped to intercept drifting mist, gather droplets, and channel them into fissures or basins. Some open their pores only when struck by a particular rhythm of sound. Some turn slowly toward repeated tones, as plants elsewhere might turn toward light. Here, sound is a kind of nourishment, or at least a signal for growth. Where a tone persists, vegetation leans into it. Where silence dominates, surfaces dry and biological crusts withdraw.

Beneath all this, and perhaps more important than any visible creature, lives the engineered layer. It is not a population in the ordinary sense. It has no clear bodies, no obvious faces, no movement that can be followed with the eye. It exists in the mineral skin of the basin, in the calcium-like crusts, in the black glassy veins, in the porous white material that seems grown rather than quarried. It responds to frequency. It repairs. It adjusts. It reorganizes matter at scales too small for direct sight. When the correct resonance passes through a hollow tower, faint lines of light may appear along a crack, glowing like heated wire, then fade as the surface seals itself. When rain strikes a sequence of gutters, sluice-gates change without visible machinery. When a sustained noise becomes damaging, nearby surfaces roughen to absorb it; when a signal must be carried farther, other surfaces smooth themselves into reflectors.

This layer is not dead technology. It is not a set of tools left behind by vanished builders. It is too responsive, too integrated with fungal crusts and water films and insect behavior. It may be descended from machines, or from organisms trained into machine-like habits, or from something that was never either. It behaves like a careful gardener made of physics. It dissolves dead matter, recombines mineral grains, clears blocked channels, and encourages new growth where moisture and sound coincide. It does not stand apart from the ecology. It is woven into it. The city’s construction and its living processes are not two systems but one continuous operation.

And through all of these layers moves something that is harder to name.

If one must say who lives in the basin, the answer begins here: not with a species, not with a tribe, not with a single ruling intelligence, but with a distributed commonwealth of moisture, vibration, growth, and appetite. The basin is inhabited by a resonance that has become active enough to maintain itself. It is not a creature with organs in the way a body has organs. It is more like a vast, wet instrument that has learned to play itself, and then learned that playing itself is a form of living.

This inhabitant has no fixed shape. It is present wherever the system adjusts. It is in the way a tower changes pitch when humidity rises. It is in the way an insect swarm veers away from a dry plaza and toward a moist shaft. It is in the way a predator’s stillness causes the surrounding chorus to reorganize. It is in the way repair lines appear when a particular echo returns. It is in the way rain chooses one district and not another. It is in the low engineered hum that seems to come from the ground itself, a hum that may be the oldest voice in the basin, older than the animals that have learned to use it.

It does not think in words, if it thinks at all. It thinks in gradients. It remembers in mineral seams, in root patterns, in the orientation of fungal shelves, in the wear of channels, in the timing of echoes. Its attention is not a gaze but a redistribution of sensitivity. When something unusual happens—a falling stone, a new animal, a visitor’s footfall—the basin does not look toward it with eyes. It listens by altering itself around the disturbance. An echo may arrive a fraction too soon. A droplet may pause on a membrane. A swarm may brighten and then dim. A nearby tower may begin to ring at a frequency that seems related to the intruder’s breathing. The place tests what enters it. It asks, in its own way, whether the new rhythm can be incorporated.

There are moments when this distributed life seems almost to gather itself into a local presence. During heavy condensation, when mist thickens beneath the overhangs and the glowing moss dims in unison, certain chambers become charged with a sense of nearness. The air grows heavier. The sound loses its scattered quality and becomes focused, as if many small processes have momentarily agreed. In such moments, a visitor might see shapes that are not quite bodies: a tall negative space in a curtain of rain, an outline where droplets avoid falling, a column of midges whose blue flashes move with deliberate slowness, a shadow too still to be an animal and too attentive to be a trick of light. These are not true bodies. They are temporary assemblies, local knots in the basin’s larger activity. They form when a pattern becomes strong enough to recruit matter and behavior around itself. They vanish when the pattern disperses.

If there are people here, they are not people as flesh and household and lineage are usually understood. They are persons made of recurrence. They may condense out of a stable chord that persists through several rain cycles. They may be born when a particular water route and a particular acoustic signature become inseparable. They may be as small as a single chamber’s habit or as broad as an entire district’s weather. They do not occupy rooms because rooms are not their bodies. They occupy intervals: the delay between call and answer, the pressure before rain, the pause after a predator moves, the moment when a repair line glows and the stone accepts it.

Their “society” is not built around possession. There are no stored goods, no private interiors, no domestic refuse. The basin’s emptiness is their space. They do not need furniture because their needs are structural: moisture must move, sound must remain coherent, mineral surfaces must repair, biological films must grow and be grazed, predators must keep certain populations from overwhelming others. Their conflicts, if they have them, are not wars over objects but disputes over rhythm. A dissonant pattern may be corrected by redirecting water into a channel until the offending frequency is dampened. A persistent dryness may be answered by seeding a district with spores that attract condensation. A creature that becomes too loud, too numerous, or too destructive may be allowed to meet the basin’s older appetites: fungal dissolution, larval consumption, predator attention.

Their art, if they have art, is weather. They compose rains. They arrange local showers over plazas while adjacent terraces remain dry. They make one arch sing in a low chest-felt note while another answers with a crystalline upper tone. They train vines to open at precise moments so that falling droplets shatter into mist instead of striking stone. They allow certain animals to bloom, then check them, then encourage their return in altered patterns. They make the basin feel more intense, more coordinated, more alive. They do not separate beauty from maintenance. Here, beauty is maintenance performed with exceptional precision.

They may also be the descendants of the builders, or the builders’ final habit. The landscape still bears seam-lines of fabrication, crisp edges that suggest printing, cooling, lifting into place. If beings once made this basin, they are not visible as makers now. They may have been absorbed into the logic of what they made. Their intentions may persist as rules in the mineral crusts, as preferences in the water channels, as templates in the towers’ hollow throats. In that sense, the basin is not abandoned. It is inhabited by its own origin. The makers did not leave. They became the operating system, then the ecology, then the chorus.

The animals, meanwhile, are not mere extensions of this larger being. They have their own hungers, fears, cycles, and small satisfactions. The beetles do not know themselves as notes in a vast composition; they know damp stone, rival tones, the urge to call and be answered. The frogs know the taste of water films and the safety of echoing concealment. The membrane-birds know nesting ledges, the pull of warm air, the sweetness of condensation collected from their own filaments. The predators know stillness and the sudden opportunity created by silence. Yet their lives fit into a metabolism larger than any one of them. They are autonomous and also functional. They are citizens and organs and weather at once.

This is why the basin feels occupied without being populated. There is no crowd, but there is constant presence. There are no faces, but there is watchfulness. There are no conversations, but there is communication everywhere. The hum in the ground, the pulse of fungal light, the timing of insect swarms, the behavior of condensation, the subtle lengthening of corridors, the delayed arrival of echoes—all of these form a language of pressure and response. To live there is to be fluent in that language. To be alien there is to make too much noise without meaning.

A visitor would be noticed immediately, though not in the way a person is noticed in a room. The basin would detect the visitor’s footfall, the disturbance of warm air, the unfamiliar scent, the rhythm of breath, the electrical and thermal irregularity of an outside body. The first response might be small: a nearby insect chorus shifting key, a droplet falling from a bridge at an odd moment, a faint dimming of algal glow. Then the architecture would begin to test the stranger. Footsteps would be echoed back slightly changed. A hand placed on a wall might find the surface warmer than expected, or slick with moisture that seems to move toward the fingers rather than away. A tower might produce a low tone in answer to a spoken word, not imitating it exactly but probing its structure.

If the visitor resisted the noise, tried to stand outside it, to treat it as an assault or an interruption, the basin would likely become more difficult. Corridors might feel longer. Moisture might withdraw from nearby surfaces. The light would dim or bend in unhelpful ways. The hum would rise until thought became sluggish. Predators might drift closer, not because they are commanded to, but because dissonance attracts the basin’s corrective life. If the visitor instead learned to move with the sound, to step in time with the pulses, to breathe with the rising and falling of the humid air, the place might begin to soften around them. Water would appear where they were thirsty. A causeway would become easier to cross. Fungal shelves might brighten in sequence, almost like guidance. The overwhelming roar would not become quiet, but it would become navigable, even meaningful.

No one could live there by ordinary habitation. One could not move into a hollow tower and call it home, because the tower is not a house. One could not cultivate a garden in the usual sense, because the garden is already a distributed skin of moss, algae, fungi, and engineered crust. One could only live there by becoming part of its rhythm. A person would have to become a recurring pattern: a careful footfall, a stable breath, a respectful use of sound, an ability to read pressure changes. They would have to accept that their body is another resonant object in a field of resonant objects. They would have to understand that solitude, in the human sense, is impossible there. Even alone, one would be heard. Even silent, one would be listened for.

In the deepest sense, what lives in the basin is the basin’s own capacity to continue. It is a living system that has made survival into music and music into infrastructure. It is wet, hungry, attentive, and endlessly self-modifying. It is young, yes, but not immature. It is still finding its operating rhythm, still adjusting its slopes, still teaching its channels where to cut, still letting its animals discover which tones work and which damage the whole. It erodes by design. It grows by response. It repairs by resonance. It does not preserve itself as a static object. It preserves itself as a performance.

And perhaps that performance is waiting for something. The place feels like a nursery, but also like an instrument. It could be a machine for producing ecology. It could be a temple whose worship is hydrological. It could be a filter for transforming mineral violence into living surface. It could be an engine whose purpose has not yet begun, or a civilization that has outgrown bodies and now exists as weather, memory, and song.

If asked, finally, who lives there, the truest answer is this:

A chorus lives there.

Not merely a chorus of animals, though the animals are loud. Not merely a chorus of plants and fungi, though every surface sings with growth. Not merely a chorus of engineered minerals and hidden repair systems, though the stone itself answers. All of these together form a single, vast, discontinuous life: a wet acoustic commonwealth, a hollow city that breathes through towers, drinks through films, sees through pressure, and remembers through resonance.

The basin is empty only if you insist that life must be a solid body in a furnished room. In that place, life is also a standing wave, a water route, a pattern of condensation, a predator’s pause, a repair line glowing in response to the right tone. It is abundant, deafening, watchful, and pressing close. It does not need you to arrive. It is already full of voices. But if you enter it, you become part of its listening, and perhaps, if you can bear the noise, part of its living.
