What lives there is not one creature. The bowl refuses a single noun.

At first, if you could see it from the spillway without being blinded by the glare, you would think the place was inhabited by people: small, low-moving figures on wet shelves, pausing whenever the light struck too hard, turning their faces away from the glacier with the automatic grace of those who have learned brightness as a hazard. Then the image would complicate. The figures would resolve into households, into work-gangs, into watchers and repairers, into children balanced on beams no wider than a wrist. Then it would complicate again: the cliff seams would fill with birds, the pools with fish, the walls with small white mammals, the warm cracks with dark amphibians, the ice with things too slow to name. By then you would understand that the settlement is not occupied by one kind of life. It is a shared body, a cold wet commonwealth, a basin in which every living thing has been edited by water.

The people—if they are people, and that is the first uncertainty the place imposes—might be called the Hush Folk by outsiders, because their silence is the first thing one notices. They do not call themselves that, or at least not in spoken words. Their own name for themselves is closer to a sign than a sound: a cupped hand, a downward tilt, two fingers opened like water falling. If forced to translate it, they might say, Those Who Answer the Water. Or, more simply, the Cup-born.

They are not tall. Height would be a burden on narrow ledges and in low shelters, so the bowl has favored compactness: short limbs, dense bones, broad chests, thick hands. Their bodies are not graceful in the soft, lowland sense; they are sure. They move with a rolling, lowered balance, knees slightly bent, feet searching and settling without hesitation. Their feet are almost another pair of hands: wide, flexible, toes capable of spreading and gripping wet stone, arches strengthened from childhood on slick paths. Many wear soft-soled boots of layered hide, stitched tight and rubbed with fish oil, but in the warmer seep corners they often go barefoot, using skin against stone the way a climber uses palms.

Their hands are their finest tools. The fingers are short-looking until they open, then surprisingly long, with broad pads hardened by rope, stone, and cold water.
