What lives there is not easy to count, because it is not easy to separate. The hollows are inhabited the way a tide pool is inhabited, but on a regional scale: as a pressure, a metabolism, a constant negotiation over warmth, moisture, and decay. Nothing in the place belongs only to itself. A wall is a wall only until a mat of fungus makes it into food. A pool is a pool only until bodies make it into broth. A floor is a floor only until it becomes the ceiling of something buried underneath. The inhabitants are not residents of the country beneath the surface so much as conditions of it.

The first and last residents are the films and felts. They are the quiet foundation of everything else. White mycelium spreads over damp clay in broad, skin-like sheets, creeping into every crack where moisture can follow. In the light shafts, those sheets look almost like pale leather, wrinkled and faintly shiny where condensation has beaded on them. In darker hollows they are softer, feathered at the edges, lifting slightly with each warm exhalation from below. Rust-red bacterial films stain the wet stone near the hotter veins, especially where mineral water seeps slowly enough to be licked away by living things. Those films have a sharp, clean-metal smell, like wet pennies, and they attract small scrapers with rasping mouths that pass over them again and again, leaving clean tracks in the red.

In some places the fungus has become stone. Mineralized hyphae form brittle shelves along the walls, pale and layered, breaking like chalk when struck. On those shelves, eggs are laid. Mites shelter under them. Beetles rest on their edges with their heads tucked down. Where the shelves collapse, they are ground into the floor and become part of the next surface. There is also a short gray growth on many of the more traveled flats, compressed into dense patches like wet felt. It springs back slowly after being stepped on, then is pressed flat again. Over time, the paths of countless bodies make it into a kind of living carpet, not planted, not tended, only endured.

Where water stands long enough, microbial mats form wrinkled slicks over the bottoms of basins. They are not green. They are brownish, purplish, sometimes black, with bubbles rising through them in slow, gassy blisters. Those mats are rich and treacherous. Creatures walk out onto them to graze, sink a little, then struggle free, leaving torn edges that release smells of sulfur, warm clay, and old meat. The mats are food, trap, nursery, and floor all at once. They are one of the reasons the landscape feels unfinished: they can solidify a basin for a few days, then burst and turn it into slick slurry in an hour.

On top of that living skin lies the animal press.

The pale grubs are among its most visible currents. They move in slow rivers across the clay banks, thick as fingers, smooth and heavy, their bodies contracting and lengthening with a wet, muscular patience. They eat clay, fungal threads, and whatever fine organic matter is suspended in the mud. Their droppings are mineral-rich and harden into crusts when dry, so their movement literally helps build the ground they cross. When a wet pulse comes, they rise toward the surface in thousands. When the air cools and the ceiling begins to drip, they turn restlessly, seeking shelter, and their mass movement can shift entire shallow channels. They are slow, but there are so many of them that they behave like weather.

The black scavenger beetles are louder. They arrive in layered swarms whenever something dies or sheds heavily. Their shells click against one another as they crowd over carcasses, egg masses, and piles of cast chitin. The sound is not like the neat ticking of small stones; it is more irregular, drier, almost like brittle seeds being shaken in a pouch. They are not graceful, but they are efficient. They get under things, between things, into things. Their larvae are paler and softer, and they mine through the same materials the adults feed on, turning husks into powder. Where a beetle swarm has passed, what remains is smaller, cleaner, and more likely to become geology.

Mites rise from disturbed carrion like smoke. They are not all one kind. Some ride larger animals, clinging to joints or shell seams until the host dies or grows too hot. Some live in egg clusters, feeding on failed eggs and dead larvae. Some are so small they seem to be only a roughness in the air, a moving graininess that settles where decay settles. When a carcass is opened, the mite-cloud can thicken enough to dim the pale shafts of daylight, and then smaller hunters move in to feed on the cloud itself.

Springtails skip across the mineral crusts in loose, glittering bands. They graze the films and the finest fungal growth, and when startled by falling drops or sudden vibration, they leap all at once, making a sound like fine sleet striking stone. They are prey for almost everything small enough to catch them, and because they breed quickly after wet pulses, their abundance is one of the first signs that the basin is entering another fertile interval.

There are translucent worms in the wetter depressions, their bodies so clear that the pale line of their guts is visible when they feed. They twist through shallow water and saturated mud, collecting spores, bacteria, and suspended fragments. There are pink-mouthed burrowers in the marl, soft and strong, their heads opening into wet, blunt scoops as they push into the sediment. Their tunnels are short-lived, but they matter: they let water enter deeper layers, they let smells travel, and they create temporary passages that other creatures enlarge or inhabit.

The crab-shaped crawlers are flatter and harder. They move low over the stone with their legs spread wide, their shells dusted with salt and fungal spores. Some scrape mats from rock. Some carry fragments of shell or crust on their backs, not by design but by adhesion, so they look like moving pieces of the wall. A few of the larger ones have backs so roughened by mineral growth that egg clusters stick to them, and for a while they walk around carrying the next generation without knowing it. When they die, their bodies add another small ridge to the basin floor.

Above them, in the broken ceiling and along the high fissures, the predators wait. The long-legged ones are the most patient. They do not rush. They stand on the undersides of ledges or near the mouths of light shafts, their bodies pressed flat, their legs spread like measuring instruments. They feel more than they see. A wingbeat, a drip, the tremor of a grub river passing below can bring one moving in a sudden, precise drop. They are not numerous enough to control the smaller populations, but they shape them. They teach the air to be dangerous.

The winged forms make the light shafts seem fuller than they are. Heavy-bodied flies circle in slow, buzzing gyres where warmth rises. Colorless moths drift at the edges of the beams, resting on the warm stone when the air cools. Wasp-like things with papery wings hunt smaller insects or patrol nesting sites, their movements sharper and more abrupt than the rest. The smallest insects are barely individual: they become columns, veils, moving grain in the pale gold light. When a shaft is full of them, the light itself seems to thicken. When something passes through it — a predator, a falling clump of clay, a rising swarm from below — the column breaks and reforms with a soft, roaring whisper of wings.

The pools are crowded in another way. Blind fish or fish-like creatures move through them in dense, nervous schools. They are not beautiful in the usual sense. Their bodies are plain, blunt-faced, and pale-silvered, their eyes reduced to faint spots or sealed beneath skin. They flash when the light touches them, then vanish into the murk. They feed on larvae, worm-masses, and anything small enough to fit into their mouths. In the largest pools, they move in such numbers that their turning changes the water’s surface, making it ripple in quick, metallic shivers.

Eyeless salamander-like creatures live in the cracks and shallows near those pools. They are soft-bodied, strong, and quiet until they move. They can slip into wall fissures with a sinuous compression that makes them seem boneless, then emerge when the water is disturbed by spawning rushes. Their calls are not songlike. They are low croaks, throaty clicks, and short wet chirps, used mostly when moisture rises and the pools become safe enough for eggs. Their egg masses are gelatinous, stuck to stone or fungal mats, and they hatch in explosive numbers when conditions are right. If the pool dries too soon, the larvae die in writhing thousands. If it lasts, they become a feast for everything else.

There are larger creatures too, though “larger” here does not mean grand. It means heavy enough to change the ground.

Some are broad, slow, shelf-backed things that move across the flats with a ponderous, rocking gait. Their carapaces are uneven, crusted with mineral deposits, fungal patches, and the debris of other lives. They graze microbial mats and compact sediment beneath them. Their feet leave shallow prints that later fill with water and become nurseries for smaller creatures. They are not tame, not social, and not particularly clever, but they are important. They make the landscape more complicated simply by passing through it.
