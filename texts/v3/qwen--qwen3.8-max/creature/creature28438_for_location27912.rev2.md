What lives there lives as the place itself lives: without possessions, without permanent marks, without the need to leave a built trace behind. The inhabitants are not owners of the high darkness. They are its moving organs, its temporary concentrations, its breathing parts.

There are no peoples in the ordinary sense. No tribes, no villages, no wanderers with packs, no makers of shelters, no keepers of tools. If anything could be called a person there, it is not a single upright body with hands and history, but a presence distributed across heat gradients, chemical trails, electrical pulses, and slow migrating appetites. The closest thing to a “who” in that place is a pattern that keeps returning: a gathering at a vent, a pulse in the fog, a vibration passing through a herd of plated bodies, a spark chain moving along the crests and dying before it can be remembered.

Yet life is abundant. It is simply not gathered into familiar forms of richness. It appears in nodes, in pulses, in brief intensities, then thins out again across kilometers of warm dark stone. To live there is to be intermittent. To be numerous is not to be crowded.

The first and most numerous inhabitants are not animals in the obvious sense. They are the stone-feeders, the surface-workers, the slow living films that make the rest possible. Black-green crusts spread over basalt in thin, vein-like maps, not truly plant and not truly mineral, but something between colony and process. They drink moisture from fog, take energy from chemical gradients, and bind dust into living skin. Rust-colored filaments thread through pores in the rock, drawing out iron, sulfur, and trace metals. Pale mats gather at vent margins, trembling faintly when warm gas passes over them, feeding on exhalations that would suffocate most surface life.

These are the base laborers. They do not stand apart from the landscape; they are its first industry. Their bodies refine the stone. Their waste becomes food. Their death becomes film. Their film becomes crust. Their crust becomes soil enough for the next fragile layer. Without them, the plateau would be only heat and rock. With them, it becomes a living engine.

Among them grow the pipe-stem colonies: low, wiry, hollow growths that look like reeds made of ash and copper. They are not plants, but stacked alliances of organisms, part tissue, part mineral scaffold, part gas exchange. They rise only where warmth and moisture meet in stable proportion. They do not sway like grass. They stand with a brittle, porous stillness, their interiors faintly humming when pressure changes in the ground below. In some places they form ragged fringes around fissures. In others they appear as isolated clusters, each one a small vertical city of microscopic lives, each one breathing in rhythm with the exhalations of the stone.

Above these grounded forms live the drifters.

They are among the most visible signs of life in the darkness, and yet they are not easy to see as animals. They move through the warm air like slow lanterns, like bladders of living fog, like translucent seeds carried by a wind that is mostly invisible. Their bodies are thin membranes filled with buoyant gas, generated by internal chemistry and kept stable by delicate pressure valves in their flesh. From their undersides trail long sensory filaments, some meant to taste the air, some to catch microscopic food, some to sense electrical tension. Many are faintly luminous: blue-green pulses moving across their surfaces in slow patterns, brighter when they gather, dimmer when they descend.

They feed on the airborne life of the plateau: microbes lifted from warm stone, spores released by crust colonies, fine organic particles stirred up by thermal currents, even the charged mists that gather in electrostatic weather. They do not chase prey. They drift through abundance. They filter, absorb, and continue. When a strong heat column rises from a vent field, they gather into it in long ascending spirals, hundreds or thousands of them turning upward together in the darkness, their bodies flashing in weak sequence like signals passed along a wire. From far away they look like embers caught in a slow whirlwind. From below, they look like a scattered constellation lowering itself into reach.

They are not social in the way of flocking birds, but they are responsive to one another. Their pulses influence nearby individuals. In dense fog, their lights can synchronize for a few minutes, producing broad soft waves of blue-green illumination that move across the air. This is not ceremony. It is resonance, a byproduct of their electrical sensitivity and their shared response to humidity, charge, and heat. Still, to witness it would be to feel that the darkness had begun thinking in light.

Their life cycle passes through the pools. When conditions are right—after rain, when shallow basins fill with warm mineral water and the air becomes thick with spores—the drifters descend. They settle near the water’s edge or hover just above it, releasing fine reproductive material into the humid layer. In the pools, their early stages develop as tiny luminous larvae, often indistinguishable from other pool life. Some never leave the water. Some mature into forms that remain near the surface, skimming films of microbes. Others, if they survive predation and evaporation, develop gas tissues and rise. The successful ones join the aerial herds and may drift for long periods across the plateau, never touching ground except to drink moisture from fog or to spawn in temporary cisterns.

The drifters are prey, but their abundance is spread so thin that predation on them has a strange character. It is not constant massacre. It is sudden, localized, and brief.

The aerial predators are quicker and darker. They do not soar like hawks or dart like dragonflies in any simple sense. Some are slender, almost ribbon-bodied, moving on charged membranes that stiffen and relax in response to the electrical field around them. They hunt by sensing the weak bioelectric signatures of other creatures, especially the drifters. Their limbs, if limbs they can be called, end in long filaments capable of delivering a stunning discharge. When they strike, there is often a snap of blue-white light, a tremor in the fog, and then silence. The prey drops, spasms, or is caught before it falls.

Other aerial hunters are less active but more patient. They hang in thermal boundaries, where warm rising air meets cooler descending air, and wait for food to come to them. Their bodies resemble dark leaves or torn veils, their surfaces covered in adhesive sensory hairs. They are almost invisible in the mineral haze. A drifter may pass close, brush one of those hairs, and be tangled by reflex alone. The hunter does not struggle. It folds slowly inward, digests slowly, and unfolds again. In the dimness, such events are rarely seen. They are felt, if at all, as a sudden absence where a small light used to be.

On the ground, the most characteristic inhabitants are the heavy grazers.

They are not graceful. They are not fast. They are built like patience given weight. Their bodies are broad, low, and plated, covered in dark ridged surfaces that often host the same crusts they feed on. They move across basalt with a slow, even rhythm, their soft-edged feet pressing into porous stone without cutting it. Where they walk, the surface seems to close behind them, mineral moisture filling the brief impressions, crusts reknitting, no road remaining. They are not roadmakers. They are the opposite of roadmakers. They pass through the land without insisting on it.

Their shells are not merely armor. They are thermal machines. Channels and pores in the carapace allow heat to enter or escape, and side vents can open to release warm vapor when their internal temperature rises. In cooler hollows they angle their backs toward rising heat columns, absorbing warmth. In overheated basins they shade themselves against low stone shelves or stand motionless beside fissures where cooler air breathes upward. Their bodies are in constant negotiation with the plateau’s warmth. They do not escape the environment. They regulate themselves by moving within it.

They feed on microbial films, mineral crusts, and the wiry pipe-stem colonies, cropping them with broad, rasp-edged mouths. Their digestion is slow and chemical, assisted by internal symbionts that break down mineral-bound nutrients. As they graze, they also disperse life. Crust fragments cling to their undersides. Spores pass through them. Heat-loving microbes find new fissures in their waste. They are not gardeners by intention, but their movement maintains the mosaic of bare stone and living film. Without them, some mats would thicken and choke; without them, some crusts would spread unevenly. Their hunger is part of the balance.

When they gather, the plateau becomes aware of them.

They do not herd everywhere. Most of the time they are solitary or found in small groups around a reliable vent or fog basin. But at certain places and times—when a vent field exhales a rich gas pulse, when a rare rain fills a chain of shallow basins, when a thermal column becomes unusually stable—they assemble in numbers large enough to alter the sound of the land. Their movement produces a low vibration through the rock. It is not quite a rumble. It is deeper, more felt than heard, a pressure in the feet and chest. The stone itself seems to carry it. Drifters overhead may adjust their spirals in response. Predators move toward the edges of the gathering. Pool life nearby becomes more active, as if the vibration were a signal that conditions are favorable.

They do not speak, but they communicate. They sense one another by heat, by ground vibration, by chemical traces left briefly on warm stone. Their greetings, if such words can be used at all, are slow: a turning of bodies, a shared orientation toward a heat source, a brief exchange of vapor from side vents. Young ones sometimes press against the flanks of larger individuals, not for milk but for warmth and for the microbial film that clings to older shells. There is tenderness in this, but it is not human tenderness. It is the tenderness of bodies conserving energy in a vast dark place.

Their reproduction is hidden. They do not build nests. They seek warm fissures, mineral hollows, or the edges of vent fields where the stone is chemically active. There they release tough, heat-resistant capsules that absorb moisture and minerals from their surroundings. The capsules are not crafted; they are grown. Inside, the embryos develop slowly, feeding on stored material and on gases that diffuse through the capsule wall. Some capsules hatch in days. Others take much longer, depending on heat and chemistry. When the young emerge, they are small, dark, and almost indistinguishable from the stone. They begin at once to graze. Many die. Enough survive. The plateau does not need surplus sentiment to continue.

The ground predators are fewer, but their presence is woven into every congregation.

Some are long-bodied and low, moving with a fluid, hydraulic patience through shadows and shallow depressions. Their eyes, if they have them, are small and poor; their true perception lies in heat pits, electroreceptive organs, and chemical fringes that taste the air. They can sense the faint field of a grazer’s shell, the warmth of a drifting larva hidden in a pool, the static residue left by a stunned drifter. They do not chase unless they must. They approach by degrees. They wait in the blind warmth of a vent, where prey comes anyway. They lie in the thin water films where mineral conductivity carries their signals. When they strike, it is with sudden compressed force, then stillness.

Others are smaller and more numerous, living in the crevices and pores of weathered basalt. They hunt the young, the weak, the larvae, the pool creatures that stray too far from water. Their bodies are often dark with metallic sheen, their tissues able to tolerate high concentrations of sulfur and iron. They may appear as nothing more than a ripple in a dust film, a brief movement in a crack, a spark where no spark should be. They are important not because they are dramatic, but because they keep the smaller economies moving. Death in this place is not a failure of the system. It is one of the system’s common pressures.

The pools hold their own intense populations.

They are small compared with the immensity of the plateau, but they concentrate life like lenses concentrate weak light. A basin may be dry for a long time. Then fog thickens, rain falls in a narrow curtain, water gathers in a hollow, and within hours the pool becomes active. Blind eel-like creatures emerge from cysts or move out of hidden fissures. Their skin is pale or black depending on species, and their bodies generate weak electric fields used for navigation, communication, and hunting. They twist through the cloudy water, sensing one another by distortion and charge.

Filter-feeders attach to the inner walls of pools, drawing mineral-rich water through fleshy combs. Some are shaped like dark flowers. Others are flat, pulsing discs. They clean the water of fine particles and microbes, but they also concentrate minerals in their tissues, making them valuable prey. Around them move smaller creatures: crust-like crawlers, larval drifters, transparent worms, and colonies of lamp organisms that give off dull amber light. In some pools the light is steady. In others it flickers in response to disturbance, so that a single touch can make the whole basin glitter with defensive sparks.

After rain, the pools can become nurseries. Larval masses bloom in warm shallows, their bodies forming living films over the water. Some are the young of aerial drifters. Some belong to pool-bound forms that will never leave. Some are temporary alliances, colonial stages that exist only for a few days. They feed, pulse, grow, and are eaten. When the pool begins to shrink, the survivors either migrate through damp fissures, encyst in mineral slime, or descend into deeper cracks where water persists longer. When the basin dries, it appears empty again, but not truly empty. It waits with dormant life sealed in stone, in crust, in capsules, in microscopic endurance.

There are also creatures that live almost entirely at the margins of vents.

They are among the strangest inhabitants, because they seem less like animals than like parts of the geology that have learned to move. Some cling to the lips of fumaroles, their bodies flattened against hot rock, absorbing gases through specialized tissues. Others graze the pale mats that grow where vent gases meet cooler air. Some are nearly sessile, anchored by mineralized bases, opening and closing pores in their bodies as the vent breathes. They do not build chimneys or tubes, but their presence can alter the flow of precipitates, clearing small passages, redirecting minor currents, changing the chemistry of nearby films. This is not engineering. It is living. Yet the effect is maintenance. The vent field works partly because these bodies are in it.

In certain places, large slow creatures gather at vent mouths during exhalations. They stand or lie in the rising warmth, their broad surfaces exposed to gas and heat. Their shells or hides are darkened by mineral deposits. Some appear almost metallic. They may remain motionless for hours, absorbing warmth or encouraging symbiotic organisms living in their outer layers. When the vent’s breath weakens, they rise and move elsewhere. Their departure leaves no structure behind, only a temporary darkening of stone where moisture has been displaced by heat.

The plateau also supports rare forms that seem to belong to the weather more than to the ground.

Great slow aerial bodies sometimes cross the high darkness, far above the ordinary drifters. They are not numerous. They may be individuals, or they may be colonies so integrated that individuality loses meaning. Their membranes are broad and dark, adapted to the thin air, their gas tissues maintained by long-term metabolic chemistry. They trail long filaments that sample the atmosphere over wide distances. They feed on the richest airborne layers: spore clouds, microbial veils, charged aerosols stirred up by storms. They do not hover near the ground often. When they do, smaller life responds. Drifters scatter or descend. Static tension changes. The air feels heavier.

They are not rulers. Nothing rules there. They are simply large expressions of the same processes that make the smaller lives possible. If they have awareness, it is slow and weather-like. If they have purpose, it is to continue. They may live a long time, or they may be brief gatherings of many bodies that separate again under certain conditions. The plateau does not explain itself. It allows such forms to be seen once, perhaps, and then vanish into fog.

There is no civilization among them, but there is order.

The order is not imposed by law, tool, or memory object. It is maintained by need, by sensitivity, by the physical structure of the plateau itself. Life moves where heat moves. It gathers where water gathers. It disperses when chemistry shifts. It follows gradients. It answers pulses. It is shaped by the fact that energy is abundant but diffuse, that water is local, that darkness favors senses other than sight, and that nothing built can last.

Because of this, the inhabitants have no relationship to property. They do not defend places in the manner of creatures that build territories with marks or walls. They defend conditions. A vent may be occupied while it is active, then abandoned when it changes. A pool may be crowded for a day, then empty. A fog basin may fill with drifting lights, then become stone again. The land is shared not by agreement but by rhythm.

Their memories, if they have them, are not stored in things. They are stored in bodies, in chemical sensitivities, in inherited response, in the living continuity of microbial films, in the routes taken by heat and moisture. A grazer does not remember a path by landmarks made meaningful. It moves toward warmth, away from danger, into familiar chemical gradients. A drifter does not remember a spawning pool as a place with history. It responds to a combination of humidity, charge, mineral scent, and temperature that repeats often enough across generations. The plateau remembers through recurrence, not record.

This is why nothing there becomes an artifact.

Even death is quickly returned. A fallen drifter dries into film or is consumed by pool life. A grazer’s body, if it dies in the open, is broken down by microbial mats, mineral moisture, and scavenging organisms. Its shell may persist longer than its flesh, but even that is colonized, weathered, filled with crust, and eventually made part of the stone. A predator’s corpse disappears into the same economy. There are no grave fields, no bone accumulations, no relics. The place does not preserve what has been. It converts it.

This gives the inhabitants a particular dignity. They do not seek permanence. They are not lesser because they leave nothing behind. They are complete in their passing. Each life is a working phase of the plateau’s ongoing function. Each creature is a way the landscape processes heat, gas, water, mineral, and electricity. Each population is a distributed mechanism, not because it has been designed, but because it has settled into a stable relationship with the forces around it.

Sometimes, in electrostatic weather, the whole living system becomes briefly visible at once.

Fog thickens near charged stone crests. Droplets tremble in the air instead of falling. Drifters gather lower than usual, their membranes stiff with induced charge. Grazers pause, sensing the change in ground potential. Predators become more active, because electrical gradients make prey easier to locate. Pool surfaces flicker with tiny discharges. Along ridges, blue points of corona appear, crawling over mineral crusts. The darkness fills with a fine prickling silence, as if the plateau is about to say something without voice.

Then the tension releases. A slow pulse of light moves through the fog. Drifters rise. Grazers continue. Predators settle. The pools dim. The stone returns to its low breathing. Nothing has been made. Nothing has been destroyed except a temporary imbalance. The place continues its work.

At rare intervals, a deeper event occurs. A hidden gas pocket releases. A vent field opens. A broad fog bank crosses an old basin. Rain falls heavily enough to fill dozens of cisterns at once. Then life erupts in scale.

Aerial herds thicken into slow rivers of light. Grazers move from isolated hollows toward the active zone. Their vibration becomes stronger. Predators converge. Pool creatures bloom, spawn, and die in rapid cycles. Microbial mats expand visibly over damp stone. Pipe-stem colonies open their pores and release spores. The plateau becomes a single coordinated surge, though no single will directs it. It is industry without factory, production without warehouse, abundance without storage. Everything happens in the open and is spent.

In such moments, one understands that the inhabitants are not merely living on the plateau. They are the plateau living.

They are the means by which the high darkness metabolizes itself. The drifters redistribute airborne nutrients. The grazers regulate surface films and heat exchange. The predators prevent any one population from overwhelming the rest. The pool creatures concentrate and recycle dissolved minerals. The vent-dwellers modify gas flow simply by being present. The microbial crusts bind the stone and make it biologically available. Even the rare vast bodies in the upper fog are part of this, moving energy across distances too large for smaller lives to cross.

There is no need for anyone to watch.

That is perhaps the deepest truth of what lives there. It does not perform. It does not require witness. It does not become less real because it leaves no monument. The darkness is full of work, but the work is not for anyone. It is for continuation. It is for the next exhalation, the next fog, the next rain, the next heat pulse, the next generation rising from capsule, larva, spore, film, or warm mineral water.

If a traveler came there, they would not find inhabitants waiting to be understood. They would find themselves surrounded by presences that do not center them. They might see a line of dark shapes moving through fog and think it a procession. They might hear a low vibration and think it a machine. They might see lights pulsing in a pool and think it a signal. They would be wrong, and also not entirely wrong. The shapes are a herd. The vibration is life moving through stone. The lights are bodies speaking in the only language the place allows.

But no one would welcome the traveler. No one would attack them out of malice. The plateau would simply continue. The damp air would settle on them. The warmth would press through their soles. Microbial films would begin to alter whatever foreign surface they brought. The chemistry of the place would touch them, slowly claiming them. If they remained long enough, they would not be absorbed by cruelty. They would be absorbed by process.

What lives there, then, is not a set of characters but a living condition.

It is the drifter ascending in a heat column, its blue-green pulse answering others across the fog.  
It is the kiln-backed grazer moving over basalt with slow, mechanical patience, its shell breathing vapor.  
It is the static hunter striking once in the dark, then vanishing into silence.  
It is the blind pool eel turning in mineral water, reading the world by distortion.  
It is the lamp colony glowing amber in a cistern that will be dry before long.  
It is the black-green crust spreading over stone, making the first living surface.  
It is the vent-dweller opened to gas and heat, no different from a valve except that it is alive.  
It is the rare vast body crossing the upper haze, barely visible, already gone.  
It is the larval bloom after rain, sudden and brief and necessary.  
It is the electrostatic fog trembling with charge, full of suspended droplets and waiting life.

None of them are makers. All of them are doing.

They live in a place where power is everywhere and ownership nowhere. Where industry is real but has no buildings. Where abundance exists without hoarding. Where darkness is not absence but medium. Where warmth is not comfort but engine. Where life is not decoration upon the land, but the land’s continuing action.

That is who lives there.

Not a folk. Not a nation. Not a species alone.

A vast, scattered, breathing population of processes, each one dark, warm, patient, and necessary.
