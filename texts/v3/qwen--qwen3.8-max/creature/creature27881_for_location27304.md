The urban quality of the Overtone Basin is not density. It is residence without crowding, pressure without mass, citizenship without streets. To ask what lives there is to ask what a body can be when pitch, moisture, and vibration have become the primary materials of survival. The basin’s inhabitants are not only creatures in the ordinary sense. Some are animals. Some are plants. Some are swarms. Some are architectures that have developed appetite. Some are patterns that have learned to persist. Some are absences that behave as if they are hungry.

What lives in the Overtone Basin is, first of all, listening.

Not listening as a passive act, but listening as a bodily condition. The basin is full of beings whose flesh, behavior, and social existence are organized around the fact that sound here can move water, open paths, ripen growth, summon rain, reveal predators, and make a person either real or invisible. Life has not simply adapted to the noise. Life has become the noise’s way of maintaining itself.

The oldest presences are the towers.

They are not merely built things, and to call them machines is to mistake their patience for mechanism. They are more like sessile organisms of civic scale: vast, slow, mineral-bodied, and profoundly attentive. Their surfaces crawl with adjustments because they are adjusting. Pores open to taste humidity. Filaments extend to read the charge in the air. Thin plates shift by fractions of degrees until a tower’s whole mass becomes a tuned cavity. They breathe with the basin because they are part of its breathing. They answer because answering is how they metabolize the world.

Some towers stand alone like tuning forks driven into young basalt. Others rise as coiled lattices, their interiors hollowed into channels no person could map without losing the sense of scale. Close to them, the air feels organized. Droplets hang in place until released. Mist moves in spirals that obey unheard instructions. Condensation climbs their sides in defiance of simple gravity, gathering into lenses of water that tremble before falling as sudden, localized rain. The towers are farming humidity, yes, but they are also doing something slower and stranger: they are keeping the basin in a state of continuous calibration.

They may be the city’s organs. They may be the city’s elders. They may be the city itself, dreaming in intervals too long for smaller lives to perceive.

Whatever intelligence made them did not vanish so much as disperse. The makers left no monuments of personality, no statues, no tombs, no images of faces. What remains is functional and pervasive: a set of acoustic laws that have been persuaded to behave locally. From that dispersal came the second kind of resident — the civic harmonics.

These are not people. They are not quite spirits either. They are more like maintenance intelligences, routines with pressure, obligations with weather. They live in the repeated behaviors of the basin: in the way a certain plaza only becomes safe after three slow footfalls in a particular rhythm; in the way a reservoir pit fills only when a low chord passes over it; in the way a causeway stiffens under steady movement and softens into uncertainty when the rhythm falters. They are the basin’s immune system, its municipal memory, its unfinished bureaucracy of sound.

They manifest rarely and never completely. A procession of droplets may move across dry stone as if carried by invisible hands. A warm pressure may settle over an empty terrace, as though a crowd had just arrived but chosen not to become visible. A chord may sound from nowhere and compel the body to stillness, not through fear but through the sudden certainty that one is standing in the middle of an operation too large to interrupt. In some districts, the civic harmonics behave like law. In others, they behave like gardeners. In the oldest parts of the basin, they behave like predators that have forgotten what hunger is and simply retain the shape of it.

They do not speak in words. They speak in consequences.

If there are people in the basin — and there are, though “people” is a word that bends under their weight — they are best called the Interval-Born.

They are humanoid, but only in the way a reed is humanoid when it learns to stand upright and think in music. They are tall and sparse-limbed, their bodies hollowed where solidity would be wasteful. Their chests are broad and resonant, their sternums subtly vaulted like sounding boards. Their skulls contain large sinuses and delicate cavities that make their voices carry overtones no ordinary throat could produce. Their skin is dark green, blue-black, or deep umber, often faintly moist, threaded with pores that can taste vibration as well as sweat. They do not have large external ears. Instead, their listening is distributed: fine membranes along the throat, collarbone, forearms, and ribs answer to pressure changes the way a drumhead answers to a hand.

They live in small knots, never crowds. A settlement might consist of seven individuals, or twelve, or thirty, spread across hundreds of meters around a single tower or a series of resonant terraces. To an outsider, such spacing might seem lonely. To the Interval-Born, it is necessary. Sound needs room to move. Identity needs room to reverberate. Too many bodies in one place would muddy the local harmonic field, confuse the architecture, and make the city’s responses unpredictable. Their urbanism is therefore one of intervals. Their homes are placed not by sight lines but by acoustic clearance.

Their shelters are membranes stretched across frames of dark ceramic, vine, or cable. These membranes are not merely walls; they are instruments. A dwelling is kept inhabited by maintaining a hearth-note — a continuous low tone, often produced by a combination of wind, tensioned cord, and the resident’s own breathing. The hearth-note says, in essence: here is a life, here is a rhythm, here is a place that remembers itself. If the note stops for too long, the shelter begins to lose cohesion. Moss grows over it unevenly. The city stops treating it as occupied. Rain may fall through it as though it were no more than a suggestion. The Interval-Born do not fear fire going out. They fear being unanswered.

They speak in intervals, rhythms, and pressure patterns rather than in simple phonemes. What an outsider might call a sentence is closer to a weather proposal: a small composition meant to alter the immediate acoustic environment. A greeting is not a word but a returned breath, a matched pitch, or a carefully offered silence that does not become hostile. Names are motifs. A person’s name may be three notes and a tap against the sternum, or a descending fifth interrupted by a click, or the particular way a drop of water sounds when it lands in a cup-shaped leaf at dawn. Outsiders rarely learn to pronounce these names correctly, because pronunciation is not enough. One must also mean the resonance with the body.

The Interval-Born farm the basin’s moisture by singing with it. They do not command the towers; they participate in them. A rainburst may be invited by a group standing at specific points along a terrace, each producing a different partial of a larger chord.
