Subtle songs would be lost or smeared by the basin’s own acoustic glare.

If I belonged there, I would not begin as a singer. I would begin as a listener. I would begin as a body shaped by the need to be exact without becoming permanent. The first thing the basin would take from me is the habit of leaving signs. The second is the desire to keep anything. After that, what remained would be something very close to what the place itself seems to allow: a brief intelligence, a precise gesture, a creature that uses the world for one moment and then lets the world forget it.

I would find myself near one of the basin’s acoustic seams, where pale quartzite meets a darker, basaltic lip and the wind divides itself into two clean notes. Not in shelter, because there is no shelter. Not in a nest, because nothing there gathers into a nest. I would be on an open terrace, perhaps halfway between a fluted ridge and a shallow pan, where a capillary groove no wider than a finger carries condensation into a rock pocket no larger than an open hand. The pocket would be one of my centers, not because I owned it, but because it returned again and again to the same small fullness. Its surface would hold a faint green-brown film, an iridescent skin that tightened under the light. When the air shifted and mist slid across the stone, the water would bead there in silence so minute it would seem like a kind of seeing.

That is where I would be: at the edge of a hand-wide well in a bright, cool, deafening openness, with a kilometer of pale stone around me and the sky above me so hard and blue that it would feel less like a sky than a limit made of clarity.

At first I imagine I might be one of the lichens. There is something appealing about that: a consciousness spread thin over stone, waking only when moisture arrives, thinking so slowly that centuries become a kind of breath. I could be a black-green crust on a sunlit face, or one of those tight gray-white rosettes pressed so close to quartz that no animal could tell where mineral ends and life begins. I could exist without hunger in any ordinary sense, without movement, without the burden of deciding. The basin would not ask me to become more than I was. I would be accepted as a discoloration, a patience, a living stain.

But that is not quite right. The sound moves through me too much. The light demands too many small responses. The basin is not merely still; it is full of tiny events, and I feel drawn toward the quickness that answers them. I would not be only patient. I would need to notice. I would need to reach.

So the form begins to sharpen, though it resists a single name. I would be one of the basin’s momentary tool-users. A flake-turner. A listener with just enough intelligence to make a single use of the world and then release it. Perhaps I would seem birdlike: pale-winged, long-bodied, with a dark eye and a fine, hard beak capable of lifting a sharp fragment of stone. Perhaps I would seem more mammalian: small, low, furred in a short mineral-gray coat, with forepaws sensitive enough to push a pebble into a crevice or pry at a frozen film. The basin does not seem to care about our categories. It cares about sparseness, precision, and the refusal to accumulate. So maybe I would be something between: a creature with a bird’s quickness and a mammal’s tactile attention, a being whose body is organized around brief, exact interventions rather than construction.

I would not be large. Largeness would be a waste in such a place. I would be lean, dry, cool to the touch, my surface touched by dust and light until I seemed less like flesh and more like another weathered fact of the stone. If I had feathers, they would be narrow and pale, catching the sun in sudden flashes when I moved, more signal than ornament.
