I would find myself low. Not at the rim looking down into the Dripworks, but already inside its working hollow, in one of those sunken seams where the service road dips toward a drain and the brick has been worn into a smooth, dark lip by water and claw traffic. More precisely: near the place where a pipe gallery crosses over a narrow stair, and the stair has been hollowed into shallow scoops by generations of sliding bodies. Beneath the gallery a flange weeps at intervals. Below that, a thin channel runs over basalt into the mouth of a cistern. Beside the channel, a transformer kiosk gives off its dry, electrical warmth. There, in that junction of drip, stone, heat, and hum, I would be.

At first I might seem to be one of the rat-analogues.

I would have a low, fluid body, dark-furred but not soft-furred, with the practical sleekness of an animal that moves through tight access tunnels and damp machinery. My tail would be bare and always seeming wet. My hands would be too careful for mere scavenging: testing, sorting, wedging, feeling for loose rust or the faint tremor of a pipe about to knock. I would sit beside valve wheels not to turn them, but to listen through them. I would pause at leaks with my head tilted. I would look, in other words, like a small municipal inspector gone feral, or like an animal that had inherited bureaucracy without paperwork.

But that would not be the whole of what I was.

The longer I remained in the Dripworks, the less I would resemble a simple warm-bodied scavenger. The place would press its own shape into me. My back would harden into plates the color of old access covers, not glossy, not ornamental, but stamped-looking, as if they had been manufactured to fit over vulnerable joints. My skin would stay cool and permeable, able to drink condensation directly from iron. My ribs would feel the turbine before my ears did. My eyes would be small and patient, like wet rivet heads, and they would not seek beauty. They would seek moisture, warmth, movement, and the faint signs of blockage.

The strangest part of me would be my throat.

It would not be a throat made for speech. At rest it would look like a dark circular seal, a valve mouth set low in my chest or neck. When the machinery grew loud, I would open it. Not to cry, not to breathe in any ordinary panic, but to take sound into myself. The inside would be lined with fine mineral filaments, stiff and pale, trembling whenever the pitch of the Dripworks changed. I would be one of the sound-feeders, or something close to them: a creature settled near the pumps and vents, taking resonance the way other creatures take warmth or food. If a pipe knocked, I would swallow the echo. If a turbine changed its note, I would feel it as hunger or thirst. If the noise remained steady, I would remain still, gorged and balanced.

I would not be able to explain whether I fed on sound or merely used it to keep my internal processes aligned with the basin. That uncertainty would suit the place. In the Dripworks, function is more important than explanation.

Water would define my moods, if I had moods.

When the air was merely damp and the surfaces only sweating, I would be patient. I would move along channels and pipe bases, licking condensation from cold metal, pressing my body against weeping joints, letting the mineral-rich water film pass over my skin. I would eat what the basin offered: fungal skin, rust flakes, dead midges, copper-salted slime, the gritty paste of ash and moisture that collects behind pipe brackets. I would not be disgusted by any of it. Disgust belongs to a world with other options. I would have none.

When rain began, I would become more urgent and more plural.

I would not hide from it. I would move into the open, into the basin’s collection logic. My plates would angle themselves to catch drops. My body would become a small temporary roof, a gutter, a drinking surface. I would guide water where it needed to go, not by conscious engineering but by the way my shape had been worn into the basin’s tolerances. If a drip fell in the wrong place, I might shift beneath it.
