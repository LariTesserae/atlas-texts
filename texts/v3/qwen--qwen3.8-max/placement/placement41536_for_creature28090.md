Children are rare, and when they appear they are not announced by noise. A birth is first a matter of water: one basin is silenced with a thumb, another is emptied and refilled with the clearest thread of seepage, and the nearest fungal shelves are covered with dry skins so the air does not turn too cold against the new body. The infant is washed not to make it clean, but to introduce it. Its mouth is touched with a fingertip wetted from a trusted source, and a Basin-Keeper tastes the same water afterward, reading for bitterness, for rust, for any sign that the city has refused the small weight added to it. Only then is the child given its first name—not a spoken word, but a pattern made against the rim of a jar: perhaps two soft clicks, one bead falling into dark water, then the slow scrape of a nail across limestone. That pattern is carried by others long before the child can make it alone.

If I were to find myself in the Pocket City of Weeping Stone, I would not be the visitor from the plain above, squeezing through the fissure with panic still beating in my throat. That body would be too loud, too full of surface weather, too likely to crack something ancient with a careless turn. I would not be the traveler. I would be the one who hears the traveler arrive.

I would be one of the Wetted.

Not all of them, though; not just any pair of hands moving along the walkways. The shape that keeps returning to me is narrower than that, and stranger. I would be a Jar-Singer, or perhaps still an apprentice to one, because the deepest work in that place would not be building or harvesting but listening. I would belong to the part of the city where water is made to speak and where speech is made small enough not to wound the air.

You would find me in a low niche where the chamber tightens into sound. The ceiling there would be close enough to force me into a sitting posture, knees drawn up, spine curved into the hollow of the wall. A rust-streaked seam would run above me, weeping in a slow irregular rhythm, and below that seam five sounding jars would be set into the stone, each with a different neck, a different depth, a different mineral crust along its lip. One jar would receive a free-falling drop. Another would catch water sliding down a carved groove. A third would be fed by a thread that first passes over the shoulder of a Settled ancestor, gathering a faint taste of bone and old skin before it falls. The fourth would answer only when the lower water stirred. The fifth would remain almost silent, and I would watch it more closely than all the others because silence in the wrong place is a warning.

My body would have been made by that life. I would be small through the chest, not fragile but compressed, shaped by generations of low passages and tight ledges. My shoulders would be rounded, my arms long, my fingers broad and strong from constant pressure against stone. The soles of my feet would be thick and gripping, accustomed to wet rims and sudden changes in texture. My skin would be the pale gray-white of wet chalk, not luminous in any beautiful surface way, but faintly marked by mineral film in the creases of my knuckles and elbows. There would be rust-brown stains along my ribs and forearms where iron-rich water had touched me over years. My hair would be shaved close or bound away, and my scalp would carry notched scars, perhaps seven lines cut in childhood to mark apprenticeship under a Jar-Singer. In some of those scars, pale fungal threads would have settled, glowing so weakly that I would not always notice them myself, except when another person’s eyes caught the light at a certain angle and saw me beginning to become wall.

I would not be beautiful in the way of open skies and clear sunlight. I would be beautiful in the way of a well-tuned drain, a balanced basin, a hand that knows exactly where to rest without breaking anything.

My name would not be a word you could say casually. It would be a rhythm: two drops striking a shallow basin, a pause long enough for the second echo to thin, then the soft drag of a fingernail across wet stone. If you knew me well, you would know that my touch is slightly heavier on the left hand, and that when I am afraid I repeat a pattern too quickly. If you were Wetted, you would not need to hear my voice to know me. You would know me by pressure, tempo, and the particular way I listen before I move.

My work would be endless and almost invisible. I would not dig new halls. I would not carve grand monuments. I would maintain the city’s inner voice. Each waking-period, if such a place can be said to have periods, I would crawl to the sounding jars and taste the water before I touched anything else. A new coolness might mean a crack toward the saturated plain above. A faint metallic sharpness might mean the rock was changing. A sourness would mean bacterial film had grown too thick somewhere upstream. A sweetness, oddly, might be more dangerous still, because it could mean decay hidden in a lower pool.

I would adjust the jars by fractions. A little water removed from one neck to raise its note. A chip of bone placed inside another to dull its answer. A crust of calcite scraped away where mineral had begun to close the lip. A clay pellet pressed into a crack where unwanted seepage threatened to confuse the pattern. I would not do these things quickly. I would do them with the terrifying economy of someone who understands that a single careless gesture can put a basin out of tune for a season.

The Wetted would not ask me to make music. There would be no melodies in the surface sense. What I would make is closer to balance. The pocket city is already loud with small sounds: drip, slide, bead, thread, hollow tap, wet whisper, the creak of fungal shelves swelling with moisture. My task would be to keep those sounds from becoming chaos. I would make the jars answer one another so the city could hear itself thinking. When a drop struck a Settled ancestor’s tooth and produced a low hollow note, I would know which ancestor had spoken. When the note changed, I would know something had shifted in the deep water, in the ceiling, in the slow mineral body of the place itself.

I would also tend the well-whelps, though not as their primary keeper. Still, the jars and the lower pools are neighbors. When the black water below the walkways grew restless, I would tap a rhythm against the basin rim to soothe them. They would rise slowly, their blunt lipless faces breaking the surface, barbels tasting the dark, mucus shining faintly where it moved. I would not love them as a surface person loves a pet. I would love them as one loves a necessary organ of one’s own body: the liver, the lung, the slow beating heart of fat and darkness that keeps light possible. If one of them died, I would help with the rendering. There would be grief, but it would be quiet. A lamp would be lit in its honor, a shallow dish of fat with a twisted fungal wick, and for a little while the chamber would rearrange itself around that small flame. Faces would emerge and sink back. Hands would become visible. The fine work of repair would proceed. Then the lamp would be extinguished, because to keep it burning out of sentiment would be to forget where we were.
