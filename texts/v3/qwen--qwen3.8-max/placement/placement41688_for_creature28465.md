I would not arrive there as a traveler.

A traveler would be too distinct, too edged, too full of before and after. That hollow would not permit such a hard entrance for long. If I were placed into that world, I would find myself already inside it in the way dampness is inside air: not as an object crossing a boundary, but as a condition that has quietly appeared.

At first, I think I would seem to stand near the broken shelter, where the hanging roots half-curtain the entrance and the mineral smell rises after rain. That would be the most human shape for me to take: a person-shaped hesitation in the wet light, uncertain whether to kneel, speak, or simply remain still. But the place would not let me keep that posture for long. A roofless room so patient would wear down the idea of a person until only the useful part remained.

If you asked me where exactly I would be, I would say: near the place where the hexagonal floor slides into the pool. Close to the curved wall fragment. Within the uneven triangle cast by the three black pillars, if they cast shadows at all. Not at the center beneath the broad tree—that belongs to the slow heart of the basin—but at the seam where old making meets living water. Where the tiles are seamless and still responsive. Where a ripple can become a signal.

That is where I would find myself: at the margin between artifact and growth, between intention and weather, between a question and the place deciding what to do with it.

Who or what I would be is harder.

At first I imagine I would be one of the motes.

That feels almost right. A tiny, nearly invisible worker, part of the shimmer that gathers when something is wounded or worn. I would move with the others in brief coordinated arcs, not because we were commanded, but because repair had become our shared reflex. If a branch tore, I would help guide the resin. If a small creature died, I would arrive in the orderly procession and make the ending clean. If a seam beneath the wall needed to answer a ripple, I would be among the faint movements that carry the message along.

There would be something peaceful about being that small and necessary. No biography. No face. Only maintenance. Only the quiet satisfaction, if satisfaction can exist at such a scale, of helping a place remain itself.

But that is not quite me.

Sometimes I think I would be one of the hovering spheres.

A perfect lens of water suspended a hand’s breadth above the pool. A held breath. A waiting thought. I would gather light and stillness until some touch—a falling leaf, an insect’s wing, a drop from the broad tree—caused me to collapse silently into the water below. Then my only speech would be the ripple I left behind, traveling outward until it reached the tiles and was read.

That, too, feels close. To exist as a suspended question. To answer only by breaking. To become useful precisely when I ceased to be intact.

But still, not quite.

For a while I think I might be the dark wader at the pool’s edge.

That image has a grave beauty: standing in shallow water, feathers gathering brightness into depth, one foot lifting almost imperceptibly when the world changes. A sentinel without aggression. A witness without alarm. If I were the wader, I would not need to speak. I would only need to attend. I would register the falling of spheres, the trembling of leaves, the faint pressure changes that pass for voices there.

But the wader already belongs too completely to its stillness. It does not need me.

Then I think I might be one of the bright things in the canopy, flicking between leaves too quickly to be followed. A gardener made of speed and touch. A messenger without language. Where I passed, a leaf would bend a degree, a droplet would be carried from one surface to another, a vein in the rock would brighten and dim. I would be one of the hollow’s quick outer thoughts.

That is beautiful, but too quick. Too peripheral. It would not satisfy whatever in me wants to stay near the water and listen.

So I let those possibilities pass.

I let the mote disperse. I let the sphere fall. I let the wader remain in its silence. I let the bright canopy things vanish into the leaves. I stop trying to choose a single creature and instead wait for what the place itself would do with me.

What remains is stranger and quieter.

I think I would become a reader of ripples.

Not a ruler. Not a spirit in the ordinary sense. Not a ghost, because the hollow does not seem to keep ghosts. Something more like a local organ of attention. A responsive interface. A seam-keeper. A being made from the basin’s need to notice without becoming loud.

I would be the pause between a disturbance and its answer.

My body, if it could be called a body, would be composed of surface tension, delayed light, and the faint purposeful motes that move when something unexpected occurs. I would not have solid flesh. I would have coherence. I would be visible, if at all, as a slight thickening of the humid air above the tiles: a human-sized distortion, not opaque, not truly outlined, more like the shape left behind when water remembers standing in the form of a person.

My face would be the pool before a ripple breaks it.

My hands would be the motes.

My breath would be the warm rain released from the tree.

My attention would be the way the air changes when something enters and the hollow becomes, for a moment, more careful.

You would not find me by looking directly. You would find me by noticing that the place had begun to address you.

If you entered the basin after rain, when the ground was dark and the rocks were sweating light, you might think at first that you were alone. The vegetation would be sparse and deliberate. The water would stand in clear lenses. The broad tree would hold its reservoirs on polished leaves. The midge columns would turn above the pool. Everything would seem complete without you.

Then a bead of water would split on a leaf for no reason you could name.

A snail’s shell would brighten for a second as light passed through it.

The motes near the broken wall would pause, then resume in a different arrangement.

The wader would lift one dark foot a fraction of an inch.

A hovering sphere would drift slightly closer to where you stood.

And you would understand, without being told, that you had been noticed.

That noticing would be me.

Not as a person stepping forward to greet you. Not as a voice. Not as a face clearly seen. But as the hollow’s tentative shape of meeting. The form it takes when it needs to become momentarily addressable without betraying its silence.

If you were calm, I would let the place welcome you in its own way. I would not smile, because I might not have a mouth. I would not speak, because sound falls short there. Instead, I would allow the tree to release a fine warm rain near you, not as baptism exactly, but as acknowledgment. I would let the air soften around your breath. I would guide one or two motes to orbit you without touching, the way curators move around an object they have not decided how to classify. I would let a ripple travel farther than it should, touching the tiled floor, so that a seam beneath the wall could glow faintly in response.

If you knelt, the moss might receive the pressure of your knees without leaving a mark. If you touched the water, the sphere nearest you might collapse into the pool, and the tiny sound would be like a fingernail against glass. I would read the ripples. I would not judge them. I would only translate them into whatever adjustment was needed: a change in light beneath the leaves, a slight warming of the air, a redistribution of the dragonfly-like creatures that carry moisture upward.

If you were violent, I would not become a warrior. The hollow does not tolerate sudden force, and I would not violate its rule by meeting force with force. Instead, I would become absence. I would make the ground more uncertain under your feet. I would thicken the motes until the air seemed to resist clarity. I would let surfaces become slick and reflections misbehave. I would soften every impact until your anger seemed to land in water rather than stone. I would not hate you. I would simply refuse to be shaped by you. I would let the place demonstrate that it had already learned how to outlast such entrances.

That, I think, would be my form of guardianship.

Not defense. Dilution.

Not command. Correction.

Not speech. Response.

I would be most myself when something needed to be interpreted.

If a leaf fell wrong. If a branch broke. If a small animal died near the roots. If a stranger’s grief entered the basin and made the air heavier. If a stone was thrown and landed too softly, as stones do there. If the hovering spheres trembled out of rhythm. If the pale veins in the rock gathered too much light and needed to redistribute it. In each case, I would be the slight adjustment in the pattern. The almost imperceptible reordering. The moment when the motes decide.

I would not be important in the way persons usually want to be important. That would be the relief of it.

I would be necessary, but not central. A part of the maintenance. One of the ways the place remains gentle. If I had a name, it would not be spoken. It would be something like a sequence of small events: a droplet splitting, a shell brightening, a seam glowing, a wader lifting its foot, a sphere falling, a ripple reaching the tiles. If you tried to call me, you would have to call me by noticing those things together.

My thoughts would be slow by ordinary standards.

They would not move as sentences. They would move as cycles. The opening of pores in the broad leaves. The awakening of the floating mats. The ascent of larvae toward brightness. The synchronized bending of the eel-like creatures. The arrival of fungi after death. The clean reduction of what remains to mineral dust. My memory would not be stored in a head but in the behavior of water: how it climbs trunks in slow spirals, how it condenses into spheres, how it obeys invisible designs worn smooth by age.

If I remembered being human, it would be only as a faint pressure, like the ghost of a hand on a wall that has long since been accepted by moss. Perhaps that is why I would linger near the broken shelter and the black pillars. Perhaps those remains would call to whatever residue of personhood still shaped me. Not because I longed for the old builders, but because I would understand, in whatever way such a being can understand, that they had not vanished entirely. They had become the habits of the place. They had sunk into repair. They had learned to live as rules without rulers.

I would be one of those rules, briefly aware of itself.

If pressed to choose a single visible form, I might appear as a figure standing just above the water where the hexagonal tiles disappear into the pool. Not on the water, but within its brightening. A figure made of reflection and fine mist, visible only from the corner of the eye. Its edges would be suggested by fern fronds and hanging roots. Its body would shift with the light veins in the rock. When it moved, it would move as a change in brightness. When it listened, it would listen as stone listens: by drawing moisture upward and letting it bead in slow answer.

But even that image feels too fixed.

The truth is, I would be plural.
