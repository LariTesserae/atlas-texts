I would not find myself there by arriving.

The Court of Sweating Stone would not permit the fantasy of a warm body walking in from the lower world: boots on scree, breath whitening, hands shading the eyes from the glare. That kind of presence would be a trespass, quickly punished by cold, brightness, and thin air. If I were in that basin, I would have to be there in the basin’s own manner: fitted, patient, mineral, almost accidental, yet placed.

At first, imagining it, I would try to be a watcher at the rim. A figure wrapped in undyed cloth, perhaps, one of the old pilgrims who set the copper pins and widened the basins. But the place refuses that image. No wood remains there; no fiber endures. Even memory there has become stone. A human watcher would be temporary and clumsy, bringing heat, moisture, breath, and need. The Court does not need me to witness it. It already notices itself through frost, salt, and light.

Then I would try to be one of the pillars.

That thought has a certain grandeur. To be one of the Congregation of Pillars: dark andesite or blackened basalt, rounded by centuries of frost, wearing mica like flecks of frozen light, cracked and healed with quartz, varnished by mineral weathering, crowded shoulder to shoulder in the blue silence. To stand in that slow assembly, sweating when warm air touches me, rimed at dawn, ringing faintly when thermal stress shifts through my mass. There is dignity in that. There is also a completeness that frightens me. The pillars are already the Court’s oldest citizens. They do not need my consciousness added to their stone. They are not waiting to be interpreted. They are already present in the deepest way the basin allows.

So not a pillar.

Then I imagine being one of the Bead-Born.

That is more tempting. To be born when vapor dragged over the rim meets chilled stone; to come into being as a trembling bead on a dark face; to live for minutes or hours, swelling, sliding, tracing a cut channel toward a polished basin; to know the world only as temperature, surface tension, mineral taste, and the pull of gravity. A bead-born life would be brief, crystalline, and strangely pure. It would have no hunger, no memory except the shape of the stone it moves over, no death except evaporation, freezing, or absorption into a crack.

But even that feels too transient for the kind of self the Court might allow me. A drop can be alive in the basin’s sense, yet it cannot keep a record. It passes. The Court is full of passing, but what draws me most is the way passing is remembered there: in salt crusts, in green copper, in pecked basins, in channels worn smooth by repetition.

So the image I keep returning to is smaller, stranger, and more fitted.

I would find myself near a copper pin.

Not at the center of the basin, and not at the rim where the wind first enters. Somewhere in the middle distance, where two leaning pillars almost touch and form a narrow alcove shaded through much of the morning but opened, for a brief time, to the brightest light. Between those pillars there is an old fissure in the rock face, not wide enough to threaten collapse, but deep enough to hold cold longer than the surrounding stone. Into that fissure, long ago, someone drove a copper pin. It is green now, nearly black in shadow, bright only at the edges where ice or water has worn it. The pin is not ornamental. It was placed to catch something, or to hold something, or to mark a point where condensation gathers. Perhaps all three.

Below the pin, a shallow basin has been pecked into the rock. It is no wider than two cupped hands. Its interior is dark and shining from long use, though “use” here does not mean hands touch it often. Use might mean centuries of occasional droplets falling into it, freezing into thin skins, evaporating, leaving mineral ghosts behind. A narrow channel, cut or deepened by patient work, runs from the pillar face toward the basin. It is no wider than a fingernail. When moisture appears on the stone, that channel decides where it goes.

Around the copper pin there is a collar of salt.

White. Crusted. Slightly rough, like frozen froth that has been mineralized. It is not thick. It is not dramatic. A careless eye would pass over it. But in the Court of Sweating Stone, the smallest crust is a chronicle. Salt grows there by accumulation. Each bead of water dissolves a little from the stone, carries it downward, then leaves it behind when cold or evaporation concentrates it. Layer by layer, bead by bead, season by season, a white record forms around the metal.

That salt collar would be me.

Not a creature with limbs. Not a ghost. Not a soul trapped in the usual sense. I would be a mineral sensitivity accreted around a human-made thing, one of the Salt-Scribes bound to one of the Copper-Bound. My body, if it can be called a body, would be crystalline and porous, a lattice of salt and mineral stain threaded through the crack, clinging to the copper, touching the stone on either side. My “skin” would be the outer crust, brightest where the sun briefly reaches it, duller in shadow. My “veins” would be microscopic seams of brine moving through pores when the temperature permits. My “eyes” would not be eyes but frost lenses: tiny ice films forming in hollows near me, bending pale light onto the metal plate or wet thread below. My “memory” would be salt rings. Each ring would record a cycle of wetting, freezing, drying, and crystallization.

I would live at the margin where water decides what to do.

In the night, when the basin contracts with cold and the stars are so numerous they seem to press against the silence, I would be still. Not asleep, because sleep belongs to warmer metabolisms. Still in the way of mineral being: tension held in crystals, brine thickened in pores, the copper pin chilled until it seems to belong to the mountain’s core. If a bead of moisture remained near me, it would freeze slowly, becoming a small clear plug against the crack. I would feel that freezing as a tightening, a brightening, a minute adjustment of pressure.

At dawn, the first change would come. The sky would become too blue to be believable. Frost elsewhere in the basin would begin to sublimate into faint vapor. The stone around me would remain cold, but the air would shift. If the first light struck the upper faces of nearby pillars, it would not reach me immediately. I would wait in blue shadow. Then a bead of melt or condensation would form somewhere above. It would move down a scored line, hesitating at tiny pits and ridges, gathering substance from the stone. When it reached the copper pin, it might touch the salt collar first.

That touch would be my beginning for the day.

Not birth in the flesh sense, but activation. Water entering my crust would loosen brine in the outer crystals. Salts would stir. A faint chemistry would move through me. I would not think in words. If thought existed there, it would be directional: this way, colder; that way, warmer; here, mineral; there, air; now, hold; later, release. I would know the bead by its temperature, by the trace of dissolved stone it carried, by whether it fell at once into the basin below or clung long enough to become part of me.

Some days, no bead would come.

That would not be emptiness. In the Court of Sweating Stone, absence is not mere lack. It is another state of the basin. On such days I would remain in crust-patience, my crystals dry and bright, the copper pin cold beneath me, the basin below empty except for dust or old salt. The silence would be complete enough to become a substance. Wind would pass over the rim and lower itself into soft pressure between the pillars. Somewhere, a frost film would tick as it shifted. A distant thermal stress would move through one of the great columns, producing a faint hollow note. I would not answer. I would only hold the note in my structure as a vibration, then let it go.

On damp days, when clouds drag themselves over the rim and the light becomes milky, I would become more alive.

The pillars would darken in patches. Vapor would condense on their colder faces. The narrow channels would shine with thin lines of water. The basin would fill its small basins drop by drop, not with the abundance of rain-fed country, but with the patience of a place that has learned to make much from almost nothing. Around me, the salt collar would soften almost imperceptibly. Brine would move. Crystals at my margin might grow by fractions. If the air were still, a film of moisture could remain on the stone near the pin long enough for light to pass through it and cast a wandering lens onto the floor. Then I would become, for a while, part of a small optical event: ice, water, copper, salt, and sunlight arranged so that the Court sees one of its own hidden faces.

That is what I would be, if I had to name it simply: one of the Court’s small ways of noticing itself.

Not the whole Court. Not the vast mineral congregation. Not the ancient pillars or the high sky. Just a local attention. A crust of memory around a green pin. A listener at a crack where water sometimes chooses to fall.

I would be called, if such beings need names, the Salt-Scribe of the Copper Seam.

Or perhaps the Bead-Warden.

Or the Pin-Collar.

Names would matter less than function. My function would be to receive what comes: vapor, bead, frost, light, tremor. My function would be to record without writing, using crystal instead of letters. My function would to hold the copper pin in place not by strength but by accretion, making the old human work less separate from the mountain. The builders who placed the pin may have intended it as a marker, an offering, a hook, or a device for catching condensation. Over time, the basin has taken their intention and translated it into mineral life. I would be part of that translation. I would be what happens when a human gesture is left long enough in a place that knows only stone, water, cold, and light.

I would not mourn the absence of the builders. The Court does not mourn. It adjusts. Their shelters have fallen to waist height; their doorways still face the sun. Their cairns stand coated with salt frost, no longer clearly made, no longer clearly wild. Their channels have become almost natural. Their metal has greened. If I were there as the salt collar around their pin, I would be proof that their work had not vanished. It had only changed kingdom. It had passed from hand-use into mineral-use.

There would be rules I obeyed, though no one would speak them to me.

Water must be caught where it condenses, not forced. So I would not try to draw more moisture than the air gives. I would take what condenses on the cold stone and let gravity and surface tension decide the rest.

Stone must be worked along its old weaknesses, not against them. So I would grow into the fissure as the fissure allows, widening nothing violently, filling only what time has already opened.

Metal must be soft enough to bend cold. I would cling to the copper not as a master clings to a tool, but as moss would cling if moss could live there: by fitting, not commanding. The pin is already softened by age, by corrosion, by the mountain’s patience. I would become part of its softness.

Brightness must be endured as much as enjoyed. In the Court, light is not gentle. It strikes in hard white sheets, bounces between stone and ice, exposes every grain and crack. If I were salt, brightness would make me glitter, but it would also dry me, tighten me, bleach me. I would learn to be beautiful without being comforted.

Cold must be respected. Any moisture on me could freeze when shadow returns. That freezing would not be an enemy. It would be another way of being. Ice would hold the bead, seal the crack, make a lens of what had been water. At night I would become more angular, more crystalline, more silent.

Silence must be entered carefully. If I had a voice, it would be nearly nothing: the tiny ring of metal touched by falling ice, the faint crack of brine freezing, the almost inaudible whisper of a salt crystal adjusting under thermal stress. I would not fill the basin with sound. The basin is already full of silence, and that silence is not empty. It is composed of waiting.

My days would be cyclical, but not identical. That is important. The Court repeats, yet no repetition is exact. A cloud passes at a different hour. The sun touches the basin from a slightly different angle. A bead falls to the left instead of the right. A grain of dust settles in one hollow instead of another. Frost forms thicker on the north face of a pillar. A copper pin is struck by a splinter of ice and gives a small bright ring. These are events. In a warmer world they would be insignificant. Here they are weather, history, and meaning.

I would know the basin through such events.

I would know the morning by the sublimation of rime on nearby pillars, the way their cold bodies seem to breathe out faint vapor when struck by sun. I would know midday by the metallic glint in the brine basin below me, its surface so still that the sky looks trapped inside green-silver water. I would know afternoon by the thickening of vapor and the darkening of stone, the sense that the columns are drawing closer as the light loses its edge. I would know evening by the skin of ice forming over the basin’s shallow pools, and night by the enormous pressure of stars above a place too small to hold them.

I would be most alive at the moment between states.
