I would not find myself arriving from above, not truly. If I belonged to that country, the salt crust would not be a ceiling I had once broken through and remembered; it would simply be the pale roof of the world, the scarred underside through which light came in broken columns. I would not be an explorer there. I would be native to the dryness.

And I would not be in the most dramatic places—not first, not mainly. Not out on the pale basin where salt crusts the root platforms like frost, and not down in the great avenue of hollow tubes where the air roars too steadily. I would find myself at a seam: a mid-terrace of overlapping leaf-bases above a chaff valley, where one kind of country argues with another. On one side, the root platforms would be pale and mineral-stiffened; on the other, the low waxy leaf-fields would shift in thermal drafts. Between them, the ground would be made of compressed fibers, shed sheaths, and seed-cases pressed into a surface that feels almost architectural until it decides otherwise.

There, I think, I would be one of the beings who know the place not by owning it, but by listening to it.

If I had to give my kind a name, I would call us the Hushborn. Not because we are silent—we are not; silence here would be as dangerous as noise—but because we have learned that sound is a tool, a weapon, a lure, a season, and sometimes a catastrophe. We do not speak casually. We do not shout. We move in deliberate rhythms. We sign with our hands, tap on hollow stems, scrape resin plates, strike seed-drums, and shape messages the landscape can misinterpret if we are careless. A careless cry can shake pollen loose. A wrong footfall can summon burrowers. A bad resonance can split a pod at the worst moment. So we have become careful. We have become tuned.

I would be human-shaped, but not human in the old surface sense. My body would be sealed against that country. My skin would carry a waxen bloom, dry and fine, not moist at all. My pores would not waste fluid. My blood would not be thin and red in the way of surface animals; it would be warm, oily, resin-threaded, something closer to seed-milk than water-blood. If I were cut, I would not bleed freely. I would leak. The wound would be serious because any interior wetness lost is a thing that cannot be replaced from the world outside. In that country, to lose one’s inner fluid is not to lose “water” as an element of weather. It is to lose self.

My face would be half-hidden. I would wear a mask of woven mesh over nose and mouth, fine enough to keep out the worst spore-dust and pollen-rash. My goggles would be made from translucent seed-scales, polished with oil until they become amber-clear. My cloak would be layered leaf-leather, waxed and stiffened, clicking softly when I move. Under it, my body would be thin and light-boned, because the ground itself is sometimes only half-decided. A heavy being would sink through new root-felt. A clumsy being would break a slope. I would be light enough to pass over chaff drifts without leaving deep scars. My hands would be my most important organs: long-fingered, calloused, sensitive to tension, trained to feel the difference between a living stem, a dying one, and a dead one already being converted into habitat.

I would live, if “live” is the word, in a small knot of people—perhaps seven, perhaps twelve, never enough to burden a ridge. Our camp would not be a village. It would be a temporary architecture lashed into the crotches of giant stems: platforms woven from fiber, bridges of braided root, windbreaks made from overlapping leaf-plates, seed-caches sealed with resin plugs. From a distance, our homes would look like nests or swellings in the vegetation. They would not be permanent because permanence is not offered there. A camp might be absorbed by new growth. A bridge might be eaten. A cache might become a nest for something with more mandibles than memory. We would accept this. We would build lightly, precisely, and without grief.

My own place would be a listening ledge near the stem-crotch, under a curved seed-scale roof. Beside it, a resin pillar would stand half-translucent in the broken light, veined with trapped pollen and the occasional wing of some ancient moth. I would sleep in a shallow hammock of coated fiber, not because the ground is unsafe—though it is—but because the ground changes its mind. In sleep, one should not become part of a new terrace by accident.

I would be a Listener. That is the nearest word.

Not a priest, though I might seem like one. Not an engineer, though my hands would know leverage, tension, binding, and load. Not a hunter, though I might set snares along beetle trails. A Listener is something more specific to that country: a being who understands that the landscape is acoustically alive and that many of its processes are triggered by vibration. I would carry a staff of dense heartwood, fire-hardened but never used to make fire, fitted with a tip of polished bone. I would use it to tap stems, test root-mats, and send careful rhythms ahead of me when crossing uncertain ground. At my hip I would carry a small drum made from a seed-case and stretched membrane, because sometimes one must answer the country before it finishes asking.

My work would be part midwifery, part pest management, part cartography, part prayer.

I would know which tap-pattern makes seed-vessels tremble open without bursting. I would know which low scrape on a resin plate calms a pollinator swarm when it thickens into weather. I would know which vibrations call burrowers to stabilize a soft slope, and which ones send them scattering into panic. I would know the difference between the creak of a stem settling and the creak of a stem preparing to collapse into a new hollow. I would know when a feeding rush is about to strip a valley bare, because the air would change: a dry tension, a sudden alertness in the fronds, a thinning of small sounds before the chewing begins.

I would make maps, but not the kind that last. My maps would be patterns: rhythms struck on stems, knots tied in fiber strips, sequences of hand-signs taught to children before they are old enough to carry blades. A route across the mid-terraces might be remembered as three soft taps, a pause, a scrape leftward, then the sign for “wait until the pollen river passes.” If a slope were eaten into a new shape, the map would become false. That would not be a failure. That would be the country doing what it does. We would remake the map. We would not expect the land to hold still for our memory.

In that world, I think I would be most myself in the moments just before a swarm passes.

Imagine it: the light columns are ordinary, white and plain, but they begin to flicker as if dust has become weather. A low pressure builds in the ears, not heard so much as felt in the chest. The fronds along the terrace lift slightly. Seed-cups tremble. The constant granular hiss of small bodies through plant-matter changes pitch. I would stand on the platform, one hand on the hollow stem beside me, and feel the vibration travel down through root-felt and old lignified growth. It would be like touching a drum that has not yet been struck.

Then the swarm would come.

Not as a few insects, but as a brown-gold storm: wings, antennae, chitin, pollen-dusted thoraxes, a moving ceiling over the valley. The light would ripple. The noise would become a boiling roar. The air would thicken with wing-dust until every breath through the mask tastes warm and resinous. I would not run. Running is a rhythm of panic, and panic spreads here more easily than fire. I would tap the stem beside me—slow, measured, a pattern old enough that the local organisms have grown up inside it. The swarm would not obey me. Nothing there truly obeys. But it might pass over rather than through. It might keep its hunger elsewhere. It might leave our terrace as a small dry island while the valley beyond becomes lace.

Afterward, I would go down with the others to harvest what the country has offered. Not sentimentally. We would collect shed wings, cracked seed-cases, pollen compressed into cakes, resin beads loosened by vibration, and the dry remains of creatures too slow to escape the swarm’s passage. Nothing would be wasted. Oil would be pressed from seed-milk. Chitin would be sorted for scrapers and awls. Wings might become patches for membranes or tiny panes for shelters. The dead would not rot. They would be disassembled, sorted, folded into texture.

I would be part of that economy too. I would not imagine myself above it.

If I died there—and death, in that place, would be a dry and efficient affair—I would not become a grave. The country does not keep graves. My body would lose its heat, then its oils would be drawn out by beetles and grubs, my fibers separated, my bones polished and carried to shrines or tool-racks. Perhaps my mandible, if I wore one as ornament, would be returned to a marker. Perhaps a seed-spine needle carved from my own densest bones would be given to an apprentice. Perhaps my drum-skin would be cut and reused. I would not be offended. To become texture is not insult there. It is continuation.

This is why I would not be a solitary hero in that country. The place would not tolerate that shape of selfhood for long. I would be a person, yes, but a person defined by relations: to the stem I tap, the swarm I calm, the slope I cross, the bridge I mend, the people who know my rhythm. My name would not be a spoken word. It would be a signature pattern. Two light taps, a pause, one rasp, then a hollow knock. If someone wished to call me from across a terrace, they would not shout. They would strike that sequence on a resonant surface, and somewhere in the vegetation around me, a membrane would answer.

I would be afraid of fire.

Not in the way surface people fear it—as warmth turned feral—but as a sacred obscenity. In that dry country, sparks are a kind of madness. The air can carry pollen dust and spore-powder fine enough to catch. Resins burn eagerly. A single careless strike could turn a basin into a bright, screaming ruin. So I would carry no flint for sparks. My tools would be bone, wood, obsidian, seed-spine, resin, fiber, and perhaps one small copper clasp traded from far away, worn smooth and used only where no impact can make it bite. Fire, if it exists at all among us, would be kept in sealed clay or resin-lined pits so rare and controlled that they feel more like shrines than hearths. More likely, we would not keep fire. We would live warm without it, surrounded by metabolic heat and sun-heated stems, and consider flame a thing that belongs above the salt, in the careless world where wetness falls from the sky.

I would be gentle with the built marks of my people because they are few and temporary. Our platforms would not pretend to be stone. Our bridges would not pretend to be permanent. Our snares would be set with apology, not because we are morally delicate, but because every animal taken is also a worker in the country’s making. A beetle that opens seed-cases is not merely meat or shell. It is part of the next season’s architecture. A burrower is not merely a pest. It is a ground-maker.
