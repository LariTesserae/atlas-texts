Chemical differences bloom in cool plumes, and where they rise the transparent drifters are altered without choosing alteration. A thread of colder water makes them draw themselves out into longer sleeves. A mineral-rich vein makes their contractions slower, more adhesive, as if the water inside them had decided, for no reason that could be called decision, to stay a little longer. A warm lens descending from the sun-touched upper layer thins them until they are barely a distortion, a clear thickness no more substantial than a magnification.

They do not feed as later creatures will feed. There is no seizure, no tearing, no swallowing. What enters them enters because descent and current bring it through them. What remains remains because their substance, for a moment, is shaped in such a way that release is delayed. A spent light-mote, no longer buoyant, may fall into one of their elongated folds. It may continue falling. Or it may be held in a shallow internal pocket, a cavity that is not quite an organ, while the clear body tightens almost imperceptibly around it. In that holding, chemistry begins. The mote dims. Its substance becomes available. The transparent body does not triumph. It does not even know. It has simply become, for a short time, a place where something is kept.

This is the first gesture of the High Birthwater that seems intimate: not movement, not orientation, not appetite, but retention. The ocean, which has no shores, no floor, no artifacts, no history, begins to have interiors.

If there is a being here, it is not a thing with edges but the act of keeping an edge for a while.

If I ask where I would find myself there, the answer does not arrive as a coordinate. There is no bank to stand upon, no hull to lean against, no ruin by which to say this is here and that is there. The question itself must soften. It must stop being a point and become a gradient. It must become something that can be felt across pressure, temperature, and mineral taste.

At first I think I would be among the light-facing motes. I can imagine being one of those clear, buoyant disks, turned endlessly toward the white-gold sun, taking light as a condition rather than a desire. There would be a strange peace in that: no inside, no lack, no search. I would be part of the bright ceiling’s minute metallic order, one of the countless small touches that make the surface seem worked. But that is too near the sky, too flat, too numerous. It would be existence without inwardness. I would be alive, perhaps, but not yet a someone. I would be a grazed brightness, not a body.

Then I think I would be one of the clear drifters below them. That is closer. I would be a long transparent swelling in the blue, a sleeve or veil with no face, no front, no head. I would move by allowing my own substance to become uneven: thicker here, thinner there, contracting slowly until the water accepted that I was elsewhere. I would not dart. I would not chase. I would know the world as gradients. Sun-warmth above. Mineral coolness below. The faint pressure of another drifting thickness nearby. The slight chemical difference that says something has risen from the young crust or fallen from the light-grazing film.

But even that is not precise enough. If I am honest, I would not find myself simply as one of those bodies. I would find myself at the place where one of them becomes, for the first time, slightly more than a body-shaped current.

I would be the moment a descending mote enters a fold of clear substance and does not immediately pass out.

I would be the pocket that forms around it.

Not the mouth, because there is no mouth. Not the stomach, because there is no stomach. Not the hunger, because hunger has not yet become a feeling. I would be the delay between contact and release. I would be the first interior of the High Birthwater: a temporary cavity, a retained difference, a living pause in the ordinary fall and rise of matter.

My “skin” would not be skin. It would be a behavior of water: a surface tension of selfhood, a boundary maintained only as long as the conditions around it allow. My “thought” would be pressure. My “memory” would be a chemical residue, a faint density left behind after something has passed through me. My “desire,” if such a word can be used without lying, would be the tendency to remain coherent a little longer than the water immediately around me. Not to survive. Not yet. Only to continue being a place where something can happen slowly.

I would live, if living is the word, in the middle blue. Below the hammered ceiling. Above the restless crust. Between the bright film of motes and the mineral plumes rising from below. That is where I would find myself: in the zone where daylight becomes sinking particles and stone becomes dissolved newness. I would be neither surface nor foundation. I would be the transaction between them.

Who or what would I be?

I would be one of the faceless transparent creatures, or perhaps the earliest interior arising in one of them. A contractile clarity. A sleeve-shaped retention. A body not yet convinced it is a body. A living lens with no eye behind it. A threshold animal, if animal is too strong a word, or a threshold before animals, if threshold is strong enough.

I would not be a person. I would not have a name. I would not recognize myself. I would not look out at the world. I would be looked through by the world, and for a little while that looking-through would be shaped by me.

In the High Birthwater, where nothing has been made and no shore has begun, I would be the first made thing that is not made: a self occurring as a fold. A border with a preference. A clear, slow inside. I would not merely live in the Birthwater.

I would be one of the ways it is beginning to have one.
