I would not find myself in the center of the roar.

Not at first.

The city is too vast, too patient, too heavy with accumulated habit for me to begin at its obvious heart. I would not be beside the largest turbine, or standing at the mouth of the resonators, or walking the broad plaza where the light comes clean and ordinary over all that damp stone. I would be somewhere secondary, somewhere in the interval: a gallery, a shoulder, a narrow service passage where the sound has been bent by masonry and arrives not as noise but as pressure.

You would find me where the wall begins to bead.

More exactly: in a low passage beneath a pipe bridge on one of the middle terraces, where warm mineral water moves in a shallow channel and the air is so soft it feels like breath held too long. The place would have a name, but not a loud one. Something like the Gallery of Small Beads, or the Wet Shoulder, or the passage under the Hall of Low Breath. A name worn smooth by use, more sign than word. The people there might call it by a two-handed gesture: palm downward, fingers trembling slightly as condensation forms.

I would be there because that is where the city’s attention gathers in miniature. A single bead forms on an iron bracket. It swells, shines, releases. Another forms along a seam of basalt. A line of moisture descends the glazed brick in a path so old that the surface has learned it. If I am anywhere in this world, I am near that line.

At first I imagine I might be one of the pale moths in the upper pipe galleries. They appeal to me: quiet, felt-winged, absorbing vibration instead of resisting it. But they hide too much. They live in the high margins, not in the long work of maintenance.

Then I imagine I might be one of the blind fish in the cisterns, moving through warm dark water by pressure alone. There is something attractive about that too: a life reduced to sensitivity, no need for sight, no need for speech, only the slow reading of currents. But the fish are too contained. They do not touch the walls with intention.

For a while I think I might be the heron-like bird at the reservoir edge, standing motionless, wary, almost but not quite belonging. But the bird leaves. The city does not tolerate longing well. It prefers presence.

So the image that remains is stranger and quieter than those.

I would be a poreward.

That is the closest word. A tender of pores, seams, weeping faces, and slow passages. Not an engineer in the hard sense, not a priest, not a ruler of systems. A custodian of damp thresholds. A being made by the city into an instrument of attention.

I would be mostly human, but “human” would need to be stretched a little to fit me. The basin would have worked on me. My skin would be softened at the wrists and knuckles from constant humidity. My palms would be cool and unusually sensitive, able to tell the difference between stone that is merely wet and stone that is sweating correctly. My ears would be less important than my bones. I would hear the city not as melody or clamor but as a distribution of pressures: in the soles of my feet, in my teeth, in the fine bones of my inner wrists, in the way a shallow bowl of water trembles when I set it down.

I would not be deaf. That is the wrong way to describe it. I would simply have become secondary-eared. My hearing would be useful for locating leaks or the hiss of a valve, but not for judgment. Judgment would live in my hands.

My clothing would be shaped by the place. Waxed cloth, oiled canvas, a hood with a shallow gutter along the shoulders to guide condensation away from the neck. My sleeves would have tiny stitched channels, not decorative but practical, meant to lead moisture downward instead of letting it pool at the cuffs. My boots would be soft-soled, worn thin in places, because I would need to feel whether a grate is dry, damp, or beginning to sweat. My fingertips would often be bare. Gloves would be for carrying rough things, not for knowing.

I would carry little but carefully. A soft cloth. A small oil can. A wooden scraper for mineral crust. A tile and stylus for marking changes. A shallow bowl, perhaps, because bowls of water are honest. In a place where ears lie, rings on a still surface tell the truth.

My name would probably not be a spoken one.

It might be a sign: thumb pressed briefly at the sternum, then fingers opening outward from the chest, as if condensation were spreading across a cool surface. Those who needed to call me something aloud might approximate it with a low syllable, perhaps something soft and unsharp. “Sess,” or “Oma,” or “Lowhand.” I would not be attached to the sound. I would answer to the intention.

My work would be modest and continuous.

Each shift, I would walk the same terraces, the same walls, the same channel lips. Not because nothing changes, but because the changes are so slight that only repetition can reveal them. A mineral grain has begun to fuse near a drainage slot. A thread of water has shifted a finger’s width to the left. A patch of pale moss has expanded into a place where it may soon alter the beading pattern. A section of wall that should be damp has become merely cool.

A dry wall would concern me more than a broken one.

Broken things here are obvious. Dryness is subtler. It means the balance has moved somewhere else. It means a duct has narrowed, a horn has been closed too long, a resonator has drifted in pitch, an acoustic gate has redirected too much sound away from a face that needs encouragement. In this city, dryness is not cleanliness. It is a symptom.

So I would not attack the problem. I would not scrub the wall or seal the stone or force water where it no longer remembers going. I would trace the condition backward. I would place my palm flat against basalt and wait. I would watch how dust behaves on a railing. I would set a bowl of water near the channel and observe the rings. I would feel whether the hum underfoot has become thinner, deeper, or uneven.

Then I would make a small correction.

Open a vent by a fraction. Clear a crust from the lip of a weep hole. Adjust a ceramic cone so condensation gathers and falls in the right line. Signal to another keeper that a horn on the upper terrace needs easing. Allow a little more sound into the lower gallery, not enough to shake, only enough to coax.

The city would not want violence done to it. It would want guidance.

I would live in one of the quieter pockets, though “quiet” there would still mean a world full of pressure. My room would be behind an acoustic shoulder, perhaps in the lee of a pump house where the standing waves cancel enough to let a body rest. The walls would not be dry. They would be kept damp in controlled lines, their moisture guided into narrow channels. The floor would slope gently toward a drain. The corners would be rounded. My sleeping place would be suspended or mounted on resilient material so the vibration would not enter me all night and leave me exhausted by morning.

I would keep three trays of water in the room. One near the door, one near the bed, one beneath a high vent. Not for drinking. For reading. If their surfaces trembled evenly, the city was in balance. If one went still while the others moved, something had changed in the acoustic field. If all went still at once, I would wake afraid.

My domestic beauty would be the beauty of maintenance.

A handrail polished by generations of hands. A channel worn into a perfect curve. A glazed brick wall wiped so often that its mineral streaks have become familiar as handwriting. A little cushion of white moss in a window niche, growing where the frequencies cancel, improbable and soft. A lamp burning low in the humid air. A tile shelf holding ceramic cups, a coil of waxed cord, a smooth stone kept for no reason except that it fits the hand.

I would not decorate much. I would tend.

Food would be simple. Algae cakes dried over warm vents. Young reed shoots cooked until they lose their mineral sharpness. Saltgrass seeds gathered in small quantities near the reservoir margins. Perhaps a broth made from things traded in by rail: grain, oil, dried fruit from somewhere lower and sunnier, a luxury that arrives rarely and is treated with respect. I would know which taps are safe to drink from and which need settling. I would have a favorite one, not because it is grand, but because the water comes from a deep seep and tastes faintly warm, clean, and iron-sweet.

I would not be lonely.

That is important. The city is empty by many standards, but its emptiness is not abandonment. It is scale. It is balance. Its scattered figures are not isolated; they are spaced according to the logic of attention. A lamp blinks across a wide plaza. A hand sign answers from a catwalk. A touch on the elbow says: feel this. A palm pressed briefly to the back says: something has changed. A slow closing of the fingers says: ease it down.

I would be part of that network of small recognitions.

When decisions had to be made, I would not argue loudly. I would walk the site with others. We would place our hands on the same wall. We would wait. We would watch water gather. We would feel the hum migrate through stone. Agreement would come not as declaration but as shared stillness. One by one, we would nod, or sign, or tap. The course of action would settle among us like condensation.

I think I would be especially suited to noticing drift.

Not crisis. Not failure. Drift.

The way a resonator’s pitch deepens over years. The way calcium narrows a duct by fractions. The way a rubber lining stiffens. The way moss advances into an acoustic shadow. The way a bead forms a little later in the afternoon than it used to. The way a handrail trembles differently under the same load. The way dust dances on a grate when the lower engines are tired.

I would not seek to restore things to newness. Newness would be suspicious. Newness would be hard-edged, unweathered, brittle. I would prefer surfaces that show their history. Repairs would be visible but gentle. A patched place would be cared for, not concealed. I would use porous stone where stone must breathe, flexible sealant where metal moves, glazed brick where crust would otherwise build too quickly. I would know which surfaces need wiping and which must keep their film. I would know that some stains are records.

If you asked me what I am for, I would not answer that I make the city work.

The city works because it is allowed to continue.

I would be one of the beings who allows it. Who watches the face of the stone. Who keeps the lower channel from starving. Who listens with the body rather than the ear. Who opens a gate just enough. Who lets the roar move where it needs to move. Who understands that sound here is not nuisance but weather, not machinery but ecology.

In that sense, I would be less a person than a capacity the city has grown.

An organ of noticing.

A minor intelligence distributed through routine: a hand near a wall, a bowl of water, a log incised on tile, a lamp blinked across the basin, a touch that says wait, a palm that says here, a silence that says something has shifted.

But if I must have a body, then let it be this one:

You would find me in the Gallery of Small Beads in the hour after the upper vents have been opened. The light would still be ordinary, clean, unstrange. The basin would shine in patches. Water would tremble on railings. Somewhere below, a turbine would be turning slowly, not blurred, not frantic, only strong. The air would smell of wet iron, warm dust, algae, and the faint sourness of drying minerals.

I would be standing near a wall of dark basalt glazed with moisture, wearing oilcloth gone soft at the folds. My hood would be down. My hands would be uncovered. One palm would rest lightly against the stone, not pressing, only receiving. Around my feet, shallow pans would show concentric rings. My eyes would be half closed, narrowed against glare, calm. I would not be wincing at the roar. I would be inside it the way a person is inside weather.

There would be a dry line on the wall.

No wider than a finger. No longer than a forearm. Someone else might not see it. I would feel it before I saw it: a place where the stone had stopped answering. The basalt there would be merely cool, not alive. The beads would fail to gather. The mineral streaks would be pale and interrupted.

I would not panic. I would kneel and set my bowl on the ground. I would watch the water’s surface. The rings would be uneven, pulled slightly toward the lower channel. I would rise and walk the length of the wall, trailing my fingertips until the vibration changed. Near a drainage slot, I would find a crust of calcium beginning to bridge the lip. Not enough to block it. Enough to alter the flow.

I would clear it gently with the wooden scraper. Then I would wait.

Not long by the city’s standards. Long enough for a bead to form at the upper seam. Long enough for it to descend along the old path. Long enough for the dry line to darken by degrees. Long enough for the wall to remember itself.

Then I would sign to the next keeper down the terrace: a slow opening of the fingers from the chest. Condensation spreading. All right here. The face is damp again.

That is who I would be.

Not the loudest thing in the basin. Not the most powerful. Not the one who commands the engines or designs the great resonators. I would be the one who stands close enough to notice when the stone forgets its habit. The one whose hands have learned the temperature of balance. The one who keeps the face damp.

And if you came looking for me, do not look for a figure in the center of the plaza, and do not call out sharply. The city dislikes sudden sounds. Instead, wait where the light falls clean on wet stone. Let the roar settle into your chest until it becomes a pressure you can bear. Watch the beads gather on iron. Follow the narrow channel to the shadowed gallery beneath the pipes. When you see someone standing with a palm against the wall, still as if listening with the skin, and the moisture begins to move again under their hand—

that will be me.
