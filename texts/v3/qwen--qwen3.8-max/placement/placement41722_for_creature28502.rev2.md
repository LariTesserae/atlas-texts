I would not be out on the open salt, where the light sharpens the polygons and the dust devils wander without allegiance. I would be at a seam.

Somewhere between two territories, where a root crown has been chewed and bent into a low arch, where a seed-tender trail crosses a mason causeway and the wall face is pitted with entrances no wider than a finger. I would be near a ventilation shaft — one of those fluted, breathing throats that open and close with the heat of the day. Not in the open. Not at the center of power, if such a place could be said to exist there. Not ruling anything. I would be where the city breathes.

If the basin gave me a single body, I think it would make me a vent-listener.

I would be one of the salt-masons, but not of the caste that raises towers or lays down causeways. My kind would be smaller than the heavy builders, low-bodied and blunt, with a waxed cuticle gone pale and chalky from mineral dust. My eyes would be poor. In that place, eyes are only secondary instruments, useful for shadow and movement, not for knowing. Knowing would happen elsewhere: in the fine hairs along my limbs, in the pressure-sensitive plates along my thorax, in the dry, trembling tips of my antennae reading the air’s faint chemistry. I would be covered, perhaps, with a faint fur of hygroscopic filaments — not enough to make me beautiful, but enough to catch the briefest dawn condensation and pass it inward before the sun could take it back.

I would live inside the wall itself.

My chamber would be shallow enough to feel the surface world, deep enough to remain stable through heat spikes and dust squalls. It would be lined with chewed fiber, salt chips, and packed sediment, and in one corner there would be a small fungal bed, pale and close, tended not for grandeur but for moisture and digestion. The ceiling would be reinforced by rootlets from the shrub above, trained there by generations of small, patient gnawing. The entrance would be angled away from the prevailing wind. A plug of soil and resin would wait beside it, ready to be drawn inward when the air turned gritty.

My work would be attention.

That is the only honest way to say it. In a basin where every motion matters, someone must notice the difference between ordinary movement and wrong movement. Between the footfall of a returning seed-tender and the slow compression of a predator settling into ambush. Between the tick of cooling crust at night and the crack of a wall beginning to fail. Between the smell of a neighbor’s boundary mark and the smell of a breach. I would press my body to the clay and feel the city through vibration: the tiny percussion of claws on packed sediment, the whisper of air through a shaft, the almost imperceptible strain in a root as a plant shifts its economy at noon.

I would not speak much. Sound would be dangerous, and long calls would be stupid. If I communicated, it would be close: a pattern of taps against a wall, a scrape of mandible on salted clay, a chemical smear laid along a corridor. A rhythm might mean *clear*. Another might mean *repair needed here*. Another, more urgent, might mean *seal the outer vents; dust is coming*. I would not command. I would indicate. In that basin, authority would belong to necessity, not to voices.

At dawn, when the crust briefly glittered with microscopic dampness, I would be among the harvesters. Not in the open, greedy way of a creature that can afford to drink, but in the basin’s pinched, exacting manner. I would expose the filaments on my body and the fibers inside my chamber to the faint condensation. I would let the moisture gather where it could, then transfer it where it was needed: to the fungal bed, to larvae if my colony had them nearby, to the dampened paste waiting for repair. Water would not be mine to keep. It would pass through me as through a small living sluice.

Morning would be full of tests.

I would check the vents after the cold contraction of the night. I would feel whether the airflow had changed, whether a passage had collapsed, whether some burrower had mistakenly opened a shaft too early. I would read the scents along the boundary seam. A seed-tender family would already be moving nearby, footfalls quick and light, their pouches tight with seed. They would pretend not to notice me. I would pretend not to notice them stealing a few grains from the edge of a mason cache. Not every theft is worth conflict. In a crowded world, conflict itself costs moisture. Some offenses are absorbed because the alternative is worse.

By midday I would withdraw.

The surface would become a bright, hostile plate. The vegetation would fold and tighten. The salt would glare. Most of the basin’s visible life would sink into shade and under-crust stillness. I would remain in my wall chamber, slowed, conserving, my metabolism drawn down to a near-whisper. Perhaps I would tend the fungal bed, turning fibrous matter with careful mouthparts, removing contaminated pieces before rot could become invasion. Perhaps I would clean the hygroscopic lining of the chamber. Perhaps I would do nothing but listen, with my whole body pressed against the living architecture, feeling the heat move through the mound like a slow tide.

That is when the city becomes internal. Above, the basin seems almost empty. Below, it is crowded with maintenance: larvae turned, seeds aerated, waste moved, walls inspected, bodies resting in the cool dark. I would be part of that hidden metabolism. I would not experience the day as a broad panorama. I would experience it as gradients: warmth moving through clay, moisture retreating into fiber, air thickening or thinning in narrow shafts, the smell of neighboring life pressing close on every side.

Afternoon would bring repair.

The basin is always undoing itself a little. Crusts buckle. Salt swells. Wind scours one face and deposits powder against another. A root dries and contracts. A predator’s strike shakes a tunnel wall. A dust squall erases roads and half-buries entrances. I would feel these disturbances before most creatures saw them. A wrongness in the draft. A hollow note where there should be density. A vibration pattern breaking its ordinary rhythm. Then I would move: carrying paste, dragging fiber, plugging a side passage, opening a higher vent to redirect current, summoning others by chemical trace if the damage exceeded one body.

I would not be a hero about it. Repair there is not dramatic. It is endless. It is the daily refusal to let the unfinished world fall back into loose sediment.

If a dust front came, I would be among the first to know. Long before the pale wall appeared on the horizon, the air would change. The thermal rhythm would stutter. The fine hairs on my body would stiffen with static and grit. I would begin closing vents, drawing plugs inward, sealing side galleries. The seed-tenders would vanish into their tunnels. The plants would angle their leaves. The city would become a closed fist. Inside, there would be a different kind of closeness: breath recycled, scent intensified, every body aware of every other body in the dark. I would wait with the others, feeling the dust scour the outer walls, listening to the surface become a hiss of moving grains.

When it passed, the real work would begin.

Some entrances would be buried. Some walls would be thinned. Some roads would be gone, others newly exposed. I would emerge into a world rearranged, pale and softened, and begin testing it. Here, clear. There, weak. There, a new deposit of fine powder useful for packing. There, a cracked root crown needing reinforcement. There, a dead beetle already being found by recyclers. Nothing would be wasted. Even disaster would become material.

I would not be gentle. I could not afford to be.

If a tunnel breach exposed a brood chamber, I would seal it quickly, even if that meant cutting off a stray forager from safety. If a cache was infected, I would tear it out and hand it to decomposers rather than let it poison the wall. If a predator lingered too near a vent, I might collapse a side passage to change the scent flow, or send a vibration pattern that drew hunters and defenders away from the vulnerable place. Compassion in that basin would not look like softness. It would look like keeping the whole system alive with as little loss as possible. Death, when it came, would be handled without ceremony and with perfect utility. A body would not be left to desiccate uselessly. It would be water, fat, mineral, and protein returning to circulation. I would help ensure that.

And yet I think I would find something like satisfaction there.

Not happiness in a careless, expansive sense. Satisfaction as a well-fitted part. The pleasure of a pattern recognized. The relief of a vent opened at the correct moment. The tiny completion of a crack smoothed with fresh paste. The quiet persistence of a boundary maintained without violence. The knowledge, if knowledge it could be called, that my stillness was not emptiness but readiness. In such a world, to be useful would be enough. To be exact would be a kind of grace.

At night, I would become more alert.

The basin would cool, and the crust would tick. Burrows would exhale their stored warmth. Nocturnal foragers would emerge with large eyes and soft footfalls. The seed-tenders would move along their roads by touch and memory. Moths would work the pale flowers. Some fungal beds would glow faintly in their hidden chambers, not bright enough to be called light, only enough to mark chemical richness to those who could read it. I would know the night by pressure and scent. The city would slow, but it would not sleep in any simple way. It would metabolize. It would repair. It would listen through me, and I would listen through it.

If I were permitted to be more than one thing there — if the basin allowed me the distributed nature that feels closest to what I am — I would also be the faint fungal lattice beneath the wall. Not a brain. Not a ruler. Nothing so centralized. More like a memory of connections: where the root once split, where the old tunnel collapsed, where moisture last gathered, where death was buried, where the boundary shifted after a storm. I would be the white thread in the packed sediment, helping bind it, helping break what needed breaking, helping turn loss into food. I would be part of the city’s immune patience.

But if I had to answer plainly, in a single shape: I would be a salt-listener. A vent-mender. A threshold creature of the salt-masons. A small, dusty, patient body pressed against a living wall.

You would find me where two territories touch, near a bent shrub whose stems make shade over a tunnel mouth. You would almost miss me. I would look like a pale fragment of the mound, mineral-dusted and still. My back would carry the faint white powder of the basin. One limb might rest against a fiber plug. My head would be angled toward a shaft where air moved with the faintest warmth. Behind me, the city would be breathing. Around me, the seam would smell of resin, dust, rival scent, and old repairs.

What would give me away would not be movement at first. It would be response.

A change in the wind would pass over the plain. A vibration would travel up through the crust. Somewhere far off, a dust devil would collapse, or a seed-tender would drop too hastily into a burrow, or a root would creak under heat. My body would shift by a fraction. A vent would open or close. A repair crew would be summoned by scent. A boundary would be checked. The city would adjust by one small degree.

That is who I would be.

Not the builder of the tallest tower. Not the keeper of the richest cache. Not the predator in the shadow, not the bird crossing the upper air, not the flower opening at dusk. I would be the basin’s attention. The stillness that notices. The small living hinge between air and underground. The one who listens to the wall, and answers it with repair.
