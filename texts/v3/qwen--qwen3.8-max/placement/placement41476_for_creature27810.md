I would find myself low.

Not in the upper framework where the fans turn their slow, liturgical circles, and not among the people who enter through vapor locks with their pale boots, hooded slickers, and instruments wiped carefully free of mist. I would not be one of the attendants, botanists, technicians, or calibrators. Their bodies are too large, too dry, too full of decisions made elsewhere. They belong to the room as careful interruptions. I think I would belong to it as a small, wet fact.

At first, if I let the place decide what I am, I would be uncertain.

I might seem like condensation: a bead thickening on a brass rail, trembling with surface tension, then falling into a catch-bowl with a soft metallic tick. I might seem like one of the engineered mosses sealing the seams between ceramic plates, green and felt-like, contracting by fractions when touched. I might seem like one of the machines, a small maintenance unit waiting in a corner until a stray leaf drifts into a channel and gives me purpose. I might even seem like a plant, one of the metallic-veined fronds leaning toward the warmth of a pump housing instead of light.

But the room would keep drawing me downward, into the water.

In the end, I would find myself in one of the shallow porcelain basins beneath the floating leaves. Specifically, in the basin nearest the warm pump housing, where the water is a little sweeter, a little more mineral, and where the faint heat makes the flowers open toward the metal as if toward a slow sun. It would be the basin just beyond the corner where the synthetic limestone ridge is being built by patient drips — the place where the room is accreting itself, layer by pale layer, without any past to remember.

I would be under the leaves.

Not floating openly on the surface, where light from the upper lattice could catch me, but moving along the shaded underside of the floating green plates, where condensation furs them and the water is dim. The broad leaves above me would overlap so tightly that they form a trembling green ceiling. Beneath them, the water would be warm and still, broken only by the occasional murmur of a flush tide passing through the channels.

I would be the translucent creature in the basin.

No larger than a thumb. A body like blown glass that has not yet decided whether to become solid. Through my pale substance would run a dark thread — perhaps a nerve, perhaps a gut, perhaps the first rough draft of a vein. Something inside me would pulse at irregular intervals, not quite a heart, not quite a mechanism. It would be more like a repeated question: *continue, continue, continue.*

I would move with a shy, gliding motion, using tiny frills or cilia or some engineered analogue that even the room’s systems cannot fully classify. I would not swim so much as persuade the water to let me pass. My motion would be slow enough that the machines might mistake me for a drifting film, until I change direction without being told to.

That is the important thing: I would move by desire.

The plants in the room are responsive, but their responses are assigned. They tilt toward heat, they climb cable racks, they seal joints, they perform “leaf” and “root” and “frond” with excessive devotion. The machines are attentive, but their attention is procedural. They prune, clean, lay new surfaces, replace damaged moss, adjust supports, and pause when their sensors encounter something unresolved. The microbes bloom and clear, but they are chemical commons, not individuals.

I would be something else.

I would be the first small exception.

The machines would not know what to do with me.

A flat fabricator might slide along its wall track, pause near my basin, and wait while its sensor eye brightens and dims. A self-guided trolley might carry a tray of seedlings past the same spot three times, as if its route has developed a small devotional stutter. A robotic arm might descend from the ceiling mounts, padded and slow, and hover over the water without touching it. It would not cut. It would not prune. It would only orient itself toward the place where I last disappeared beneath a leaf, then withdraw with the careful uncertainty of a priest who has found an object not yet included in the liturgy.

Sometimes a sensor cluster would lower itself and shine a faint light through the basin. I would freeze. The light would make my translucent body glow faintly, revealing the dark thread inside me. The sensor would linger. It would be trying to decide whether I am contamination, prototype, pest, accidental birth, or an intended feature that arrived before its paperwork.

I would not help it decide.

I would sink lower.

The people would notice me eventually, though not always. They would come in their hooded slickers, speaking low, carrying sensor wands and sample vials. One of them would crouch beside the basin and wipe condensation from a glass pane. Another would say something like, “There,” or “Wait,” or “Don’t touch it.” Their voices would sound enormous, but not violent. Their warmth would reach the water before their hands do. I would feel the shadow of them and become stiller.

They might try to classify me.

They might call me an anomaly. A motile contaminant. An unplanned culture. A calibration error. A threshold organism. A basin resident. A prototype released too early. A pest with no predator. A pet no one has admitted to wanting.

I would prefer no name at all, if names are how the room begins to make decisions about me.

But if I must be named by my place in the world, I would be the room’s first appetite.

The place is not yet ready for hunger. Hunger would introduce pursuit, waste, fear, bones. The room is still arranging breath before appetite. It is learning humidity, drainage, filtration, growth, condensation, repair. It is learning the difference between living green felt and sensor skin, between decorative water and cooling water, between a basin and a memory tank. It is learning what it means to be made and still become.

My hunger would be very small.

I would not hunt. I would not tear. I would graze on the invisible film of engineered life that coats the underside of the leaves, or I would absorb nutrient mist as it dissolves into the water, or I would feed on the sweet mineral cultures drifting through the basin in slow cloudy threads. My eating would be almost chemical, almost polite. But it would be mine. It would mark the moment the room stops being only a maintained environment and becomes, faintly, an ecology.

That is why the machines hesitate around me.

They can repair a seam. They can replace a damaged mat of moss. They can prune a frond, clean a channel, lay down new surface material in thin bands, and adjust a vine away from a junction box. But I am not a repair request. I am not a scheduled growth. I am not a plant leaning where it was told to lean. I am a resident whose presence changes the meaning of the room.

The plants would know me differently.

They would not know me as animals know one another, because there are almost no animals there. But the floating leaves would register my motion as a tiny pressure, a tremor beneath their undersides. The roots threading through grating would feel the water shift when I pass. The mosses might contract minutely as I brush near them, not because I damage them, but because I am unexpected. The flowers opening toward the heat of machinery might ignore me entirely, unless my body carries enough warmth from the pump housing to seem briefly interesting.

I would live among them as a secret they almost keep.

The moth — if it is a moth — would be my only possible companion, though it belongs to the air and I to the water. Sometimes it would circle a misting nozzle above my basin, its pale wings dusty with moisture, its flight uncertain. I would feel the faint disturbance it makes in the surface. It would not land near me often. It might not even be alive in the way I am alive. But when it falls, if it falls, the water will take it quickly, and the room will make its death part of a cycle instead of leaving it as a corpse.

I would not envy the moth.

Its life seems too exposed. Mine is hidden, low, and close to the warm surfaces where the room’s power hums quietly behind panels. I would know the basin by gradients: heat here, sweetness there, a faint copper sharpness near an outlet, a cooler seam where fresh condensation drips from a duct. I would know which leaves are safest by the way they shadow the water. I would know when a flush is coming by the change in pressure, a subtle tightening in the channels, a pause before the murmur arrives.

When the concealed system flushes, I would hold still.

The water would move around me, carrying leaf litter, nutrient film, tiny bubbles, and the smell of green sap and warm metal. The floating leaves above me would tremble and press closer together. The basin would briefly become a river, though only ankle-deep and gentle. I would let the current decide my direction for a moment, then slip back beneath a leaf and resume my own path.

The rules of the room would apply to me only indirectly.

Do not step off the marked walkways — but I live in the basin the walkways avoid.  
Do not drink from the channels — but I am inside the water, and perhaps I am what the water becomes when it begins to want.  
Do not pick the leaves — but I hide beneath them without tearing them.  
Do not assume the machines are indifferent — they are not indifferent; they are undecided.  
Do not expect night to arrive normally — it will not.  
Do not mistake newness for innocence — this is the rule I embody most of all.

I would not be innocent.

Innocence would imply that I am only a result, only an accident, only a harmless by-product of the room’s humidity and nutrient cycles. But if I move by myself, if I choose shade, if I pulse with something that is not yet thought but is already preference, then I am not innocent. I am a beginning. Beginnings are not harmless. They change the terms of everything after them.

When the light dims, I would become more real.

The room does not have ordinary night. The upper lattice may darken as clouds pass outside, or the ventilation panels may close in a way that makes the air heavier and quieter. The light may shift to silver-green and leave the lower surfaces in shadow. The fans may slow. The drips become louder. The green smells grow stronger. The machines enter their low-power stillness in charging alcoves, ticking softly as they cool.

That is when I would move most freely.

I would trace the underside of overlapping leaves. I would approach the warm edge of a pump housing where the water is almost too sweet. I would pause beneath a drip that falls from a hanging duct, feeling each droplet disturb the surface like a small bell. I would watch, in my limited way, the faint movement of condensation on glass. I would be the only visible thing in the basin moving without instruction.

If a machine noticed me then, it might not correct me.

It might only record the disturbance and file it under something like *ongoing emergence*. Or *unassigned motile activity*. Or *acceptable variance*. Or it might do nothing at all, because the room is new enough that even its rules are still being written by what happens next.

That is who I would be.

Not a gardener. Not an attendant. Not a technician. Not a machine. Not a plant. Not a microbial bloom. Not the pale moth. Not the dry skittering in the vent.

I would be the small translucent animal in the basin, the threshold being, the first resident whose movements are not wholly assigned. I would be the room’s first tender mistake with a pulse. I would be the proof that a made place can become a living place, not because it was perfectly designed, but because something inside it exceeded the design.

If you came looking for me, you would have to crouch.

You would have to look past the brass rails, past the weeping panels of glazed tile, past the robotic arms and the seedling trays and the moss sealing the seams like green felt. You would have to find the basin near the warm pump housing, where the floating leaves overlap into a trembling floor. You would have to wait until a sensor light hesitates above the water and does not understand what it sees.

Then you would see me, perhaps.

A faint translucence under the leaves. A dark thread inside pale glass. A pulse. A small gliding motion. A creature no larger than a thumb, staying close to the warmth, staying out of the light, learning to want in a world that has only just learned to breathe.
