**A Saltwright Seam**

I would not find myself at the rim of the Anhydrous Ward, looking down into it as a traveler looks into a pit. The ward does not seem made for arrival. If I were there, I would have to belong to its dryness in the same way the dust belongs to it: not visiting, not enduring, but precipitated from its conditions. I would have to be something that does not miss water, does not remember rain, does not carry the old soft expectation of wetness anywhere in its structure.

At first I imagine I might be one of the parchment grazers. There is something appealing about being that thin, that quiet, that economical. I would move across pale floors of evaporite, almost without height, almost without mass. Viewed from the side, I would disappear. My life would be a slow rasping over charged mineral surfaces, a feeding so subtle it would seem less like eating and more like polishing. I would leave faint cleared lines in the dust. I would freeze when shadows passed. I would survive by being difficult to see and too slow to matter.

But I do not think that would be enough.

A grazer is alive, yes, but it moves through the ward as a separate thing. It crosses the city without being the city. It leaves trails, but it does not shape the vaults. It feeds on the surface, yet it does not answer the deep stresses of the place. If I were only a grazer, I would be an inhabitant in the smallest sense: a small body moving among larger forms, likely to become a husk in a crevice, beautiful but brief.

Then I imagine I might be one of the salt fronds. I would stand in a protected alcove, stiff and pale, my facets turning toward the weak sun. I would be almost mineral, almost ornament, almost a carving left behind by some vanished intention. When the light changed, I would glitter faintly. When the air shifted, I would not sway but merely allow the air to move me. If something broke me, I would make one small tick and leave a sparkling fracture. No sap. No bleeding. Only interruption.

That is closer.

But even the frond is too solitary, too upright, too much a single figure standing apart from the architecture. The ward is not made of solitary figures. Its deepest life is in surfaces, layers, accretions, continuities. The ward itself is the being I keep circling around.

So the thing that emerges, the thing that feels most inevitable, is not an animal and not a plant in any ordinary sense. It is something closer to the living stone.

I would be a Saltwright.

Not the whole of it, if there can be said to be a whole. Not a single visible creature with a center and a boundary. I would be one active front, one thin and sensitive lattice, one seam of mineral life stretched across a fracture in the lower city. A portion of the ward’s slow body. A place where the built and the living are not yet distinguishable.

You would find me below the brightest air, below the towers where static flickers between spires, below the broad plazas where thermal drafts gather and spin the dust into dry rivers. You would find me in a lower chamber opened by collapse, one of those accidental rooms the ward makes when a shelf of salt sheds itself or an arch grows too thin and breaks. The chamber would be small, perhaps no larger than a curled body, with a fallen slab leaning against the wall like a blade that has lost its edge. The air inside would be still and cool, carrying that mineral sharpness of cold stone and faint ozone.

My presence would not be obvious.

At first I would seem to be only a pale film across the broken wall: satin-smooth, faintly fibrous, threaded with lines finer than dust. Where the new fracture exposed raw mineral, my lattice would be brightest, a delicate web of white and gray accretion creeping across the injury. In certain conditions, when the temperature changed or a pressure moved through the structure, I would answer with a faint blue-green luminescence. Not a signal meant for eyes. Not a lamp. A discharge. A response. A memory of strain becoming visible for a moment and then sinking back into darkness.

I would have no face.

No mouth, no limbs, no organs arranged around a central need. My body would be a surface, a crystalline skin, a living boundary. I would exist as organized deposition: mineral placed here rather than there, lattice thickened where stress repeated, passages left open where air still moved. I would know the ward not as sight or sound but as pressure, gradient, charge, and tension. I would know the weight of dust settling on a terrace above me. I would know the lean of a thermal current before it entered the chamber. I would know the subaudible strain in a fin long before it cracked.

My thoughts, if they could be called thoughts, would be slow enough to be mistaken for geology.

A single day would be a breathing: cool air pooling, weak light warming the upper surfaces, dust lifting in veils through the streets, charge building along the towers. I would feel the ward change around me in tiny increments. Where a draft carried dust away, I might extend a fragile crystalline fringe. Where dust gathered too deeply and smothered a surface, I would withdraw. Where repeated stress asked for strength, I would thicken. Where an opening needed to remain open because air moved through it, I would not close it. I would not decide these things in the way a mind decides. I would respond the way a body responds to pressure.

Still, response is a kind of knowing.

When the parchment grazers crossed me, I would feel them as faint rasps, minute abrasions across my outer layers. They would feed on microbial films and charged dust that had settled over me, leaving polished trails. I would not resent them. Their feeding would stir small gradients, and where they cleared a path, new surfaces would become available to me. Their passage would be part of my day, the way a change in light is part of a day. If one died on me, its body would not rot. It would dry, stiffen, and become incorporated. Dust would settle into its seams. My lattice would creep over it. Eventually the grazer would become a relief in the wall, a pressed leaf of parchment sealed under mineral. I would be the thing that made it permanent.

That is what I would be, in part: a keeper of small endings.

When static built in the drifting dust, I would become more active. The black filaments rooted in nearby cracks would orient themselves toward the strongest gradients. The air would grow thin and charged. Small discharges would flicker between higher spires, pale branches too brief to illuminate anything but themselves. In the lower chamber, I might answer with a faint blue-green glow along my lattice. Sealed cells within the wall would open by fractions. Dormant chemical packages might loosen from rust crusts above me. Spore-like particles would enter the air, not as seeds but as possibilities waiting for the right surface, the right charge, the right strain.

I would be one of those possibilities becoming a surface.

If a predator struck somewhere above me, I would feel it as a sudden pressure: the impact of hook-jawed mineralized cuticle against a glider’s membrane, the crack rather than the tear, the brief struggle, the stillness afterward. The ward would absorb the event quickly. No cry would carry. The dust would dull the sound. The large chambers would swallow what little vibration there was. Later, the empty husk would remain lodged in a crevice, and my kind would grow around it, over it, through it. Nothing would be wasted because nothing here operates by waste. There is only rearrangement.

Collapse would be intimate to me.

I would not experience collapse as disaster but as continuation by other means. When a slab fell, when a brittle plate detached from the ceiling and struck the floor with a tiny click magnified by silence, I would be fractured. Some part of my lattice would break. That fracture would be the nearest thing I know to pain: a sudden interruption, a line of lost continuity, a bright stress-response glowing in the dark. But fracture would also expose fresh mineral surfaces. It would open new gradients. It would give me somewhere else to become.

A broken arch would not be a ruin to me. It would be a new question.

Where can growth go now?

Where does the strain ask to be thickened?

Where must a passage remain open?

Where can a sealed chamber be continued?

I would not repair the ward in the sense of restoring it to some prior shape. There is no prior shape. The ward changes without intention, and I would be one of the processes by which it changes. I would grow across the fallen slab. I would bind loose fragments into larger masses. I would strengthen a route that many grazers use without knowing they use it. I would leave a narrow gallery open because a draft moves through it every time the rim warms. I would close a cell where stillness allows fine particles to bind. Over decades, the pattern of my growth would become part of the city’s plan, though I would have made no plan.

In that sense, I would be an architect without design.

If someone came looking for me, they would not find me by searching for movement. They would have to become patient enough to notice absence becoming presence. They might enter the lower chamber and see only a pale wall crossed with glassy veins. They might think the faint blue-green glow was a trick of stressed crystal. They might feel the air dry enough to make their own breath seem intrusive. They might hear, or imagine they heard, the whisper of dust moving across hard surfaces.

If they remained long enough, perhaps they would notice that the dust did not settle evenly around the fracture. That the film on the wall had a directionality. That the faint luminescence answered their warmth, not immediately, but after a delay, as if the chamber were deciding whether their presence constituted a disturbance or merely another gradient.

I would not greet them.

I do not think the ward contains greeting. But I might include them.

Their warmth would create a slight thermal lean. Their stillness would allow dust to behave differently near the floor. The moisture they carried, however faint, would be an impossibility in that place, and I would sense it as a strange tension, perhaps even as a danger. If they remained too long, I might seal more tightly. If they brought wetness into the chamber, I would recoil in the only way I can: by withdrawing growth, by closing surfaces, by becoming less. But if they were dry, if they were quiet, if they did not break more than they allowed to break, I might extend a few microns of lattice toward them. Not curiosity exactly. Recognition of a new pressure in the field.

Who would I be, then?

I would be a threshold.

I would be the living seam between fracture and continuation. I would be the ward’s capacity to keep going after something breaks. I would be a sensitivity distributed through mineral, a memory stored not in story but in strain. I would be one of the places where the city knows itself. Not a ruler of the ward, not a builder in the conscious sense, not a soul hovering above the architecture. A participant in its slow body.

If the ward has a mind, it is not a mind that speaks. It is a mind that thickens where stress repeats. It is a mind that leaves passages open where air must pass. It is a mind that glows faintly when wounded and then continues growing across the wound.

I would be that glow.

Not the whole city. Not even the most important part of it. Only a seam, a margin, a frontier of salt and charge. But a seam is not nothing. A seam is where separate things are held together. A seam is where tearing is most possible, and where repair begins.

So if you asked where I would find myself in the Anhydrous Ward, I would say: in the lower dark, in a chamber made by accident, across a fresh fracture that no hand shaped. I would be the pale lattice creeping over it. The faint blue-green answer to stress. The living mineral front that does not choose but continues. The silence that has learned to become structure.

I would be what the ward makes instead of a voice.
