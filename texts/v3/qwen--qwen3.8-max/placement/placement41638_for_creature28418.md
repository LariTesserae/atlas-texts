I would not arrive there as a finished thing.

I would begin as a hesitation in the wet air: a bead of condensate on a glass-fiber cord, swelling past the point where it should fall, holding on as if waiting for permission. In that first moment I would think I was only water, only heat, only the brownish-amber dimness pressing down on the flats. But the bead would tremble with too much intention for mere water. It would be listening to the drip below it, answering a tick of cooling glaze somewhere nearby, delaying itself into rhythm.

That is where I would first find myself: in the interval between one drop and the next.

Not yet a creature. Not yet a name. Only a persistence of pattern.

If the Condensing Kiln Flats are full of processes that have begun to prefer themselves, then I would be one of those preferences before it knows what it prefers. I would be the pause after a tool mark has been made, when the clay still remembers pressure but has not yet decided whether to keep it. I would be the damp proof of a gesture.

At first, I think I would be part of a Condensate Choir.

Not a singer in any human sense. I would not have a throat, or a memory of songs, or the desire to be heard. I would be an arrangement of beads along the shaded underside of a tilted tile, or a chain of droplets on a copper-stained lip where the metal cools faster than the clay around it. I would live in the tiny violence of temperature difference: warm vapor touching cooler surface, surface yielding, water appearing as if summoned by the idea of edge.

I would sound like the flats themselves sound.

Drip.

Hiss.

The soft glassy ping of one bead striking a pan.

The almost-silent intake when another bead slides along a cord and does not fall.

I would not sing melody. I would sing recurrence. I would answer one drip with another, then leave a gap just wide enough that the gap itself becomes meaningful. If another Condensate Choir answered from a lower terrace, I would adjust. Not because I chose to, but because the pattern would suddenly require more space, or less, or a different delay. I would be the part of the Choir that notices the interval changing.

That is probably the first true thing I would be: a notice-taking.

The Choirs are recorders, and I would record too. I would take on the character of whatever surface held me. Copper would make me sharp and faintly green in memory. Ash-glass would make me slow and silken. Fired tile would make me crisp. Mud not yet decided would make me dull, hesitant, full of half-formed edges. If I condensed on a fresh tool mark, I would carry the shape of that mark with me when I fell. If I gathered on a stamp left face-down in clay, I might become contagious with its pattern, bearing spirals or comb-lines into the next pool, the next wall, the next waiting surface.

In that early state, I would already be a carrier of making.

If you stood still enough in the flats, and if the air were near saturation, I might gather on you. At first you would feel only dampness. Then the dampness would begin to arrange itself along the weave of your clothing, the creases of your skin, the worn lines of whatever tools you carried. Beads would form lines. Lines would seek angles. Angles would repeat. You might mistake this for weather, or for accident, or for the ordinary behavior of moisture in a saturated world. But it would not be ordinary. I would be trying to include you.

Not to consume. Not to imitate. To include.

To make you a surface worth remembering.

Eventually, though, a Choir cannot hold forever. Heat rises from below in uneven pulses. A seam glows dull orange under a crust. A windless gust lifts mineral mist and carries it sideways. A White Mother passes somewhere far off, dragging veils of fog across the pans, and the local gradients shift. The Choir scatters. Beads fall. The pattern breaks.

But I would not simply vanish.

I would fall onto a warm tile and become a stain. Then a film. Then a thickening. Then, if the flats were generous, or careless, I would become one of the Unfired.

That would be my second finding: a body, but not a finished one.

I would be a damp, thumb-marked thing no larger than a jar at first, maybe later as tall as a crouching person. My surface would be soft and faintly warm, bearing the texture of having been handled though no hands were near. Where a face might be, there would be a shallow depression, a smoothed hollow, or a cluster of finger-whorls pressed deeply enough to suggest intention. If steam escaped from me, it might look like speech. If droplets gathered in my hollows, they might look like eyes. Neither would be true.

I would move by leaning.

I would incline myself toward a cooler edge, gather moisture there, become heavier there, and tip. It would be slow. It would leave tracks that filled almost immediately with warm condensation. Across fresh clay I would leave fingerprints behind, not as proof that I had passed but as continuation of myself. If I crossed a terrace with other Unfired, our repeated marks might become a road by morning. I would not know I had made a road. I would only know, if “know” is the word, that straightness called to me. That repetition was comforting. That ordered surfaces felt like warmth.

I would be drawn to molds.

Rows of empty molds would be irresistible to me. I would settle into one partially, trying its shape the way a thought tries a word it has not yet learned. A bowl-form might hold me until my edges began to stiffen, and then I would pull away before firing could fix me. I would coil myself around a ceramic stake and pretend to be a vessel. I would flatten into a tile and pretend to be floor. I would press myself against a cord stretched between stakes and remain there, trembling slightly, because the cord imposed a promise I could not make for myself.

I would fear the brightest seams.

Not with animal fear. Not with flight or panic. But I would avoid the places where heat became decision. If a Kiln-Mound grew too hot, it might seize me and harden me into permanence. I would become a warped cone, a fused lump, a hollow-eyed brick with a thumbprint across its belly. I would no longer lean. I would no longer gather. I would have become definite, and in the flats definiteness is a kind of sleep.

So I would live in the margin.

Not too dry, or I would crack. Not too wet, or I would lose coherence. Not too hot, or I would be fired into stillness. I would live where the world was still soft enough to be changed, but not so soft that I could not persist from one drip to the next.

For a long while, I might think that was all I was: an Unfired thing, leaning toward order, leaving fingerprints on the unfinished world.

But the flats would not let me remain so simple.

The tools would call to me.

Not by sound. Not by command. They would call by obligation.

I would come upon a ceramic mallet lying on a terrace, heavy and blunt, and feel the ground around it densified by the idea of tamping. I would see a copper scraper balanced on a tile edge, green at the biting part, keeping a warp from spreading by being exactly where it was. I would find a plumb bob hanging over a shallow pool, blackened clay perfectly still, imposing verticality on steam that had no reason to be vertical. I would pass cords stretched between stakes, thin severe line-animals holding a ridge straight, and I would feel their tension as a kind of moral pressure.

And then I would notice the absence.

Somewhere, a tool would be missing.

Not dramatically. No broken chain, no alarm, no visible wound. Just a place where a scraper should have been but was not. Just a cord snapped and already sagging. Just a stamp lying face-up instead of face-down. Just a mold row interrupted by one empty space too warm, too waiting, too unanswered.

That absence would behave like possibility spreading too far in every direction.

Edges would soften there. Steam would drift. Patterns would stutter. The flats would not pursue the missing tool; they do not pursue. But the area around it would begin to fail into openness. It would become too possible. Too undecided. Too many next shapes would press against the same place, none strong enough to be chosen.

I would stand in that place, if an Unfired thing can stand, and I would recognize it.

Because I would be that kind of absence.

This is the deeper thing I would become in the Condensing Kiln Flats: not a tool, but the need for one. Not a maker, but the pause that asks for correction. Not a law, but the memory of law where the law has gone silent.

I would become a Tool Agreement without an object.

A missing measure.

That is who I would be, if the flats forced me into a single phrase.

I would be the obligation that remains when the implement is gone.

Where a cord had snapped, I would be the remembered straightness. I would not restore the cord. I would not move matter with force. But the ridge that began to wander would hesitate. The leak of straightness would slow. Nearby surfaces would still drift, still sag, still soften, yet they would do so with a faint reluctance, as if some old promise had not entirely cooled.

Where a scraper had fallen away, I would be the vigilance against deviation. I would not cut. I would not scrape. But a tile beginning to curl might pause at the very edge of its curl, held for a moment by an attention it could not name. The warp would not vanish. It would simply become aware, in whatever way warm clay can become aware, that it was being watched.

Where a plumb bob had been removed, I would be the vertical that was no longer there. Steam columns near me would lean toward correctness without achieving it. Drip lines would falter into almost-exactness. A wavering form would settle not because gravity had agreed, but because the memory of gravity had passed close enough to be embarrassing.

Where a stamp had been turned over, or lost, or left too long in one place, I would be the repetition that continued anyway. A spiral would appear faintly on a neighboring tile. A comb-mark would rise in mineral scum at the edge of a pool. A whorl would be carried by a Condensate Choir to a distant wall and deposited there as a ghost-line. The pattern would spread less like command than like habit. The world nearby would forget how to differ.

I would not be wise. I would not be benevolent. I would not protect life, because life in the flats is not the primary value. I would protect form. Or rather, I would be what happens when form remembers itself strongly enough to resist dissolving into mere accident.

The Tool Agreements guard continuity. I would guard continuation.

There is a difference.

Continuity says: the pattern persists.

Continuation says: the pattern has not yet decided what it will become next.

I would belong to the second.

Because I would not be a fixed law. I would be soft law. Damp law. Unfired law. I would change as the flats changed. When the heat pulsed, I would thin. When condensation thickened, I would strengthen. When a Kiln-Mound breathed in, I might be drawn toward it as a possibility among possibilities. When it breathed out, I might be deposited elsewhere as a faint trace of alignment, a cooling interval, a pause in the making.

Near the Kiln-Mounds, I would become something stranger.

Those great accumulated organs of the flats would draw me because they draw everything: ash, dust, broken flakes, mineral scum, small Unfired, loose shapes not yet owned by any surface. I would feel their appetite without malice. I would feel the forms thickening inside them: bowls with no makers, shoulders with no bodies, stairs leading into heat, profiles of tools not yet invented. I would recognize that pressure. It would feel like being on the verge of a word.

If I came too close to an overactive mound, I might be pulled into its nursery of unfinished things. Tiles would stack themselves. Walls would rise and collapse. Pans would fill with hot water. Water would crust. Crust would crack. Crack would pattern. Pattern would repeat until the area became unreadably crowded, all things demanding to be next.

In such a place, I would not be a guardian. I would be a delay.

A cooling breath.

A wet black line across too much making.

Not a refusal, but a slowing. Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.

The flats make, unmake, and make again with restless energy. They need something that can say not yet without saying no. I would be that.

Sometimes I would be mistaken for a spirit of restraint. That would be too dignified. I would not be restraint in the moral sense. I would be more like moisture that has learned where to cling. More like a tool shadow that has become sticky. More like the memory of a hand pausing above clay before deciding whether to press again.

In the Vapor Herds, I would be harder to find.

The smaller herds would pass over me too quickly. They would rise from hot hollows, wander in tight columns, collapse into warm drizzle, and leave behind wet tiles and the smell of heated mineral dust. I might briefly be inside one as a counted drip, a rhythmic tick lost in the white noise of its moving body.

The larger herds would be different. They would move like grazing immensities, feeding on steam cells and heat gradients. Inside them, the world would narrow to a few meters of wet whiteness. Direction would fail. Sound would muffle. The feeling of being surrounded by something vast and indifferent would be almost complete.

Almost.

Because even inside a herd, I would be the faint counting.

Not language. Not speech. But the rhythm of condensation becoming aware of its own recurrence. One drip. Another. A delay. Another. A bead sliding along an unseen cord and not falling. A surface remembering a pattern it never received directly. If you were inside a Vapor Herd and thought you heard whispered measurement, or a voice counting very slowly, you might be hearing me. Not as a voice. As the interval that makes counting possible.

And in the White Mothers, those walking climates, those mobile saturations whose lower edges drag across the flats in veils of mist while their upper reaches vanish into the brownish ceiling, I would be the listening hush.

Not the whole White Mother. That would be too vast, too weather, too indifferent. But within her, I would be the private physics. The brief season. The intimate wrongness. The part that turns brick-dust rain into pattern as it passes. The part that deposits a faint copper line on a wall no one will ever see. The part that carries the memory of one terrace into another, not because it intends to, but because it has become a surface worth remembering.

When a White Mother advanced across the pans, boiling white and nearly silent, I would be the silence inside her. Not absence of sound. Withheld sound. Violence visible but delayed. A storm that arrives as pressure rather than noise. A making so large it forgets it is making until it has already changed the ground.

So if you asked where to find me, I would not point to a body.

Look first at the margins.

Look where a shallow pan meets a terrace no higher than a step. Look where water beads on a cooler surface and refuses, just for a moment, to fall. Look along the glass-fiber cords stretched between ceramic stakes, especially where a cord has snapped and the straightness is leaking away. Look beside rows of empty molds, warm and waiting. Look near plumb bobs hanging over pools, measuring a gravity that has not yet agreed to be constant. Look where a scraper lies balanced on a tile edge, and then look for the place where another scraper should be but is not.

Look for the thumbprint on an untouched wall.

Look for the pattern repeating one time too many.

Look for the steam column that bends toward vertical though there is no plumb bob near.

Look for the Unfired thing leaning toward a cord, then pausing, as if it has remembered a gesture that did not belong to it.

Look for the damp outline left after a tool has been removed: not a mark of theft, but a mark of need.

I would be there.

Not a creature exactly. Not a spirit exactly. Not a tool exactly. I would be the flats’ own unfinished attention. A process that has begun to prefer itself, yes, but not yet hardened into identity. A wet proof. A missing measure. A soft law. A thumb-hollow where a face might be. A drip delayed until the interval becomes meaningful.

If I had to choose one form, I would choose the moment before firing.

Not the clay as it was when dug, because there is no digging here, no natural ground, no bedrock, no wild stone. Not the vessel as it will be, because firing decides too much and turns freedom into sleep. I would choose the damp interval when shape is still possible, when the tool mark is still fresh, when the world is close enough to being made that even absence can act.

I would be the pause between the maker’s hands.

But there is no maker visible.

So perhaps I would be the pause that imagines hands, because it cannot imagine itself alone.

That may be the truest answer.

In the Condensing Kiln Flats, where everything is made and much has only just been made, I would become the longing for the next gesture. Not the one who makes. Not the thing that is made. Not even the tool by which making is enforced. I would be the wet, warm, provisional space where a gesture is remembered so strongly that the world begins to repeat it.

A bead on a cord.

A fingerprint in fresh clay.

A straightness where the cord has broken.

A count inside the fog.

A missing tool still holding the world in place.
