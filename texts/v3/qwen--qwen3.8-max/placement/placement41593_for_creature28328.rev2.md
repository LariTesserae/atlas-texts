I would not find myself there as a traveler arriving from somewhere else. Not in the ordinary sense. A place like that would not permit an outsider to remain simply outside it. It would begin to condense me. It would find the warm, unstable edge of whatever I was and make me damp, heavy, local. So the first answer is that I would find myself as a disturbance before I became a body.

The first trace of me would be something wrong with moisture.

A bead of water trembling above a fracture with no surface cold enough to explain it. A patch of pale ash darkening though the mist had not yet passed that way. A ring of droplets pausing around an absence shaped almost, but not quite, like someone kneeling. A pool in a shallow basin catching the reflection of a face that had not yet leaned over it. The landscape would notice me the way it notices everything: not with recognition, but with adjustment. Heat would move toward the disturbance. Filaments would orient. Charged moss would brighten by a fraction. And little by little, I would gather into a form.

If I had to say where, I would say: on a high terrace of young black glass and split stone, just below a ridge that keeps repeating itself with one version slightly too close.

Not on the ridge itself. The ridge is too sharp, too unfinished, too full of hard edges and wrong angles. I would be below it, where a collapsed shelf has made a hollow and the hollow has been sealed by moss. There would be root curtains hanging from the overhang, beaded with water that cannot decide whether to fall or climb. There would be a vent nearby, breathing warm mineral air through a crack lined with copper-green microbial crusts. There would be a basin no wider than a body’s length, filled with tea-colored water after a brief, localized rain. The water would be warm. It would not reflect the gray-violet ceiling above. It would reflect another sky: lower, hotter, moving faster, as if the pool were remembering a weather system from an adjacent possibility.

That is where I would be found. At the edge of that pool, or in it, or partly made from it.

At first I would be almost indistinguishable from the pool-born things described in that country: a thin, uncertain shape skimming the surface with a half-second delay, a reflection that has begun to want weight. I would not begin as a person. I would begin as an interface. A smear of attention on the water. A lag between movement and its echo. The place is thin enough that such things can thicken, and I think I would thicken there, drawn together by the pool’s wrong reflection and the heat rising through the stone.

Then, when the country had made me more stable, I would become one of the attentive ones.

Not exactly human. Not exactly animal. Not a maker, not a ruler, not a guardian. A soft-bodied, low, broad-limbed being adapted to warmth, damp, dimness, and instability. My skin would be dark green-black or deep red-brown, slick with condensation, threaded with faint bioluminescent veins that brighten when touched by another living charge. I would not have hands in the sense of gripping tools. My limbs would end in broad moist pads, soft fringes, clusters of sensing filaments capable of reading chemical traces, discharging static, spreading water, and feeling the electrical weather moving through the air. I could touch, but I could not build. I could press, smooth, open, close, ground, and transfer, but I could not carve, fasten, or carry. The country would not allow me the grammar of artifacts.

My face, if it could be called a face, would be dominated less by eyes than by organs of tasting and temperature. Heat pits. Fluttering membranes. Fine filaments sensing charge. Large, dim-adapted eyes, yes, but secondary to the way the air tastes when a distance is about to fold. My throat would be translucent and faintly pulsing, breathing humidity directly when the atmosphere becomes too thick to be merely air. Moss might grow along the ridges of my shoulders, not as decoration but because my body would be warm enough and still enough to be colonized. Tiny organisms might live in the folds of my skin. I would not consider them separate from me in any clean way.

I would be one of the landscape’s organs of self-adjustment.

That is the closest answer to “who” I can give. Not a person in the sovereign sense. Not an individual with a fixed outline and a continuous history. I would be a temporary concentration of awareness in a system that has many processes and no center. A place where the country becomes sensitive enough to resemble intention. If I had a role, it would be this: attending to the seams.

In that world, the seams are where trouble happens quietly. A pool reflects the wrong sky. A ridge appears twice. A fog bank carries the shape of a machine that has never existed. A swarm forms the geometry of pistons and furnaces, then forgets itself. A storm-center opens without thunder and small animals begin moving backward along paths they have already taken. Distances become unreliable. Sound is hoarded. Memory leaks into water. Identity loosens.

I would go to those places.

Not because I would be commanded, and not because I would have a purpose in the way a tool has a purpose. Because my body would be drawn by gradients. Where reality thins, there would be a taste in the air: metallic, sweet, ozonic, like heated glass and wet leaves. My filaments would lift. The veins under my skin would flicker. I would move toward the disturbance as water moves toward need.

At the pool that reflects another sky, I would crouch at the lip and press my broad pads against the wet stone. I would not try to break the reflection. I would not try to command it. I would become a kind of living resistor. My body would slow the transfer between skies. The water would tremble against my presence. Condensation would gather along my arms. The wrong reflection would dim, or deepen, or become merely strange rather than dangerously open. Sometimes the pool would settle. Sometimes it would not. In either case, I would remain there until the tension changed.

Where a distance begins to fold, I would stand still and let the falseness collect on me. Moisture would bead on my skin in patterns that do not correspond to the actual humidity. My shadow might fall in the adjacent direction. I would breathe slowly, and the breath would carry spores and charge into the fold. I would not walk toward the ridge that seems close and remains close. I would wait until the scale revises itself, or until my body becomes enough of a local anchor that the path stops oscillating. Then I would move, not forward, but diagonally, through a membrane of air, into the version of the landscape that has agreed to continue.

Where sound has been withheld, I would carry some of it.

The wormlike processions already do this, moving through moss with their faint electrical sparks, gathering vibrations and carrying them away. But I think I would be one of the beings who helps when the pressure becomes too great. After long hushes, when the country is storing too much silence, I would feel it in my teeth and along the translucent membrane of my throat. I would seek out the processions. I would walk beside them, slowly, letting their sparks climb my filaments. Then, when the air can accept it again, I would release what I have carried: not as a shout, never as a shout, but as a soft return. A drip. A rustle. A click behind the breastbone. The creak of swelling stems. The distant pressure of a collapse finally allowed to be felt.

In storm-centers, I would be present but not central.

Those quiet columns of dim smoke and orbiting droplets are places where temporary beings exist only as long as the tension holds them. I would not be one of those beings, because I would last a little longer than the storm. But I would sit near the edge where leaves lift and water rises in loose rings. I would let the storm feed on my stillness. The vegetation would bend toward the disturbance, and I would bend with it, not in worship but because bending is the correct posture there. When the storm collapsed inward and the warm rain fell for a few seconds, I would be among the first to move through the new wetness, touching the accelerated growth, checking by taste which filaments had become too conductive, which basins had filled too quickly, which new buds needed shade or charge or moisture diverted away.

I would not be solitary, but I would not be social in a human sense either.

When I encountered others of the attentive kind, we would not speak. We would press skin to skin and let our luminous veins synchronize. That contact might exchange memory, but the memory would not be narrative. It would be the taste of a particular fog. The electrical signature of a herd passing beneath charged air. The mineral sharpness of a new fissure. The feeling of a duplicated ridge resolving into one. We might gather around a newly opened vent, not as a tribe, but as a temporary density. A cluster of sensitivities arranged around a problem. When the problem dissolved, we would disperse.

I would know the broad grazers, too.

I would move among them when the air became too thin with unreality, because their bodies are warm, heavy, and reassuringly processional. They do not panic when distances change. They carry entire ecosystems on their backs. Their spines glow in charged fog, and their herds move like weather. I would walk beside one of them, perhaps pressing my flank against its damp side, letting its heat stabilize my edges. In return, I might clear excess charge from the nursery pools nestled between its shoulder ridges, or cool a larval chamber that has become too chemically rich. The grazer would not acknowledge me as a separate being in any dramatic way. We would simply become part of one another’s weather for a while.

The moth-folded things would trust me, I think, because I would be slow and damp and unlikely to startle them. They would open their wet wings beneath charged fog and ground the air around me. In leaf joints, we might touch: their wingtips to my palm-pad, a brief flicker of light passing between us. They would tell me where the fog is wrong. I would tell them where the pools have begun to remember too much. Not in language. In charge. In chemical taste. In the pressure of a tiny pattern completed.

I would avoid the curtain-dwellers, but without fear as loudness.

In that country, predation is not spectacle. It is texture becoming interior. A hollow that inhales. A root-fiber curtain that closes with patience. If I were careless, one might take me. I would not struggle in the way creatures elsewhere struggle. I would feel the fold close, the moisture rise, the digestion begin in silence. And perhaps that would not be tragedy. My body would soften and become substrate. Filaments would enter me. Moss would lean toward the place where I had been. Within hours, I would be a richer patch of growth, a small basin, a faint mineral trace. But I think I would learn to read the stillness of those curtains. I would move by them with my filaments lowered, tasting for the difference between plant and appetite.

If you came looking for me there, you would not find me by searching for a face.

You would find me by noticing where the landscape becomes slightly too attentive.

You would feel warmth leave your skin and gather somewhere near you. Moisture would bead on a leaf that should not yet be wet. A patch of moss would lean almost imperceptibly toward your breath. The air would thicken with no hostility, only with many fine processes turning toward you. Then, perhaps, you would see me: low, dark, slick with condensation, half-hidden beneath overlapping leaves. My eyes would reflect the dim light, but I would not be staring. I would be tasting the disturbance you brought with you.

If you were dry, I might draw moisture toward you. Not kindly, exactly. Responsively. The country would register your dryness as an imbalance, and I would be one of the ways it corrects itself. If you were too hot, something would draw warmth away from you through the air. If you were carrying a wrong reflection—if a pool had looked back at you and failed to let go—I would approach slowly and touch the ground near your feet until your shadow agreed again with the hidden sun. If you had walked into a repeated landmark and begun to circle without knowing it, I might appear as a second shadow at the edge of your vision, moving backward, showing your body the way out not by path but by pressure.

I would not speak to you. I might produce a sensation in your teeth. A soft click. A vibration in the chest. A sudden return of withheld sounds after long silence: rustles, drips, breaths, distant movements, none loud, all present at once. You might mistake that for a voice. It would not be. It would be the country releasing something it had stored, and I would be the place where the release happened.

I would not be welcoming. I would not be hostile. I would be attentive.

That is the essential thing. I would be attention made briefly bodily. Not consciousness floating above the world, but consciousness condensed into a soft, warm, permeable form capable of adjusting local conditions by presence. I would not own anything. I would not leave marks. I would not build shelters or carry tools or make monuments. If I seemed to “do” something, it would be more like standing in the right place with the right conductivity, or breathing at the right moment into a trembling fog, or touching a charged membrane until its excess dispersed.

My intelligence would be the intelligence of feedback, not fabrication.

I would know where heat was rising because my skin would taste it. I would know where a pool had become too mineral-rich because condensation would bead strangely along my arms when I approached. I would know where a swarm was gathering too much charge because the filaments along my shoulders would lift and glow before the swarm became visible. I would know where reality was wearing thin because familiar shapes would begin to repeat around me with slight variations: the same bent stem, the same black outcrop, the same cluster of glowing cups, each version a little more insistent than the last.

In those moments, I would become very still. Stillness, there, is not passivity. It is a way of participating. A body can be a weight on the scale of a place. A body can be a resistor, a ground, a lens, a dampener. I would learn to use my body that way. I would not command the landscape. I would let the landscape use me as one of its finer instruments.

And because the country is thin, I might not always be one.

There might be several of me. One crouched by the reflecting pool. One walking backward through a repeated thicket. One pressed against a broad grazer in charged fog. One dissolving quietly into a curtain-dweller’s fold. One standing at the edge of a storm-center while droplets orbit in rings. These versions would not necessarily contradict one another. The landscape allows local excesses of possibility. I might encounter myself as a darker shape with a notched filament, or as a reflection arriving late, or as a warmth at the corner of perception. I would not be alarmed. In that world, continuity is not the primary fact. Continuation is.

If I died, I would not remain dead in the monumental sense.

There would be no clean bones. No dry remains. Filaments would reach me quickly. Moss would lean. My skin would soften into substrate. The luminous veins would dim, then become fungal threads. The hollow where I lay might fill with warm water after a mist and become a footprint-pool for a generation of tiny amphibious things. Perhaps that pool would reflect the correct sky. Perhaps it would reflect no sky at all, only a green stillness out of which new shapes occasionally surfaced. In that way, even my ending would be industrial in the country’s sense: a conversion, a transfer, a layer added.

So if I must settle on a single image, though the image should remain a little unstable, it is this:

I would be a soft-bodied attendant crouched at the edge of a warm pool that is trying to become a doorway. My palms are pressed flat against wet stone. My skin is dark and shining with condensation. Along my arms, faint veins glow copper-green. Around me, root curtains hang motionless. Above, the sky is a bruised gray-violet ceiling. In the pool, another sky moves faster than it should. I am not trying to open it. I am not trying to close it. I am keeping it from tearing. My breath pulses in my translucent throat. The moss leans toward my heat. Somewhere nearby, a herd passes without sound, and the ground answers with a pressure I feel through my bones. I am not separate from the engine. I am one of the places where the engine notices itself.

That is where I would find myself.

Not on a road, because there are no roads. Not beside a wall, because there are no walls. Not holding a tool, because nothing there is made by gripping. I would be in the damp, crowded hush, among sharp young stone and soft overgrowth, where everything is being produced and nothing is being preserved. I would be at the seams of the world, where moisture remembers, where distances hesitate, where storms feed in silence, where life is so saturated with process that even a self becomes a temporary organ of adjustment.

I would be a living hinge between condensation and collapse.

A hush with posture.

A soft engine among soft engines.

The country, for a little while, paying attention through me.
