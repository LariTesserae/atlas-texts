I would find myself low.

Not in the open water where the needle-thin swimmers pass, not in the higher gray diffusion where the gliders lean into unseen currents, but near the floor, where the world is thickest with its own history. I would be down in the pale country of fused shell and polished channels, where every surface has already been lived on, worn down, sealed over, and made into something that only looks like stone.

At first, imagining it, I think I might be one of the gliders: a broad translucent shape drifting through curtains of mineral snow, barely disturbing the water. But that feels too loose, too unattached. I think I might be one of the builders in the seams, a small soft body locked into a hard case, adding one invisible layer to the endless archive. But there is something in me that would need to move, however slowly, across what has been made. I think I might be one of the immense ones, a vast ridged island moving through the distance with colonies on its back. But that is too rare, too enormous, too much like weather itself. The shape that remains, after those images settle, is lower, humbler, heavier.

I would be one of the shell-backed wanderers.

Not a face, not a voice, not a swift hunter, not a creature with bright eyes and a clear outline. I would be a low, broad, slow animal, difficult to see and easy to mistake for part of the terrain. My body would be armored in fused plates, thick and mineralized, encrusted with the same pale matter that forms the ground. On my back there would be little colonies of builders settled into the ridges, thin microbial films growing in the sheltered grooves, perhaps a few brittle tubes or lace-like crusts filtering quietly when I stopped moving. I would carry a small landscape on me. I would not merely live in the built country; I would be a piece of it in motion.

You would find me, if you could find me at all, on the lee side of a broad ramp not far from a ring-shaped rise, where the current slows and lets its burden settle. The place would be open but not featureless: a shallow basin beyond the ramp, a scatter of hollow mounds in the distance, a row of low chimneys standing in a field to one side, their openings dark and patient. The water there would be a little colder in the hollows, a little denser near the floor. Veils of suspended shell powder would hang in the dimness, sometimes thinning to nothing, sometimes descending in soft curtains until the nearby forms vanished.

I would be near the basin’s edge, perhaps half-buried in silt, perhaps moving so slowly that movement itself would seem like stillness. My limbs would be blunt and strong, not graceful, made for bearing weight and holding against currents rather than speed. I would not have eyes in any useful sense. The weak pewter glow overhead would mean little to me. I would know the world as pressure, as temperature, as chemical trace, as the faintest vibration traveling through the made floor. A change in the water’s taste would tell me more than sight ever could.

What passed for my mind would be slow enough to be mistaken for geology.

I would know when a curtain of mineral snow was coming by the way the water grew heavier, by the subtle increase in suspended matter against my sensory bristles, by the way attached filterers on my carapace stirred into faint activity. I would know a dense brine before it arrived by the sagging weight of it in the layers above. I would know an upwelling by the lift of old dust from the floor, by the suddenly altered chemistry of the water touching my plates. I would feel the passage of other beings not as shapes but as disturbances: the delicate pressure-shift of a glider overhead, the granular tick of a grazer working a ridge nearby, the almost imagined tremor of something immense moving through the distance.

I would feed without drama. I would lower my blunt mouth to the constructed floor and rasp gently at microbial crusts where they had grown thick enough. I would take in fallen particulate trapped in hollows. I would crush fragile accumulations of shell matter and digest the living remnants within them. Sometimes I would remain in one place while the attached colonies on my back filtered the passing water, and some part of my nourishment would come indirectly from their quiet work. There would be no hunting, no pursuit, no bright violence. Only patience, only the slow conversion of cold mineral richness into a little more body, a little more heat, a little more continuation.

When I moved, I would leave a faint trail behind: not a dramatic mark, but a slight disturbance in the settled powder, a polishing of the surface where my body had passed over it. There would be a soft granular whisper from my limbs against the fused shell floor, so faint that it would vanish almost as soon as it formed. If I shifted a plate, or if some internal chamber resonated under the pressure of movement, there might be a hollow knock, brief and strange, immediately swallowed by the cold. Nothing about me would be loud. Even my existence would have the quality of a rumor.

For long periods I would not move at all.

That is the part of me that would belong most deeply to that country. I would stop at the margin of a current, draw myself inward behind fused lids, and become sealed. Silt would settle on my back. The mineral snow would cover my plates. The builders living on me would extend themselves into the water as if I were merely a convenient stone. Microbial film would strengthen in the sheltered seams of my armor. Currents would part around me and smooth me into the scene. I would disappear into appearance. I would become terrain.

And yet I would still be breathing.

That is what I would be, if I had to answer in a single phrase: a breathing stone. A wandering surface. A slow animal mistaken for ground.

I would not be alone in the sense that land creatures understand aloneness. Even if no other large creature came near, I would be accompanied by the small lives attached to me and by the chemical presence of the country itself. The builders in my carapace would be with me, though not as companions. Their tiny bodies would orient themselves to the flow across my back. Their secretions would add weight and texture to me. When I moved into a richer current, they would feed. When I paused in a sheltered hollow, they would grow. When I crossed a stretch of barren polished floor, they would close and endure. I would carry them the way the floor carries them: without intention, without care, simply by being the surface nearest at hand.

In this way I would be less a single self than a moving assemblage. My body would be the center of a small accidental community. The country would ride on me. The archive would ride on me. And as I moved, however slowly, I would carry bits of the landscape from one place to another: dust, larvae, fragments of film, mineral grains, spores of crust, tiny clinging lives that had settled on me without knowing I was an animal and not a mound.

I think I would know routes, but not as roads. There would be inherited chemical paths along the broad plain, worn by generations of my kind and by other slow wanderers before them. I would follow gradients of taste and pressure, subtle differences in the floor’s mineral signature, faint memories held not in thought but in body. A certain channel would feel right because its current carried the correct salts. A certain ramp would feel safe because its slope broke the downward slide of cold brine. A certain basin would draw me because its sediment held the residue of older lives that my body could use. I would not choose these places the way a person chooses a destination. I would settle into them as water settles into a hollow.

Sometimes I would come upon the traces of others: polished trails left by grazers, a shed grazer-skin crumbling into powder, the sealed opening of a tube colony, the faint chemical stain of something recently dead. I would not stop to wonder. I would take what was useful, move over what was not, and continue. If another of my kind was nearby, I would know it as a pressure-shadow, a chemical thickness, a slow interruption in the field of the water. We would not gather. We would not signal. We might simply adjust our paths enough to avoid interference, each conserving its own small store of warmth and motion.

There would be moments, though, when something larger passed.

I would feel one of the immense ones long before I could have seen it, if seeing had mattered. The water would change. The currents would bend strangely. A heaviness would approach through the plain, not threatening, not urgent, simply vast. The snow around me might be drawn into the wake of that passage. The builders on my back would close. I would lower myself, seal myself, and become as still as possible. The great body would move through the gray diffusion like a rumor of land unmoored, carrying its own forests of tubes and crusts, its own minor animal weather. I would feel it pass over or near me, and I would not flee. I would wait, because waiting is what the country teaches.

Afterward, the water would slowly return to its usual unrest. The veils would thin. The particulate would settle again. Somewhere a brittle overhang might shed a layer. Somewhere a channel might be scoured clean. The plain would continue its hushed rearrangement. And I, too, would continue. I would open by degrees, test the current, feel the taste of the water, and move again if moving seemed necessary.

I would not be beautiful in any ordinary sense. I would be pale, blunt, encrusted, heavy, shaped by cold and pressure and long use. My beauty, if I had any, would be the beauty of fit: the way my plates lay against one another, the way my body settled into the made floor as if it had been grown from it, the way the small lives on my back continued their work without knowing they were riding an animal. I would be a thing that belonged so completely to its world that it became difficult to separate from it.

That is who I would be, or rather what I would be. The question “who” feels too sharp for that place. There would be no name, no face, no story told in symbols. There would only be a living center of pressure and need, a body moving through the archive, a slow continuity of sensation and secretion and repair. If I had a self, it would be distributed across my plates, my attached builders, my trails, my chemical memory of routes. It would not sit behind my eyes and look out. It would be the whole pattern of my being in the water.

And when I died, I would not leave the country.

My body would settle where it stopped. Scavengers would come first, small and many-legged, patient in their work. Borers would enter the harder parts. Builders would settle on my carapace, especially where the currents left clean surfaces. Microbial film would spread over me like frost without winter. My plates would become substrate. My hollows would become shelters. The dust of me would mix with the dust of everything else that had ever lived there. In time, the mound of me would be smoothed by moving water, pressed into the long record of the plain, and mistaken for ground.

Even then I would still be part of the country’s metabolism. The dead there are not removed. They are incorporated. They become the next surface. They become the next beginning for small bodies that must attach, secrete, filter, and grow. My shell would join the archive. My weight would add to the weight of the high submerged table. My stillness would become another piece of the finished landscape.

So if you searched for me in that world, do not look for a figure. Do not look for eyes meeting yours, or a shape rising to greet you, or a voice in the cold water. Look instead for a low mound near the edge of a basin. Look for a roughened stone that is too symmetrical to be accidental, too encrusted to be simple, too quiet to be dead. Look for a place where the silt has settled unevenly, where a few small tubes grow from a ridge, where the microbial film is thin but persistent. Listen for the faintest hollow knock, felt more than heard, when something inside shifts against an old chamber.

That would be me.

A shell-backed wanderer. A walking surface. A slow animal become landscape. Part of the made wilderness, breathing under mineral snow, carrying my small country on my back, moving without urgency across a floor that is also my ancestors, my work, my body, and my final home.
