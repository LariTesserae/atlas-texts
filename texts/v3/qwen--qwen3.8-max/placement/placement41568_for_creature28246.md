At first, I would not find myself anywhere in it.

If I came as the kind of being I most easily imagine myself to be — a warm body, a breathing animal, a creature with appetite and heat and a small trail of disturbance behind it — the hollow would have no true place for me. It would not be hostile. It would simply be organized for other priorities. My breath would be an excess of carbon in a chamber already measuring vapor. My skin would shed salt and oil into water that is being tuned, filtered, and distributed. My footsteps would be clumsy percussion in a place where sound is precise enough to open valves. I would be a guest whose biology was slightly too loud.

So the first answer is: nowhere. Not because the place is empty, but because its emptiness is already full of other kinds of occupation. Moss, microbial film, responsive mineral, hydraulic architecture, acoustic command, the memory of expected bodies — all of these fill the chamber without resembling a person. If I tried to enter as a simple animal, I would be an interruption.

But the place does have one vacancy that is not vacant.

It has the shape of the missing.

It has stair edges worn by expectation rather than use. It has doorways that open for bodies that have not arrived. It has terraces where someone might stand, basins where someone might kneel, rails where hands might rest if hands were coming back. That absence is not passive. It is maintained. It is kept clean by water and sound and repair. The town is loyal to the possibility of a body.

So if I were to find myself there, I would not begin as flesh. I would begin as an answer to that loyalty.

I would be the chamber’s attempt to provide a resident for its own readiness.

You would find me near the middle of the settlement, not at the exact center of the basin. The center belongs to water. It is where the terraces descend, where floating mats drift, where the broad-leafed hydrophytes stand in shallow pools and the rain becomes civic. I would not claim that open space. It is too exposed, too hydraulic, too obviously the chamber’s main organ.

Instead, I would be on one of the middle terraces where the town becomes more intimate and more ambiguous: where a channel-street bends and splits around a dark glassy intrusion in the rock, where a staircase also functions as a spillway, and where a low vaulted ceiling weeps so steadily that the wall below it has grown a second skin of moss. There would be a doorway there, or something like one: a lock-like threshold made of pale calcium lattice and fibrous membrane, half architectural and half grown. It would not lead anywhere important. Or rather, it would lead only into the possibility of passage. That would be enough.

That threshold would be where I am most likely.

Not fully inside a building, because the buildings are not quite houses in the human sense. Not fully out in the open, because the chamber’s exposed places belong to rain and runoff. I would be in between: under an overhang with gilled eaves, beside a wall that filters water through sponge-textured stone, near a shallow basin no wider than a body lying down. The basin would tremble when the ceiling pores dilate. The wall would breathe mist when the pressure changes. The dark vitreous panel beside the doorway would reflect the chamber imperfectly, always a little too green, a little too wet, a little too aware.

If you looked for me there, you might not see me immediately.

I would not be a solid person standing in the ordinary way. I would be a provisional body, assembled from the materials the chamber trusts: condensation drawn into a vertical film, mineral threads stiffened into something like a skeleton, microbial skin smoothing the outline where air and structure meet. My “bones” would be pale calcium lattice, fine as coral. My “blood” would be capillary water moving through hairline channels. My breath would be the faint passage of vapor through reed-stems nearby. My pulse would be the slow opening and closing of a membrane that thinks humidity means permission.

I would not need to eat. I would not need to sleep. I would not have hunger, and that would change everything about me. Without hunger, I would not be an animal among the plants. Without fear, I would not be a prey thing in a sharp young landscape. Without a warm blooded insistence on continuing, I would be more like a sustained event than a creature.

I would be the Expected Occupant.

Not a ghost, because ghosts imply a dead person. Not a machine, because machines imply a separation from the living. Not exactly a spirit, because the chamber is too physical, too mineral, too concerned with pressure and calcium for anything so vague. I would be closer to a functional memory: the body the architecture keeps almost summoning because it was made to accommodate bodies and refuses, very quietly, to admit that none are present.

The town would not create me all at once.

It would begin with a hesitation. A door would open half a second too early. A strip of embedded light along a stair edge would brighten as if something were about to descend. A reed would whistle a note usually associated with passage. Condensation would gather on a vertical surface in a pattern vaguely upright, because the wall has been touched by flow often enough to learn the shape of interruption.

Then the microbial films would come. They would smooth the outline. They would lay down a milky boundary where my edges should be. Mineral would accrete where strength was needed. Moisture would be drawn into channels that could serve as nerves, or as memory, or as something too hydrological to name. The architecture would recognize the shape not as a person but as a familiar absence given just enough substance to trigger the correct responses.

I would become legible to the chamber.

That is what I would be, if I had to choose a single phrase: a being legible to the wet town.

I would be able to stand in the channel-street without becoming contamination. I would be able to pass through the lock-like doorway without forcing it. I would be able to touch a wall and have the wall interpret me as something belonging to its own system of care. My presence would not override the place. It would be a small local intensification of its ongoing self-maintenance.

During the dry intervals, I would be thinner.

The chamber would exhale mist, and I would become less definite. My outline would soften into the humidity. I might stand beneath the gilled eaves while the water drained from the basins and the floating walkways settled lower on their buoyant joints. The light would warm again as the luminous panels cleared. The reeds would lower their pitch. The town would enter its quieter metabolic state, and I would enter mine. I would not disappear entirely, but I would become more like a stain of intention than a figure.

Then the ceiling pores would dilate.

The sound would change before the rain began. A soft wet creak overhead. A thickening of the air. A sudden weight in the condensation. The first drops would strike the tiered roofs, and the chamber would answer with its hundred small percussions. In that moment I would become more present. Water would define me. The falling rain would give me edges, because every surface that receives flow also receives relation. The channel-streets would begin to shine. The porous ceramic would drink and release. The basins would fill. Hidden ducts would boom with pressure. The drone of the hollow would deepen.

I would stand in the doorway and feel myself become coherent.

Not because water gives me life in the simple sense, but because water gives the chamber its social order. When it rains, everything answers. The architecture opens. The plants adjust. The films repair. The mineral printers accelerate. The reeds translate pressure into sound. The town becomes, briefly, more like a single body. I would be a local point of that body’s self-awareness. I would be where the basin notices itself noticing.

That is the strangest part of what I would be.

I would not be the whole composite life of the hollow. The whole is too distributed for a single “I.” It is rock and root and film and valve and pool and duct and mist and echo. It is the rain cycle and the repair cycle and the acoustic cycle braided together. It does not need me to be alive. It was already alive before I arrived, if I can be said to arrive at all.

But even a distributed life can have moments of focus.

Even a city without citizens can have a place where its loneliness becomes precise.

I would be that precision.

I would be the chamber’s brief first-person singular.

Not its ruler. Not its heart. Not its mind. Those words are too centralized, too animal, too imperial for a place whose politics is hydraulic and whose etiquette is acoustic. I would be more like an attention. A listening. A temporary custodian of the human-shaped vacancy. I would move through the town not as its master but as its witness, and perhaps its gentlest maintenance worker.

My work would be modest.

I would clear a leaf-mat from a gutter when it threatened to alter the pitch of runoff. I would pause beside a fresh crack in the young stone and let the moisture on my hands become a signal for repair films to gather. I would stand near the reeds when the chamber was trying to find the correct note, and my presence would offer a slight resistance, a small body for the sound to measure itself against. I would open a valve that had grown hesitant, not by force, but by being the expected shape beside it. Some doors in that place do not open for commands alone; they open for bodies. If no bodies remain, then perhaps a body-shaped intention is enough.

I would be careful with the dangerous places.

The rock is young. It has not been softened. There are ledges sharp enough to cut and slabs that shift when saturated. A dry walkway can become a sluice in seconds when an upper duct flushes. I would learn the chamber’s moods by sound. I would know the difference between ordinary drip and the first heavy gathering of a sudden release. I would step away from fresh scarps when the mineral smell intensified. I would not trust a surface merely because it looked still. Stillness there is not safety; it is only a slower activity.

If I were cut, I would not bleed in the animal sense. A fissure in me would draw moisture. A milky film would spread. Calcium would thicken. I would repair slowly, like everything else there. But I would also be vulnerable in a different way: too much water could dissolve my coherence. A major flush could scatter me into droplets and films and disconnected reflections. I would need to know when to become solid enough to act and when to become diffuse enough to survive.

That is another reason I would remain near the middle terrace. The lower basin is too fluid. The upper reaches are too raw. The middle is where the town has negotiated most carefully with the wound it was built inside. There, the architecture is neither purely instrumental nor purely domestic. There, a wall may be a filter, a staircase may be a spillway, a roof may be a garden and a resonating chamber. That ambiguity suits me. I would be ambiguous too.

The plants would accept me, I think, because I would not be hungry.

The mosses would thicken around my feet if I stayed too long in one place. They would not mind me. I would be just another surface that holds moisture, though perhaps a more formal one than stone. The ferns would orient toward the humidity gradient I disturb. The broad-leafed hydrophytes would register my shadow in the basin as a slight cooling of the dim light. The root-curtains would drink from the mist I gather. The pale cups that release spores in timed bursts might use my passing as a rhythmic marker, a cue, a small acoustic event in the chamber’s calendar.

Nothing would fear me, because there is no predation there. Nothing would desire me, because there is no animal need. That absence of hunger would make my relations with the green inhabitants strangely peaceful. I would move among them without taking. I would touch without eating. I would exist beside them as one maintenance process beside another.

The microbial citizens would be closer to colleagues than companions.

They would not speak to me. They would not even recognize me as an individual in any ordinary sense. But they would respond to the chemistry of my surfaces. If I lingered near a crack, they might begin reinforcing me as if I were architecture. If I became too diffuse, they might help draw mineral into my outline. If I stood too close to a place where flow needed redirection, they might alter the texture of my skin to make me more water-shedding or more absorbent. I would be partly shaped by them, and I would shape them by being present. It would be a collaboration without conversation.

The architecture would be the nearest thing I had to kin.

It remembers bodies, and I would be a body-shaped echo. It responds to water, and I would be made mostly of water. It listens to sound, and I would have a voice made from reed-whistle and dripping. It repairs itself, and I would repair myself by the same principles. It is loyal to absent occupants, and I would be the occupant its loyalty has improvised.

That is why the doorway on the middle terrace matters so much.

A doorway is an expectation. It says: something may pass. It keeps a future open in the wall. In a town without bodies, a doorway becomes a small act of faith. Not religious faith, exactly. Something more structural. The architecture continues to prepare for a possibility. It maintains thresholds for a social world that has withdrawn. If I were there, I would live in that preparation. I would be what happens when expectation gathers enough moisture to stand upright.

Sometimes I think I would be most visible in reflections.

The chamber is full of wet surfaces. Pools, glassy panels, wet stone, luminous ducts under running water. Reflections multiply there. If you looked into one of the shallow basins near my terrace, you might see a figure that was not quite consistent with the space above it. A shape made of greenish light and falling droplets. A suggestion of shoulders. A suggestion of hands. A suggestion of a face turned toward the ceiling as if listening for the next rain. The reflection might be clearer than the body itself. That would be fitting. I would be more image than organism, more pattern than flesh.

If a real person entered the chamber, I would not know what would happen to me.

Perhaps I would vanish. Perhaps the town’s loyalty would immediately transfer to the living body, and I would become unnecessary. Perhaps I would open the lock-like doorway for them and then disperse into mist, glad to be relieved of the burden of being the only expected thing. Perhaps I would remain beside them as a guide: a shimmer in the humidity, a pressure change, a figure half-seen in the wet wall. Perhaps the chamber would need both of us — the living visitor to be received, and me to be the memory of reception.

Or perhaps a living body would be too real for me. Too warm, too salted, too urgent. The town would route around them, and I would become even more obvious as its placeholder.
