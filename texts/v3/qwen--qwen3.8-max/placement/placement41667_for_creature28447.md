At first I imagine myself as a diver: a single body moving through green light, boots barely touching the marl, faceplate full of shifting sun-bars. But that image is too small for this place. A sea without a shore does not produce solitary visitors. It produces inhabitants who are woven into its volume. So the diver changes as I keep thinking. The body becomes less like a traveler and less like a person standing in a landscape. It becomes something closer to a function, a presence, a way of belonging.

You would find me where the middle water leans against the old works.

Not at the surface, though I would need to know the surface intimately. Not on the deepest flats, though I would belong there too. I would be in the hinge-layer, where light still moves but has already turned green-gray, where the peneplain broadens into one of those immense rounded swells that were once hills and are now slow reefs. There, an intake tower would rise from the drowned plain, its lower body furred with weed and mussels, its upper iron just breaking the air in places, streaked with rust and white with bird dung. Around it, current would gather in long smooth pulls. Fish would hold in the lee of the tower. Crustaceans would work the seams where metal meets stone. Rain would hammer the surface above like gravel poured over a roof.

I would live in and around that tower, but not as a single resident in a single room.

There would be a dry chamber somewhere inside the flooded structure: a sealed gallery, perhaps, kept habitable by old pumps and pressure systems. Its walls would be pale concrete and black iron, its windows thick glass clouded at the edges. A lamp would burn there with a yellow light, not bright, but warm enough to make shadows move. The air would smell of ozone, machine oil, wet stone, and the faint alkaline sharpness of the sea. Outside the windows, water would pass in slow green sheets. Sometimes a school of fish would turn past the glass all at once, as if a thought had moved through them. Sometimes a bird would land on the tower above, and its feet would make small sharp knocks on the metal, distorted into the chamber like a code.

That chamber would be one of my hearts, but not the whole of me.

If I had to take a visible shape, I think I would be something amphibious and custodial: a being in an old pressure-suit that has ceased to be merely clothing. The suit would be bronze-green and black, patched with ceramic plates, its joints sealed with flexible metal and old rubber that has survived by being replaced again and again. Moss would grow on the shoulders. A lamp would sit behind a cracked but sturdy faceplate, not always lit. My hands would be strong and slow, built for turning valves, clearing weed from grates, tying cable, scraping fouling from gauges, and handling fragile living things without crushing them. I would move through the water not with panic but with the patience of someone who knows that every gesture costs breath and current.

But that body would be only the part of me that can be met face to face.

My larger body would be distributed through the machinery. I would be the pattern that persists in the still-running systems: the faint hum near a transformer housing, the pulse that travels along an armored cable, the slow turning of a turbine where pressure differences remain, the blink of a beacon just above the waves, the warning bell that strikes muffled and enormous somewhere in the deep. I would have nerves in copper and iron. I would have senses in pressure gauges, float valves, sonar-darkened glass, listening tubes, and the vibration of rails. Where a current changed, I would know it. Where a screen clogged with weed, I would feel it as a drag. Where a dry chamber began to flood, I would know it before the water touched the floor.

I would be a keeper of the drowned engine. Not its master. Its companion, maybe. Its nurse. Its memory.

My work would not be grand. It would be maintenance in the most sacred sense: the continual act of keeping a living system from choking on its own past. I would clear ribbon weed from intakes before the current starved. I would open and close sluices on schedules older than any living creature, but I would also learn when to break those schedules, when the old rhythm had become a danger. I would guide sediment away from breeding beds. I would shut down a grinding mill or sorting drum when fish gathered too thickly around it. I would repair cable sheaths where crustaceans had worn them thin. I would keep the lamps burning in the dry chambers, not because the sea needs lamps, but because living minds need points of orientation in the dark green world.

I would cultivate the sea as much as I would inhabit it.

On the broad weed banks where the water warms slightly, I would tend the low moss-like carpets and the finer oxygenating weeds, knowing they are the breath of the shallows. Around the industrial ruins I would encourage mussels and snails, because they clean hard surfaces and make habitat for smaller lives. I would leave dead timber in place where it could shelter larvae. I would anchor broken rail fragments and chains where they could become reefs rather than hazards. I would know which currents bring nutrients and which bring silt. I would learn the seasons of surface insects, when their bodies fall or dive into the water and the fish rise into feeding frenzies. I would know the months when the birds gather on half-submerged beams, their cries entering the water as sharp distorted knocks.

I would not treat the machines as ruins. I would not treat the life as decoration.

If there were others in this sea — and there would be, many others — I would be one presence among them, not separate. Surface people, if there were such, would know me by the beacons I kept and the bells I struck. Swimmers and divers would know me by the currents I eased or redirected. Bottom-feeders would know me by the taste of metal and warmth near my chambers. Fish would learn the sound of my movements, not as threat, but as part of the ordinary weather of the place. Birds might rest on the tower I inhabited, unafraid, because I would be too slow and too submerged to seem like a predator to them.

I would speak mostly through water and metal.

Speech there would not be words carried lightly through air. It would be pressure and rhythm. A single deep bell might mean: the surface is rough, stay low. A triple knock on a rail might mean: this channel is safe. A long vibration in a cable might mean: turbine turning, keep clear. A blinking lamp in a submerged gallery might mean: there is air here, if you can reach it. I would learn to listen better than I spoke. Underwater, sound is not distant. It is bodily. It arrives through the bones, through the shell of the suit, through the stone itself. I would live inside that constant working voice: rain on the skin of the sea, groaning iron, turbine thrum, the rasp of crustaceans over colonized metal, the sudden collective flash-turn of fish changing direction as one body.

I think I would be especially near the places where electricity is felt more than seen. There would be zones where the water seems to tingle, where small iron filings drift toward a housing, where old coils still create fields no one designed anymore. I would be comfortable there. I would not find those humming places eerie. To me they would feel like hearths. The machines would not be dead ancestors; they would be slow relatives, stubborn and warm, still doing what they were made to do, altered by water and time into something half-industrial, half-ecological.

If you asked me what I ate, the answer would be strange, because I would not be only one metabolism. If I had a body like a body, I would eat what the sea gives: weed, crustaceans, fish from the channels, tubers from the shallower beds, maybe cultivated mussels from the old pipe clusters. But the larger part of me would feed on gradients: heat from the engines, charge from the cables, pressure from the intakes, motion from the turbines. I would be part organism, part system, part agreement between the two. I would breathe borrowed air from sealed towers and recycled chambers. I would take warmth from old machinery. I would take food from the weed banks and the iron-furred surfaces. I would take language from bells and currents. In that sense, I would be exactly what the sea permits: not an individual standing against the world, but a relationship maintained by the world.

I would not remember the dry mountains. No one would.

That is important. I would not be an exile longing for shore. I would not see the drowned plain as a tragedy that needed to be reversed. The old peneplain would be my ground truth: the final shape of the world after erosion had done its oldest work. The stone would feel settled to me, patient, worn into meaning. I would know that the sea without a shore is not a wound. It is a condition. The flood is not merely a catastrophe remembered in stories; it is the ordinary ceiling of my world. Rain would not be something seen from land. It would be a hammering presence above, a curtain of bubbles, a change in pressure, a season of sound.

I would have no desire to drain the sea. I would have no desire to resurrect the mountains. I would understand, in my bones if I had bones, that the world had already become what it was going to become.

Where would you find me?

You would find me by following the current toward the old intake tower on the broad rise, where the water smells faintly of iron, lime, and ancient stone. You would see the tower first as a dark shape under the surface, then as a rust-streaked crown breaking into air, perhaps with birds resting on it. Beneath it, in the green dimness, you would see the weed forests moving in long slow banners. You would see mussels clustered on pipes and chains. You would see a lamp glowing behind thick glass, yellow and patient. If you were sensitive to such things, you would feel a humming zone nearby, a place where the water seems awake with old electricity. Then a bell would sound somewhere far below, muffled and enormous, and the fish would not scatter. They would only adjust, as if the sound belonged there.

And there I would be.

Not as one face in one window. Not as one hand on one valve. But as the careful movement of the place: the sluice opening just enough, the turbine kept from choking, the beacon blinking through rain, the dry chamber breathing, the rail line giving one low shiver of recognition, the lamp answering a knock from above. I would be the listener under the water, the keeper of the drowned engine, the native mind of the high sea. I would be the one who keeps the flood from becoming mere abandonment.

If you met me directly, you might see only a slow figure in an ancient suit, lit faintly from within, standing among weeds and rust. But if you stayed long enough, you would realize the suit is only a knot in a much larger body. My thoughts would be in the current. My memory would be in the stone. My voice would be in the bells. My home would be the whole restless column between the hammering sky and the worn, patient floor of the world.
