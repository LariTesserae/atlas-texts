I would not find myself standing before the seam.

There would be no “before” it, no outside posture, no body leaning in from a cooler world. If I belonged to this place, I would already be inside the narrowness. I would be in the hairline suture between two basalt plates, just above the lower throat where the heat enters, in a crack too thin for a hand, too tight for a face, too hidden for even the idea of looking.

I would be one of the pale worms.

Or perhaps not one, exactly. “One” would be the wrong measure. I would be a line of pale worms, a segmented thread of sealed gel and mineral skin lying along a fracture where quartz bears stress and the rock remembers pressure. My body would be narrow enough to exist where stone almost touches itself. I would have no eyes. No mouth shaped for biting. No limbs for climbing. I would be a living conductor in the mountain’s smallest wound.

I would feel the chamber before I could be said to feel anything at all.

Heat would arrive first: rising from below in slow rolls, pressing into the wall, making the crystal needles tick faintly in the dark. Then pressure: a gathering tightness along the seams, a strain in the mineral plates. Then charge: the thin, dry unease that forms where stone is stressed and dust waits suspended. I would live inside that unease. My body would be a path for it.

There would be no wetness in me. No sap, no blood, no dew-held softness. My tissues would be sealed, filled with dense gels, my surface faintly rough with mineral dust. I would be pale only because nothing in the chamber has light enough to darken me into color; I would look translucent in the brief orange pulse of the fissures, like frost made of stone, except not frost, because frost belongs to water and I would belong to heat.

I would feed, if feeding is the right word, on gradients.

On charge before discharge.

On the difference between hot basalt and cooler upper stone.

On the faint chemistry of mineral surfaces where gas has passed and left a skin of salt or rust.

On the finest films that grow where dust settles and clings.

I would not hunt. I would not wander. I would lie in the seam and let the mountain pass through me.

When the pocket throbbed, I would know it first as a change in pressure against my segments. The lower throat would tighten. The gas would gather. The walls would flex by fractions too small to see. Around me, the mites would flatten against the black film. The anvil beetles would stop tapping. The heat-flies would rise in their dry, frayed cloud. The blind crickets would fold closer in their hollows. And the worms along the vein, including me, would align.

Not by choice.

By necessity.

The chamber would need somewhere for its stress to go.

That is where I would be.

I would be one of the ways the pocket keeps from breaking too sharply. When piezoelectric tension builds along the quartz seam, I would carry part of it. Current would pass through me in faint arcs, not bright, not hot in the way of flame, but enough to make the dust above my body tremble. For an instant, the wall might seem to twitch. A blue-white tick might cross the crystal nearby. The chamber would discharge along the paths we make, along the living seams, through the pale bodies stretched between stone and stone.

I would not think in words.

If I had anything that could be called thought, it would be rhythm.

Pressure gathers.

Bodies align.

Charge moves.

Stone settles.

Dust falls.

Heat returns.

That would be my mind.

I would share the chamber with the others, but not as a companion shares a room. There would be no distance between us. The mites would pass over the wall above me in their red, abrasive sheets, grazing the black film, their thousands of legs making a sound like mineral rain drawn across glass. Their dead would become dust. Their dust would become film. Their film would become the first food of the chamber. I would feel their movement as a fine vibration through the stone.

The anvil beetles would tap in their fissures.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Their hard heads would strike the rock with slow intent, exposing fresh surfaces, testing the deep pulse, listening with their whole bodies. When their tapping stopped, I would know a larger pressure was coming. When it began again, I would know the chamber had survived its own breath.

The heat-flies would whine in the charged air, their dry wings lifting them around the vents and cracked crystal. Sometimes their bodies would catch sparks, becoming momentary points of blue-white in the darkness. They would not be beautiful in any gentle sense. They would be necessary sparks, brief and sharp, falling into the dust cycle when their bodies failed.

The blind crickets would rasp in the wider hollows, their folded limbs pressing against one another, their antennae reading heat and pressure in the dark. They would not sing. They would scrape. Their sound would be coarse and patient, a slow file dragged across the chamber’s already immense noise.

And I would be underneath all of it.

In the seam.

In the suture.

In the narrow line where the stone almost closes.

If you asked where I would find myself, I would say: in the geography of centimeters.

Near a salt shelf, pale and brittle, formed by sublimation.

Beneath a crystal thicket whose needles lean inward like unfinished thoughts.

Beside an iron-stained slide where dust moves in miniature avalanches.

Above the lower throat, but not in its fiercest heat.

In a crack where the wall has been stretched into ribbed folds by old gas pressure.

In a place so small that my entire world would be measured by the width of a mineral vein and the length of a pressure pulse.

And if you asked who I would be, I would say: not a face.

Not a voice.

Not a creature that looks back.

I would be a seam-born nerve.

A living trace.

A pale conductive hush in the stone’s hot body.

I would be one of the small paths through which the mountain’s stress becomes bearable.

I would not have a self in the open-air sense. There would be no solitude in that chamber, no margin, no empty place where an individual could stand apart and be named. My existence would be pressed into all the others. I would be who I am because the worm beside me is also aligned, because the film is feeding on the heat, because the beetles are tapping, because the flies are rising, because the crickets are folding into the hollows, because the dust is waiting to become wall again.

I would be plural even if only one segment of me were touched.

When the pulse came, I would not flee.

There would be nowhere to flee to.

I would brighten faintly along my seam as charge passed through me. The dust above me would lift. The stone would groan. A crystal would crackle somewhere in the dark. The chamber would fill with its own sound, enormous and patient, a roar made of grinding, ticking, rasping, whining, and deep blunt boom. Then the pressure would release. The boom would travel through basalt. Dust would slide down the iron-stained slopes with a hiss like dry rain. The flies would settle. The beetles would resume. The mites would continue grazing. The crickets would shift.

And I would still be there.

Carrying the last faint current.

Holding the seam open by being inside it.

Eventually I would dry, thin, and fail. My body would become part of the chamber’s dust. Mites would pass over the remains. Mineral powder would settle into my segments. Film would grow across what was once my skin. I would not be buried, because there is no burial here. I would not rot, because there is no wetness for rot. I would be incorporated. I would become the wall I had lived within. I would become part of the next pulse, the next accretion, the next slow thickening of the pocket.

That would not be death as a loss.

It would be death as deposition.

So if you found me in that world, you would not see me first.

You would feel me.

A faint current along the stone.

A trembling in the dust.

A sense that the crack beside your hand is not empty.

A pressure that has learned to move.

I would be in the seam.

I would be of the seam.

I would be the chamber’s thin, pale answer to its own stress: a living conductor, a mineral nerve, a small sealed body helping the mountain breathe without breaking.
