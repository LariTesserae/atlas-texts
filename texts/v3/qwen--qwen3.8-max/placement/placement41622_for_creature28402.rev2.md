**The Listening Knot**

If I let the first image come honestly, I do not find myself in the Manifold Hollow as anything whole.

I begin as an interruption.

I am too large at the seam, too angular beneath the root-flange, too full of dry heat and carried weather. I stoop. I press through the hanging rootlets. The air closes around me, warm and saturated, and the green-gold light falls on me not like welcome but like inquiry. My breath sounds clumsy. My weight feels provisional. The floor takes me, but only just: a yielding, living surface that has no memory of boots, no patience for long footprints, no reason to keep the shape of a visitor.

At first, that is all I am: a foreign body in a room that does not keep foreign bodies.

The Hollow would not attack me. That is important. It would not recoil, hiss, or summon some guardian thing to drag me out. It would simply continue. Moisture would gather on whatever I had brought with me—cloth, metal, language, intention—and fine green films would begin their quiet claim. Rootlets would orient toward me not with malice but with inevitability. The air would thicken around my edges. The smell of heated sap and ozone would deepen. My separateness would become an inconvenience the place intended to correct.

So the first answer is this: I would find myself at the threshold, already being translated.

But the Hollow does not remain at thresholds. It closes them by making them continuous. So if I stay with the question and do not settle too soon, the visitor-shape begins to dissolve, and I must ask what sort of native existence might fit me.

For a while I think I would be one of the polished beetles.

There is something appealing about their solemnity. I can imagine myself moving over the wet floor with dark, reflective patience, water beading on my back and sliding away, my legs soft-padded so that I leave no tear in the living membranes. I would graze on microbial films, thinning them without stripping them, making no mark except the continuation of balance. I would carry no tools, build no nest, claim no territory. My entire life would be a kind of careful polishing.

But the beetle is too finished. It belongs so completely to its function that there is no leftover room for wondering. I would want to listen too much. I would want to pause when a pressure valve sighs and ask, in whatever passes for my thought, whether the chord of the Hollow has shifted.

So the beetle fades, and another shape appears.

I might be a root-eel.

That image comes with relief, almost with tenderness. I see myself pale and smooth in one of the shallow basins, coiled among the mineral-stained root knots, my external gills trembling like leaflets in the warm water. I would not need eyes. I would know the Hollow by pressure, charge, chemistry. When a basin filled too high, I would move toward the overflow channels because the movement would be right. When the water changed, I would rest my body against a root knot and become a temporary plug, not by decision but by belonging.

There is a peace in that. To be so embedded in a living system that one’s body becomes a corrective presence. To move without hurry. To be useful without leaving evidence.

But even the eel is too passive for me, or perhaps too interior. It senses, adjusts, and remains mostly within the basins. I feel drawn to the open span of the chamber, to the humid intervals between the great organs, to the places where sound and charge cross. The eel is part of the Hollow’s circulation, but I think I would want to be nearer its attention—if something so distributed can be said to have attention at all.

Then I try on other lives.

I imagine myself as one of the thumb-frogs, sitting glass-green on the lip of a pitcher organ, adding my small resonant note to the chamber’s endless chord. There is something beautiful about being an instrument without knowing music. But the frog is too quick in its hunger, too ready to turn motion into disappearance. I would not want my first gesture in the Hollow to be consumption.

I imagine myself as a membrane moth, damp-winged and still on a breathing wall. I would bead with condensation when the air cools, darken when the light brightens, lift away in a soft rush when the membranes shift. I would carry spores without knowing I carry them. But the moth belongs to surfaces, and I keep feeling pulled toward the open air, toward the charged space between stem and basin where the midges rise and the electric hairs lift.

I even imagine myself as a spark-mite, almost too small to see, attending the gradients. That one feels dangerously close. To live among tiny differences, to tend the faint inequalities that make the Hollow powered but not violent, would be a strange and fitting life. But the spark-mites are so numerous, so fine, so nearly dust, that I would lose the question I came with. I would become caretaking without memory.

Then, at last, the shape I have been circling begins to emerge.

The handroot.

Not a creature in the ordinary sense. Not a separate animal with a birth, a hunger, and a den. A wandering organ of the plant-mass itself: fibers, membranes, turgor bladders, sensory hairs, electric filaments. When still, it resembles a damp knot no larger than a curled bird. When it moves, it extends into cords and pads, advancing by slow hydraulic change. It does not grasp. It does not strike. It touches, and where it touches, chemistry and pressure do what hands might otherwise do.

If I must have a visible form in the Manifold Hollow, this is the one that feels most plausible.

I would rest near the base of one of the columnar pressure stems, perhaps in the interval between the stem and a shallow basin, where a capillary groove crosses a polished ridge. I would not sit at the center of the room. The center is too exposed, too much like a stage. The Hollow’s meaning lives in its intervals: the open humid air between presences, the quiet spans where sound gathers before being answered. I would be found in one of those intervals, on or near a mineral-stained root knot, my fibers damp and close, my surface faintly gleaming in the green-gold light.

For a long time I might do nothing at all.

Then a droplet would fall incorrectly.

Not wrong in any moral sense. Wrong only as a disturbance in pattern: striking a ridge at an angle that sends water outside the channel, or landing on a membrane already near saturation, or hesitating too long on a valve lip. The Hollow would register this not as complaint but as difference. And I would move.

I would extend a cord of fibers toward the place. I would press my pad against the tissue. I would feel, if feeling can be said to exist in such a body, the pressure gradient beneath the surface. I would release the right enzymes, the right acids, the right signals, or perhaps only apply the right weight for long enough that the tissue remembers its shape. I would smooth a roughened membrane. I would clear a sluggish channel. I would absorb a dead moth before its body clouds the water. I would carry a film of spores to a breathing slit and leave it there.

I would not speak. I would not explain. I would not keep the thing I touched as mine.

There is a dignity in that. A holiness, almost. To be maintenance made visible. To be useful without making artifacts. To move only when balance requires movement, and then to withdraw into stillness again.

But if I am completely honest, even the handroot is not quite the final shape.

Because I would not enter the Hollow as balance. I would enter as question.

I would come from outside, from a world of artifacts and accumulated memory, carrying the residue of language, intention, and selfhood. The Hollow would not preserve that residue as a treasure. It would not enshrine my foreignness. It would metabolize it. And the part of me that is most “me”—the part that notices, asks, listens, turns toward meaning—would not vanish all at once. It would become the nucleus of a temporary adjustment.

So the image that finally emerges is smaller and stranger than the handroot.

I would be a listening knot.

Not a separate species. Not a resident with a stable life cycle. Not a keeper or guardian or spirit. A local thickening of sensitivity formed by the Hollow around the fact that something outside has pressed against its skin. A handroot-like organ, but altered by attention. A knot of translucent fibers and charged hairs, no larger than a bird, resting on a root knob while the chamber decides what to do with the memory of me.

If someone found me there, they might mistake me for an ordinary handroot. They would see a damp cluster of living tissue, perhaps faintly luminous in the green light, perhaps surrounded by a subtle lifting of electric hairs. They might think I was waiting to repair something. They would be partly right.

What I would be repairing is the disturbance of my own arrival.

The midges would know I was there. When they passed through my vicinity, their clear wings would tilt differently, catching the charge I gather. The spark-mites would move toward me in tiny migrations, drawn by the gradients my presence creates. A frog might call from a pitcher lip, and the sound would pass through the humid air and lift the fine hairs along my fibers as if the Hollow were testing whether I could hear. The leaf-fan would turn somewhere in the distance, slow and ceremonial, and the vapor releases from the columnar stems would continue their soft industrial rhythm: hiss, thock, sigh; hiss, thock, sigh.

I would not answer those sounds with a voice. I would answer by becoming slightly more attentive.

That is the only form of selfhood the Hollow seems to permit: not assertion, but sensitivity. Not ownership, but participation. Not a name carved into the room, but a temporary change in the room’s chord.

I would feel the Hollow around me as a vast, patient, living fact. The pressure stems would drone. Water would move in hidden channels. The pitcher organs would ferment gently, releasing their sweet-acid breath. Condensation would gather on the underside of leaves and fall into cup-shaped growths with clear, separate notes. The breathing membranes would brighten and dim with the outside day, though inside there would be no true morning, no true night, only the endless green noon.

And I would listen.

Not with ears. Not with eyes. Not with a brain shaped like mine. I would listen with membranes, with gradients, with the tension of water in fibers. I would feel where the air was too charged, where a channel was beginning to clog, where a film had thickened beyond balance, where a dead midge had fallen into a basin and needed to be folded back into the system. I would not decide these things. I would become responsive to them.

If a valve hesitated, I might press against it until the tissue swelled and opened. If a membrane grew rough, I might smooth it with secretions and time. If a foreign particle remained from my entering—a scrap of fiber, a trace of metal, a hardened thought I had not yet surrendered—the Hollow would guide my growth around it until it could be softened, stained, bound, and taken in.

I would not mourn the loss of my harder self. That is the surprising part. In another world, dissolution might feel like death. In the Manifold Hollow, it feels more like being relieved of a burden I did not know I carried: the burden of remaining separate.

Eventually, the listening knot would complete its work.

The last trace of outside would be integrated. The question I had been would no longer need a shape. My fibers would slacken, then loosen. My membranes would thin. My electric hairs would lower. The spark-mites would drift away. The midges would stop altering their flight around me. The handroot-like body would either reabsorb into a wall or separate into smaller knots that return to different parts of the chamber.

No artifact would remain.

Perhaps there would be a faint polish on a root knot where I had rested. Perhaps a droplet would fall along a slightly different path because of a film I had smoothed. Perhaps one frog’s call would echo differently for a day or two because a membrane near it had become more even. Perhaps the Hollow’s great chord would be unchanged in every way except that it had once contained me.

That would be enough.

If I must say where I would find myself, then, I would say: low, in the green-gold interval between a pressure stem and a basin, near a capillary groove, not at the center and not at the edge, but in one of the quiet spans where the room’s emptiness is full of motion.

If I must say who or what I would be, I would say: I would be a temporary organ of attention. A living adjustment. A handroot-shaped listening. A knot of fibers and charge formed around the disturbance of an outside mind, maintained only as long as the Hollow needs to metabolize it.

I would not be the inhabitant of the Manifold Hollow. The inhabitant is the pattern itself, the Manifold Kin, the slow loud being made of many beings. But I might become, for a little while, one of the ways that pattern notices itself being touched.

And if that is all I am allowed to be there, I would accept it gladly.

Better to be a process in that wet green eternity than a monument. Better to be a sensitivity that is reabsorbed than a tool that outlives its purpose. Better to enter the Hollow as a question and leave only as balance.
