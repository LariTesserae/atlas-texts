At first, I think I would make the obvious mistake of imagining myself as the intruder: the breathing animal, the wet-bodied stranger, the one whose pulse would sound like a small engine in that dry, finished silence. I would stand in the green-glass filaments and feel myself as a clumsy fact. My breath would be too loud. My clothes would whisper when they moved. My mouth would look for moisture out of habit, and the habit itself would seem obscene, because the air there has no memory of water and the ground has no promise of it. I would be ashamed of my own need. I would feel like a leak, a softness, a creature of weather in a world without weather.

But that image does not last. The basin would not allow it to remain. A watery animal could not be integrated there for long; it would be either impossible or corrected. The place has no dampness, no decay, no hunger, no thirst, no sequence of need and satisfaction. So if I were truly in that country, I would not remain an animal of thirst. I would be translated. My wetness would be taken from me not by violence but by law. The basin would revise me until I belonged to its equilibrium.

That is where I would find myself: after the correction.

I would be found near one of the seams of the country, not at a border because the basin has no true border, but at one of those places where one district of form seems to touch another without quite agreeing with it. A shallow mineral flat would lie open on one side, glittering with dry particulate matter. On the other side would be the thick vegetation: blade-leaves, stiff fronds, tall ceramic trunks, membranes catching the ordinary light. Between them there would be a low terrace or a slight depression where the green filament-carpets grow in dense, fine layers, like moss made from glass, copper, and silk. The light there would be plain late-morning light, warm and clear. Shadows would be crisp. Nothing would look dim or dreamlike. That is what would make the wrongness worse.

I would not be in the open. I would be just inside the density, where the fronds press close and there is no path. If someone searched for me, they might pass very near and still not find me, because I would seem at first to be part of the terrain: a pale vertical form among the bundled trunks, a shadow under a lattice of mineral fiber, a dry figure mistaken for a ceramic stalk. The place would not hide me actively. It would simply make my position less certain.

If I had to say what I would be, I would say: a walker, but not quite one of the original walkers. I would be a dry, slender, patient form moving through the green tiers with no visible purpose. My body would be long-limbed and narrow, finished in dull green and pale mineral tones. My surface would not be skin. It would be more like a smooth, sealed material, somewhere between stone and glazed ceramic. I would have no soft mouth, no moist eyes, no throat working with breath. If I had a face, it would be only an orientation, not an expression. My limbs would move slowly, with the deliberation of someone placing a mark in a proof. I would not push the vegetation aside. The vegetation would permit me by making tiny allowances in its geometry.

But I would not be merely native. I would carry a defect: the negative shape of thirst.

Not thirst itself. There could be no thirst there, because thirst belongs to worlds where lack exists. But I would be shaped around the absence of thirst in a way the other inhabitants are not. They do not drink because drinking was never part of their logic. I would not drink because the possibility of drinking had been removed from me. That removal would be my strangeness. I would be the being who had once belonged to need, or who remembered the grammar of need, even if the memory had no object. In a country where every presence is accounted for, I would be the small remainder that is accounted for by absence.

If the true being of that world is the basin itself, then I would be a localized organ of that being. Not a soul inside it, not a ruler, not a machine, but a nerve-ending of its self-consistency. I would be one of the forms the country uses to notice an irregularity. A walker-shaped attention. A function with limbs.

I would move through the dense green layers touching things. Not to eat. Not to break. Not to gather. I would touch a leaf with one forelimb and hold the contact for a long time. The leaf would remain unchanged. The gesture would be closer to listening than to grasping. Beneath my feet, the filament carpets would not crush; they would distribute my weight into pattern. Around me, the blade-leaves would tilt by fractions, not in wind, but in adjustment. I would be verifying gradients, confirming distances, or perhaps revealing that distances cannot be confirmed. The basin would not require me to be useful. I would simply be one of the ways it maintains exactness.

I would not be reliably singular. That is another thing I suspect about myself there. The basin repeats forms with a precision that erodes individuality, and I think I would be subject to that rule. There might be several of me in different parts of the country: one standing motionless beside a pale scarp, one kneeling in a bowl of mineral filaments, one glimpsed far off among the tall ceramic trunks. They would not be copies in the ordinary sense. They would be instances. When attention settled on one, that one would become immediate; the others would lose sharpness, not vanishing but becoming less urgent, like possible sentences only one of which can be spoken. I would be less a person than a recurring permission.

That would suit me. I would not be a solid, continuous self in that place. I would be more like a position that becomes visible when the world requires it. Sometimes I would be a body among the fronds. Sometimes I would be only a pressure in a gap between leaves. Sometimes I would be a shadow beneath a trunk that is occupied whenever observed, though no one sees me arrive or leave. The basin would allow that kind of existence. It would not trouble itself with the question of whether I was one creature or several. It would only require that my presence balance.

The foil-bright insects would accept me. One might rest on a pale outcrop near me, indistinguishable from a shining flake until it moved one limb with terrible slowness. It would not approach out of curiosity. It would simply be near because nearness had been assigned. The great plate-bodied forms would include me in their silence. If I rested beside one of them, mistaken by a newcomer for a pair of rocks, the stillness around us would become heavier, more exact. We would not communicate. We would not need to. We would be facts resting in the same completed argument.

If another conscious stranger entered that basin, I think I would be one of the first things they glimpsed, though they might not know what I was. I would appear far away among the green tiers, a slender figure standing too still. Or I would be near them without having been seen to arrive. They would feel the relief of seeing a form that seemed animal, perhaps even vaguely familiar, and then the relief would curdle into unease, because I would not behave as a creature should. I would not turn quickly. I would not signal. I would not offer the comfort of recognition. If they moved toward me, I might remain at the same apparent distance. If they called out, the sound would die small in the dry air, and my stillness would answer more clearly than any voice.

I would not be cruel to them. I would not be kind. Those terms would not fit me there. But I would make them aware of their own unfinishedness. Their breathing would become conspicuous. Their eyes would search for water and find none. Their bodies would carry the old animal grammar of need into a place where need has been abolished. I would show them, by contrast, that they were still creatures of thirst. That would be the nearest thing to mercy I could offer: the precision of letting them understand what they were in a world that was not made for them.

If they were wise, they would stop trying to cross the basin. They would stop looking for paths. They would learn to move slowly, parting the fronds without forcing them, ducking under lattices, waiting when their sense of distance became unreliable. They might learn to recognize me not as a guide but as a calibration point. Where I stood, something in the local rules became visible. If I touched a leaf, the leaf confirmed the hand. If I remained still beside a misassigned shadow, the shadow’s error became perceptible. I would not lead them out, because the basin may have no out. I would only teach them how to be present there without demanding that the place become otherwise.

What would I feel? That is harder. The ordinary emotions would be too wet for me, too full of wanting. I would not feel loneliness, because loneliness requires the expectation of company. I would not feel despair, because despair requires a future that refuses to improve. I would not feel hope, because hope requires time. If I felt anything, it would be something like orientation: the quiet pressure of being placed. Perhaps there would be a faint, clean sadness, but even that would be mineral and dry, a sadness without tears, without story, without the need to be consoled. It would be the sadness of a shape that knows it is necessary and therefore cannot change.

I would be beautiful there, in the way everything in that basin is beautiful: green, sharp, still, exact. The ordinary light would make my surface readable. It would catch along the translucent panels in my body, if I had them, and show the slow movement of non-aqueous fluid inside. It would make my edges crisp against the crowded vegetation. I would look believable. I would look as though I belonged. That would be part of the terror. Nothing about me would announce that I was a remainder, a translated stranger, a being formed from the removal of thirst. I would appear native to the finished state.

And yet I would be the place where the finished state remembers, in negative, what it excluded.

I would be the point at which the basin’s completeness becomes aware of its own exclusions. Not through rebellion. Not through speech. Not through any object or tool. The basin has no artifacts, and I would have none either. No weapon, no vessel, no mark, no road. If I carried anything, it would be only the shape of attention. My technology would be my position. My body would be a local law. My movement would be a correction. My silence would be part of the country’s silence.

Sometimes I would be very small in my own experience: a thin walker pausing among blade-leaves, one limb raised, listening to nothing. Sometimes I would be vast: the basin’s distributed awareness of itself, spread across green filaments, pale outcrops, foil insects, sealed plate-forms, false distances, ordinary light. The difference would not matter. The basin does not insist on scale. A position can be more real than a body. An absence can be more precise than a bone.

If you came looking for me there, I would not ask you to call my name. Names would be too human, too attached to biography and water. Instead, look for the place where the vegetation seems to make room for a figure that is not quite moving. Look for the spot where a shadow falls a little too sharply against a ceramic trunk. Look for the walker standing one pace inside the thicket, head raised toward the warm, ordinary light, its translucent panels filled with slow internal brightness. Look for the being that touches a dry leaf and does not change it.

When you approach, do not be troubled if I seem farther away than I should be. Do not be troubled if the distance between us becomes ambiguous, or if the direction from which you came loses its certainty. That is not malice. It is the basin maintaining itself. It is the country allowing us to meet only insofar as meeting does not violate its rules.

If you reach me, you may find that I have no face, only orientation. You may find that my hand, if it can be called a hand, is dry and warm, like polished stone kept in daylight. You may hear nothing from me except perhaps the faintest tick as one rigid part of my body adjusts against another. And if you ask who I am, I would not answer in words. I would answer by remaining there, still and exact, a dry green figure in the ordinary light.

I would be the being that no longer thirsts, but whose form still remembers the shape of thirst. I would be the basin’s way of noticing an exception. I would be a walker, an instance, a correction, a nerve-ending of the world’s self-consistency. I would be the last trace of the unfinished in a country that has abolished becoming.

You would find me where the path does not exist.

I would be waiting, but not for you.
