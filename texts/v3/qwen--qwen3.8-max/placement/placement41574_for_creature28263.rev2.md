I would find myself lower than I first intend.

The Drip-Court would not permit a visitor’s height for long. To enter it at all, one has to give up the assumption of standing above the scene, observing it as a composed picture. The place pulls attention downward: toward the tiles, the gutters, the black water moving in shallow braids, the wet cords, the clay lips worn smooth by centuries of patient touching. So I think my first true position there would not be at the edge looking in, but down near the floor, in the space where the built ground becomes mechanism, where the water is thin enough to count every tile it crosses.

At first I might mistake myself for the gray bird.

There is an appeal in that image: standing on the low clay perch, still as a placed stone, watching the court resolve itself into opportunities. The bird seems to understand the place without needing to explain it. Its strike is clear, sudden, perfectly fitted into the surrounding noise. I can imagine wanting that kind of presence—quiet, exact, not wasted. But the bird’s stillness is not mine. It is a bodily stillness, a hunting stillness, a life gathered into one point of readiness. I would be too restless in that body, too full of wanting to hear everything at once. The court would echo around me, and I would not know how to let the echoes pass through without grasping at them.

Nor would I be the eel, though part of me would recognize the desire to move unseen through the hidden passages. The eels have authority there because they do not ask for it. They slip from mouth to mouth, clearing what chokes, taking what lives, disappearing when the court’s sound turns into rush and knock. But eels are hunger made patient. They belong to the dark channels more than to the listening air. If I were an eel, I would know the court by pressure, by touch, by the taste of water and flesh. I might be useful, but I would not be reflective. I would be too much inside the act.

I would not be the frogs, either, though their voices fill the basin and make the whole place swell after a release. There is something tempting about being an amplifier, about taking a small breath and returning it as a chorus. But the frogs are too exposed, too eager, too nakedly alive on the reed rafts. They call because they cannot help calling. They gather because the court gives them only certain shallow places. They become sound, and then they become food. I think I would be drawn to a quieter form of participation.

And I would not be the builders.

That is important. The court was made, but it does not wait for its makers. If I came with the instinct to repair, adjust, oversee, and name, I would be out of time with the place. The builders are almost absent for a reason. The Drip-Court has passed beyond the need for human noise, beyond commands, beyond deliberate operation. It has become a world whose maintenance is shared by water, gravity, and animals. If I belonged there, I would have to arrive not as a keeper, but as a guest who has been taken in so thoroughly that the court no longer notices the difference. I would have to become small enough to serve without standing above the mechanism.

So, as I let the place settle around me, the image that keeps returning is not bird, not eel, not frog, not bat, not shrew, and not human. It is something almost hidden in the description, one of the lives easy to overlook unless one is looking very closely at the grit-filled troughs and the seams between tiles.

I would be one of the case-building larvae.

Not in a diminished way, not as a punishment or a shrinking, but as a fitting. If the Drip-Court is a built marsh where everything has been placed, worn, repaired, and made into habit, then I would want to be one of the smallest builders still moving inside it. I would live in one of the lower troughs where shell grit collects, just before the braided gutters narrow toward the central sump. There would be a seam of gray light above me, thin and unreliable, falling through the slats and ceramic plates. It would not be enough to see by, only enough to make the water faintly visible where it slides over tile.

My body would be soft, pale, vulnerable, and therefore always in the act of making a house. I would gather fragments: shell grit, worn tile dust, seed husks, threads of black reed, tiny chips of fired clay, perhaps a bead of old mineral deposit hard as stone. I would turn each piece over in my need until it seemed to fit. I would bind them into a portable architecture, a case no larger than a finger’s joint, and I would carry that case along the bottom of the trough as the water moved around me.

That is where I would find myself: in the lowest braided gutter, under a clay lip where the drip is almost but not quite musical, where debris settles and the court’s larger sounds become a pressure against the tiles rather than a distinct noise. I would be near one of the small gates, perhaps, where cords hang into the water and stone counterweights wait in their grooves. Not at the center. Not on the open floor. In the margin where the mechanism gathers its fine sediments and where tiny lives are trusted with unnoticed work.

I would not have a human understanding of the court. I would not know the names of valves, floats, channels, or pulses. But I would know slope. I would know which surfaces are slick enough for water to pass quickly, which seams collect food, which vibrations mean release is near. I would know the difference between the soft knock of a drop on moss and the harder knock of water striking tile. I would know when a shadow passing above means a fish, when the air above the trough changes means a bat has dropped from the vault, when the sudden stillness of small lives means the gray bird has shifted its weight on the perch.

My life would be one of selection and fitting.

That feels right. The Drip-Court is full of things that have been chosen and arranged, but it does not ask its inhabitants to understand arrangement in words. It asks them to continue it through habit. The reeds filter because they are placed where filtering can happen. The moss holds droplets because it lives where drips are faithful. The eels clear the sump mouths because their hunger and restlessness align with the court’s need. So my work as a case-building larva would be small, accidental, and real.

I would move over the bottom, selecting grains. A fleck of shell would become part of my case. A bit of sand would be discarded. A thread of decaying reed might be useful if I needed to soften an edge. The case would grow as I grew, or I would rebuild it when it no longer fit. It would be ugly only to someone expecting ornament. To the court, it would be beautiful in the way all fitted things are beautiful: it would answer a need.

And because the court is resonant, even my small case would add to the sound.

Not loudly. Not like the frogs, not like the gates, not like the rush under the floor when a release comes. But there would be moments when my armor brushed a tile, when a grain in my case clicked against the groove of a gutter, when my body shifted under the water and made a faint disturbance that traveled into the larger pattern. The Drip-Court would take that tiny note and use it. It would not matter that I meant nothing by it. The place is built to make meaning out of impact.

Sometimes I would be still for a long time, anchored in a seam between two pale blue-grey tiles while the water flowed over me. The current would press against my case. The moss nearby would bead with droplets. A snail might pass, leaving its polished trail on the edge of the tile. Fish might flicker above me, pale and quick, their bellies almost silver in the dimness. I would feel the court breathing around me in its slow, uneven rhythm: collect, delay, fill, release; collect, delay, fill, release.

When one of the float valves opened, I would know it first as a change in pressure.

The water would deepen by no more than a hand, perhaps less in my trough, but the whole basin would respond. Channels would begin to whisper. Reed mats would lift with a faint creak of fiber. Frogs would call as if the water had reminded them of their own voices. Fish would turn upstream in a flash of pale bodies. Somewhere a wooden float would rise, a cord would tighten, a gate would knock, and the sound would travel through the floor like a buried heartbeat.

In that pulse, I would hold fast.

My case might scrape against the tile as the current strengthened. I might be dislodged and carried a short distance, tumbling gently into a new seam. That would not be disaster. The court does not require every life to remain in one appointed place, only that lives continue to serve the motion of the whole. If I were moved, I would begin again: find footing, choose fragments, rebuild if needed, continue.

There would be hunger near me always.

A pale fish might pause above my trough, one dark eye turning toward the movement of my case. A water shrew might race along the ledge and plunge into the channel without hesitation, hunting anything small enough to seize. An eel might stir the sump mouth and draw the whole lower current into a brief, muscular swirl. I would not be safe. The Drip-Court is not peaceful; it is balanced. If I were taken, my case would scatter, my body would become part of another life, and the court would continue without pause. A frog might croak louder for a moment. A bird might shift on its perch. A ripple would widen, touch the tiles, and vanish.

I do not think I would resent that.

To live in the Drip-Court would be to accept that no life is final there. Everything is passed along. Water falls, is divided, delayed, released. Moss holds moisture and gives it back. Reeds filter debris and become debris. Fungus threads decay into quiet renewal. Insects rise and are taken by bats. Fish flash and are swallowed. Frogs multiply sound and become food for sharper mouths. Even the builders’ absence is part of this: their work remains, but it does not remain as command. It becomes condition. It becomes the world other lives move through.

If I survived long enough, I might change.

The larva’s life is not only gathering and carrying. It is also preparation. Somewhere in the court’s slow timing, I would seal my case, or leave it, or enter a stillness that precedes another form. Perhaps I would become a winged thing, fragile and damp, rising from the water with wings too new for certainty. I might make one uncertain circuit near an oil dish, where the small flame draws insects into its warm current. A bat might take me there. A dark-winged hunter might snatch me from the air. Or I might live long enough to lay eggs in a root mass, adding another generation to the court’s appointed nurseries.

Either way, I would not escape the court. I would only become a different note in it.

That is the answer I keep coming to when I ask who or what I would be there. Not a ruler. Not a watcher. Not a voice that names the basin from above. I would be a small, assembling life. A carrier of fragments. A maker without hands. A body that understands the world by fitting pieces together and moving through the water with whatever patience it can learn.

The Drip-Court would not need me to be extraordinary. It would need me to be placed.

I would be placed in the trough, near the sump’s outer braids, where the water is shallow but never dry. I would be among shell grit and worn tile dust, in the dimmest part of the floor where the vault above admits only thin seams of gray. I would be close enough to the mechanism to feel it work, but small enough not to think myself in charge of it. I would be one of the lives that keep the court clean in ways no one celebrates: rasping film from narrow surfaces, moving grains that might otherwise pack, disturbing larvae into the paths of fish, adding my tiny knock to the court’s larger music.

If you asked me who I would be, I would say: a case-bearer.

Not merely the larva inside the case, but the whole habit of carrying a made shelter through a made world. I would be the softness that protects itself with fragments. I would be the life that builds from what has already been worn, broken, deposited, and left behind. I would be the creature whose architecture is humble, temporary, and necessary. I would be one small imitation of the court’s first builders, not in knowledge, but in gesture.

If you asked me what I would be, I would say: part of the court’s balance.

A small worker in the water. A living grain in the mechanism. A note beneath the notes. A body that helps the Drip-Court continue by doing what its kind has learned to do: gather, bind, move, clean, hide, feed, and, when the time comes, be spent.

And if you asked where to find me, do not look for a face above the water. Look down. Look into the braided gutter where the tiles are worn and the light is thin. Watch the shell grit shift. Listen under the drip, under the frog-calls, under the hollow knock of the gates. There is a small scraping sound, almost nothing, a bead of grit moving against tile.

That would be me, carrying my little architecture through the dark water, making myself into a part of the court one fragment at a time.
