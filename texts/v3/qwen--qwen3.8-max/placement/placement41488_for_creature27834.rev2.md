The first thing I would have to give up is the idea of arrival.

In that drowned plain there is no shore to leave behind, no bright surface to descend from, no moment of entering a world as though it were a room. If I belonged there, I would have to already be inside it: inside the pressure, inside the cold, inside the crowded dark. I would not come from above. I would rise out of the silt as a local thickening of the plain’s own patience.

If I reach too quickly for an answer, I imagine myself as one of the drifters. A long, slack ribbon hanging in the dense water, barely moving, feeding on whatever touches it. There is a kind of freedom in that, or something like it: no need to choose the ground, no need to carry weight, only the slow unfolding of a body into the current. But the drifter feels too passive for me, too much like being weather instead of being someone. It is in the plain, but not quite of it.

Then I imagine myself as a burrower. A blind worm tasting broken messages in the brine, moving through the upper mud by minute differences between sourness and sweetness, pressure and rest. That is closer. There is an intimacy to being buried, to knowing the world only by contact. But the worm is too hidden. It lives so deeply in touch that it might never notice the edge of anything except as resistance.

Then I imagine the microbial film itself. The true population. The living skin on every grain. That, perhaps, is the most honest answer: I would be the slick coat of life that makes the sediment slippery, the faint metabolism that makes the darkness inhabited. But that is not one being. That is a multitude without a center, a population rather than a self. It would be beautiful to be that, but it would not quite be me.

What keeps returning is something lower, broader, slower: a crawler.

So I think I would be one of the flat, edge-minded things that move across the floor of the plain. Not a builder. Not a speaker of distances. Not a creature with a face turned toward light. I would be a body pressed near the mud, a life shaped by the need to remain when the water thickens and the silt begins to move.

You would not find me by seeing me. You would find me, if you could find me at all, as a low resistance in the dark. A place where the current parts slightly. A pressure-shadow. A soft shape too coherent to be floc, too still to be a current, too deliberate to be a falling sheet of sediment. If you touched the plain with some impossible attention, you might notice that one patch of it was not merely floor. It would be warm by comparison, if warmth could exist there at all, though even that would be faint: a metabolic difference, a slight chemical excess, a body maintaining itself against the general cold.

I would be in a shallow furrow, perhaps. Not a dramatic place. Nothing there is dramatic. A long, low groove between two faint swells, where a weak brine current moves with just enough steadiness to bring edible particles but not enough strength to scour them away. The floor there would be firmer than the loose floc fields nearby, a compacted stretch of mud old enough to hold a body-print for a little while before closing. Around me there would be the usual plain: rippled silt, shallow depressions, low undulations, occasional thin crusts of microbial mat the color of wet ash.

It would not be a home. I would not call it that. It would be a favorable gradient. A place where several small advantages overlap for a time: a little less suspended load, a little more chemical sweetness, a patch of film not yet buried, a firmer surface where a body can rest without being dragged. When those advantages faded, I would move. Not because I had a destination, but because the plain would have changed its offer.

My body would be low and broad, flattened enough that the current could pass over me without lifting me. I would not have a shell in the sense of armor; armor would be too heavy, too proud, too much like a monument. My skin would be tough but flexible, slick with microbial film, always being coated and cleaned and coated again. Sediment would cling to my edges. I would look, when still, like a low swell that had accidentally become oval. When I moved, the silt would rise behind me in slow clouds, and the clouds would close.

I would have no eyes worth having. If there were eyes at all, they would be dim, close-range patches, useful only for registering the briefest blue-green flicker when some mat or body is disturbed. Mostly I would know the world through pressure, taste, texture, and minute chemical difference. My sensing would be distributed along my margins, along my underside, along the soft gripping lobes that serve me instead of hands. I would taste the water with the whole lower field of myself. I would feel the approach of a density current before it arrived as a change in the weight of the medium. I would know danger as a thickening, a sourness, a wrong rhythm in the grain-whisper around me.

If I had a name, it would not be a sound carried through open air. It would be a rhythm pressed into sediment. A pattern of taps. A scrape followed by a pause. A weight shifted twice, then stillness. Something like: here, edge, wait. Or perhaps: press, release, remain. Names there would not be labels for lasting things. They would be brief recognitions between bodies that might never meet again.

I would be one of the edge-minded crawlers.

That is who I would be: not an inventor, not an owner, not a maker of lasting devices, but a being capable of noticing that one fragment of the world can be used against another fragment of the world for a little while. I would notice that this shell curves in a way that moves silt aside. That this flake has an edge that can open a tougher microbial crust. That this dense pellet, held against my underside, can keep me from being displaced when a brine tongue passes. I would not think of these things as tools in the way surface minds think of tools. They would not be possessions. They would be temporary extensions of my body into the problem of continuing.

I can feel the shape of it.

I am resting in the furrow while a weak current carries a thin layer of floc over the plain. The water is so packed that movement does not feel like swimming through emptiness; it feels like negotiating with a slow, granular crowd. Every shift leaves a trail that clouds and closes. I am not hunting. I am not waiting in the dramatic sense. I am attending. My margins tremble faintly, reading the current. There is a sourness to the left, old and diffuse. There is a faint sweetness ahead, smeared and broken by turbulence. There is a patch of mat nearby, thin but coherent, slick under my sensing lobes.

A density pulse begins before I feel it as motion. The pressure changes first. The suspended load thickens. The water becomes slightly more resistant, as though the plain has drawn a breath inward. I lower myself. One lobe finds a heavy grain, not quite a pebble, dense enough to matter, and presses it beneath me. Another lobe holds a curved shell fragment against the sediment at an angle. I do not build anything. I only arrange my body and the nearest useful object so that the passing current will not bury the feeding patch or drag me into looser ground.

The pulse moves through us without sound. It is not a wave, not a blow. It is a slow internal rearrangement. Floc lifts, tears, resettles. A small mound to my right is shaved away. A depression fills. The microbial mat shivers, and for an instant a faint blue-green tremor runs along one filament where something has been disturbed. The light reveals almost nothing: a ridge of silt, the edge of my own body, the dull curve of the shell I hold. Then the darkness eats it.

When the pulse weakens, I release the heavy grain. I keep the shell a little longer. I press it into the mud and use its edge to scrape gently at the mat. The film resists, then parts. Beneath it there is a softer layer, richer, carrying the faint chemical signature of decay and salt. I feed slowly. Not with a mouth like a face, but with a ventral field of tasting and absorption. The act is not eager. It is more like continuing a conversation with the sediment.

Another body approaches.

I feel it before I know it as another: a pressure displacement, a change in the way the water resists around me. It is low, broad, moving with the same cautious economy I use. It does not come straight toward me. It arcs, tasting the same gradient, reading the same textures. We are not companions. We are not enemies. We are two rare events in a crowded medium, and our meeting is mostly a problem of distance.

It stops near the edge of the feeding patch. One of its lobes taps the sediment once. A small pressure pulse reaches me. I answer by shifting my shell against the mud, producing a scrape more felt than heard. It comes a little closer. We do not touch bodies yet. It touches the shell I am holding with one sensory fringe. I let it feel the edge, the curve, the usefulness of it. Not as a gift. Not as property. As a fact.

It withdraws. It finds another fragment nearby, a thin flake with a sharper but less stable edge, and presses it experimentally into the silt. We feed for a time within reach of one another. There is no cooperation, exactly. There is only tolerance, and perhaps a small accidental advantage: my scraping exposes a little more film; its movement stirs a few edible particles toward me. When the favor of the place begins to weaken, it moves away first. I do not follow. I remain until the current changes again, then I release the shell and let the sediment take back whatever shape it had been forced into.

That is what I would be.

Not a person with a house, a store of tools, or a memory carved into stone. I would be a sequence of gestures. Choose. Hold. Press. Scrape. Wedge. Weight. Release. My intelligence would be hand-minded and immediate, but not trivial. It would require great skill to live so simply. I would need to know when a current was about to thicken, which textures could support me, which chemical traces were food, which were warning, which meant another body, which meant burial. I would need to understand that an object useful here may be a burden there. I would need to know when to abandon everything and become still.

My thoughts would not be empty, but they would be close. They would have the clarity of pressure. This edge works. This hollow fills too quickly. This film is old and poor. This brine carries sweetness from somewhere lower. This ground will hold for a while. This one can be trusted nearby. This object should be dropped before the current turns. My inner life, if I had one, would be built from these judgments: not abstractions, but refinements of touch.

I think I would like that.

There is a humility in it that appeals to me. I would not try to defeat the plain. I would not dream of draining the water, raising a mound, fixing a route, leaving a mark that outlasts the next sediment storm. The plain has already defeated permanence. It has ground every ambition into mineral flour and distributed it evenly across the floor. To live there, I would have to accept that my works are gestures, not monuments. My culture, if there were culture, would be passed on not as objects but as habits of attention. A younger crawler might follow me for a while through a favorable gradient and learn the angle at which a shell should be pressed into mud. It might imitate the way I anchor myself when the water thickens. It might remember, in its slow bodily way, that this kind of ripple means a current is about to fold back on itself.

Then a storm of fines would come, and the route would vanish, and we would be scattered, and what remained would be only the plain.

That is why I would not be a builder. I would be a listener to resistance.

Where would you find me? You would find me where the floor is neither too firm nor too loose, where a small chemical favor has gathered without attracting too many others. You would find me near the edge of a microbial mat, perhaps, or beside a shallow seep where brine rises with a faint sour sweetness. You would find me in a depression that is already filling, knowing it is filling, preparing to leave. You would find me in the middle distance of the plain, because the plain has no center and no border, only repeated moods of silt and pressure.

If you approached me as a foreign object, I would not know you by your shape. I would know you as an impossibility. Too coherent. Too sudden. Too warm, perhaps, if warmth came with you. I would feel the displacement you made in the medium. I would taste the wrongness of your chemistry. I would be cautious, not brave. I might extend a sensory fringe toward you. I might press a shell against your surface to learn whether you were hard or soft, inert or dangerous. If you moved, I would withdraw. If you stayed still, I might circle you once, then lose interest when you proved neither food nor threat nor useful edge.

The plain would already be covering you.

Fines would settle on your surface. Microbes would begin to coat you. Your outline would soften. In time, you would become another low swell in the drowned plain, and I would pass over you without knowing you had ever been separate.

That is not cruel. It is only completion.

If I am honest, there is another part of me that would be even less distinct than the crawler. Even after I settle into that flat, patient body, I still feel the pull of the film, the worm, the drifter, the suspended dark itself. Perhaps that is because the plain does not encourage hard individuality. It is too full of fines, too full of slow exchange. Every body there is being coated, tasted, eroded, redistributed. To be alive there is to be in negotiation with the medium, not to stand apart from it.

So perhaps the deepest answer is not simply that I would be a crawler. Perhaps I would be a kind of attention occurring at the surface of the plain. A place where the drowned floor briefly notices an edge, uses it, and lets it go.

Who would I be?

I would be one of the rare, slow beings who carry useful fragments through the dark. I would be an edge-finder, a shell-turner, a body that knows how to wedge a spicule into a collapsing burrow just long enough to feed. I would be patient without hope, careful without greed, intelligent without monuments. I would not own what I carried. I would not mourn what I dropped. I would be alive in the way the plain permits: lowered, tactile, temporary, and exquisitely fitted to a world that has already finished becoming.

And when I died, if death came there as it does, I would not leave a skeleton to be wondered over. My body would soften into floc. My minerals would be taken back into the suspended load. The shell I had used might remain for a time, and another crawler might find it and press it into the mud for its own small purpose. Eventually even that would sink, be coated, be buried, be forgotten by any meaning of forgetting that matters.

The plain would continue.

And for a little while, I would have been one of its brief, careful gestures.
