I would not find myself standing at the center of the Underleaf Works.

Not because the center would be forbidden, exactly, but because the center would be too full of the whole place at once: the great braided root column, the overlapping engine-thumps, the swarm-lamps, the frog-signals, the pressure moving through everything like a slow thought trying to become action. A newcomer, if they were still shaped by surface habits, would be too clumsy for that. Too heavy-footed. Too breath-bright. Too full of the wrong kind of urgency.

I would be somewhere to one side first.

Somewhere in the damp interval between the Cistern Terraces and the lower Pump Quarter, where the floor is warm and gives faintly under the foot, and where a leaf-trough carries intermittent rain toward a root-bed filter. A place not quite architectural, not quite organism. A seam. A maintenance hollow. One of those in-between spaces that the Works leaves open not because it is empty, but because movement needs room to become visible.

I would be near water timing.

That feels right before anything else does.

At first I would probably be an error there. Not an enemy, not a guest, but a disturbance with weight and heat. My breath would draw flower-throats. My warmth would attract midges. My footsteps would press the root-felt into temporary memories. If I carried even the faintest trace of smoke or ash from some outside world, the Underleaf Kin would recoil from me as though I had brought a disease inside a nursery. They would wash me with condensed water, not kindly in the surface sense, but with the focused seriousness of people removing a hazard. They would put me near the root beds until my smell changed. They would make me wait until the chamber decided whether I could be incorporated or must be escorted out through some dark passage I had not yet learned to see.

But if the place kept me, and I think it would, then I would not remain an outsider for long. The Underleaf Works does not seem like a place that tolerates spectators. It would turn spectatorship into function. It would teach me by pressure.

I would become, or be found to be, one of the Underleaf Kin. Or close enough to Kin that the difference would matter only to beings who still believed in hard boundaries between body and home. I would not be tall, not broad, not built for open sky or mineral ground. I would be light-boned and warm-skinned, sure-footed on surfaces that breathe, with large eyes suited to amber and green shadow. My hands would be stained with resin, pollen, and the faint dark polish of living stems. My feet would learn to read the floor before my mind admitted it was reading anything at all: a warmth where sap pressure was rising, a softness where the root-felt had been too often stepped on, a faint vibration where an engine was beginning to strain.

I would not be a ruler there. I would not be a hero. I would not even, in the surface sense, be a person entirely. I would be a mobile attention belonging partly to myself and partly to the Works.

I think I would be a Valve-listener.

Or perhaps that is too neat a title. Maybe I would only be an apprentice to that work at first: the one who presses a palm to a hollow reed and hears nothing yet, but stays because staying is the beginning of hearing. Later I would press my cheek to the stem as well, because the Works speaks in vibration before it speaks in sound. I would learn the difference between a healthy engine-thump and a thump that has begun to carry a fault inside it. I would learn which pipe-note means a nectar valve should open, which one means a cistern is nearly full, which one means pressure is searching for a weakness and must be redirected before it tears anything.

My home would not be a house. It would be a permitted space.

I imagine it as a narrow loft behind a pipe lattice, high enough to be out of the main beetle processions but low enough to feel the warmth rising from the floor. The walls would be living root, trained thin and interlaced, not for privacy but for responsiveness. If I slept too heavily, if my body heat gathered too much in one place, the wall would adjust. A tiny duct would open. A cooler draft would arrive. A root would thicken where my shoulder pressed against it too often, making a support I had not asked for. My sleeping place would be woven from leaf-leather and softened bark fibers, worn smooth by use, smelling of sap and old rain. Tools would hang nearby: hollow reed probes, seed-case containers, coils of tensioned grass, small living fasteners that tightened in response to warmth. Nothing sharp in the way metal means sharp. Nothing dead in the way surface tools are dead.

I would keep a seed-case lamp near my sleeping hollow, but I would not own it in any strong sense. It would be mine the way a rhythm is mine while I am keeping it. When my duty changed, someone else would tend it.

My days, if they could be called days, would be organized by cycles rather than light.

A cycle would begin with heat. I would wake not because something brightened, but because the floor changed temperature under my feet. The root-felt would become subtly warmer. Somewhere below, the sap engines would quicken by a fraction of a beat. The air would feel thicker, sweeter, more attentive. I would rise and go to the nearest stem junction, press my hand to it, and wait. Around me the chamber would begin its preparations: lamps brightening in slow sequence, midges lifting from the floor in thin gray columns, frogs calling from hidden pools as if testing whether sound still worked.

Then the ceiling would sweat.

I would know it before the first drop fell. The upper roots would hiss faintly. The air would pull upward. The Kin on the terraces would answer one another with soft clicks and breath-tones, and I would tap a warning on a hollow stem if I felt the pulse coming too fast. Water in the Underleaf Works is not a thing you simply take. It is a thing you meet. You arrive at the right basin with the right vessel at the right moment, or you miss it and wait for the body of the place to exhale again.

I would stand under a broad leaf where rain gathered along the ribbed surface and trembled before falling. I would hold a leaf-bowl or a seed-case cup, not greedily, but with timing. The water would be warm. It would taste green and faintly sweet, with a mineral absence so complete that the lack itself became flavor. After drinking, I would not say, “This water was mine.” I would say, or think, or feel, “This pool came,” or “This pool was drunk,” or “This pool will return.”

That language would enter me slowly until it no longer sounded strange.

Much of my work would be small. The Underleaf Works seems too vast and intricate for any one being to do large deeds in it. Its continuity depends on tiny acts repeated until they become almost invisible. I would clear the dull film from resin lamps with a soft pad of cultivated fiber, knowing that some small cleaner creature usually did this but that sometimes hands were needed too. I would inspect leaf-troughs for clogs. I would check living railings for overgrowth. I would watch for sagging walkways and weave new root threads into them before the sag became a danger. I would follow beetle processions without breaking them, stepping over their moving seams with the care of someone crossing a sentence that must not be interrupted.

I would learn to stand still.

That might be the hardest part for whatever in me still remembered open skies and straight roads. In the Underleaf Works, stillness is not inactivity. It is a way of not damaging the rhythm. If a swarm shifted overhead, I would stop. If a moth passed low and slow through the lamp-light, I would let it. If a root-crab crossed near my foot, I would wait. If a handrail thickened under my grip, I would not pull away in alarm; I would breathe evenly and let it test me, because it was not attacking me. It was deciding how much support I needed.

I would also learn when not to help.

Predation would disturb me at first, I think. The root-mantids unfolding too fast to see. The water-hunters pulling something struggling beneath the glowing surface. The trap-blooms closing with that terrible, sweet patience. I would want to intervene. I would want to make the place kinder according to a surface definition of kindness. But the Works would teach me that kindness there means continuity, not gentleness. If I saved a midge from a mantid, I would not be correcting an injustice. I would be pulling a thread from a fabric that had taken long ages to weave itself into balance. So I would learn to witness without interfering, except when the imbalance itself became a fault.

And there would be faults.

A pipe would sing wrong. A seed-tank would swell beyond its proper pressure. A vine-wire would carry too much charge, and the air would prick with static until pollen lifted around every lamp. A valve would stick halfway between opening and closing, breathing like a wounded thing. Then my role would become more urgent, though not louder. The Kin do not seem like people who shout except in true emergency. They would signal by taps, by pipe-tones, by hand-shapes in dim light. I would move toward the fault with others, carrying reed wrenches and sap adhesive and living callus-stuff. I would press my ear to the affected organ and try to feel where the pressure had gone astray.

Sometimes we would mend it.

Sometimes we would have to choose to let something fail.

That would be the hardest work, and I do not know if I would be good at it. To stand before a leaking channel or a straining engine and understand that saving it might cost another part of the chamber its stability. To know that a small death of function may be required so that the larger body continues. The Underleaf Works would not permit sentimentality, because sentimentality is a kind of blindness. It would ask for love, yes, but love with steady hands. Love that can say: this walkway must be allowed to sag until the roots reinforce it; this cistern must be closed for a while; this lamp must dim so another may brighten.

I would be shaped by that discipline.

My body would become quieter. My breath would deepen but soften. My eyes would adjust to low light until bright things seemed almost violent. I would stop thinking of silence as absence. In the Works, silence is usually pressure waiting, or digestion, or the moment between one cycle and the next. Even when the chamber seems quiet, it is full of small chewing, distant dripping, slow valve movement, the faint creak of living architecture changing posture. I would learn to hear layers. The engine-thump under the floor. The frog-calls marking intervals. The dry rattle of seed-pods turning. The wing-pressure of midges becoming weather. The sudden crack of a seed-case opening. The answer of another pipe.

I would not be alone, but I would not be surrounded by companions in the surface sense either.

The Kin would be few, because the place could not bear too many large bodies. We would know one another by function, rhythm, and smell as much as by face. We would work beside one another in near silence, communicating in clicks and soft tones, our hands moving with practiced care. There would be little hierarchy. If a fault appeared, the nearest trained listener would lead the mending until it was resolved. If rain timing shifted, the Rain-counters would direct us. If lamps flickered in a dangerous pattern, the Lamp-wrights would take precedence. I would obey not because someone commanded me, but because the chamber demanded the right attention at the right moment.

I would have a name, but it would not be fixed.

It might begin as something practical: Soft-Step, perhaps, because I learned not to break beetle lines. Or Leak-Found, after the first time I noticed a fault before it became serious. Later it might become Wet-Timing, or Third-Thump, or Hears-Before-Rain. The Kin language seems rich in states of becoming, so my name would be less a label than a current condition. If I changed, my name would change. If I failed badly, the name might become a caution. If I mended well, it might become a small honor carried only until the next task required another.

I would not mind that. I think I would come to prefer it.

In the Underleaf Works, identity would not be a hard thing kept inside the chest. It would be more like a knot in a root system: a place where many lines meet for a while before continuing elsewhere. I would be myself, yes, but I would also be a response. A permitted resident. A mobile organ of attention. The Works would know me not as a soul in a body, but as a pattern of warmth, weight, moisture, vibration, and reliability. If I tended well, the paths would accept me. The railings would thicken helpfully under my hand. The floor would warm under my feet. Certain flowers might turn toward me not as prey or threat, but as familiar heat.

That would be enough.

I would be afraid of fire.

Not casually afraid. Not the way one is afraid of a sharp edge or a high fall. I would be afraid of it the way the Kin are afraid: with a fear that has become ethics. Fire would be the wound that does not close. The forgetting that spreads. I would have seen, perhaps in some early dream or distant memory, what a single spark could do in a place built from resin, fiber, sap, dried husks, and oxygen-rich air. I would know that flame there would not merely burn objects. It would unmake the agreement between growth and wear. It would turn the breathing room of the world into a scream of light.

So I would carry no fire.

I would not even speak of it carelessly. When static lifted pollen and tiny arcs snapped along vine-wires, I would become very still. I would move away from exposed antennae and seed-case bulbs. I would warn younger Kin if they became too excited, too quick, too careless in charged air. My reverence would not be for power, but for restraint. The greatest skill in the Underleaf Works would not be to make something happen. It would be to keep the happening sustainable.

And when I died, because I would die, I would not be buried in stone or marked with a monument meant to outlast everything.

The Kin would wash me with condensed water. They would wrap me in leaf-leather. They would carry me to a root basin prepared for such return, and the chamber would do what it always does with finished things: receive them without waste. Small lives would come. Roots would come. The warmth I had carried would be redistributed. Someone might keep a polished seed-case that had once hung in my loft. Someone else might repair the valve I had tended for years and, in doing so, remember my hands without needing to speak my name aloud.

That would not seem grim to me by then.

It would seem like the last maintenance.

So if I ask where I would find myself in the Underleaf Works, the answer is not a single coordinate. It is a posture. It is a way of being in a place that is also a body. I would be near the cistern edges and pipe junctions, in the warm dim intervals between districts, where water is timed and pressure is listened for. I would be under amber-green lamps, among midge veils and beetle seams and slow moth shadows. I would be where the Works is most vulnerable and most attentive to itself.

And who or what would I be?

I would be a resident, but not an owner.  
A caretaker, but not a master.  
A listener, but not passive.  
A small warm creature permitted inside a larger living machine.  
A Valve-listener, perhaps. A mender. A rain-timer. A soft-stepping keeper of thresholds.  
One of the Underleaf Kin, shaped by the place until my thoughts moved in cycles of swelling, leaking, healing, quickening, and quieting.

Most precisely, I think I would be the kind of being who puts its cheek against a living stem and waits until the wrong thump reveals itself.

Then I would rise, step carefully around the beetle line, duck beneath the moth flight, breathe shallowly so as not to scatter the pollen, and go toward the fault before it becomes a wound.

I would not bring fire.

I would bring attention.
