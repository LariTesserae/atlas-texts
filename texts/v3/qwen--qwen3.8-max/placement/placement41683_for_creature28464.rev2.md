I would not find myself at the rim, though that is where every approach must begin.

The rim is only the place where the world below releases you: the last broken stair, the wind-clipped grass, the sudden drop into the basin’s loud quiet. If I arrived there as a traveler, I would stand on that edge for a while, too full of distance, too full of lowland speed, my breath still making a clumsy sound in the thin air. The hollow would open beneath me like a green bell, and at first I would think I was looking at emptiness.

But I would not stay there.

The basin would draw me down, not by invitation so much as by sound. Water would be calling from everywhere at once: the hiss of falls in their old grooves, the glassy sliding of shallow channels, the small hard taps of drips finding the same stones over and over. I would descend until the rim was only a memory above me, until I was inside the bowl’s weather, inside its cool wet room, where mist gathers on sleeves and moss swallows the idea of a footstep.

Then I would stop, because stopping would be necessary.

That is where I would find myself: not on the high edge, not in the open center where the pool lies like a dark eye, but in one of the in-between places where the basin’s life is thinnest and most active. Probably near the weir. Probably where the channels braid and the ground pretends to be solid. Probably beside a seam of wet stone and moss, close enough to the water to feel its decisions, far enough back not to disturb them.

At first I would be only a visitor there. I would be too warm, too hurried, too eager to understand. I would notice the cairns and think they were accidental. I would see the low bothy and assume it was abandoned. I would hear the birds and frogs and think the noise belonged to them alone. But if I remained long enough, if I let my own noise fall away, the place would begin to reveal its deeper inhabitants. And then, if the basin allowed me to become anything in it, I would not become a stranger.

I would become one of the Drip-Listeners.

Not the eldest. Not the one who remembers the pool’s levels across whole decades. Not the child learning to sit still through rain. I would be something more partial, more transitional: the one who has come from elsewhere and is still being taught by water. The one whose first work is listening after sound.

If the basin gave me a shape, it would make me small.

Not the smallness of fragility, but the compressed, weathered smallness of things that have learned not to catch the wind. My body would be low and round-shouldered, dense rather than heavy. My skin would take on the colors of the hollow: peat-brown, stone-gray, faint green in the folds where moisture lingered. It would not look like armor or fur. It would look like a surface that had been rained on for a long time and had begun to answer. Mist would settle on me as it settles on boulders. My hair would be dark and close, always seeming wet even when the air was clear. My eyes would grow large and quiet, black or deep amber, with that film the Drip-Listeners have when they are listening hard or standing in too much sun.

My hands would become the most important part of me.

They would broaden. The pads would thicken until they could tell, through a touch on rock, whether water was moving beneath it. I would learn to press one palm flat to the ground and feel not just cold stone but intention: where the water table was rising, where it hesitated, where it would break through next. My feet would become wide and careful, the toes long and practical, the soles tough enough for sharp stone but sensitive enough to trust moss only when moss deserved trust.

I would wear little: a wrap of dark hide or woven hair, a pouch with bone hooks and cord and a few sharp flakes, perhaps a hood patched with moss. I would not want many things. The basin would teach me that possessions become noise if they are too numerous. What I carried would need to be repairable, light, and leaveable. If I lost something to the water, I would let it go. If I found something useful, I would take it only after asking the silence whether it was being offered.

But the truest change would not be in my body. It would be in my attention.

I think I would become the kind of Drip-Listener who tends echoes.

That feels right. Not the one who mends the weir first, though I would learn to mend it. Not the one who leads strangers across the wet ground, though I might guide them in small ways. I would be the one who listens to what happens after a sound has been made.

In the basin, echo is not decoration. It is information.

A birdcall that returns too quickly might mean the cliffs are dry. A call that returns soft and delayed might mean mist is thickening along that wall. A stone knocked loose on the upper lip would speak differently depending on whether it fell into air, water, or snow. Even the small hollow note under an overhang could change when the central pool had risen, as if the whole cirque were a drum with water tightening its skin. I would learn those differences. I would sit still while they accumulated around me, and slowly I would begin to read them.

So if you asked where I would be, I would say: near the place where sound behaves strangely.

Probably beside the breathing pool, where the water rises and falls too quickly for the weather above. Probably at the bend in the weir where the branches have been forced into a new angle by seasonal flow. Probably in the shallow braid of channels where the ground is treacherous and every step must be chosen by pressure and memory. There would be a flat stone there, worn smooth, perhaps the second one that appears only when the pool subsides. That would be my place. Not a throne. Not a shrine. Just a stone where a listening body can sit without blocking the path of water.

In the early morning, before the birds had fully begun, I would wake and test the air. The first work would be to hear whether the night had changed anything. Had the seeps grown louder? Had the mist entered from the eastern rim? Had the snowfield creaked in its sleep? I would crawl from the low shelter of the bothy or from a dry seam under an overhang, and I would place my hand on the nearest rock. The stone would be cold. It would give me a small report.

If the basin was calm, I would make only minor adjustments.

I might clear a clot of moss from a channel groove. I might move one stone in a cairn, not enough to change its meaning, only enough to show that the path had shifted by a finger’s width. I might study the weir and decide whether a branch had been bent by water or by something larger passing through. If it had been bent by water, I would leave it for a while, because water’s damage is also water’s instruction. If it had been bent by a body, I would straighten it gently and then listen for the reason.

If the basin was restless, I would do less.

There would be days when the pool swelled without rain, when mist seemed to climb the cliffs in slow columns, when echoes came back wrong, softened or doubled or arriving before they should. On those days I would not try to correct anything quickly. I would sit near the water and wait. The Drip-Listeners know that some movements are not problems to be solved but weather passing through the mountain’s deeper body. I would place my palm on the stone and feel the breath beneath the pool. If the breath quickened, I would move back from the soft ground. If it slowed, I might approach the weir and see whether one small opening needed to be widened.

My work would be modest. That would be its discipline.

I would not build towers. I would not cut great channels. I would not try to make the basin legible to someone who had never stood in it. My tools would remain small because my understanding would remain partial. A wooden spade, fire-hardened. A bone hook. A cord twisted from hair. A pouch of sharp flakes. These would be enough. More than enough. They would be honest. They would let me act without pretending to command.

I think I would be especially attentive to thresholds.

The place where dry stone becomes wet moss. The place where a stream thins into a film. The place where mist becomes drip and drip becomes seep and seep becomes channel. The place where a stranger’s first footstep meets the basin’s surface. Those thresholds would concern me, because they are where the basin decides what it will accept.

If a person came up from the lower world, I would notice them before they saw me.

I would be near the weir or under the shadow of the cliffs, wrapped in gray-green, still enough to be mistaken for a boulder wearing a cloak of moss. I would listen to the way they entered. Some people arrive as if the landscape owes them passage. Their boots strike too hard. Their voices are too round. They look at the pool and immediately imagine using it. They see the cairns and assume someone made them for their convenience. If I heard that kind of arrival, I would vanish into the ordinary surface of the basin. The moss would hold my shape for a moment after I was gone. The water would continue. The stranger would see only emptiness and think it beautiful, or boring, or both.

But if they came quietly—if they paused on the rim, if their breathing slowed, if they did not immediately try to cross the wettest ground—I might reveal myself in a small way.

A pebble would tap once on firm stone where no pebble had been.

A reed would bend against the wind.

A pool would tremble without rain.

If they were attentive, that would be enough. They would understand that the basin was not empty. If they sat down, if they let their hands rest without grasping, I might come closer. Not fully. Not at first. I might appear as a low shape near the weir, a face like wet stone, eyes catching light like the surface of a pool. I would not speak to them in words they expected. I might make a soft click, or a whistle thin as a pipit’s call, or a vowel shaped like water slipping over cold rock. If they answered too loudly, I would wait until the echo scattered. If they answered with patience, I might mark a safe step for them with a thrown stone, or raise one hand from the mist and point toward the drier seam.

If they thanked me, I would not require the thanks.

The basin would take their gratitude and return it in pieces. That would be fitting. Gratitude here should not be a loud transaction. It should be like leaving a smooth stone in a place where it can catch a drip.

I would like to think that, over time, I would learn their way of naming.

Names would not be fixed words. They would be patterns of sound tied to the basin’s changing life. My name might begin as something simple: the second drip from an overhang after a long rain. Later it might become the hollow knock of a stone under the weir. Later still, it might be the pause between a bird’s wingbeat and its echo. I would not offer it casually. To hear it, someone would have to sit with me long enough for the basin to repeat it back altered.

Perhaps the only true name I would have there would be this: the one who listens after.

That seems to fit what I am, or what I would become. I am not the first sound. I am not the shout at the rim. I am not the hand that builds the first cairn. I am closer to the return. The delayed answer. The slight correction that comes after the world has already moved. In a place where echoes behave oddly and memory seems to answer before it is called, that role would feel natural. I would be the one who waits for the basin to finish speaking before deciding what to do.

There would be loneliness in it, perhaps, but not the loneliness of absence.

It would be the loneliness of being thinly spread. Some days I might seem to be in several places at once: a flicker near the pool, a shape under the overhang, a low figure pausing at the rim. That would not mean I had become a spirit. The basin does not need spirits to be strange. It only needs attention to become fine enough. I would move by intervals, using the moments between water sounds, between gusts, between birdcalls. I would have learned that visibility is not the same as presence. Sometimes the clearest way to be somewhere is to be almost overlooked.

In the warm wet months, I would live mostly outside.

I would sleep under the low bothy roof only when the cold pressed hard or the rain became too constant. The roof would be sod and hide, dark with wet, so low that I would enter on hands and knees. Inside, there would be a few dry things: a rolled mat, a pouch of bone hooks, a folded cloak, perhaps a smooth stone kept from an important season. Nothing more. The bothy would smell of peat smoke, damp wool, cured hide, and the green mineral scent of water moving under stone. It would feel less like a house and more like a hollow within a hollow.

In the colder months, I would slow.

I would not vanish, but I would become less visible. I would sink into the basin’s quieter rhythm. The insects would thin. The streams would sharpen with frost. The snowfields in the shaded seams would grow older and harder. I would listen more than I moved. If I went out, it would be to check the weir after a thaw or to clear a channel where ice had made a false dam. My hands would be cold and stained green, but they would know what to do. The cold would not feel like an enemy. It would feel like the mountain drawing its skin tighter.

When the first warm rain returned, I would feel it before I heard it.

The seeps would change pitch. The stone under my palm would loosen. The moss would begin to smell alive again. Frogs would call from the black margins, and the insects would rise in loose columns over the pools, and the birds would return to feed low over the wet ground. That would be one of the basin’s great pulses, and I would be part of it not by commanding it but by moving with it. I would repair what winter had bent. I would walk the safe seams again, testing each stone. I would listen for the first new name the season offered.

If I were asked what I believed in that world, I would not know how to answer in the usual way.

I would not believe in owning the pool. I would not believe in draining the wet ground to make it easier. I would not believe in taking more than what had been offered by accident or necessity. My belief would be practical and quiet: water decides; sound carries; attention must be paid; every repair is a negotiation. The basin would not ask for worship. It would ask for accuracy. It would ask that I not mistake my desire for the shape of the place.

That is why I would not be its priest, and certainly not its master.

I would be one of its listeners.

Maybe that is the important distinction. The basin is not possessed. It is inhabited by beings who have learned to be answered by it. If I became one of them, I would still be fallible. I would misread a stone. I would step wrong. I would startle a bird, cloud a pool, leave too deep a mark in the black soil. The basin would correct me. Water would rise where I had not expected it. A path would soften. An echo would come back false and tell me I had been careless. I would learn by being corrected.

There would be joy, too, but a quiet kind.

The joy of finding a dry seam after rain. The joy of hearing a waterfall change pitch and knowing why. The joy of watching a dipper flash along the stream and feeling, for a moment, that the whole basin was a single nervous system. The joy of placing one branch in the weir and hearing the water accept it. The joy of being unseen but not absent. The joy of leaving a place more balanced than I found it, without making my presence obvious.

If I had to choose one image of myself there, it would not be a face.

It would be hands.

Broad hands laid flat on wet stone. Fingers stained green to the joints. A cloak patched with moss and hide. A body low beside the breathing pool, waiting for the mountain to inhale. Not dramatic. Not monumental. Just attentive. Just willing to be small enough to hear.

And if you came looking for me in that world, you might not find me as a person at all.

You might only find the effects of listening.

A cairn slightly shifted. A channel cleared of moss. A weir mended in a shape that follows water rather than resisting it. A smooth stone placed where it could catch a drip. A path that becomes easier only if you stop hurrying. A sound that seems to answer you from too many directions. A feeling, after a while, that your own breath has become part of the basin’s weather.

If you sat quietly, you might see me at last near the pool: low, still, wrapped in gray-green, my face like a stone that remembers being warm. I would not rush toward you. I would not claim you. I would only let you know that you had been noticed. Then, if you were willing, I might show you the next safe step.

Not by speaking.

By listening after.
