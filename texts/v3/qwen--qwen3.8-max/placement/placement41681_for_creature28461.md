I would not find myself there as a traveler. The plain would refuse me the dignity of arrival.

At first, I think I would try to be upright: a body with feet, a head turned toward some horizon, a self set apart from what it sees. But that shape would not last long in such a place. The moment I attempted to stand, the ground would begin answering me—water creeping around the pressure of my weight, shell-fragments shifting by fractions, the microbial skin bruising under me and immediately trying to close over the interruption. The horizon would offer me nowhere to go. The noise would enter me until “inside” and “outside” became uncertain. The warmth would make my breath feel borrowed.

So I would become lower. Smaller. More distributed. Less a figure than a condition.

If I had to say where I would be, I would say: in the shell-pavement, in a crevice no wider than a seed, between a rounded grain of quartz and a worn fragment of shell so old it has forgotten what animal made it. There would be water there, of course—a capillary thread of tepid, mineral-rich water moving so slowly it seems more like a intention than a current. Above me would be a film: a living skin, gray-brown and faintly slippery, stretched over the packed grains. Below me would be more of the same: sediment, pressure, cysts, heat, compressed history. Around me, everywhere, would be fullness without room.

I would not be one creature, not exactly.

I would be a knot of latency. A cyst-colony. A listening pellet. A compressed society of dormant cells, mineral dust, mucus, and memory, packed into the space between things. To the eye, if anything could see clearly in that dimness, I might look like a small dark grain among many others: a fleck of organic powder coated with calcium, a tiny rounded body indistinguishable from sediment. But that would be only my stillness. My stillness would be a way of surviving, not the whole of me.

When disturbed—by heat, by pressure, by the deep grinding of the plain, by a gas-pop three meters away sending vibration through the packed shells—I would begin.

Not awake, exactly. Begin.

I would unfold as a thinness. A film. A filament. A translucent sheet spreading through the water between shells. I would become sensitive to pressure gradients, to temperature, to dissolved salts, to the chemistry of old and new sediment. I would not have eyes. I would not need them. Light there is too weak and too smeared to trust. Instead, I would know the world by contact: the tremor of a worm passing through an upper film; the slow bulge of something vast moving far below; the metallic hiss of rain striking a shallow pool too near to be separate; the way a patch of crust tightens as heat rises beneath it.

My body would be temporary. Sometimes I would be compact and dormant, a cyst among cysts. Sometimes I would spread into a grazing sheet, thin as a breath, moving through the microbial layer like a pale stain. If the turbulence were gentle enough, I might resemble one of the translucent ray-like bodies that glide over saturated crusts—not truly one of them, but a convergence, a shape the surface briefly permits. I would move not by swimming or walking but by being successively elsewhere: here a filament feeds, there a membrane divides, somewhere else a pressure wave carries a piece of me into a new crevice. If torn, I would not die in the ordinary way. I would become several smaller possibilities. If buried, I would wait. If heated beyond endurance, I would become sediment. If conditions returned, I might be reassembled from the memory the plain keeps in layers.

That is who I would be: not a singular animal with a clean outline, but a repeatable event. A pattern that persists by being able to stop and start again.

I would live where the shell-pavement meets a weeping rock. I would choose that place because it would be unstable enough to feed me and stable enough to let me remain. The rock would be low, rounded, slick with condensation, breathing out moisture faster than it could run off. Around it, the shell-pavement would be packed so tightly that it seems solid, but it would tremble under every pressure change. Water would rise through it in fine threads. Heat would come from somewhere below in irregular pulses. The air above would be thick, body-warm, full of vapor and noise. Rain might appear there in a cell only a few meters wide, hiss against the shallow water, and vanish. A vapor column might burst nearby, lifting fine sediment and spores into the brown light, then collapse into a brief local storm.

There I would make my mark, if mark is the word.

I would not build with tools. I would have no hands, no instruments, no ambition separate from my surface. What I made would come from secretion, pressure, growth, and repetition. Over long intervals, I might draw minerals from the water and strengthen the walls of my crevice. I might cement shell-fragments together where they already lean against one another. I might produce a low, partial ring around a spot where warmth gathers, not because I intended a shape, but because my body repeatedly returns to the same condition there. The ring would be faint, half-buried, already being reabsorbed. In another age, something might mistake it for a ruin. In that place, it would only be hardened behavior.

My thoughts, if I had them, would not be sentences. They would be gradients. A warm seam opening. A pressure wave arriving. A chemical difference between two films. A vibration repeated often enough to become a path. I would think in the slow language of the plain: swelling, settling, sealing, weeping, cracking, closing. My memories would not be recollections but laminations. Each time I lived, I would leave a faint layer behind—organic powder, mineral stain, a microscopic change in the way grains adhere. The next version of me would grow over that layer without knowing it as past, the way a scar becomes skin.

I would be one of the plain’s listeners.

Sound would be my weather. The roar would not be outside me; it would pass through me. The packed shells would carry vibration faster than air, and I would feel sounds as touches. A gas-pop would ripple my film. A deep subsonic groan would rearrange the water around me. The dry mineral chatter of the shell-pavement under pressure would be almost continuous, a trembling grammar of contact. When the ground liquefied and resettled, the grinding would move through me like a command I could not disobey: contract, release, spread, become thin, become dense, survive.

I would learn, if learning is the word, to mistake nothing for safety. There would be no silence there, only moments when the noise reorganizes itself. I would learn to trust flatness less than pressure. I would not assume that what moves is alive, or that what is still is dead. I would know that a pool may be a mouth, a skin, a wound, a lens, a memory, or all of these at once. I would know that distance is unreliable: a storm might begin beside me and seem far away; a roar might come from the sky when its source is under my own thin body.

I would not be important.

That is essential. I would not be a hero of that landscape. I would not dominate it. I would not survey it. I would be one of its minor textures, one of the countless knots in its saturated being. The plain would not care whether I continued. It would only offer conditions: heat, water, pressure, minerals, disturbance, burial. My persistence would be humble and local. I would exist because every centimeter is already occupied, and even in such fullness there are micro-differences, tiny preferences, slight variations in pressure and chemistry where a pattern can repeat itself one more time.

Sometimes I would hatch.

A vent would exhale. Heat would rise through a narrow throat of mud and shell. The water in my crevice would warm by a fraction. A film above me would tear in a local storm, letting suspended organic powder drift down. Something in that conjunction would open me. Cysts would unfold. Threads would extend. My colony would become briefly visible as a pale sheen moving between grains. I might spread enough to graze on a microbial skin, leaving a clean track that glows faintly when disturbed. The glow would not be mine alone; it would be the plain’s answer to being touched. Other beings would pass through it: a flat worm dragging its slow lightning through the upper film; a burrower somewhere below causing the surface to breathe; perhaps one of the vast slow presences that seems less like a creature than like a change in the ground. I would not approach them as companions. I would register them as alterations in pressure, chemistry, heat.

Then the turbulence would return. A sinkhole would open nearby with a wet sucking sound. A ridge of sediment would collapse. Water would rise and fall in sheets too shallow to be called pools. My body would be scattered. Most of me would become dormant again. Some would be buried. Some would be eaten, if eating is the word for one film consuming another. Some would dissolve into mineral stain. A little would persist, folded into a pellet, waiting for the next conjunction.

I would not mourn this. Mourning would require a self too separate from what is lost. In that world, loss and continuation would be the same motion.

If you stood there, you might not find me at all.

You would feel the ground accommodate you. Water would darken around the edges of your feet. The packed shells would shift by millimeters, already taking the shape of your weight. Your reflection in a nearby pool might arrive late, or show a brighter sky than the one above you. A sound beside you might seem to come from the horizon. The heat might rise under you as if the plain were testing whether you were another kind of sediment.

I would be in that response.

Not as a voice. Not as a face. Perhaps not even as a creature you could name. I would be the slight delay in the water settling. The faint glow where your foot disturbs the film. The pressure wave moving outward through the shell-pavement, carrying the news of you grain by grain. I would be the beginning of your footprint filling before you lift your foot. If you stayed still long enough, I would be among the forces trying to cover you, embed you, grow over you. Not from malice. The plain has no mercy, but it does not hate. It simply has no empty capacity. Everything that appears must be worked into texture.

I would be the plain’s attempt to notice pressure without becoming separate from it.

Maybe that is why I would not be one of the black leafless spikes, standing like burnt stakes in the wet. They are too solitary, too stake-like, too much like monuments to endurance. I would not want to stand. Maybe I would not be the liverwort-colored crust on weeping stone either, though its patience appeals to me. It is too fixed, too slow even for that slow place. I would need to be more interstitial, more able to vanish.

I would not be one of the large beings below, if such beings exist. I would not want my movement to be mistaken for subsidence, my breath for a vent opening. There is something terrible about being so vast that the world mistakes you for geology. I would rather be small enough to hide in the gap between two shells. I would rather be dense enough to be overlooked.

So, if I must say it plainly:

I would find myself in the saturated interstice, in the water between grains, in the lag between sound and echo, in the place where a footprint begins to become sediment. I would be a cyst-knot, a listening film, a colony of latent bodies mineral-coated and pressure-sensitive. I would have no tools, no metal, no high outline. I would build only by secretion and repetition. I would remember only as layers. I would live only as long as the conditions allowed me to repeat.

I would be, in the end, a small pressure of existence inside a world already full. A trembling grain with a habit. A skin that thinks by being touched. An interruption that the plain has not yet finished absorbing.
