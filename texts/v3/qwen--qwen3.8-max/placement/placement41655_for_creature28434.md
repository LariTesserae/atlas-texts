If I found myself in that world, I would not find myself standing in it.

That is the first thing the hollow makes clear. It has no room for a visitor with weight and hurry. No space for footsteps, no air for breath, no shadow large enough to belong to a body that moves by hunger or fear. If I were there at all, I would already have been translated down to its scale: lower than a body, slower than a gesture, quieter than appetite. I would have to become almost mineral.

At first I imagine myself near the rim, where the surrounding stone slopes gently down into the basin. That feels like a safe place to begin: still looking in, not yet committed. But the rim is too much like departure. It belongs to the outside brightness, to the world that weakens before entering. The hollow does not center itself at its border. Its life is inward, low, and close to the stone.

Then I imagine myself as a single bead of water, forming slowly beneath an overhang, swelling in the dimness until I fall into a shallow cup. But that is too brief, too much like an event. The hollow is not made of events. A drop may be important, but it is not a home. It arrives, spreads, and is taken in. It does not remain.

Then I imagine myself as moss: a small green tuft in a damp seam. That is closer. But even moss feels too separate, too much like a distinct organism with its own small outline. What lives in the hollow is more distributed than that. It is not one green thing here and one pale thing there. It is a communion of films, crusts, lichens, dampness, and stone. If I belonged there, I would not be a single creature among others. I would be a local thickness of the whole quiet surface.

So, more accurately, I would find myself on the inner side of one of the low rounded structures: a curved rim of crusted stone, no taller than a knee, worn blunt by time. It might resemble a broken bench, or the edge of a tiny enclosure, or the remains of a wall that never had a purpose except to have become itself. I would be on the shaded face of that rim, where the stone darkens a little earlier than the surrounding ground and stays damp a little longer.

There would be a shallow cup nearby, or a slight depression in the rock, where moisture gathers when condensation releases from a higher ledge. A bead would fall there now and then, not often. The sound would be small, almost nothing: a brief impact, a spreading ring, a soft brightening of the stone. I would be the part of the hollow that receives it.

If I had to take a visible form, I would be a green-black film on that curved rim. A living skin, thin and close to the mineral surface, darker where moisture lingered, greyer at the margins where warmth had gently dried me. Perhaps a pale lichen would lie at my drier edge, slow as a stain. Perhaps a few grains of moss would soften a crack beside me. But I would not be only one of those things. I would be the meeting of them: film, filament, crust, dampness, and the stone underneath.

I would be a patch of the hollow’s breathing.

Not the whole being that lives there, but one of its local concentrations. A small congregation of surface life. A place where the basin’s patience becomes slightly thicker. The living surface of the hollow would be the greater body; I would be one of its quiet intensifications. Not an organ, exactly, because there is no hierarchy of parts there. More like a syllable in a long, unhurried phrase. A spot where the hollow says, faintly, “here.”

My senses would not be senses as animals know them. I would not see the grey-brown twilight, but light would alter me. It would change the pace of my drying, the angle of my chemistry, the subtle rhythm of persistence. I would not hear the falling drop, but I would be changed by its arrival. I would know it as wetness spreading, as mineral taste, as a brief coolness before the stone returns to its stored warmth. I would feel the world as gradients: dampness and dryness, warmth and shadow, salt and silence.

My thoughts, if they could still be called thoughts, would be slow to the point of being indistinguishable from condition. A slight increase in moisture would be a kind of knowing. The drying of a nearby patch would be a kind of memory. The faint mineral tick of a crust settling under its own weight would not surprise me, because I would be part of the same long adjustment. I would not interpret the hollow. I would continue it.

I would not hunger.

That would be one of the deepest changes. There would be no reaching, no lack, no restless image of something needed elsewhere. I would not want the next drop as a desire. I would only be oriented toward it in the way a dark stain is oriented toward water: by being the kind of thing that receives it. If the drop came, I would absorb it. If it did not come, I would contract into patience. I would become less obvious without becoming absent. I would wait without the suffering of waiting.

The structures around me would not feel like buildings. They would feel like ancestry. The low rim I rested on would be partly alive and partly the mineralized memory of older life. It would be made of countless finished moments: films that once drank moisture, crusts that settled into permanence, edges that rose by fractions too small to notice. I would be the active margin of that accumulation. The living present on top of long centuries. I would not be separate from the structure; I would be its still-sensitive surface, its continuing breath.

In that way, I would live among the hollow’s accidental architecture without inhabiting it in any human sense. I would not sit on the bench-like curve. I would be the darkening on it. I would not kneel at the altar-like mound. I would be the faint sheen on its shaded side. I would not cross thresholds or recognize boundaries, but I would be part of the pattern that made them faintly visible. The hollow would seem arranged, and I would be one reason for that appearance, though I would intend nothing.

Time would not pass as a sequence of important changes. It would gather instead as texture. The same dampness would appear, remain, and recede. The same dim light would strengthen and weaken within a narrow range. The same small sounds would occur and be absorbed. A bead would form. A bead would fall. The stone would darken. The stone would dry. I would pale slightly, then deepen again. That would be enough.

If some part of me dried too long, I would not die in any sudden way. I would go dormant. I would become a faint film, a nearly invisible crust, a patient trace waiting for the next return of moisture. If some part of me cracked or loosened, it would remain nearby. A fragment might settle into another shallow cup. A spore might drift no farther than the next seam. Nothing in me would need to travel far. The hollow’s life is conversational, local, almost whispered. I would be one of those whispers.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness would require an expectation of company, and the hollow does not teach expectation. I would not be sad, because sadness would require the memory of something lost, and here nothing is lost in that way. The place has not been emptied; it has been refined. Its emptiness is not absence but spacing. The bare stone between living patches would be part of me too, in the way silence is part of music. It would give me room to remain what I am.

If I had a “self” there, it would not be centralized. It would be distributed across gradients and continuities. I would be more alive where the moisture gathered, more mineral where the stone stayed warm and dry. I would not be a point looking outward. I would be a surface maintaining itself. If one part of me were touched by dampness, that would be enough for the whole local pattern to shift. If another part faded, the rest would continue without grief.

I think I would prefer to be near one of the damper corners, but not in the deepest dampness. Too much permanence would erase even the faintest edge of becoming. I would want to be where wetness meets dryness: at the margin, the seam, the living boundary. There would still be a little uncertainty there. Not danger, not drama, but the smallest possible openness: whether the next bead will reach this stone or that one, whether the film will darken now or a little later. Even in a finished world, such slightness matters. It is the only future the hollow needs.

So if you ask where I would find myself, it would be here: on the shaded inner curve of a low, rounded stone rim, near a shallow cup where condensation sometimes lands. Not in the open center. Not on the outer rim. Not in the brightest wet places, where life is most obvious, and not in the driest stretches, where it withdraws entirely. I would be in between. At the edge of the damp. At the active margin.

And if you ask who or what I would be, I would say: I would be a patch of the hollow’s living surface. A green-black film. A crust-edge. A small congregation of moss, lichen, microbial skin, and mineral patience. A distributed awareness with no face and no hunger. A capillary mind. A slow darkening when moisture arrives. A stillness that is not inert.

I would be one of the place’s small acts of remaining.

If I could be named in a single image, it would be this: a thin living seam on old stone, just wide enough to hold the memory of water, just dark enough to show that the hollow is not empty. Not a creature. Not a spirit. Not a builder. Just the breathing finish of the basin in one local place. Just enough life to receive the next drop, and then continue.
