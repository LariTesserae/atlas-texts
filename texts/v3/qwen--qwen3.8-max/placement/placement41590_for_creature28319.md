At first, if I answer honestly, I am not one creature. The place refuses the clean outline of a single figure. It is too thick, too busy, too full of overlapping hungers and small architectures for that. So several possible selves rise up in me before one begins to hold.

For a little while I imagine I would be one of the pale grubs, moving in the slow river across the clay banks. That would be a kind of peace: no separate will, only the long muscular patience of feeding, moving, depositing, becoming part of the ground itself. I would eat fungal threads and fine wet sediment. I would help make the floor by passing through it. When the condensation rain began, I would turn with thousands of others, and our restlessness would reshape channels. But that self is too much a current and not enough a witness. It belongs too completely to the basin.

Then I imagine myself as one of the long-legged predators on the underside of a high ledge. That is another temptation: to be aloof, patient, feeling the air for tremors, dropping with terrible accuracy into the press below. There is a cold elegance to that life. It would let me stand above the crowding, if only slightly. But it is still too simple. It is hunger made precise, not curiosity. It would not build a cup just to see whether a trickle could be persuaded to fill it.

Then a third image appears, and it stays.

I would be one of the makers.

Not a city-builder. Not a king of the hollows. Not some bright, triumphant intelligence rising above the swarm. I would be small, cautious, practical, and constantly interrupted. I would be a creature of the margins, the ledges, the half-sheltered alcoves, the places where water can be caught but not controlled. I would live in the seam between geology and animal pressure, where a little ingenuity can mean the difference between a dry store of fungus and a flooded ruin.

If you wanted to find me, you would not look in the center of the largest basin. You would look where the terrain pinches between two low terraces, perhaps near a tilted slab of ceiling that lets ordinary daylight fall in a pale, dusty slant. Not directly under that light shaft — those places are too crowded, too loud with wings and hunger — but just to one side of it, under a lip of mineral-crusted stone where a cold draft meets warmer air and the ceiling beads with moisture.

There would be a hollow there. A scrape. A place no bigger than a curled body, or perhaps two if another of my kind had recently passed through. The floor of that scrape would be lined with clay that had been worked, pressed, and hardened near a warm vent until it became something like crude ceramic. Around its lower edge there would be a low weir made of packed marl, shell fragments, and bits of shed carapace, arranged not beautifully but functionally, to guide a trickle into a shallow catch-basin. The basin would be small. Its value would be immense.

My shelter would be more windbreak than house. It would be stretched over a frame of curved bone and stiffened chitin, covered with cured fungal mats that had been dried, pressed, and rubbed until they became a pale, leather-like skin. The whole structure would be tied down with sinew and weighted with stones, because the basin is restless and nothing stays put for long. It would smell of musk, wet chalk, old smokeless heat, and the faint copper tang that hangs near mineral water. It would not look impressive. It might already be half-reclaimed: a colony of mites in one corner, a fungal fuzz creeping along the lower seam, a beetle larva working the edge where a drop of moisture keeps landing.

I would let that happen. In that world, trying to keep everything clean and separate would be a kind of madness. You learn which intrusions are dangerous, which are useful, and which are only the basin breathing into your belongings.

As for what I would be — I think I would be humanoid only in the sense of having hands. Otherwise, I would be shaped by the place, not by any memory of the surface.

My body would be low and dense, built for pressing through crowded spaces rather than standing tall in open air. I would have a broad chest, warm-blooded but not overheated, able to tolerate close, damp heat for long periods. My skin would be hairless, thick, and slightly felted with a grayish bloom that helps retain moisture and resist abrasion. It would be mottled with mineral dust and faint stains from fungal spores, so that when I stayed still against a wall I would almost disappear into it.

My face would be blunt, not delicate. The air there is full of spores, dust, and fine particulate movement, so my nostrils would be slit-like and closable, set low on either side of a short muzzle. My eyes would be hooded, adapted to pale shafts of daylight and the gray diffusion under broken ceilings, but not large or romantic. They would be practical eyes, good at noticing motion, moisture stains, and the first tremor before a collapse. I would not have fine human ears; the world is too loud in the wrong ways. Instead, I would feel much of it through the ground, through pressure changes in the throat, through small hairs along my forearms and shoulders.

My hands would be the true event.

They would be long-fingered, strong, and more sensitive than they looked. The fingertips would have tough pads, but the nails would be broad and hardened, useful for scraping, prying, and pressing clay. One edge of each nail might be worn into a tool of its own by constant use. My thumbs would lock well, giving me the ability to grip a flint flake or tie a sinew knot with real control. I would also have a clever mouth — strong, flexible, able to carry objects, soften sinew, test the mineral content of clay, and perhaps make clicking or throaty calls when visibility was poor.

I would wear very little, because clothing that stays wet too long becomes a burden. A wrap of fungal leather across one shoulder, perhaps, and a pouch made from a creature’s crop, hung against the hip. In that pouch: a flint flake with a worn edge, a bone awl, a small needle carved from a spine, a lump of dried fungal tinder, a few polished shell fragments, and maybe a smooth pebble that had once been used as a hammer and had become too familiar to discard. Tools in that world would not be precious in the way metal tools are precious. They would be extensions of the body, lost, replaced, stolen by flood or scavenger, remembered only by the marks they left.

I would also carry a cup.

This is important. In a landscape where water is the closest thing to currency, a cup is not a domestic object. It is a future. It is the difference between drinking at a crowded pool where predators may be waiting and taking a small stored measure from your own catch-basin while the rest of the basin is still unsettled. My cup might be a hollowed piece of shell, or a fired clay bowl no larger than two palms pressed together. It would be chipped, repaired, wrapped in sinew where it cracked. I would know its weight better than I knew my own name, if I had a name at all.

My days would be organized around moisture.

Before a condensation rain, I would feel it before it fully arrived: the draft from a high fissure, a change in the smell of the air, a subtle quickening in the fungal mats, the way winged insects rise or fall in the light shafts. I would move quickly then. I would clear the small channels leading to my basin. I would pull fungal strips under cover. I would secure racks of drying membrane. I would wedge a shell fragment into a weak spot in the weir. I would check the clay-lined scrape for cracks. If I had eggs or stored grubs in a cooler hollow, I would raise them onto a ledge.

Then the ceiling would begin to drip.

At first it would be single drops, striking stone with separate, spaced sounds. Then the patter would thicken. Then the washing hush would come, and the whole basin would change its mind. I would draw back under my windbreak, not because I was afraid of water itself, but because water makes everything else move. The floor becomes unreliable. Burrowers surface. Pool creatures panic or spawn. Scavengers follow new currents. A ledge can fail. A channel can cut sideways in minutes. In such a place, the safest posture is often stillness, attention, and the willingness to abandon what cannot be saved.

I would sit with my knees drawn up, listening to the rain that had no clouds behind it. I would watch my catch-basin fill. If the flow was right, I would let it run through a first channel that carried away silt before diverting the cleaner water into the smaller cup. If it was too strong, I would break a section of the weir with a stone and let the surge pass elsewhere. The work would be immediate, physical, and never finished. A weir is not a machine. It is a negotiation.

After the rain stopped, the real labor would begin.
