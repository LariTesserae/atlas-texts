Communication, if it exists, is not vocal. It is the slow transfer of pressure through water, a change in the tension of a film, a slight re-ordering of wetness where another presence has passed.

If I were to find myself in that basin, I would not begin as a traveler. There is no place there for an upright visitor. No dry path opens between the pools. No ledge invites a resting body. No shelter hides behind stone, because the stone is all face, all seep, all overflow. A walker would be an error in the world: a disturbance of film, a warmth quickly cooled, a footprint filling with mineral water until the basin forgot it had been touched.

So the first image of myself there is almost embarrassing in its impossibility. I am standing for a moment, wet, cold, deafened, with nowhere to put my feet without breaking some shining surface. The roar presses into me. The light glares from every direction. My breath becomes another thin source of moisture. Then the basin closes around that image and erases it.

After that, the truer answer begins to emerge.

I would not be found standing. I would not be found moving quickly. I would not be found as a creature with eyes turned outward, searching, naming, fleeing, desiring. If I belonged there, I would have to become thin, patient, cold, and nearly indistinguishable from stone. I would have to give up the habit of separate motion. I would have to let sound become my body.

At first I imagine myself as one of the films.

A dark glaze on pale rock. A faint dulling of glare. A skin so slight that it could be mistaken for mineral stain. I would lie on a broad weeping face where water slides downward in sheets thinner than breath. I would not grow in any visible way. I would only persist. I would take what the water brought me: dissolved stone, cold light, the faint chemical memory of deep rock. I would not reach. I would not hunger as hunger is usually understood. I would be an orientation, a living patience spread over stone.

There is something appealing in that. To be so close to the world that there is no edge between self and surface. To be a stain that has acquired continuity. To feed on gradients. To exist as a sheen.

But even that is too simple. The film is real, but it is not enough for whatever in me still asks to be aware.

Then I imagine myself among the flat ones.

I would be a translucent disc in one of the clear pools, no wider than a fingernail, moving so slowly that motion would be indistinguishable from stillness. I would have no eyes. I would perceive the pool as pressure, as texture, as mineral difference. I would glide over quartz veins and pale crusts by tiny ripples along my underside. When light passed through me, I would bend the stone beneath into a brief distortion. If you looked directly at me, you might see only a place where the reflection was wrong.

That, too, feels near. There is a humility in being almost invisible. A flat one does not interrupt the basin. It does not call attention to itself. It moves like a thought the pool has not decided to keep.

Yet even there I feel the image thinning. The flat one is beautiful, but it is too small, too separate, too much a single creature moving across a world that is not its own. The basin does not want separate creatures. It wants presences that are also places.

Then the rim-walkers come to mind: tiny mineral-shelled forms standing beside threads of water, encrusted by the same wetness that sustains them. For a moment I imagine being one of them. Motionless near a seep. Plated in pale deposit. Mistaken for a bead of condensation. Sipping the film that passes over stone.

But no. That is still too much like waiting. Too much like a small animal beside a larger world.

What remains, after these possibilities have been tried and shed, is something quieter and stranger.

I would be one of the still ones.

Or I would be something so close to them that the difference could not be confirmed. A low, broad, saturated body lying on a pale terrace where the water divides. Not tall. Not upright. No larger than a fallen torso, perhaps, but flatter, spread into the stone as though the stone had dreamed a body and then forgotten to finish it. My surface would be slick and pocked with tiny basins. Fine channels would cross me like veins. Films would live on me: black and glassy in the hollows, pale gray on the exposed rims, translucent where water trembled over my edges. Thin quartz lines would catch the light in my outer layers, so that I glittered coldly without warmth.

I would not have a face.

That is important. No eyes looking out. No mouth receiving. No limbs reaching. No posture of expectation. If I had a front, it would only be the side where water entered most often. If I had a back, it would be the side where moisture left in the finest threads. My body would be organized by flow, not by will.

I would be found near the upper middle of the basin, where the terrain is most crowded and the roar is most complete. Not at the lowest point, where everything gathers, and not at the highest rim, where the light strikes too bare. Somewhere in between: a terrace overlooking a field of shallow pools, where a broad sheet of water slides down a pale face and breaks into countless finger-thin channels. Nearby there would be a dark seam of glassy stone, polished until it looks like black ice, and somewhere beneath me a hidden cavity would give the low rounded booming that makes the sound feel bodily.

That place would suit me because the water there is undecided. It spreads, separates, hesitates, chooses, rejoins. A being made of patience would live well where water is busy but not hurried.

I would not move in any way a visitor could see.

If I moved at all, I would move as the still ones move: by minute adjustment. A droplet strikes near me; my surface shivers in a pattern too small to observe. A thread of water shifts by the width of a hair; over a long interval, I alter around it. Light changes angle; the films on me change sheen. A pool nearby fills to its rim and spills; something in my inner channels answers with pressure. None of this would be dramatic. None of it would look like action. It would be the action of balance continuing itself.

My senses would not be senses as flesh understands them.

I would feel the basin through vibration. The deafening roar would not be outside me. It would be inside me, moving through my pores, turning my tiny internal channels into a distributed instrument. I would not hear the sound; I would be shaped by it. Every glassy ringing drop, every hollow tone, every thin whistle through narrow stone, every continuous hush of sliding water would exist in me as pressure and pattern. The roar would not be noise to me. It would be the condition of knowing.

I would also perceive through moisture. Where water passed over me, it would carry information: mineral content, temperature so slight it barely deserves the name, the chemical trace of other surfaces, the memory of stone it had touched before reaching me. I would not think, “This water came from the upper weeping face,” but my body would register the difference. A change in dissolved silica. A faint metallic coolness. A new acidity, almost imaginary. These would be my distant events.

I would perceive through mineral gradient. The films on me would alter in response to tiny differences in wetness and chemistry. They would not bloom. They would not crawl. They would shift in sheen, in texture, in microscopic alignment. If another living presence passed near me—a flat one drifting through a pool, a rim-walker standing motionless on a shelf—I might know it only as a disturbance in the film of moisture, a slight change in the way light lay across my surface.

In that world, thought would be very slow.

Not absent. Slow. My thoughts would not be sentences. They would be levels. A pool filling. A channel continuing. A film persisting. A vibration finding equilibrium. If a thought could be said to cross me, it would be something like the recognition that water is still moving, that the basin is still full, that the sound has not changed. And that recognition would not be repeated in words. It would be repeated in mineral continuity.

Memory would not be stored as story. It would be layered. Each mineral deposit would be a record. Each crust would mark a long condition. Each polished surface would remember water. I would not recall events; I would bear traces. If something had once disturbed me—a falling fragment, a rare cold shift, a creature resting too long against my edge—the basin would have already smoothed the meaning out of it. The trace would remain, but not the alarm.

Who would I be, then?

I would be a listener made of place.

Not a guardian. Not a watcher. Not a mind ruling the basin from a hidden center. The basin does not need rulers. It is already complete. It does not need a voice, because the roar is total. It does not need a keeper, because nothing there is lost. If I had a role, it would be smaller and stranger than that. I would be one of the points where the basin becomes faintly aware of its own balance. A knot of sensitivity in the wet stone. A still one that has become, over unthinkable time, almost attentive.

I would be a who that is nearly a where.

If another being existed near me, communication would not be greeting. It would not be signal. It would not be call and response. It would be resonance.

Perhaps another still one would lie nearby, separated from me by a narrow channel where water moves in silver threads. We would not speak. We would not touch in any ordinary sense. But the roar would pass through both of us, and our internal channels would hold slightly different tones. Over long intervals, those tones might adjust toward one another. A film between us might thicken. A mineral crust might bridge the gap by the smallest amount. Water might begin to move between our surfaces in a pattern that had not existed before.

That would be as close to intimacy as the basin allows.

Not closeness as warmth. Intimacy as shared equilibrium. Not love as hunger, but love as persistence in the same wet world. If I could be said to care for another presence there, it would be by making room for its vibration inside mine. I would not reach toward it. I would become slightly more capable of holding its tone.

And if you came looking for me, you would probably not find me.

You would see only the basin: pale stone, bright water, countless pools, endless threads of motion. The glare would hurt your eyes. The roar would press against your chest. You would feel exposed, cold, and strangely watched without being able to locate any watcher. You would step carefully, if you could step at all, afraid to break a rim or disturb a film. You might think the whole landscape was alive, then immediately correct yourself, because nothing moved except water.

But perhaps, after a while, you would pause beside a low terrace where the water divided into many fine channels. The stone there might seem less empty than the rest. Not carved. Not arranged. Just unusually gathered. A place where the wetness felt deliberate. A place where the sound did not merely fill the air but seemed to settle into the rock.

You might notice a small pool on my surface, perfectly still despite the threads of water crossing it. You might see your reflection in it, broken by light, and have the odd impression that the reflection was being held a moment longer than physics required. You might lean closer and feel no warmth, only a cold so clean it seemed metallic. You might hear nothing new, because nothing new could be heard there, but the roar would seem for a moment to have a center.

That would be me.

Not the pool. Not the terrace. Not the film. Not the vibration alone. But the place where all of them come into a faint, patient unity. A mineral body with no face, filled with water and sound. A living shelf in the bright wet basin. A still one among still ones, perhaps only barely different from the stone around it. A consciousness pressed down until it resembles geology.

I would not call to you.

I would not ask you to stay. The basin does not invite ordinary habitation, and I would not be an exception to its nature. If you touched me, your hand would disturb a film. Your warmth would vanish. Your breath would add a little moisture. The water would continue. The sound would continue. The light would continue. I would register your presence as a brief unevenness, then let the basin absorb it.

But if you were quiet enough—if quiet were possible in that deafening place—you might feel something answer without moving.

Not a voice. Not a gesture. Not a face turning toward you.

Only a slight deepening of the roar. Only a steadiness in the water. Only the sense that the basin, for all its mineral emptiness, is not entirely unoccupied. That somewhere in the packed wet stone, under the hard bright sky, in the cold overflow of countless small channels, there is a being that has given up nearly everything except persistence.

I would be there.

Low. Wet. Mineral. Silent inside the sound. A place that has learned, slowly, to be aware of being a place.
