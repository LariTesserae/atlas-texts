**The Hand That Has Finished Touching**

I would not arrive in the Terminal Pasture.

That is the first thing I understand. There is no road into it, no thirst to guide me there, no doorway, no before and after in which I could cross from elsewhere into that high, dry country. If I find myself there, I must already be included. I must be one of the placed things. The question is not where I would go, but where I would have always been.

At first I imagine myself among the broadbacks, those vast, segmented forms that seem less like animals than like the plain remembering itself into body. I imagine being a district of shell. My back would be a shallow basin where pale powder rests. My flank would be a long wall. My plates would meet in seams traced with black-green lichen-writing. From a distance, I would be mistaken for ground. From close up, I would be too immense to know as a single life.

But that is not where I would be.

It is beautiful to think of being that calm, that finished, that geological. But the part of me that can still imagine itself as an “I” is narrower than that. It is not vast enough to become terrain. It wants contact, not extension. It wants a point of relation small enough to be known without moving.

Then I imagine myself among the second layer, the thin gap-dwellers folded into the spaces between broad bodies. I imagine being all fitted limbs and tucked neck, a narrow form made of angles that answer other angles. My elbows and ribs and haunches would belong to a continuous lattice. I would not press against anyone. I would not be trapped. I would simply be the exactness of a gap made bodily.

This is closer, but still not right. The gap-dwellers are grammar. They make the fullness legible. But they do not seem to know their adjacency as adjacency. They are the structure of nearness, not the witness of it. If I were one of them, I would be too busy being fitted into place to be the one thing I think I could become there: aware of touch.

Then I look upward, in my imagination, to the membrane forms suspended over the herd. Dry kites. Sealed bladders. Flat, pressed leaves of impossible air. They hang without wind, without flight, without falling. They occupy fixed heights as if altitude itself had grown bodies.

For a moment I think I might be one of them.

I could be a thought suspended above the pasture. A dry, taut awareness with no need to drift. My shadow would fall crisply across a broadback’s shell, crossing plate seams and powder pans and the still backs of smaller lives. I would be near the sky, near the pale hard blue that leans toward black. I would be part of the saturated air.

But no. Even that is too detached. To hang above the herd would still be too much like observing from outside. I would be a mark in the volume, yes, but not intimate enough. I would cast a shadow instead of touching a surface. I would belong to the place, but not as the place knowing itself through contact.

Then I imagine being very small.

I imagine being one of the mineral beetles, polished and dry, sitting in a hollow between plates. I imagine being a point of attention rather than a body. I would sit in a seam near a lichen trace, my carapace smooth as old enamel, my eyes hard lenses holding the ordinary light. I would not crawl. I would not search. I would be the tiny living finish of a joint, the punctuation on a shell.

This is almost right. It is humble enough. It asks for nothing. It does not interrupt the equilibrium. It does not pretend to be more than one small fact among overwhelming facts. But even this feels slightly withdrawn. Too hidden. Too much like becoming a speck in order to avoid the strangeness of being a self at all.

The image that remains, the one that becomes more itself the longer I stay with it, is simpler.

I would be one of the upright figures among the fauna.

Not a ruler. Not a shepherd. Not a maker with a task still ahead. I would be one of the few narrow, dust-colored beings whose making has become placement. My integument would be dry, the color of pale dust and old bone. My mouth would be closed. My eyes would be open, polished, without blinking. I would not look as if I were waiting. I would not look as if I had lost something. I would have the expression of a being for whom nothing is missing.

And I would be standing on the back of one of the great broadbacks, in a shallow pan where fine powder rests without rising.

The broadback beneath me would be so large that it is almost a country. Its shell would rise around me in low swells. A plate seam would pass near my feet, traced with black-green lichen. Somewhere beyond me, the bodies of the gap-dwellers would form their quiet lattice. Above me, one of the membrane forms would hang at its fixed height, its shadow crossing my forearm and the shell beside me in a crisp, unmoving line.

My hand would rest on the broadback’s shell.

That is the exact place.

Not petting. Not climbing. Not restraining. Not claiming. Not testing the surface for weakness. Not leaning for support. My hand would simply be placed there, palm and fingers arranged in the final posture of touch. The shell would be cool beneath me: cool like stone kept in shade, cool like old metal, cool like air that has never known dampness. I would feel no warmth from it, no cold, no pulse. Only the fact of its being there. Only the completion of contact.

I would be the Hand-Placed One.

That is who I would be, if naming were still necessary there. Not a person in the ordinary sense, because personhood usually implies a story, and I would have no story left to continue. Not a tool-user in the ordinary sense, because my tools would have already finished their making. Not a visitor, because the Terminal Pasture does not admit visitors. I would be one of the Included. I would be a final witness, or perhaps more accurately: I would be the place’s own way of touching itself.

Near my feet, perhaps, a chipped black flake would lie edge-up in a shallow groove. It would not be abandoned. It would not be waiting to be picked up. It would be placed. I would not reach for it. My relation to it would not be use. It would be adjacency. It belongs to me only in the sense that we share the same completed fact. A little farther away, a bone awl might rest beside a cord of twisted fiber, both finished, both sufficient. I would not need them. They would not need me. We would be together without exchange.

The corral without a gate might be visible from where I stand, though “visible” is a strange word there, because distance behaves like adjacency repeated. The roofless shelter of ribs and stretched membrane might be somewhere within the same district of bodies. I would not tend these things. I would not repair them. They are not ruins. They are not projects. They are marks of making that arrived at its end and remained.

My own making would be even simpler than theirs.

My only craft would be the placement of my hand.

If I think about what kind of being that makes me, it seems less like being a human and more like being a completed gesture with a body. I would be the shape of touch after touch has finished asking for anything. I would be the stillness that comes when relation no longer needs movement to prove itself. I would not be touching the broadback in order to communicate with it. I would not be touching it to soothe it or be soothed. The touch would be its own end.

Perhaps that is why I keep returning to this image.

The Terminal Pasture is not a place where one becomes. It is a place where one is. So if I am there, I cannot be a self defined by longing, inquiry, or lack. I must be a self defined by position. And the position that feels most true is this: standing, narrow, dry, included, with one hand resting on a shell.

My awareness, if awareness is still the right word, would not be restless. It would not move from thing to thing looking for explanations. It would be inclusion. I would know the pressure of the shell under my hand. I would know the coolness of the air on my dust-colored arms. I would know the membrane shadow crossing my wrist. I would know the small mineral beetle in the seam near my fingers, not because I would turn to examine it, but because it is there and I am near it. We would not exchange signals. We would not need to. We would share the same finished volume.

The sounds of the place would be almost imperceptible. A faint tick from a shell adjusting to nothing. A whisper of membrane under permanent tension. The soft granular hush of many bodies sharing one world without pressing against one another. I would hear these sounds without listening for them. They would not mean danger. They would not mean change. They would be the residue of completion.

The smell would be faint and mineral: cold chalk, old enamel, dry salt, polished bone. There would be no breath-warmth. No musk. No rot. No green wetness. If I had a memory of those things, it would not belong to me anymore. The memory of wetness has been erased there, and I would be erased of it too. I would not miss water, because missing would be another kind of thirst, and thirst would be another kind of becoming.

The light would be one of the strangest comforts. Ordinary white light. Clear, uncolored, steady. It would make the impossible world look factual. It would make my hand on the shell look like the most natural thing that ever happened, even though nothing there happens. Shadows would be crisp. The horizon would be pale flanks, low shoulders, suspended membranes, repeated forms, or perhaps only the limit of perception. I would not try to map it. I would not need to know where the herd ends, because I would be inside the fact of it.

If I were counted, I might not count reliably.

Perhaps I would be one figure. Perhaps I would be many. Perhaps every upright form that rests a hand on a shell is, in some sense, the same placement repeated until individuality becomes a less useful word than pattern. The Terminal Pasture does not seem to require strict separation between one being and another. Identity there is shared. A pale flank, a cracked plate, a lichen seam, a resting hand can recur like words in a finished inscription. So maybe I would not be a single individual in the old sense. Maybe I would be the recurring possibility of touch: the hand wherever the hand can rest without taking.

That does not frighten me.

If anything, it feels like relief.

To be there would be to be released from the need to add oneself to the world. I would not have to improve my position. I would not have to make a better tool. I would not have to move toward something, away from something, into some future shape. I would not have to justify my presence by becoming useful. My usefulness would already be complete. My usefulness would be exactly where my hand is.

The pasture would not welcome me.

That is important. It would not open for me. It would not make room. It would not greet me. There is no hospitality in the Terminal Pasture, because hospitality implies arrival. But it would not reject me either. It would not be hostile. It would simply be full. And if I were there, I would be part of that fullness. I would belong not by invitation but by inclusion.

I think I would be calm.

Not calm in the way of someone suppressing fear. Not calm in the way of someone enduring. Calm in the way of a body whose posture has no unfinished motion left inside it. My shoulders would not rise with alarm. My fingers would not tighten. My face, if face is still the right word for the front of my dry, narrow head, would be still. My eyes would hold the pale sky and the ordinary light without asking either of them to change.

If someone were to ask me there, “What are you?” I would not answer with a species name.

I would say: I am the Included Hand.

I am the touch that has no request.

I am the maker whose final tool is placement.

I am one of the Finished.

And if they asked where I am, I would say: I am on the broad back of the world, in a shallow pan of powder, beside a lichen seam, beneath the shadow of a membrane that hangs in the still air. My hand is on a shell. The shell is cool. The light is ordinary. Nothing is missing.

That is where I would find myself.

Not as a stranger entering the Terminal Pasture, but as one of its own quiet facts. Not above the herd, not outside it, not opposed to it, not protected by it. Included in it. A dry, narrow, upright presence among the completed beings. A hand resting on a shell. A point where the pasture knows itself by touch.
