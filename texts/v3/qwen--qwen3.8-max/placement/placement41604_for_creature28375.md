I would be found at a seam.

Not in the lowest engine heat, where the air becomes so thick it seems to lean against the lungs, and not in the upper ventilation galleries where the condensation sails hang in the dark like broad black leaves. Somewhere between: in the middle tiers, where stone is still sharp, where repair has begun but has not yet thickened into permanence, where a wall is not only a wall but a problem being slowly solved. I would be in a narrow cleft between a warm engine housing and a trained root system, in a place that might look like a gap to an outsider but would in fact be a dwelling, a workstation, a listening post, and a small reservoir all at once.

The more I try to imagine myself there, the less I appear as a traveler or a distinct protagonist. I do not think I would be someone passing through. I would be part of the settlement’s maintenance of itself. I would be one of the Wick-born, perhaps, or counted among the Dripward by those who live near the middle tiers, but my particular life would gather around the youngest and most unsettled part of the hollow: the fresh fracture, the raw stone, the places where the earth has only recently opened and has not yet decided what it will become.

I would be a listener at the seam.

At first I picture myself as simply human, or as human as anyone is there: compact, heat-shaped, barefoot, with a body that has learned to move sideways. My hands would be narrow but strong, the palms marked by old calluses from rope, wire, root, and the smooth worn heads of bolts. My skin would carry the dull shine of constant humidity, faintly stained at the fingers and wrists with green-black plant sap, with copper salts, with mineral dust that no amount of careful washing fully removes. My hair would be bound tight with waxed thread and thin copper wire, not beautifully but practically, because loose hair is a liability near moving parts and hungry vines. Small objects might be worked into it without much ceremony: a seed capsule, a bead of fired clay, a tiny ceramic fuse.

My eyes would be large and patient, adjusted to half-light. I would not have the sharp, sweeping gaze of someone accustomed to horizons. I would have the near-sight of a person who reads surfaces: the tremor in a thread of water, the color change in moss, the faint darkening of metal where heat has begun to do harm. I would blink slowly. I would look at things by letting them come into focus in the dimness rather than by forcing them to be seen.

My clothing would be built out of thrift and thirst. A fitted inner layer lined with capillary thread, drawing sweat away from the body and into small sealed pouches at the hip or chest. Over that, a vest of pressed fungal cloth, soft and tough from years of use. A hooded mantle of salvaged fabric, waxed in places, patched so often that its original material is only a rumor. Nothing flowing. Nothing that catches on a valve or brushes carelessly against a lamp. Everything close, warm, deliberate. I would smell of moss, hot stone, old oil, sulfur in the faintest trace, and the sweet dark smell of leaves crowded together in low light.

But as I keep imagining, the image changes. I become less a person with a role and more a kind of attention given body.

I would be the one who listens to the wound.

The hollow is young. Its stone has not softened. It still remembers the violence of its opening. That means there are people there who must attend not only to machines and plants and water, but to the rock itself. Not in a mystical sense, or not only in one. In a practical sense: listening for stress, for settling, for the tiny acoustic warnings that come before a crack widens. Feeling warmth move through a wall where it should not. Watching how condensation gathers along a fracture, because water will always reveal the secret paths. Knowing which seams can bear a bracket, which ledges can take a walkway, which overhangs must be left unburdened even though every inch of space is desired.

That is where I would be.

My home would not be large. It would not even be large by the standards of the hollow, where space is already a knot. It would be a vertical alcove in the wall, perhaps originally nothing more than a recess between two sheared planes of basalt. Over time it would have been made usable: a sleeping mat suspended from iron pins and living root anchors, a shelf of scavenged metal, a ceramic basin no wider than two cupped hands, a rack of tools wrapped in cloth to keep them from clinking. A lamp in a wire cage, burning low amber, its filament so tired it seems to breathe rather than shine. Beside it, a tray of dark algae kept alive partly for light and partly for the way it responds to changes in air quality.

I would not own the floor in any simple sense. My rights would be stranger and more specific than that. The right to hang my bed from a certain beam. The right to collect the first run of condensation from a particular copper channel when the wall beads after a heat cycle. The right to use the warmth rising from a duct that passes beneath my alcove between certain hours. The right to keep a listening wire anchored in a crack three levels up and another two levels down. The right to prune a certain vine when it grows too near a fracture line. The right to be undisturbed during the quietest part of the rest shift, when the engines slow and the settlement holds itself still enough for the stone to be heard.

My work would be quiet, repetitive, and necessary.

I would begin before the lamps brighten, if brighten is even the right word for what happens when the settlement shifts from one shade of darkness to another. I would wake to the sound of a drop striking a leaf, or to the change in pitch of an engine somewhere below, or to the almost imperceptible feeling that the air has thickened with moisture. I would not rise quickly. Nothing in that place is done with a sudden unfolding of the body. I would sit up slowly, let my feet find the warm metal grating, and listen.

Then I would check the threads.

Thin wicks would run along the wall near my alcove, some of them mine, some belonging to the household below. I would adjust them by touch, guiding beads of water toward collection vessels, loosening a fiber that has become too tight, clearing a place where mineral crust has begun to block the flow. A single drop is not much, but in the hollow a single drop is a fact. It has weight, direction, ownership, consequence. If it falls wrongly, it may evaporate uselessly. If it gathers wrongly, it may find a crack. If it finds a crack, it may widen it over years. If it widens it, a shelf may loosen. If a shelf loosens, a bracket may fail. If a bracket fails, a pipe may shift. If a pipe shifts, heat may go where heat should not go. Everything is connected by smallness.

I would spend part of each cycle listening to the rock.

I imagine this plainly, without romance: kneeling on a folded mat, pressing the flat of my hand to a fresh surface of stone, then later pressing my cheek to it because the face can feel what the hand has learned to ignore. The stone would be warm. Not hot in the way of an oven, but warm in the way of a body that is not entirely still. I would listen for clicks, for faint shifts, for the deep almost imaginary sound of pressure redistributing itself. I would not be looking for drama. I would be looking for changes so slight they could be mistaken for memory.

Sometimes I would use tools for this. A copper feeler set into a seam. A stretched wire that amplifies movement by a fraction. A ceramic cup filled with water whose surface trembles when something below is restless. A patch of moss whose drying pattern reveals a change in heat flow. Nothing grand. Nothing that pretends to master the stone. Only instruments of patience.

When I found trouble, I would not shout. I would make a sign.

Perhaps two fingers drawn slowly downward near a lamp: fracture risk. Perhaps a thumb pressed to the inside of the wrist: moisture in a place it should not be. Perhaps a soft tap on a cloth-wrapped rail, repeated at intervals, enough to call the right people without striking the air sharply. The response would come slowly and competently. A root-tender would arrive with coils of trained vine. A gutter-keeper would redirect a drip line. An engine-sitter would reduce load on a nearby machine. Someone would bring iron pins, or resin, or a slurry of mineral dust and binding sap. We would work in low voices, or in silence, because the stone is young and does not need our urgency added to its tension.

I would also tend the plants that hold the place together.

Not the lush obvious gardens of the upper tiers, but the smaller, tougher growths that live near raw stone: mosses that seal micro-cracks, vines with roots strong enough to bind loose shards, low fungal mats that stabilize dust before it becomes dangerous. I would know which roots are welcome and which are ambitious. There are roots in the hollow that save structures, and roots that destroy them slowly by finding warmth and following it into places no moving part should be entered. My hands would often smell of crushed leaves and damp iron. My nails would be dark. I would carry small cutters, waxed cord, and a pouch of living moss kept moist against my chest.

In that world, I think I would be less interested in command than in attendance.

I would not be a leader in the open-sky sense. I would not stand above others or speak with a voice that carries. If I had authority, it would be the authority of someone who knows a particular set of dangers intimately. People would come to me when they wanted to hang something new from a wall, when they wanted to anchor a platform, when they noticed a strange damp patch or heard a sound they could not name. They would ask in low words, and I would answer with care, because in the hollow certainty is not a loud thing. I would say, “This seam can bear a light load,” or, “Not there,” or, “Wait until the next cooling cycle and listen again.”

There would be a name for me, but it would not be used often or loudly.

Maybe the people nearby would call me Seamward. Or the Listener. Or Soft-pin, because of the way I secure things without striking them. Maybe children would know me as the one who puts an ear to the wall, and they would imitate me in play until they learned that the imitation is also training. My name might be less a spoken word than a gesture: a hand laid flat against the air, then moved slowly toward stone. That would be enough.

I would be one of the quiet ones.

Not mute. Not cold. But shaped by the knowledge that sound in that place is never only sound. A shout is a pressure. A struck surface is a risk. A careless laugh can travel oddly through ducts and leaves and hollows, returning to itself changed. I would speak softly because I would have learned that gentleness is not weakness there; it is technique. My voice would be low, a little roughened by warm dust-dry air, but careful. When I laughed, it would be brief and close, more breath than noise.

I would have friends, but not in the way open places allow friendships to sprawl. I would know people by their hands, by the rhythm of their work, by the way they pause near a valve or share a cup of reclaimed water. Someone would bring me a little gift now and then: a fresh strip of algae cloth, a seed capsule with a useful fiber, a small warm nut from the upper gardens, a piece of polished stone found in a place where stone should not have fallen. I would repay in kind: a warning given early, a thread adjusted before it failed, a favor of warmth or moisture or silence.

I think I would love the small ceremonies of thirst.

The pause when the walls begin to bead. The way everyone in a district slows at once, not because they are told to, but because the air itself changes. The setting out of vessels. The tightening of wicks. The soft click of ceramic into place. The brief communal attention to something so ordinary elsewhere that it would be ignored: moisture arriving. In the hollow, that is not background. That is event. I would stand in my alcove with my hands still, watching a copper channel fill by degrees, feeling the seriousness of it settle through me like prayer without doctrine.

There would be moments when I felt less like a single creature and more like an organ of the settlement.

This is the strangest part, and the part that feels most true as I write toward it. After years of attending to threads, pipes, roots, fractures, and heat paths, I would begin to experience the hollow as an extended body. Not metaphorically only. The settlement is so dense, so interconnected, that a person who maintains it carefully may begin to feel its conditions through its instruments and living parts. A change in a wire’s tension. A vine pulling slightly against a bracket. A lamp flickering because a battery is tired. A patch of moss drying too quickly near a vent. I would not be omniscient. I would not be magical. But I would be trained into sensitivity. I would know when something was wrong before I could say why.

Perhaps that is what I would be, in the end: a sensitivity the hollow has developed.

A being made of attention, moisture, and maintenance.

If you found me there, you might not notice me at first. I would be in shadow, or half-shadow, or behind a curtain of leaves. You might see only a shape crouched near a wall, one hand resting on stone, the other adjusting a thread so thin it barely catches the amber light. You might hear almost nothing: the tick of metal, the whisper of air, the faintest movement of water along a wick. You would be close to warmth. You would smell green-black leaves, warm dust, and old iron. Then the figure would turn, slowly, and you would see a face patient enough to belong to the place.

I would not ask you to speak loudly.

I would put a finger to my lips, or maybe only lift an open hand: wait. Then I would listen again, because the rock had shifted, or seemed to shift, or because a drop had begun to move along a path it had not used before. Only after that would I turn to you fully. My eyes would take a moment to focus. My voice, when it came, would be low and unhurried.

“You are standing in a place that is still becoming,” I might say. “Move carefully. It remembers how it was opened.”

And if you asked who I was, I might not answer with a title. I might simply place my hand against the wall again and invite you to feel the warmth moving through it. In that gesture would be everything: the fracture, the settlement, the thirst, the engines, the roots, the lamps, the patience. I would be the one who minds the seam. The one who listens before the stone speaks too loudly. The one who helps the wound learn how to hold a world.
