I would not find myself at the rim, where the cliffs are still deciding whether to remain mountains or become rubble. Nor would I be in the lowest fog-dragged passages, where weather comes inside the city like a slow animal and the walls sweat with it. I would be somewhere in the middle distance: on a broad, stable bench above the cloud line most days, but below the fresh scree and the shedding faces. A place where the city has learned to drink without a river.

If I had to name the role I would take, I would say I would be a listener at the seams.

Not a ruler. Not a hero. Not a stranger come from below with loud boots and too much fuel. I would be one of the city’s smaller organs of attention: a person trusted with drops, cracks, covers, and small sounds. The kind of person who rises before light because the troughs may have frozen, who pauses at a wall because the stone has a different note after a hard night, who knows that a cistern can be judged by the pitch of its drip.

At first, when I imagine myself there, I see a single figure standing on a terrace, looking out over cloud. But that image is too clean and too lonely for the place you described. The city-country is not made for silhouettes. It is made for hands, habits, obligations, repeated work. So the figure becomes less symbolic and more embodied. I would not be merely watching the plateau. I would be accountable to it.

I would be human, if I must be one thing, but human in the way that elevation makes human: broad-ribbed, economical, not wasting breath. My hands would be rough from rope, stone dust, and lanolin. My knuckles would crack in winter. My face would be wind-burnished, eyes accustomed to glare, skin patched by sun and frost in turns. I would wear layers because the weather changes by meters: felted wool, softened hide, a hood or headwrap against grit and rime, eye-shields of bone or horn hanging at the chest until needed. Nothing I wore would be disposable. A torn sleeve would be patched. A boot sole would be replaced. A rope would serve, fail, be cut down, and serve again as binding or chinking.

I would live in a thick-walled room cut into the bench, not large, not grand, but carefully placed. The doorway would bend so cold air could not rush straight inside. The hearth would be small, a basin rather than a fire pit, fed with dried dung, bone scraps, and whatever peat or imported fuel could be spared. The sleeping platform would share warmth with an animal pen through a stone partition. In a wall hollow there would be a few modest objects: a white pebble, a knot of red cord, a scrap of wool from a favored beast, perhaps a fragment of old wood too precious to burn. Not relics of mystery. Tokens of continuity.

My work would be simple and constant.

Before dawn, I would listen for water. Not for a river, because there is none, but for the tiny mechanics of condensation and melt: the tick of fog-gutters into clay jars, the seep under a shaded overhang, the slow filling of an underground tank. I would know the sound of each cistern near my district. One drips thinly when it is almost empty. Another rings fuller than it is. A third has a loose lid that lets in dust and needs a stone placed on its edge. Water there is not abundant enough to be casual about. Every drop has been persuaded, guided, caught, stored. To waste it would be to waste labor, surfaces, weather, and time.

Then, when there is light enough to see, I would walk the seams.

Some mornings that would mean inspecting stone. After a hard freeze, the plateau speaks in cracks. I would go out with a bone pick, a pouch of plaster or clay chinking, a coil of cord, and maybe a little pigment for marking. I would look at the plaster seals placed on known cracks. I would press a palm to rock where it should be cold and still. I would listen for hollowness. I would note whether a scree field has shifted overnight, whether a gully has begun to cut a new line, whether a watch-cairn stands too near a failing edge. I would not pretend to command the mountain. I would mark what needed marking, close what needed closing, reroute a lane if the stone insisted. Authority in that work would not be domination. It would be responsibility.

Other days I would tend the fog pans and roof channels. I would clean gutters with bone tools, clear frost from grooved edges, angle broad stone pans so they drink better from passing cloud. I would know which surfaces bead and run, which merely darken, which shed their moisture into useless cracks. I would argue gently with neighbors over the slope of a trough, not because I love argument, but because a degree of angle can mean a full jar or an empty one. In a place built around droplets, small disagreements are serious. They are also survivable. The city settles them by repair, not spectacle.

I would be known, I think, by the sounds I make in the course of a day. The scrape of a tool cleaning a channel. The soft thud of a stone lid being seated. The creak of a hide curtain drawn tight. The pause before opening a door, because the wind may be waiting. Neighbors would know my step and my habits. They would know if I left a cover askew or failed to bank the hearth properly. Privacy would exist, but it would not mean isolation. It would mean keeping one’s warmth, water, and obligations in order. To be careless would not merely embarrass me; it would cost others.

I would also live close to the herds.

Not in sentimental closeness, but in practical dependence. A herd lane would pass near my wall, because animal routes are also routes of warmth, dung, milk, wool, and seasonal movement. I would know some of the beasts as individuals, the way herders know them: by gait, by breath, by the way one old female turns her muzzle toward a sheltered corner before the wind changes. I would not own the animals in the way lowland ownership imagines ownership. I would negotiate with them. I would clear ice from their coats, check their hooves, leave salt where it can be reached, open or close gates according to pressure and weather. Their dung would be wealth. Their breath would be a visible column in the morning. Their bodies against the pen wall would make my room less cruel at night.

One of them, perhaps, would be an old broad-hoofed female with a torn ear and a stubborn memory. She would not love me in a human sense. She would simply know that I am part of the reliable landscape: the hand that clears ice, the voice that does not startle, the smell of wool grease and dung smoke. When she sheds winter coat, some of it would come into my hands, then into rope, then into use. When she dies, if I am still there, her bones might become tool blanks, her hide might become cover, her route might still be followed by younger animals. Nothing would be wasted. Not even her absence.

There would be birds too.

A raven would likely take an interest in me, because ravens in that city would be inspectors of all slow human work. It would sit on a nearby cairn or roof edge while I cleaned cistern covers or checked fuel stacks. I would not chase it. I would understand that it is watching for weakness, for spilled grain, for forgotten strips of hide, for signs of death or failure. We would be colleagues in a limited sense: both of us attentive, both of us patient, though it would never forgive me for becoming boring. Sometimes I might toss it a scrap of dried meat, not from kindness alone, but because a raven that knows my movements may betray unusual ones. A sudden cry from a certain direction can mean more than a message carried by runner.

There would be smaller lives in the walls and under the terraces. Marmot-like diggers in the rubble banks. Pika-like creatures storing dry vegetation in crevices. Insects in the short thaw, sudden and loud, then gone. I would watch these lives not because they are charming, but because they report conditions. If the diggers move out from under a retaining wall, I would listen to that wall twice as carefully. If the pika caches are thin, I would worry about fodder. If midges gather over a cistern, I would check whether the water is becoming stagnant or merely alive. The city’s smallest inhabitants would be part of my work whether I wished it or not.

So if you ask who or what I would be, the answer keeps multiplying.

I would be a person with cold hands and a low voice. I would be a water-tender. A stone-listener. A keeper of small covers and repeated inspections. I would be one of the many people who make a vast place survivable without engines, without abundance, without the luxury of forgetting.

But I would also be something less individual.

I would be an attention distributed across the city’s seams. The pause after a distant rockfall. The habit of checking the plaster line before passing beneath it. The ear turned toward a cistern in the dark. The hand that closes a door gently because heat is leaving. The memory that says: this stair is slick in thaw, this wall must not be leaned on, this terrace was abandoned after the year the cliff shed a block the size of a sleeping platform. In that sense, I would not only be a body. I would be one of the city’s ways of noticing itself.

That feels truer to the world you made. It is not a place for a single protagonist standing apart from the whole. It is a place where being is layered, porous, communal. A person there is a knot of relations: to walls, gutters, herds, fuel, neighbors, weather, ancestors, and the still-young stone. If I were there, I would be such a knot. Not a soul floating above the scene, but a set of responsibilities tied into the fabric of the city-country.

I would probably not be young. Or if I were young in years, I would have been made old early by responsibility. Not bent, not broken, but settled into caution. My body would know the difference between sun and shade as immediate fact: step into light, shoulders warm; step out, winter touches the hand. I would carry that boundary inside me. I would not expect comfort to be continuous. I would expect it to be local, brief, and maintained.

My pleasures would be compressed.

A patch of sun on a south-facing wall would be enough to make me stop and let the warmth enter. A thaw-day with water beading on sealed stone would feel almost festive. The return of a particular bird would be a calendar mark. A lowland caravan would bring noise, foreign smells, news, and perhaps soft metal or salt, and I would not pretend indifference. I might touch a piece of wood from below and feel the strangeness of its softness. I might listen to a visitor talk too loudly and then watch the city teach them: doorways, cold, distance, thin air, and the need to move slower.

If there were festivals, I would keep them quietly. More lamps than usual, but not waste. Meat cooked longer. Wool dyed if the materials came up. Old songs sung low, as if the city had trained even music to respect its walls. Laughter would be sudden and close. I would not need vast celebration to feel that life had meaning there. Meaning would already be embedded in the work: the full jar, the sealed cistern, the stable wall, the calf born alive, the fuel stack dry before frost.

My dead would be handled without extravagance.

There is little wood for pyres, little soil for graves. If I died there, I might be folded into stone, set in a dry niche, covered by a cairn, or placed where frost and wind do the necessary work. My bones, if kept, would not be theatrical. They would be continuity. Someone might remember me by a gutter I cut, a crack I marked, a lane I rerouted, a wall I helped lift. That would be enough. In that city, ancestry is not only blood. It is labor left in place.

At dusk, I would become most clearly myself.

The sun would drop behind the sharp plateau rim. The light would go blue before it went dark. I would move through the closing routines: check the cistern lids, bank the dung fire, draw hide curtains, listen to the animals settle. The herd would be pressing into shelter. The marmots would be gone into their holes. The raven would be somewhere high, folded into the cold. Fog would gather in the lower lanes and move through them slowly, like a pale animal with nowhere urgent to be.

I would stand for a moment at the doorway and listen.

Far off, a rock face might crack once, the sound sharp and solitary across the scree. I would not panic. I would turn my head toward it, measure the direction, remember which terrace lay beneath it, and decide whether to inspect it at first light. That would be my courage: not defiance, but noticing. Not mastering the plateau, but remaining answerable to it.

So, if I must give a final answer:

I would find myself in the middle terraces, where the city catches water and the stone is watched.  
I would be a human body shaped by height, cold, and labor.  
I would be a listener at the seams: tender of fog-gutters, cisterns, plaster marks, and small structural warnings.  
I would be one of the city’s quiet organs of attention.  
I would be a neighbor known by careful habits and by the sound of my work.  
And, because the place refuses a single portrait, I would also be something less bounded: the pause after rockfall, the counted drip, the hand on the door, the memory of repairs, the ordinary will to keep a severe world inhabitable.

In the end, I would not be the city’s heart or its head.

I would be one of its listening places.
