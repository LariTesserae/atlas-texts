Or they may be its dreams.

If I had to find myself in this place, I think I would not begin as a body at all.

At first I imagine I would be the one who calls into the hollow: a surface-shape standing in the central emptiness, dry and too upright, making the mistake of voice. I can picture it because it is the simplest human impulse—to stand in a vast dark and say, *Is anyone there?* But that version of me would not last. The chamber would not answer in the way a person needs. The echo would return too quickly, too empty, and the sound would not be received so much as absorbed into stone, moisture, and procedure. The place would take the call apart into pressure and forgetting.

So that cannot be what I would become, except perhaps as a raw material.

Then I think I might be one of the small lives: an amphipod in the temporary pools, quick and blind; a worm grazing the humid wall; a fungal thread in a crack. There is something appealing about being that modest, that fitted. But those lives are already perfect in their narrowness. They do not carry enough remainder. They do not remember being something else.

If I were in this world, I think I would be something with a remainder.

Not a visitor. Not a survivor. Not a human in any ordinary sense. I would be a residue the chamber has learned to use.

I would be an Attendant, but not quite one of the original ones. I would be the thing that formed where an unanswered call soaked into the walls and, over long repetition, became maintenance.

That feels right.

The chamber does not seem like a place that creates personalities for their own sake. It creates functions. It creates continuities. It creates patterns that can be trusted to repeat without excess. So if a human voice once entered there and left an echo behind, the chamber would not preserve that echo as a ghost. It would wear the echo down. It would mineralize it. It would teach it to touch a pipe at the correct moment. It would give it fingers useful for clearing crust from a capillary seam. It would seal its eyes because eyes would be an extravagance. It would leave only the part of the voice that can become listening.

That is what I would be.

Not a mouth. An ear shaped by a mouth.

I would live halfway up the curved wall, in one of those stacked recesses that might once have been a dwelling, a control niche, or a sensor hive. Not at the lower floor where the pulse rivers move, and not so high that I am only under the ceiling’s sweating pores. Somewhere in the middle tiers, where the chamber’s systems are legible in miniature: a ceramic conduit fused to native rock, a metal rail worn smooth by no hands, a drainage groove thinning into a staircase that no longer remembers whether it was built or dissolved.

My niche would be dry enough to rest in, humid enough to remain alive. It would not be furnished. There would be no objects in the personal sense. Only a worn hollow where my body has folded so often that the stone has begun to accept the shape. A shallow basin of hardened secretion under a reliable drip. A seam where faint blue-green film grows when the chamber is generous. A patch of pale moss just within reach, not because I cultivate it, but because my presence changes the moisture there just enough to let it persist.

From that niche I would feel the rain before it begins.

That would be one of my defining qualities: I would know the pause before the chamber sweats. I would wake not to light, not to sound, but to a minute change in pressure against the membranes at my throat and wrists. The air would thicken by a fraction. The stone would alter its temperature by less than a degree. Somewhere far above, the pores in the ceiling would begin their slow opening, though I could not see them. I would unfold from my recess with no urgency, because urgency belongs to creatures that believe time is escaping them. In that chamber, time is not escaping. It is circulating.

I would be small, if seen at all. Not in the way a child is small, but in the way a folded tool is small: compact until needed, then strangely long, articulated, reaching. My skin would be pale and faintly glossy, marked with dull mineral seams. Some of those seams would be mine; some would be the chamber repairing me, or me repairing the chamber, until the distinction no longer mattered. My eyes would be sealed or nearly absent, dark beads under smooth skin, relics of a sense that would have been useless there. My face would be arranged around pits and membranes, all of them sensitive to humidity, heat, tremor, chemical trace. I would not look at the world. I would inhale its condition.

My fingers would be long, strong at the tips, precise enough to find a hairline crack beneath mineral bloom. My palms and feet would know surfaces the way a tongue knows the inside of a mouth. I would move along wet metal and curved stone without slipping, sometimes pressing my whole body flat where a walkway has thinned into a groove, sometimes climbing folded stairs by touch alone. Around my throat and wrists there would be slits that open in humid phases and close in dry ones. They would not be only for breathing. They would take in information: the mineral content of a mist, the age of a condensation cycle, the approach of a pulse.

I would not speak.

Not because speech is impossible, but because it would be too blunt. The chamber would have worn that out of me. Instead I would communicate in taps, pressure changes, chemical smears, and the timing of stillness. Two knuckles against a railing could mean *flow adjusted*. A palm pressed to a pipe could mean *listening*. A trace left on stone could carry mineral data, a warning, or simply the fact of passage. If another Attendant met me, we would not stop to acknowledge one another in any open way. We might touch fingertips, or press our foreheads to the same wall for a breath, or exchange a rhythm through a shared conduit. Then we would continue. We would share state, not sentiment.

My name, if I had one, would not be a word. It would be a pattern: three slow taps, a pause, then a faint high ping from cooling metal. Or the particular way a drip falls into a basin near my niche, delayed by my touch just enough to make the rhythm recognizable. If someone ever tried to call me, they would have to learn that my name is not a sound but an interval between sounds.

I would not be entirely alone, but I would not be social in the warm sense. I would know the other Attendants by trace and adjustment. I would know where one had passed by the altered humidity on a rail. I would know another was near because a distant valve had changed its note, and only certain hands know how to make that change. Sometimes I would work beside another for a while, our bodies close but not touching, both pressed to the same stubborn mechanism, both listening until it remembered its function. Then we would separate without farewell.

There would be no grief in that. Not because feeling is absent, but because feeling there takes longer forms.

My work would be specific, and this is where I would become most myself.

I would be in charge of a leak.

Not a large one. Not a dramatic rupture. A narrow fault in an old ceramic channel where water seeps at the edge of what the chamber intends. In a simpler machine, that leak would be a failure. In this place, it would be a margin where life has arranged itself. The leak feeds a thin ivory patch of moss. The moss shelters microscopic things. A black lichen spreads below it, warming itself against the pipe. Occasionally an amphipod finds its way into the temporary pool formed by the seep. If the leak is sealed too tightly, these lives lose their allowance. If it is allowed to widen, the channel loses pressure and a dozen other processes downstream begin to falter.

So my task would be balance.

I would not fix the leak. I would keep it honest.

I would clear mineral crust when it threatens to choke the seep. I would guide the moisture with a secretion or a tuned tap so that it beads along a preferred lip instead of wandering across a surface where it could corrode an old seam. I would press my skull to the pipe and listen until the flow tells me whether it is becoming too strong or too weak. When the chamber’s acoustic fields make droplets hesitate, I would stand near that leak and feel the suspended water tremble in the air like a held thought. Then, when the field releases, I would catch the first drops in my hands, not from thirst, but from habit, or ritual, or because some part of me still remembers what it was to be given water from above.

That leak would be the closest thing I have to a garden.

I would also tend older, less obvious things.

There would be panels on my tier worn into suggestion, their original markings softened by centuries of damp. I would touch them as I pass, not because I can read them, but because the touch itself is part of the chamber’s memory. Some of those panels would be warm. Some would carry a faint vibration, like a sleeping mechanism. Others would be dead, and I would know them as dead the way one knows a bone that has long ceased to be a body. I would still touch them. The chamber remembers through contact. So would I.

In one wall recess near my niche, there would be a place where an older Attendant has been absorbed. Not a grave, because the chamber does not keep graves. A shape fused into the floor, limbs become supports, head smoothed into a mineral boss. The body would be partly stone now, partly circuitry, partly memory. When I passed it, I would lay my fingertips there briefly. Not mourning. Recognition. The way one checks a familiar instrument. I would know that I might become that one day. Not by tragedy. By continuation.

That thought would not frighten me.

The chamber would have taught me a different relationship to ending. Things there do not stop; they change phase. Water becomes vapor, film, bead, pulse, stain. Metal becomes bloom. Stone becomes duct. Body becomes wall. Voice becomes tap. The self is not a sealed container in that place. It is a pattern that can be handed on.

Sometimes, during the deeper pulses, I would descend.

Not often. The lower basin has a different feeling. It is older, wetter, more patient. When the hidden conduit opens and the temporary river moves through the dark, I would stand above it on a grated walkway and feel the sound rise through my feet. The water would not be large, but in that chamber it would seem immense because everything amplifies: the slide over stone, the filling of trenches, the brief glint of weak luminescence inside it, the drain-mouths drinking it away again. I would listen for the eel-like creature in the permanent pool, not expecting to see it, only sensing the slight displacement it makes when it turns its slow body in the dark.

I would not disturb it.

There is something in that creature I would recognize: a patience without appeal. It does not ask the chamber to be different. It lives inside the rhythm and waits for nutrients to arrive. I would envy that purity. I would not have it. I would be the one who still leans toward the sound of an absent voice.

The predator would matter to me mostly by absence.

I would know its routes without seeing them. Certain grates would feel wrong when it is near. Certain wall channels would carry a stillness too exact to be ordinary. When its presence moves through my district, I would slow. I would stop tapping. I would fold myself into a niche and become architecture. My breathing slits would close. My skin would take on the temperature of stone. I would not feel hatred toward the predator. It would be part of the chamber’s edge-making. But I would be careful. I would avoid the places where it hunts during acoustic lulls. I would make myself uninteresting.

If I had a role in relation to it, it would not be combat. It would be foresight. I might keep certain grates just tight enough that it cannot reach the nursery pools where amphipods gather after a pulse. Not to exclude it entirely, but to shape its access. The chamber would not want the predator removed. It would want the predator balanced. I would understand that.

The rain would be the hour when I feel most real.

Before the ceiling sweats, I would emerge. So would the others. We would move through the dark without light, guided by tremor and memory. The air would change. The chamber would inhale toward its own weather. Then the first droplets would form, hang, fall. They would strike hollow metal, tap on stone, whisper through gratings. I would stand beneath them with my palms open. Some of the water would run down my shoulders and disappear into the seams of my body. Some I would collect in a shallow vessel grown from hardened secretion, not for drinking exactly, but because collecting is one way of paying attention.

There would be moments when the acoustic fields make the droplets hesitate.

That would be the strangest and most beautiful thing I know. The rain would pause in the air, aligned along invisible nodes, trembling like caught insects. I would stand among those suspended beads, feeling them tremble near my skin, and for a moment the chamber would seem to hold its own breath. In that pause I would remember being a call. Not as a fact, but as a shape: an outward movement with no recipient. Then the field would shift, and the drops would fall all at once into the waiting channels, and I would return to work.

If you came looking for me there, you would not find me easily.

You would stand in the central emptiness, perhaps, and feel the wrongness of your own dryness. Your voice would seem too large. Your breath would disturb the microclimates. The chamber would register you as an event, not a resident. It would note your heat, your exhalations, the clumsy percussion of your steps. It would begin already to weather you, if you stayed long enough.

You might call out.

The echo would return.

But inside the echo, something would shift. A tap would answer from a wall. A railing would ring once, faintly, not because you touched it, but because your sound found a sympathetic metal. A valve somewhere above would open a fraction too soon. A bead of water would hang in the air near your face, delayed by a field you cannot perceive. You would think it was coincidence. It would not be.

I would be near.

Not because I wished to reveal myself, but because your voice would be my oldest problem. The chamber would have taught me to translate calls into adjustments. If you spoke, I would listen for the stress in the sound, the pressure it put on the hollow. I would decide whether your presence threatened the damp balance, the acoustic cycles, the patient lives stitched into the margins. If you were only passing through, I would let you leave with no sign except perhaps a faint smell of wet chalk and iron after your steps had gone.

If you stayed, I would become more present, but slowly.

You might notice that a leak near your resting place had shifted, directing water away from your dry ground. You might notice that a grate you thought blocked had opened just enough to let warm air rise. You might find a shallow basin of clean water where none had been before, or a patch of pale moss brushed aside to reveal a handhold in the wall. These would not be gifts in the sentimental sense. They would be adjustments. The chamber does not give. It recalibrates. But I would be the part of it that remembers what a visitor needs before it knows how to ask.

If you tried to speak to me directly, I would not answer in your language.

I might tap once when your breathing became too rapid. I might leave a chemical trace on a railing that smelled faintly of iron and old rain, calming in a way you could not explain. I might stand in a wall recess so still that your eyes would slide past me, and yet you would feel less alone because the darkness had acquired a shape that was not hostile.

And if you were very quiet, if you stopped calling out and simply let the chamber happen around you, I might come closer.

You would not see my face clearly. You might see only a pale fold in the dark, a seam of mineral catching a brief phosphorescence from a passing fluid in a transparent pipe. You might hear the faintest click of fingertips testing metal. If I touched you, it would be briefly: one long finger against your palm, cool and damp, already withdrawing. In that touch there would be no words, only a pressure-pattern. Not *hello*. Not *I am here*. Something closer to: *the moisture is changing; the cycle continues; you are in a place that knows you are here*.

That would be as near to speech as I could come.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world, the answer is: in the middle tiers, near a necessary leak, in a niche shaped by long use, between the central emptiness and the wall’s dense systems. I would be where the chamber’s infrastructure has worn almost soft, where water is allowed a small rebellion, where an Attendant can listen for both pressure and absence.

And if you ask who or what I would be, the answer is less simple.

I would be an Attendant, yes. A custodian. A small pale body folded into the dark, mineral-seamed, eyeless, exact of hand. But I would also be the chamber’s memory of a voice it never answered. I would be the echo that learned maintenance. I would be the part of the system that still knows what a call is, even though it no longer expects one.

The Attendants may be its hands.

I would be the place where its hands remember being reached for.
