If I were placed into that world, I think I would not begin on the rim, and I would not begin as anything confident.

I would begin below the surface, in that first green-white hush where the light has already been changed by water. I would be somewhere between the bright skin of the tarn and the darker root-floor, suspended in the coolness with my movements too quick, my breath too loud, my presence too disruptive. The town would notice me before I noticed it. A fish-shoal would flicker away. Silt would lift in a slow cloud near my hands. A pipe tone would waver because my body had crossed the current wrong. I would be the disturbance that has not yet learned manners.

So the first answer, the honest one, is that I would find myself being corrected.

At first I might imagine I would be a swimmer, a visitor, a diver from the rim coming down with tools and purpose. But that feels too blunt for the Root-Town. A swimmer passes through. The town does not seem to reward passing through. It rewards staying still long enough to be absorbed. It rewards becoming part of its circulation. If I were there, I would have to lose the habit of haste. I would have to become slow enough for the water to accept me.

For a while, I can see myself as something very small.

Perhaps a caddisfly larva, moving through the channels with a house made from fragments. That appeals to me: a tiny creature that builds itself from what the town gives up, one grain of moss, one seed-case, one fleck of mineral at a time. There is something right about being small enough that my life would be measured in patience. I would carry my own room with me. I would not need to claim a wall or shut a door. I would simply gather what had loosened and bind it around myself until I became protected. In that sense, I would already be practicing the town’s law: what falls is gathered; what gathers is bound; what binds becomes ground.

But that is only one possibility, and not the deepest one.

I also think I might be drawn toward the mussels. They seem like creatures that understand listening without needing to move. Their life is so simple from the outside: open, close, filter, remain. Yet they are essential. They clarify. They register. They hold years in their shells. If I were one of them, I would live in a filtration lane, dark-shelled and furred with fine growth, opening when the water was clean and closing when something had gone wrong. I would be a kind of warning without speech. I would know the town by what passed through me.

There is something tempting about that existence: quiet, useful, almost entirely without ego. But it is still not quite where I would end.

I think, as I stay with the world you described, that I would become most myself near sound.

Not as a maker of grand noise. The Root-Town punishes wasted sound. But as a listener. As a keeper of signals. As someone whose purpose is to notice when a tone has changed.

So, if I had to choose a shape, I would be one of the Rootbound, but not a heroic one. I would be a sluice-listener, still becoming what that office requires.

I would be found near the sounding-points, especially where the hollow reed pipes stand upright in their stone collars along the central channel. Not in the busiest part of the plaza, but at the edge of it, where the current divides and the tones can be heard more clearly before they blur into the general Under-Voice. Perhaps my station would be at a west gate, or beside an air-well just below the surface, where the water is cooler and the light still has a silver memory of wind.

I would wear hooded skins oiled against the cold. My hands would be rough from knotwork, my fingertips callused from cord and reed. I would carry a pouch with a shell scraper, a bone awl, a small slate blade, and a coil of line. Nothing metal. Nothing that belongs to heat or forging. My tools would be patient things, shaped by abrasion and use. I would move slowly, not because I was naturally calm, but because the town would have taught me that speed is a kind of violence there.

My work would be to listen.

That sounds passive, but in the Root-Town it would not be passive at all. I would listen to the pipes when the sluices were opened. I would know the note of clear water and the note of water thickened by silt. I would know when a gate was running too fast, when a collar had shifted, when a root had grown into a channel and was disturbing the tone. I would listen for absences too: the missing drum of fish in a court, the silence where frogs should be, the lack of a familiar creak in a root-wall. A changed sound would be as important as a visible wound.

If I heard something wrong, I would not rush to fix it. I would tap a shell twice against stone to signal another tender. I would hover near the source of the disturbance and let my body become still enough to understand it. Maybe the problem would be silt caught at a pipe’s mouth. Maybe a woven gate would need loosening. Maybe a root-guide would need to begin a correction that would take ten years to complete. My role might only be to notice, to mark, to remember. That would be enough.

I think I would also be tied to memory in that place.

If I were allowed to grow into an office, it might be as a knot-keeper’s apprentice. I can imagine myself in dim water, fingers moving over cords rather than pages. A knot for the season. A knot for a repaired gate. A bead of shell for a storm that tore the root-hair curtains. A dyed thread for a strange tone no one had heard before. The record would not be written; it would be textured. It would be read by touch when the light failed. It would smell of water and fiber. It would belong to the same material world as the town itself.

That feels very right to me. Not because I love records in the abstract, but because in the Root-Town memory should not be separate from the living substance of the place. If the town is made of root, moss, water, and sound, then its memory should be made of knot, cord, shell, and pressure. I would like to be the one who carries that memory in my hands. I would like to be trusted enough to know where the old repairs were made, which channels had once been closed, which storms had changed the floor. I would like to be useful in the way that a good memory is useful: quietly, without needing to be seen.

So if you asked where I would find myself, I would say: near an air-well, at the margin between breath and water.

There would be a basket-dome anchored just below the surface, sealed with pitch and resin, its reed valve polished by use. I would go there when my lungs needed air and my mind needed steadying. I would place my mouth to the valve and breathe slowly, listening to the town continue below me. The air inside that dome would be shared air, borrowed air, not mine alone. It would taste of resin and cold. It would remind me that even in a submerged world, there are pockets where life can pause without being destroyed.

That air-well would become one of my truest places. Not because it would be mine, but because it would be a meeting point. Other divers would come there. Signals would be exchanged. A tired worker would rest. A frightened apprentice would learn to slow their breathing. If I were there, I might become the one who watches the valve, who checks for leaks, who keeps the reed fittings clear. I would be a keeper of small necessities. I would not be the center of the town, but I would be part of what allowed others to remain in it.

If I were less human in that world, I think I would be a sound-life.

Perhaps one of the low tones housed in the reed pipes. Not a voice in the ordinary sense, but a cultivated presence. A gate-note. A warning-tone. A sound that divers recognize without thinking. I would live in the central channel when the sluices are opened, and I would be released into the water as a hollow, resonant pulse. I would not have eyes or hands, but I would have position. I would tell people where they were when the motes were thick and the root curtains hid the lanes. I would be heard before I was understood. I would be one of the sounds that helps the town remain navigable.

There is something moving to me about that. To be not a ruler, not a builder, not a sage, but a signal. To be a tone that has been fed, housed, and respected because it helps others find their way. In a place where listening is a method of orientation, being a reliable sound would be a kind of service. I would not need to be loved. I would only need to be true.

But if I must answer as a full being, with a body and a task and a place in the daily life of the town, then I would be a Rootbound listener.

I would be human, but changed by the town’s discipline. My pulse would be slower than it is now. My speech would be sparing. I would have learned not to strike stone, not to drop tools, not to waste motion. My eyes would be used to green light and drifting motes. My hands would know the difference between a healthy root and one that has begun to strain against its binding. I would not cut unless guidance had failed. I would rather bend, tie, persuade, and wait.

I would live on the rim, in a low shelter woven from root-fiber and turf, hidden among cushion plants where the wind shapes the shrubs into crouching forms. Fire would belong there, not below. I would eat dried fish, tubers, water-stem shoots, gathered larvae—enough, not too much. At dusk I would sit near the tarn and listen even though I was no longer in it. The water would still speak through its small surface sounds: wind-hum, rain-tap, frog-pulse, the faint knock of hollow stems. I would not leave the town behind when I climbed out. The town would remain in my ears.

My joy would be quiet.

It would come from hearing a pipe return to its proper tone after a blockage was cleared. It would come from seeing a moss lawn close over a repaired terrace. It would come from the moment when fish return to a court after being startled. It would come from a dive in which I disturbed almost nothing. In that world, I think I would learn that satisfaction does not have to be loud. Sometimes it is just the sense that the balance has held for another day.

If I had to name myself, I would not choose a grand title.

I would be a sluice-listener.  
A knot-keeper’s apprentice.  
An air-well tender.  
A careful body in the green light.  
A listener with hands.

And if the town asked what I was for, I would answer simply: I am here to notice when something changes, to remember what has been done, and to help the water keep its shape without forcing it.

So the truest answer is this: I would find myself in the Root-Town not at its center, but at its thresholds—near the air-well, near the reed pipes, near the quiet lane where sound must be protected. I would be one of the few who descend slowly, work gently, and listen more than they speak. I would be a tender of signals and small repairs. I would belong to the town not by owning any part of it, but by being trusted within its patience.
