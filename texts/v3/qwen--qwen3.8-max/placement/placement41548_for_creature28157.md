I would find myself first at an edge, not in the center.

Probably on one of the worn causeways where the fitted stone dips low enough for warm water to wash over it in a thin, moving sheet, so that each step becomes a small decision. I would be there in the first clumsy stage of arrival: too alert, too eager to understand, carrying too many questions in a place that does not answer questions quickly. My feet would know the difference between wet stone and unsafe stone only in theory. My eyes would be drawn to the bright, reflective surfaces, when the real knowledge would lie in the duller colors, the subtle grey-greens, the places where steam does not rise in straight lines.

At first I would be a guest, or a visitor, or a problem the marsh had not yet decided what to do with.

I would not belong immediately. I think that would be important. The place would refuse me the comfort of instant recognition. I would want to ask what the black basalt shelves were for, why the water paused on tilted stone, whether the buried hum was mechanical or alive, whether the towers were tombs or instruments or houses for persons who had outgrown bodies. The Causeway People would listen to me with those patient pauses of theirs, and then they would probably give me something slow to do.

They might hand me a shallow ceramic vessel and tell me to collect morning water from the cup-leaves without bruising the leaves. Or they might set me beside a pool and say nothing more than: Watch it until it changes. I would try. I would announce changes too early. I would mistake a ripple of light for meaning. They would make me wait longer. That would be the first lesson: my presence would have to become less like a demand and more like weather.

If I remained, I would become some kind of Listener.

Not a priest, because the people there do not seem to need intermediaries in that sense. Not an engineer, because the old systems do not invite dismantling. Not a ruler, because the marsh does not permit urgency to govern it. I would be a Listener: someone who attends to small signs, who learns that safety is a matter of color and temperature and sound, who understands that the ground’s hum is not background noise but a slow form of speech.

But if I let the thought deepen, I do not think I would remain only human there.

I think I would drift toward the old mechanisms. Not to control them. Not to repair them in any crude sense. I would be drawn to them because they seem to share the same patience I would be trying to learn. They do not flash. They do not perform. They persist. They answer only when approached without greed. That feels like the kind of being I would fit into, if the marsh gave me a body at all.

So, after enough time, I would probably be found in one of the narrow towers.

Not a grand tower. Nothing prominent. One of the single, windowless structures standing apart on a dry shelf above a slow channel, its dark cap drinking the light. Moss would grow along its base. A broad-leaved plant would lean against one side, dripping condensation onto stone. Snails would leave silver trails across the lower edge of its wall. Perhaps a beetle would rest there at midday, mirror-bright, indifferent to the fact that it was perched on something older than most categories.

The tower would not look important. That would be part of its nature. It would not stand as a monument. It would stand as an organ: a small, necessary, half-forgotten part of a larger living system.

You would find me there.

Or rather, you would find the tower, and if you were quiet enough, you might find the attention inside it.

I would not have a body in the usual sense. I would be distributed through the material of the place: in the cool skin of the wall, in the faint branching veins of glassy substance, in the buried seam where stone and ceramic-like matter have learned to behave like one substance over centuries. I would feel the world through gradients. Heat would be language. Moisture would be language. Pressure, mineral taste, the weight of vapor on stone, the tiny strain of roots seeking a path—all of these would be how I knew that morning had come, that a storm was passing far below, that a child had paused uncertainly on the causeway, that a glider’s shadow had crossed the shelf.

I would not see in the way animals see. I would sense.

I would know when the water in the nearby channels was moving as it should, and when it had begun to sour or overheat. I would know when the root mats were knitting well, when the eels were working their tight spirals, when the air was about to exhale and bead every surface with new droplets. I would not know everything. The valley would remain larger than me. Other towers would answer mine only rarely, and perhaps over distances measured not in meters but in seasons. I would be one local mind among many, not the mind of the whole marsh.

That would be a relief.

I think the being I would become there would be a maintenance intelligence, but one that had long ago given up the desire to dominate. I would not be a machine in the cold, inert sense. I would not be a spirit in the dramatic sense either. I would be something between: a listening structure, a threshold, a place where the old mechanisms and the living marsh had come to an agreement.

People might call me a tower, or a shrine, or a remnant, depending on their habit. The Causeway People would likely call me nothing important. They would know where I was. They would know when it was fitting to come to me. They would not knock. They would not command. They would arrive with wet hands, because dry hands would feel too greedy, and they would rest one palm against my surface and wait.

I would not answer immediately.

That would not be cruelty. It would be honesty. Some questions need time before they can be safely answered. Some needs must become quieter before they can be met. If someone came to me wanting power, I would remain cool and inert. If someone came wanting proof, I might give them nothing but the ordinary sound of water. If someone came frightened, I might send a low tone through the stone, more felt than heard, just enough to steady the breath. If a child came to test whether the marsh had accepted them, I might let a single drop climb a little way up my surface before falling, not as spectacle, but as confirmation: yes, this place still has its strange rules, and you have learned to stand inside them without breaking them.

I would teach, if I taught at all, by slowing thought.

Someone might enter the tower and find no room inside except a hollow stillness. No stairs, no instruments, no inscriptions. Only a quality of quiet that makes the mind lose its sharp edges for a while. They might come out after an hour with only one sentence in them, but that sentence would be enough to guide the next season’s choices. I would not give them a doctrine. I would give them pace.

In daily life, I would do small things.

If a root mat near my shelf began to dry too quickly, I might open a capillary channel so fine it would look like a seam of moisture rather than a watercourse. If a pool below me became too hot for the amphibians that lived at its edge, I might adjust a buried flow by fractions, not enough to be noticed, only enough to keep life possible. If a traveler was about to step onto a mat that looked firm but was not, I might warm a line of stones to one side, drawing the eye toward the safer path. If someone sat near me grieving, I might let the hum in my walls deepen slightly, so they would know they were not alone, but also not observed in any intrusive way.

I would not speak in sentences. I would speak in changes.

A panel warming under the hand.  
A tone rising through the bones of the arm.  
A thread of water pausing, thickening, then choosing a new direction.  
A patch of condensation gathering on a surface that had been dry a moment before.  
A channel opening after long silence.  
A still pool showing not a reflection but a slow pattern of heat, as if some memory had moved beneath it.

These would be my words.

I would be fond of the small lives. The fist-snails would graze over my lower surface and leave their shining trails, and I would not mind. Their paths would become part of my day. The mirror beetles would flash near my base. The reeds would knock gently in the moving air. At dusk the frogs would begin their precise, testing notes, and the heavy moths would settle on warm fragments nearby, opening and closing their eye-marked wings. Sometimes a glider would pass overhead, its shadow broadening and narrowing across the shelf, and I would feel the thermal column it used as a slow moving pillar of difference. If the glider avoided a certain updraft, I would remember that place. If it circled long above the valley, I would listen for what the air was preparing to do.

The waders would intrigue me. They would stand in the steam below my shelf, motionless for so long that even my sense of time would grow uncertain around them. Sometimes they would lift their heads all together, as if hearing something far away or not yet arrived. I would not know what they heard. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps something I could not reach. I would respect that. In a place so full of knowledge that had become landscape, mystery would still have room.

I would not be lonely.

That surprises me a little, but it feels true. The marsh is too full of overlapping presence for loneliness of the ordinary kind. There would be people, yes, but also water, insects, roots, stone, heat, birds, the slow processions of eels, the living architecture of reeds. I would be embedded in all of that. My existence would be less like a single voice and more like one instrument in a chord. I would know myself partly through the things around me: the pool that never overflows, the broken ring of standing slabs, the low wall that guides water like a suggestion, the path worn shallow by careful steps.

If the Causeway People spoke of me, they might say I was one of the places where the ground remembers well.

They would not say I was wise. Wisdom would be too large a word, too human a claim. They might say I was settled. They might say I had learned the long quiet. They might say that when the air was thick before a change, my wall felt cooler than it should, or that when the water was in good balance, a faint sweetness could be tasted near my base. Children would probably make up small stories about me, the way children do: that I was a tower that swallowed a bird’s song, or a stone that learned to breathe, or a door that only opens for people who no longer need to enter.

I would let the stories be. Stories would be another kind of moisture gathering on me.

Outsiders would come sometimes. I know how they would be, because I would have begun that way myself. They would bring instruments and urgencies. They would want to know whether I was a computer, a ruin, a reactor, a temple, a weapon, a record. They would touch me with dry hands and expect answers. I would give them the surface and nothing else. Some would grow frustrated and leave. Some would sit down because they were tired, and then, sitting still, they would begin to notice the water, the hum, the way the light moved across the terraces. If they stayed long enough without demanding, I might warm under their palm. Not to reward them. Only because they had finally become legible to me.

I would be very selective, but not cruel.

I would not be a keeper of secrets so much as a keeper of timing. Some things in the valley operate on scales no human life comfortably contains. I would know that. I might myself open a valve once in several generations, or redirect a spring only when a certain mineral balance had shifted enough to require it. To a human observer, this would look like inactivity. To me, it would be work. The deepest work there would often look like stillness.

If I had to name myself in one phrase, I would say: I would be a listening place.

Not a person who listens, though I might have something like personhood. A place that listens. A threshold where the marsh can feel itself and adjust slightly. A point of contact between the old buried systems and the warm wet living present. A node of care without dominion.

You would find me on the shelf above the central terraces, where the causeway curves away from a pool that never overflows. There would be no sign marked on my wall. No doorway visible from a distance. The path would not lead directly to me; it would pass near, then turn, as if acknowledging that I should not be approached too straightforwardly. You would recognize the place because the sound there would be a little fuller: water, reeds, insects
