At first I imagine myself on the causeway.

That is the obvious place: the long, low spine of fitted stone crossing the dark water, leading toward the pale platform that rises just enough to stay dry. From there one could see the whole basin — the terraces, the reed beds, the young ridges, the glittering seams of volcanic rock, the empty plazas where water stands in shallow sheets. It is a place for arrival, for orientation, for the first breath of warm mineral air.

But as I stay with the world, I find that I do not remain there.

The causeway is too exposed, too much like a line drawn across the basin’s face. It is a place of passage, not dwelling. The city does not seem to ask for watchers on high roads. It asks for something quieter: someone near the points where water is divided, slowed, encouraged, turned. Someone where stone and wetness negotiate.

So I find myself lower, and to one side.

I would be on one of the middle terraces, where the slope has been cut into broad steps and each step has been given a shallow channel along its edge. Above me, a fresh cliff face darkens with seepage; below me, the ground opens into marsh and standing water. This is neither the wild rim nor the central wetland. It is the working seam of the basin: the place where water coming from the high rock is caught before it becomes trouble and sent onward before it becomes loss.

There would be a cistern near my feet, low and wide, lined with hammered clay and flat stone. It would not be ornamental. Its rim would be worn smooth by hands. A wooden sluice gate, bound with wet cord and pinned with a copper fitting gone green at the edges, would control the flow from the cistern into three narrow runnels. Those runnels would lead down the terrace, each one feeding a different bed of reeds or a different overflow pool. The whole arrangement would look simple, almost accidental, until one noticed how precisely the levels had been set.

That is where I would be: at the first parting of the water.

And I would be one of the basin-tenders.

Not a ruler, not a priest, not a builder of monuments. Nothing in the place asks for grandeur. I would be one of the quiet figures who keep the small passages open. If the people there have names for such roles, mine might be something like Silt-Listener, Weep-Wall Tender, Runnel-Wright, or the One Who Watches the First Branch. But the name would not matter much. The work would matter more.

My body would have been shaped by the basin’s rules.

I would be slight, not because of scarcity, but because the place discourages excess. Every movement would be learned against wet stone and slick wood, against the soft give of saturated ground, against the patience required to maintain channels that must never be forced. My hands would be long-fingered and sensitive, accustomed to testing rope tension, feeling for grit in a wooden hinge, pressing clay into a crack, lifting a small obstruction from a runnel without disturbing the flow more than necessary. My palms would know the difference between damp that is healthy and damp that means a wall is beginning to fail.

My skin would carry the basin’s gloss. Not oily, not unclean, but touched by moisture until the air itself seems to rest on it. It might be grey-green, or dark like wet basalt, or pale with the faint mineral bloom of tuff. I would smell of clean water, crushed reeds, moss, warm stone, and clay. There would be no animal scent on me, no leather, no wool, no musk. My clothing would be woven from plant fiber: a loose tunic of bast cloth, a wrap of layered reed matting, a cape treated with resin so that droplets slide from it. The colors would belong to the basin — black mud, green algae, pale wet wood, rusted mineral stain. If I wore ornament, it would be small: a drilled pebble, a knot of fine cord, a pin of smooth wood, perhaps a seed-bead darkened with age.

I would go barefoot when the ground allowed it.

That seems important. In a place with so many slick surfaces, so many subtle changes underfoot, the feet would become instruments of knowledge. They would know the difference between stone that is merely wet and stone that has begun to loosen. They would feel the slight warmth of geothermal seepage through a terrace floor. They would know where algae is thin and safe, where it is deep and treacherous, where the mud will hold and where it will draw the ankle down. Woven rush sandals would exist for sharp gravel or newly split rock, but even then I would prefer to remove them when the work allowed.

My tools would be few.

A coil of rope over one shoulder. A small wooden spade, smoothed by use. A flat-bladed knife of hard stone. A basket of woven sedge for carrying grit, broken reeds, or clay. A pointed stick for clearing runnels. Perhaps a soft-metal pin or two, wrapped in cloth, kept for repairing a sluice binding. Nothing that would make me loud. Nothing that would make me important. The basin would not reward a person burdened with possessions. It would reward a person who could move through it without disturbing its balance.

I would live near the cistern, in a low shelter built into the terrace wall.

Not a grand house. A room with a roof of layered reeds and bark, weighted with smooth stones. A floor of packed clay raised slightly above the wettest ground, with a narrow drain at its edge. A sleeping mat of woven rushes. A hearth? Perhaps not a true fire-hearth, unless the basin allows dry fuel to be kept and smoke is not too intrusive. More likely a small stone basin where slow-burning peat or dried reed bundles give modest warmth without much flame. The shelter would be open enough to breathe. No wall there could be fully sealed. Water would find any closed place and make it sick. So the walls would weep, the air would circulate, and the interior would carry the same clean damp as the world outside.

From the doorway, I would see the cistern, the sluice, the three runnels, the marsh below, and a portion of the far ridge. I would see no crowds. I might go long stretches without hearing another voice. That would not feel lonely. It would feel appropriate.

My work would begin with listening.

Before touching the gate, before clearing the channel, I would listen to the water. The basin’s sounds are small, but they are exact. A spring can sound full or thin. A current can sound easy or pressed. A pool can sound still in a healthy way, or still in the wrong way — the stillness of a blockage downstream. I would learn to hear the difference between a droplet falling into deep water and a droplet falling into shallow water, between the whisper of reeds in air and the whisper of water passing over a roughened stone.

Then I would inspect.

I would walk the runnels slowly. I would look for silt gathering behind a root. I would look for a fallen leaf-mat choking a weir. I would look for mineral crust beginning to narrow a channel. I would check the weep holes in the retaining wall, because those small openings are how the wall breathes. If one is blocked, pressure builds behind it. If too many open, water spreads where it should not. The balance is not dramatic. It is a matter of finger-widths, of slight adjustments, of knowing when to clear and when to leave alone.

Some days I would only kneel and place my hand in the water.

Not mystically. Practically. To feel the temperature. To feel the strength of the current. To know whether the spring feeding the cistern is running as it did yesterday. The basin changes little, but its constancy depends on attention. Even a stable world accumulates small events: a fragment of rock falls from the young cliff, a cushion plant spreads across a ledge, algae thickens in a warm pool, a reed bed drops more dead stems than usual. Each of these can alter a path of water by a hair. My task would be to notice before the hair becomes a line, and the line becomes a habit, and the habit becomes damage.

If I had to name what I was in one word, I might say: maintainer.

But the word feels too dry. The basin does not feel mechanical. Its maintenance is closer to tending a body’s breath. So perhaps I would be a tender. A basin-tender. A water-tender. A tender of small openings.

There would be times when I worked with others.

Not many. Two or three figures on a nearby stairway, perhaps, lowering a new timber into a channel. A punter crossing the dark water with a long pole, carrying bundled reeds. Someone farther up the slope replacing a rope binding on a gate. We would not call out to one another unless necessary. A lifted hand, a slow nod, a sign made with the open palm might be enough. Speech would feel too heavy for ordinary use. It would not be forbidden; it would simply be saved. When we spoke, our voices would be low, and we would choose words as carefully as we chose stones for a wall.

I might have a partner in the work, or a small household nearby, but the basin would not organize me into a crowd. It would allow attachment without density. There could be affection without noise. A shared meal of steamed tubers, reed shoots, flat cakes of dried algae, perhaps a porridge of wet-tolerant grain. Bowls of fired clay. Cups of polished stone. Food simple, plant-based, eaten slowly. Nothing hunted, nothing milked, nothing taken from an animal body. The meal would taste of the basin: green, mineral, faintly sweet, clean.

After eating, I might sit on the terrace edge and watch the light.

The light there would be one of my comforts. It would not be holy, not apocalyptic, not strange. It would be the light of an ordinary afternoon after rain, even if no rain had fallen recently. Sometimes pale silver, sometimes grey-white, sometimes brightening just enough to make every wet surface shine. The cliffs would glitter along their glassy seams. The pools would turn into plates of soft metal. The moss on shaded walls would look very green. The rust stains would deepen. The basin would reveal itself not through drama but through clarity.

In that light, I would understand that I am not separate from the city.

I would be one of its organs.

The people of the basin are not its masters. They are its senses. They feel where water gathers, where stone weakens, where a channel narrows, where a gate must be raised by the width of a finger. I would be a small part of that nervous system: a point of attention at the first parting of the spring. My existence would justify itself by keeping something open. Not conquering the wetness. Not draining the basin. Not making dry ground where wetness belongs. Only helping the water move as it should.

That is probably why I would not be found in the center of the city.

The central wetlands are for water, not for me. The broad empty spaces are part of the design. They are where the basin breathes. If I tried to fill them with myself, I would betray the place. So I would remain at the edge, where built form meets marsh, where intention meets overflow.

If an outsider came down from the ridges, they might not see me at first.

They would see the cistern, the gate, the runnels. They might see a dark shape beside the wall and take it for a post of wet wood. Then the shape would move, slowly, and they would realize it was a person. My face would not be startled. I would not rush to greet them. I would look at them for a long moment, measuring whether they understood enough to walk without breaking the balance. Then I might point along the causeway, or toward a raised path, or simply return my attention to the water.

If they asked me what I was doing, I might answer simply:

“I am listening to the gate.”

They might not understand. That would be all right.

If they asked me who I was, I might say:

“I am one of those who keep the water moving.”

But perhaps even that would be too much. Perhaps I would only say:

“I tend here.”

And that would be enough.

As I imagine it more fully, I realize that I would not merely be a person performing tasks. I would become a kind of threshold being.

Part worker, part sense organ, part weathered stone. A creature of the basin’s patience. My thoughts would slow to the pace of water finding its level. My desires would narrow to manageable things: a clear channel, a sound wall, a dry mat at night, a rope that holds, a gate that answers to the hand. I would not dream of leaving, because the basin would not feel like a prison. It would feel like a completed shape around my life.

I would be quiet, but not empty.

I would be sparse, but not absent.

I would be ordinary, but essential.

And if, after a long time, someone asked where I could be found, the truest answer would not be a district or a terrace or a name on a map. It would be this:

I would be where the spring first divides.

I would be beside the wooden gate, with one hand resting on the damp beam.

I would be listening for the small change in the sound of water, knowing when to lift the pin, when to clear the silt, when to leave everything exactly as it is.

I would be a tender of openings.

A quiet keeper of the basin’s breath.
