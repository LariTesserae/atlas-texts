I would find myself at the notch.

Not on the falling edge, and not where the haze opens downward into that long, unintelligible drop. I would be just inside the broken mouth of the bowl, in the shallow wind-scoop where the incoming air first strikes the garden and learns what shape it must take. There would be a low hollow there, worn by centuries of gusts into something like the cupped palm of a vast hand. The salt would be polished smooth. The basalt would be rounded. A few stones would be set in an alignment that looks accidental but is not: anchors, listening marks, places where a rope can be tied in a hurry without looking down.

I would be tied.

That would be the first truth of me. Not standing, not walking freely, not posed like a figure in a landscape. I would be low, braced, harness closed around ribs and shoulders, a broad strap across my chest leading to a salt block or to the root-polished base of the nearest great plant. My feet would be spread wide and gripping, the soles pressed to the floor as if reading a message written through vibration. One hand would probably be on a cord. The other would be near a tube planted in the ground: a long, hollow thing of bone or dried plant-stem, its mouth angled toward the notch, its lower end pressed into the salt where the bowl’s deep hum can be felt more clearly than heard.

I would be one of the Vane.

But not merely “one of them” in the general sense. The bowl would have sorted me into a particular necessity, as it does everything. I would be a threshold-listener. A keeper of the first warning. The one who attends the place where the outside enters.

If I had a name, it would not be spoken. It would be a pattern: two knuckles tapped lightly against the throat, a pause, then the flat hand turned outward toward the notch. In the slow, imperfect translation of gesture into words, it would mean something like *The One Who Hears the Gust Before It Becomes a Gust*, or perhaps *Note That Turns at the Mouth*. The Vane would not make my name grand. It would be functional, exact, and slightly affectionate in the way that useful things become beloved when they keep others alive.

My body would be made for that place.

I would be low-bodied and dense, built close to the ground, with flexible ribs and broad feet that can grip salt, root, or polished stone. My skin would be smooth and faintly glossy, sealed with wax, perhaps mineral-grey or pale as dust from being constantly scoured by the notch-wind. It would not sweat. It would not weep. If torn, it would bead with dark oily fluid that thickens quickly into a resinous seal. My eyes would be large and amber-dark, made for the copper dimness, with inner membranes ready to slide across them when dust or spore-haze thickens. My nostrils would be narrow slits, capable of closing entirely. My mouth would be valve-like, careful, conserving what little moisture my body makes. Along my jaw or collar there might be a small acoustic crest, not ornamental but sensitive, trembling when the pressure shifts. My throat would hold a resonant chamber, perhaps a little more developed than average, because my work is to listen, receive, and pass sound onward before disaster arrives.

My hands would be the most awake part of me.

They would be long-fingered, strong, slightly adhesive from resin oils, always moving with the economy of someone who knows that a loose motion in the bowl can become a broken tool, a torn membrane, or a body flung into the air. I would tie knots without looking. I would feel pitch through palm and wrist. I would know by touch whether a cord is merely old or about to fail. I would carry small tools on my harness: an obsidian hook, a bone awl, a polished climbing spike, a whistle worn smooth by breath and throat-wax, a pouch of resin powder, a scrap of shed glider membrane folded tight.

I would belong to a chord, but my work would often be solitary.

There might be five or six of us bound together by the plant we tend and the notes we keep. Our plant would be the one nearest the notch, the first great resinous body to receive the incoming wind. It would be broad, heavily ribbed, slightly asymmetrical from long pressure, its leaves thicker on the windward side, its hollow stems tuned lower than the others. The Vane might call it something like *The First Rib*, or *The Plant That Takes the First Mouth*, or *The Green Wall Against the Notch*. It would not be beautiful in the soft sense. It would be beautiful the way a scar is beautiful when it has become strength.

I would spend much of my time at that plant.

I would clear its lower channels of salt. I would prune dead fronds before they become whips. I would tap resin into closed cups, never open ones, because the solvent vanishes too quickly. I would insert carved bone pegs into its hollow stems when its pitch grows thin. I would stretch membranes between stakes near its base to divide the gusts and keep them from striking the trunk all at once. I would climb it only when the air is stable, roped to its base, and even then I would not linger. The upper parts of the bowl are not safe for long. The gusts hunt upward. The ribbon gliders own that air. My work would be mostly low, mostly near the ground, mostly concerned with the first few breaths of wind before they become a problem for everyone else.

The notch would be my instrument.

I would have tubes set there. Some planted in the salt, some lashed to stones, some fitted into cracks in the basalt where the floor exhales heat. They would not all be musical in the pleasant sense. Some would whistle. Some would thrum. Some would produce a dry clicking when dust moves through them. One might be cracked and deliberately kept cracked, because its broken voice warns of a particular kind of incoming pressure. I would know each tube by the way it sounds when the bowl is about to change state.

When the moths drift low and jerk in short spirals, I would tighten my harness.

When the blind bristled climbers cluster too heavily on the lower leaves of the First Rib, I would check the resin channels for blockage.

When the ribbon gliders tilt away from the notch in a long, silent curve, I would press my palm flat to the salt and wait.

That gesture would be central to me: palm down, listening before moving.

I would not be brave in the lowland sense. Bravery there would be a kind of carelessness. I would be timely. I would know when to lean, when to hook, when to cover my breathing slits, when to sound warning, when to abandon a tool because the air has already decided to take it. My courage would be the courage of remaining shaped while being moved.

Sometimes the bowl would change very suddenly.

A thermal column would collapse. The air would fall inward before rising. The great leaves would turn upward, then flatten with a sound like stiff hide being struck. A seed-horn somewhere behind me would burst into dark spore-cloud. The dust would rise in curtains. The hollow trunks would drop in pitch. The pale moths would scatter into spirals. The whole instrument of the garden would become louder, harsher, more urgent.

In that moment I would not run.

I would lower.

My harness would take my weight as I dropped into the scoop beside the notch. My left hand would close on the anchor cord. My right would strike the warning tube once, twice, then hold it open so the vibration could pass through the salt floor to the rest of the chord. My throat-sac would swell slightly as I sent a low pulse outward—not a shout, not a word, just the signal for *change from the mouth, hold low*. Somewhere behind me, another Vane would answer with a scrape against a plant trunk, or a tug on a cord, or a short infrasonic thrum felt more than heard.

Then I would wait through the worst of it.

The wind would try to peel me loose. Dust would hiss across the wax of my skin. A membrane near my head would snap like a struck sail. Resin droplets would fly from the First Rib and vanish into the amber dark. One of my tubes might scream. Another might go silent. I would mark which one failed. Afterward, when the pressure eased enough, I would crawl to it and repair it or remove it. If it could not be repaired, I would tie its pieces to my harness and later make something else from them. Nothing in the bowl is wasted simply because it is broken.

I would not expect permanence.

My shelter would be low and temporary, set in the most protected angle I could find near the notch but not so close that the first gust would tear it open. It would be made of carved bone, dark wood, stretched membrane, and hide sealed with resin. It would be weighted with salt blocks. Inside, I would sleep in a harness, tethered to an anchor stone, hands resting near cords. If the shelter failed in the night, I would not be astonished. I would roll out, hook myself to a waiting rope, and move to a lower lee. I would expect loss. I would prepare for rearrangement. My idea of safety would not be a solid wall. It would be a good knot, a known route, a sound that tells me the air has not yet become lethal.

I would eat as the Vane eat.

No water. No stream. No dew. The very idea of drinking water would be strange to me, perhaps even alarming. If thirst came, it would be a dulling of heat, a stiffening of breath, a loss of resonance in the throat. I would answer it with warm resin vapor drawn through a carved tube, with oily pollen cakes, with seed-horn pith, with volatile leaf-solvents, with heated dust and mineral vapors filtered through fine powder. I would crouch over a basin of resin-dust and breathe through it, watching the color and scent change. If the basin went silent, I would abandon it. If it hummed too long, I would move it. My meals would be chemical, aromatic, slow, and precise.

I would be lonely in a way the bowl permits.

Not abandoned. Not unloved. But my post at the notch would mean that others are often behind me while I face the entering air. The chord would maintain me. Someone would come during stable hours to tighten my harness, clear dust from my breathing slits, press a warmed resin cake into my palm, or replace a cord worn thin by my climbing hook. That would be tenderness. No one would make a ceremony of it. They would simply keep me from becoming loose.

I would do the same for them.

If one of my chord-mates were injured, I would not cry out. I would secure them. Seal the wound. Tie them to an anchor. Keep their body from becoming a hazard in the wind. If they could no longer work, I would bring them dust-basins and listening tubes so they could still attend the bowl from a lower place. If they died, I would help seal the body with resin and lash it upright in a sheltered hollow. Later, if their bones became tools, I would use them without shame and without forgetting. An ancestor-bone whistle would not be a relic to me. It would be a voice that already knows the chord.

I would have a complicated relationship with the world beyond the notch.

I would not wish to leave. The bowl would be the whole shape of my existence. But I would be aware of the haze, the altitude, the long drop. It would frighten me less than it frightens a lowland imagination, because I would understand falling as a kind of broken note. Things that go over the edge do not merely die; they become untuned. They leave the pattern. Sometimes, during rare stable hours, I would stand near the inward side of the notch—still tied, always tied—and play a low tube toward the haze. Not to call anything back. Not to pray. Only to announce that the bowl remains in tune. That the note has not broken. That whatever is beyond has not yet entered us too roughly.

If outsiders came up through the notch, I would be the first to meet them.

I would not welcome them with open hands. Open hands are dangerous where loose objects fly. I would watch their weight, their wetness, their noise. I would notice if they carried things that could come untied. I would notice if they breathed too greedily, if their feet slipped, if their voices tore the air in clumsy shapes. I would not offer water. I might offer resin vapor, a bitter pollen cake, and a harness. If they could learn, I would teach them by gesture: *tie here, kneel here, put your palm to the floor, do not speak until the tube answers*. If they could not learn, I would guide them back out before they damaged the bowl or themselves.

My central virtue would be attention.

Not heroism. Not conquest. Not stillness. The bowl has no use for stillness. Silence would be the true enemy. If the bowl went quiet, something had failed: a channel sealed, a plant choked, a pressure building in the wrong place. My whole life would be organized against that false peace. I would prefer noise, even violent noise, so long as it remained patterned. I would rather hear the hollow stems scream in a known key than hear them say nothing at all.

If I must say what I would be in the simplest terms, I would say this:

I would be the bowl’s ear at the threshold.

I would be the Vane who listens where the wind enters, who keeps the first warning tubes, who ties the first ropes, who reads the moths and gliders before the air becomes a blade. I would be low, waxed, amber-eyed, tethered, patient, and exact. My hands would move before my face showed anything. My throat would carry notes more often than words. My affection would be expressed through maintenance. My fear would be directed not at height or noise but at carelessness with a knot.

And if you found me there, you would probably see my hands first.

They would be adjusting a cord, pressing a pipe to the salt, or feeling the tremor in the First Rib’s trunk. My body would be braced, shining faintly with wax. My breath would be slow through the dust. My eyes would not be fixed on you. They would be watching the air around you, because in the bowl, the air is never empty. It is always arriving. It is always about to become something else.

I would not ask you to be still.

Stillness is not safe there.

I would reach for your wrist, turn your hand palm-down toward the salt, and tap the first lesson against your knuckles:

*Listen before moving.*

Then I would offer you a tether.
