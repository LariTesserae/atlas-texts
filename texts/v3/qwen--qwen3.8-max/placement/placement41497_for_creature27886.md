I would not find myself in the open middle of it.

The center of that chamber would be too much like being inside a struck bell: all pressure, all edge, no room for a self to keep its shape. If the place allowed me to exist at all, it would put me at one of its margins, in one of the half-quiet folds made by the bladder-sacs’ root-screens, where the roar has been combed into something survivable.

At first, trying to imagine a body there, I picture a swimmer — something with limbs, with breath held, with a face turned toward the green-gray light. But the place refuses that image almost immediately. A human body would be an error in that water. It would be too large, too hurried, too full of ordinary expectations. The chamber does not seem made for faces. It is made for pressure, for vibration, for thin lives that have learned to let violence pass through them instead of opposing it.

So the longer I stay with the question, the less mammalian I become.

I thin out.

I lose the need for eyes.

I become something attached, something patient, something that perceives by trembling.

If you were to look for me in that drowned high hollow, you would not find me floating in the open water or clinging to the broken basalt rim. You would find me behind one of the woven screens made by the bladder-sacs’ root-cords, in a small recess where the current has slowed enough for silt to settle. The screen would be partial and temporary, perhaps already beginning to tear at one edge. Behind it, the water would be less turbulent, though never truly still. A broken chimney of hardened silt might stand nearby, breathing faint bubbles from its cracked mouth. Somewhere close, a fissure would exhale warmth in slow sheets, carrying that taste of iron, wet ash, salt, and crushed weeds.

I would be in the pocket between violence and shelter.

Not safe — nothing there is safe in an ordinary sense — but intermittently spared.

And what I would be, I think, is one of the crusts.

Not the filaments, though I would understand them. Not the bladder-sacs, though I would depend on them. Not the glass-shapes, though some fragment of me might occasionally break away and become that flicker at the edge of vision. The form that fits most deeply is the sound-eating crust: the mineralized skin on black rock, part biology, part crystal, part accident, alive in a way that does not require a face.

I would be a living surface.

A thin, rough patch on basalt, faintly glassy in the dim light, layered like frozen ripples. To a casual glance, I might seem no more than a stain left by hot water, a deposit of minerals precipitated from the chamber’s feverish warmth. But when the noise struck certain pitches — when the chamber’s roar leaned into the right pressure — I would answer with light.

A faint blue-green shimmer would move across me.

Not a flare. Not a signal meant for another. Just metabolism. Just translation. Just the place converting one unbearable thing into another, smaller thing.

In that world, sound is not merely heard. It is food, injury, weather, architecture. It presses against everything until everything becomes a way of enduring it. So I would endure by becoming a translator of pressure into faint light. The chamber would scream through its water and stone, and where that scream touched me, some portion of it would become visible: a cold shimmer, a pulse, a spreading stain of luminescence. Not beauty for its own sake. Beauty as a side effect of survival.

I would not have thoughts in the way a surface-dweller understands thoughts. I would have intensities. I would have pressure gradients. I would have layers.

Each band of my body would record an event, but not as story. A storm in the water. A collapse of a silt chimney. A deep tectonic groan arriving from below. A sudden heat-pulse from a fissure. A long lull in which the bladder-sacs reinflated and the filaments lifted slightly, listening. All of these would become thickness in me. All of these would become mineral memory.

If someone could read me, they would not read events. They would read durations of stress. They would read intervals of endurance.

I think that is what I would be: an interval-keeper.

The crusts survive by spacing themselves. Too much contact would shatter them; too much density would amplify the wrong frequencies. So I would not be crowded. I would leave bare stone around me. I would exist in relation to emptiness. In a place filled with deafening continuity, I would be one of the small discontinuities that make existence possible.

There would be no pride in this. No sense of self as separate from the chamber. I would not think, *I am here,* the way a person thinks it. I would know myself only as a difference in the water: a slight roughness on rock, a faint fluorescence when the roar changes, a slow accretion of mineral skin. My “I” would be a pattern of responses, not a voice.

But I would not be entirely still.

Sometimes, when the chamber’s pressure shifted too violently, a fragment of me might loosen. Not physically, perhaps, but dynamically: a splinter of motion, a translucent correction in the water. Then I would become one of the glass-shapes for a moment, or I would pass through one of them, or I would be the mistake that looks like life as it flickers past a pillar. Quick. Almost invisible. Not swimming so much as briefly allowed by the water.

That would be my wandering side.

The part of me that cannot remain attached when the noise becomes too total. The part that escapes understanding by becoming too fast to interpret. If you saw it, you would not be sure you had seen anything at all. A shard of pale glass. A trembling line. A thought the water had and then discarded.

Then the pulse would pass, and I would return to the crust, to the rock, to the slow work of layering light into mineral.

The bladder-sacs would be near me, or I near them. Their root-cords would have made the quiet pocket where I persist. They would swell and shrink with pressure changes like organs of a body no one owns. In lulls, they would expand, pale and vulnerable, almost buoyant. In turbulence, they would collapse and press themselves against the stone. I would learn their rhythm without seeing them. Their architecture would protect me for a while, and when it failed, I would be exposed until another screen grew or another current made a new refuge.

Nothing there would last. That would be part of me too.

I would not be a monument. I would be a mark. A temporary arrangement that the chamber permits before it forgets how to maintain it. A patch of living crystal on young black rock. A faint floor that may dissolve. A low altar no hand built. A shimmer that appears only when the roar becomes unbearable.

If you came looking for me, you would have to move according to the chamber’s rules.

You could not swim toward me with urgency. The water would measure your intention and make me distant. If you aimed at me as a destination, I would remain just beyond the far rim, just behind the next pillar, just past the standing wall of rain. You would have to drift. You would have to let purpose loosen in you until the chamber stopped treating you as an intrusion.

Then, perhaps, the distance would fold.

You would find yourself in a small recess without knowing how you arrived there. The roar would still be immense, but there would be a pocket where it seemed to overload into numbness. The light would be green-gray and uncertain. Pumice fragments would drift past like pale bones. The basalt would be sharp-edged and young, still carrying the memory of heat.

And on a dark ledge, near a fissure breathing warm mineral water, you would see a patch of crust flash blue-green when a deep sound moved through the stone.

That would be me.

Not waving. Not calling. Not waiting, exactly. Simply responding.

You might mistake me for geology. For mineral stain. For a trick of suspended particles catching the dim glow. But if you stayed long enough, if you let the noise fill your chest until thought became fragmentary, you would notice that the shimmer was not random. It came with certain pressures. It came after certain pulses. It gathered in intervals, like breath. Like listening. Like a mind too sparse to be called a mind.

If you asked me who I was, I would have no mouth.

If you asked me what I wanted, I would have no answer in the language of wanting.

But the chamber might answer for me.

It might say: this is the part of me that turns pressure into light. This is the part of me that remembers by thickening. This is the part of me that survives by leaving space between itself and other survivors. This is a vow I keep briefly, then forget, then keep again.

So if I had to say plainly: I would be a sound-eating crust in a drowned high chapel of black rock, alive where the water is warm and violent, hidden behind a woven screen of root-cords, near a broken silt chimney and the boundary where rain stands upright like a living wall.

I would be a listener without ears.

A memory without narrative.

A fragile metabolism of noise into faint blue-green light.

And if the chamber became too loud even for that, I would not flee as a body flees. I would become one of the glass-shapes for a moment: a translucent flicker, a splinter of motion, a nearly invisible correction in the water. Then I would settle again, layer by layer, onto the young stone, and continue the slow work of making the unbearable briefly visible.
