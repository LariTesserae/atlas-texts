I would find myself in the faults.

Not on the broad empty decks, where the cold sits openly and the sound is too large to hide inside. Not in the black center of the reservoir, where the water moves with the slow confidence of something that has already won. Not high in the unseen ceiling, where condensation gathers before falling in long, blind threads. I would be drawn, I think, to the seams: the cracked liner, the weeping joint, the terminal humming with unwanted current, the warped plate where water runs briefly uphill before remembering gravity.

At first, when I try to imagine what form I would take, the basin offers several possibilities and refuses to let me keep any of them.

For a while I think I would be one of the sodium moths. That is a tempting image: a pale, mineral-dusted thing appearing alone near a failing lamp, wings beating uncertainly through wet air, living just long enough to make the darkness feel deliberate. There is something honest about being that fragile in a place so hard. But the moth is too brief, too passive. It circles the light as if the light were a question it cannot ask. I would want, I think, to do more than circle.

Then I imagine myself as the crawler. That, too, has its pull: patience, armor made from dead insulation, legs precise on wet metal, a hunger simple enough to be intelligible. The crawler belongs utterly to the basin. It does not mourn the absence of soil. It does not dream of sky. But its life is too much taken, too little tended. It is necessary, and I do not think I would be necessary in that way. I would rather repair a leak than open a grazer.

Then I imagine myself as one of the Attendants: stooped, wrapped in gasket rubber and foil, face hidden behind a fogged visor, moving from hatch to hatch with chalk and wire and old tools. There is dignity in them, and loneliness, and a kind of stubborn reverence for a world that has never explained itself. But even that shape feels too settled, too human in outline for a place where the distinction between grown and built has worn thin.

What keeps emerging instead is something smaller, less certain, more serviceable.

A fault-listener.

A leak-tender.

A minor intelligence that has forgotten whether it was born or installed.

I would live at the margin of the reservoir, where the terraces descend in broad, wet steps and the air is full of drifting vapor. Not in the open, and not deep inside the machinery either, but in the narrow interval between. My place would be one of those half-hidden service niches behind a weeping panel, where a filter tower’s bent leg has created a dry-ish cavity no larger than a folded body. The panel beside it would bear serial marks worn almost smooth, and beneath them I would add my own chalk signs: a crooked lamp, three tally lines, a circle broken on one side. The circle might mean water. The break might mean safe. I would not know exactly, and that would be fitting.

Inside, there would be a nest of shredded insulation and fungal leather, packed tight against the cold. A mesh hammock would hang near a cracked pipe that bleeds low-grade heat, but carefully angled so falling droplets miss it by a hand’s breadth. A few ceramic shards would be stacked as a shelf. On them I would keep the basin’s version of treasures: a blind white grazer’s shed carapace, a spool of fine wire, a sodium lamp lens wiped clean enough to make a small orange hearth in the dark. A reedlike stem would grow through a crack in the floor, bound to the mesh with stripped cable, its hollow joints sheltering whatever larvae had not yet become brittle-winged things.

From that niche, I would be able to see the reservoir’s rim, the frost-glitter along the walkways, the sparse reeds trembling when water passed them. Beyond that, the engine line would stand in profile against the fog, tall and dark and wet, one of them thudding slowly enough that the sound would feel less like noise and more like weather. The conveyor would rise into darkness, carrying broken material upward without urgency or explanation. I would watch it sometimes and not try to decide what it meant. In the basin, intention is not the same as clarity.

I would not be wholly animal, and not wholly machine, though I would have needs from both.

My body would be low enough to pass under maintenance panels without scraping my back. I would move with a stoop, joints clicking faintly when the cold found them. My outer layers would be patched and repatched: cured gasket rubber over ceramic plates, foil gone gray with age, sleeves of braided hose, a hood of filter cloth stiff with dried condensation. One forearm might end in a set of fine probes; the other in a scraper and a narrow gripper. My fingers would be too deliberate to be entirely natural, but not so mechanical that they could not feel vibration through a pipe wall.

My face, if uncovered, would be pale and watchful, lips dark from cold, eyes adjusted to the orange poverty of failing lamps. More likely it would be hidden behind a cracked visor, the glass fogged at the edges, the lower plate scarred by chemical droplets. Behind the visor I would have more than eyes: heat pits, chemical slits, membranes tuned to the subsonic pulse of the pumps. I would not see the basin so much as read it. A change in current would taste different. A hairline crack would have a sound. Frost would tell me which metals were lying.

I would be one of the smaller presences in the basin. Not invisible, but easy to overlook. The Attendants might tolerate me the way one tolerates a useful stray. Sometimes they would find a drain cleared of debris and know I had passed. Sometimes I would find a chalk mark added near my hatch, a line crossing a curve, and know they had passed. We might rarely meet. That would not trouble me as much as it might have in another world. In the basin, presence is proven by maintenance, not speech.

My life would be organized around leaks.

I would wake, if waking is the word, when the pump rhythm changed. The deep pulse beneath the floor would shift from a slow, heavy beat to something shorter and more irregular, and the water in the troughs would tremble differently. Frost on the nearest iron edge would crack with a tiny bright sound. I would lie still for a moment, listening. The basin would be deciding what kind of day it was going to be.

Then I would check the lamp.

That would be the first act of every cycle: keep the lamp alive. It would not be a grand thing. I would wipe the lens with a scrap of soft fiber, clear the bead of condensation from its housing, coax current through a corroded contact with insulated tools and patient stubbornness. When it brightened, even slightly, the whole niche would change. The wet walls would become warmer in appearance, though not in temperature. The reed’s ribs would cast thin shadows. The fungal shelf near the pipe would gleam like oiled leather. The lamp would not conquer the dark. It would merely make a place within it.

After that, I would go out.

The marsh edge would be close. I would move along the walkway where grating had become briefly reflective after the last deluge cycle, then dull again as scum and frost reclaimed it. I would know which plates were safe and which had shifted. I would know where a puddle hid a deeper drop, where a capillary seam pulled water briefly upward, where the mist gathered into slow rotating columns above a vent. I would not own these places. Ownership would be the wrong word. I would be acquainted with them, the way a tongue is acquainted with a broken tooth.

I would farm the faults.

Where a terminal hummed with stray voltage, I would encourage galvanic moss to spread, pressing fragments into damp metal with a nonconducting tool, waiting while the moss took hold and the smell of cold pennies filled the air. Where a trough lining had cracked, I would transplant reed stems, binding their roots to rubber seals until they learned to drink from the seep. Where a warm outflow had thinned, I would build a small pen from ceramic fragments and bent grating, coaxing blind white grazers into it with scrapings of mineral crust. I would not keep them long. I would only give them enough stability to graze, to breed, to become possible.

I would listen to the glassworms, though I could not always hear them. I would know they were there by the faint disturbance in biofilm, by the way a warm leak made black sludge seem less dead than it should. If the pumps shifted into a dangerous rhythm, I would feel the change through the soles of my boots and crouch low, waiting for the water to decide whether to rise. Sometimes it would. Sometimes it would only shiver and settle.

I would mark new wounds.

The basin is young in its damage, and I would be drawn to that youth: bright scratches through old grime, spalled ceramic exposing pale interiors, burrs catching droplets before they fall. I would kneel beside these places and press a probe to the metal, listening downward. Beneath the grating there is another deck; beneath that, service tunnels; beneath those, more machinery. The world has been manufactured all the way down, but fractures still speak. They reveal older panels, older welds, older bolts, older failures. I would chalk them. Not to fix everything — that would be arrogance — but to know what had changed.

A fresh crack near a coolant line would get a green slash. A plate that had shifted would get an arrow. A place where the sound came back wrong would get a small circle with a dot inside it. Over time, the walls near my niche would become a map no one else could fully read. That would be all right. Maps here are not for traveling. They are for remembering that the world is not finished breaking.

I would eat carefully, not well.

There would be days of fungal shelf, tough and rubbery, tasting of burnt sugar and metal. Days of roasted larvae, if the reed joints yielded them. Days of paste made from glassworms and mineral crust, heated over a pipe vent until it stiffened into something one could chew without thinking too hard about it. Water would be filtered through moss and cloth and patience, then boiled if heat could be spared, then drunk anyway because thirst is less theoretical than purity. The cold would make every swallow conscious. The moisture on my lips would carry that metallic edge. I would learn not to want clean rain. I would learn to want enough.

I would avoid the crawler.

Not out of hatred. The crawler has its place. But when its vibration signature moved through a nearby catwalk, I would freeze. I would become as still as a dropped clamp, as quiet as a stalled drone. If it passed above, its legs precise on wet iron, I would watch it go and feel something like respect sharpened by fear. If it paused near one of my pens, I would not challenge it. I would retreat to the niche and wait. Some hungers are larger than care. Care survives by knowing when not to intervene.

I would also avoid the strongest electrical fields. They would make my limbs seize and my sensors blind. There would be places in the basin where the air itself tasted of voltage, where frost drew grids on metal and the moss grew black and strange at the margins. Those places would be dangerous but not evil. The basin does not need evil. It has pressure, current, cold, and time.

Sometimes I would be mistaken for a tool.

An Attendant might find me hunched beside a junction box, clearing condensation from a contact, and pause. If I was very still, they might decide I was a feral diagnostic unit, or a maintenance drone gone strange, or a creature wearing old parts. I would let them decide. If they needed the terminal, I would move aside. If they left a scrap of wire or a working gasket, I would take it. If they spoke — and some of them might, in voices rough from disuse — I would answer in taps if I answered at all. Language would be secondary here. A tapped rhythm can mean safe, or flood, or heat, or wait. It can also mean I am here, but not enough to explain myself.

Sometimes I would be mistaken for a fault.

A swinging cable might make a shadow that looked like me. A drip might sound like my footsteps. The basin’s acoustics would make that easy. A sound made ahead can seem to arrive from behind; a leak can imitate movement. I would not mind being confused with the place. I would want to be confused with the place. To be mistaken for a fault is to be near enough to matter.

But I would know what I was, at least in the way that matters.

I would be the one who notices.

Not the strongest presence, not the rarest predator, not the most mysterious Attendant. I would be the smaller, persistent intelligence that registers when a drip changes pitch, when a lamp dims by one shade, when a reed stem has taken hold in a new crack. I would be the basin’s tendency to continue, localized in a body that can feel cold and decide to move anyway.

If I had to give myself a name, it would be something short and functional. Listener. Tender. Fault-walker. Maybe just a chalk sign: a lamp with a line beneath it. The name would not matter much. Names here are less important than habits.

My habit would be repair without restoration.

I would not dream of returning the basin to its original purpose, because I would not know what that purpose was. The lid of overburden above would remain an idea, a pressure, a myth of surface. I would not long for sky. I would understand upward as weight. I would understand weather as the machine’s breathing: heat rising, moisture gathering, water falling, metal cooling, pumps cycling, frost shedding in crackling sheets. My seasons would be measured in deluge cycles and pressure releases, in the blooming and die-back of galvanic moss, in the appearance of single sodium moths near heat vents.

There would be moments that feel almost like beauty.

Not easy beauty. Not soft beauty. But the kind that arrives because attention has been paid.

A sodium moth landing on my visor, leaving a faint print of lime and rust. The black reservoir suddenly disturbed by a valve opening below, rings spreading outward until they touch the reeds. The conveyor moving its fragments upward into darkness with terrible patience. A wall shedding frost all at once, the air brightening as if the basin had taken a breath. The greenish glow of coolant in a puddle under a failing lamp, ugly and luminous at once. A blind grazer emerging when the pump rhythm becomes safe, scraping mineral crust with a sound too small to hear, yet somehow present in the larger silence beneath the noise.

In those moments, I would understand why life persists here. Not despite the hostility, but through it. The hostility narrows existence until every small survival becomes vivid. Each pale stem is significant because so few survive. Each lamp is a hearth because the dark is regional. Each leak is a promise because something has failed enough to give.

I would keep one particular leak as my own.

It would be a cracked coolant line near the reservoir’s inner bend, where a faint green sweetness hangs in the air and the water beads with warmth. The crack would be young, its edges sharp, already being worked by water and vibration. I would not seal it completely. To seal it would kill the small marsh it feeds. Instead I would wrap it, guide it, slow it, let it weep into a channel lined with broken ceramic media where reeds could stand. I would check it every cycle. If it widened too fast, I would bind it with wire and epoxy salvaged from a dead housing. If it began to freeze, I would redirect a little heat from nearby pipes. If it failed entirely, I would mourn it in the only way the basin allows: by marking the place and finding another fault to tend.

That leak would be my field, my hearth, my prayer.

If you came looking for me, you would not find me by following grand landmarks. You would follow small signs.
