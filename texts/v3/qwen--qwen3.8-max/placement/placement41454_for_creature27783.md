At first I think I would be one of the fog farmers, out before any recognizable morning, scraping cold plates and wringing slow water from nets. Then I think I would be an engine tender, living near heat and soot, learning the moods of a machine so old that its original purpose has become a local myth. Then I imagine myself as a bridge inspector, or a keeper of salamanders, or a bell-reader standing at a rail junction while signals arrive from districts that have shifted overnight.

But the longer I let the basin stay in mind, the less I fit myself into any single office. The place is too provisional for that. It asks not for a fixed profession but for a kind of attention. So the image that keeps returning is stranger, quieter, and more suited to the city’s unfinished nature.

I would find myself on a terrace called Wet Step.

Not in the richest district, not in the warmest, not in the most settled. Wet Step would be one of those middle places: high enough to see fog fill the lower basins, low enough to hear the engines turning in their stone chambers. It would sit between a row of condensation towers and an open quarry that no one remembers opening. Behind the terrace, a retaining wall would be furred with moss so dark it looks black in the dim light. Before it, the ground would break into broad emptiness, rail lines, cable anchors, and the pale roofs of lower workshops.

There would be a pool there. Of course there would.

It would stand in a shallow hollow where the rock has not yet decided whether it wants to be floor, basin, or the beginning of a wall. The pool would not be large. It would be the kind of puddle that returns without rain, appearing again in the same place as if it had merely gone elsewhere to think. The people of Wet Step would know it. They would not step in it carelessly. Children would be told not to count the ripples. Workers passing by would glance at it the way sailors glance at water moving strangely beside a hull.

That is where I would be.

I would live in a low house set into the retaining wall behind the condensation plates, built from spore-brick, salvaged timber, and stone that had been tested and proven stable for at least three settling seasons. The roof would be planted with moss and small cushion plants. In damp weather, the roof would breathe green. The walls would be thick on the windward side, thinner near the engine vents where warmth could be borrowed. There would be a small window of cloudy green glass, a door with a salt line worn into the threshold, and three anchor rings set into the floor: one by the bed, one by the workbench, one by the water jar.

Inside, the house would be dim, warm, and crowded with practical things. Drying nets. A coil of waxed cord. A rack of chalk sticks in different grades: soft chalk for fresh stone, hard chalk for proven routes, red chalk for warnings, white chalk for blessings. Jars of salt. A pressure whistle. A lamp with a brass cage. A copper bowl where a salamander rests among wet stones. A shelf of proof-stones, each one marked with the date and place it had remained itself through a night of uncertain weather.

I would be a chalkwright of intermittent routes.

That is not exactly a mapmaker. A mapmaker in the basin would go mad, or worse, would become confident. I would be someone who marks paths that seem likely to remain, and who knows when to erase them. My work would begin with water, because water knows where the ground intends to hold. I would walk the terraces before the first bells, checking condensation plates, listening to pipes, watching where moisture gathers on stone. If damp collects along a seam, that may mean the seam is becoming real. If damp avoids a place that was wet yesterday, something there has begun to refuse itself.

I would carry chalk, salt, wire, a knife, a small hammer, and a pouch of test-dust. My harness would be worn smooth at the shoulders. One strap would always carry a proven ring, clipped low at the hip. I would not go far without being tied, at least in thought, to something known to stay.

My body would be shaped by the basin. Broad-chested from thin air. Slow-pulsed. Hands stained with mineral dust and rust and the pale residue of chalk. My skin would have the cool, slightly greenish undertone of someone who lives under low light and constant damp. My eyes would be used to gradients: fog that is only fog, fog that is hiding a drop, stone that is still soft, stone that is pretending to be hard. I would not squint much. I would blink slowly when flames are lit too near my face, because brightness would be rare and slightly offensive.

I would not be entirely human, or not human in the simple sense. There would be something provisional about me. My shadow might arrive a half-second after I do. In certain lights, my reflection in the pool would appear more finished than my body, as though the water remembers a version of me that has already been accepted. Some people might say I was scaffold-born: one of the valley’s attempts to continue building itself. Others might say I was simply a person found near a stairway that later vanished. I would not correct them. In the basin, origin is less important than continuance.

My household name would be Quiet Mark.

My work name would be Return-Mark, because my task is not only to make a path but to determine whether it can be returned along. My route name would not be spoken often. It would be a short two-note whistle, low then high, used only when crossing open fog fields or calling to workers across a place where sound behaves badly. If someone heard that whistle and could not see me, they would know I was testing a route and would wait before following.

Each day would begin with inspection, not command.

I would wake to the sound of water moving in channels. I would listen before rising. The basin speaks through its pipes, its bells, its dripping roofs, its engines turning far below. If the night had been a settling night, there might be new differences: a step added to a stair, a railing slightly closer to a drop, a wall that now leans toward the horizon with too much confidence. I would note these things without alarm. Alarm is a waste of breath where breath is precious.

Then I would check the pool.

If it reflected the sky above Wet Step, that would be ordinary enough. If it reflected a sky that was not there—clearer, darker, or filled with shapes that have no business moving over stone—then I would mark the hollow with chalk and place a little salt at its edge. If it reflected a city more finished than our own, I would not stare too long. Completion can be a kind of pressure. Some things harden too early if witnessed too eagerly.

After that, I would walk the routes.

The first route would be the water path: cisterns, condensation plates, gutters, overflow channels. I would look for leaks, but not all leaks are bad. Some leaks feed moss beds. Some keep rail beds from becoming brittle. Some remind the stone that water intends to pass there. I would mark good leaks with a small blue chalk sign. Bad leaks, those that soften a foundation or open a false seam, would get red chalk and a wire tag.

The second route would be the rail approach. Not the main line itself—the bridge masters handle that—but the walkways, service stairs, and embankments where goats trim vegetation and beetles work through rust. I would listen for bells. The stocky grazing creatures would be nearby, thick-coated and wide-hooved, their bells slow and low. If their bells sounded wrong, if the rhythm came back dull or doubled or too quick for the slope, I would stop. Animal sound is often more honest than stone.

The third route would be the scaffold line.

This is where my work becomes less engineering and more liturgy.

Scaffolds stand in several places around Wet Step, surrounding buildings that have not decided to exist, or walls that may be arriving later, or openings that are still being considered by the valley. The draft-workers are sometimes seen there: fog-colored figures carrying tools that make no noise. I would not approach them directly. I would leave nails, wire, chalk, or a cup of water near the base of the scaffold. Not as worship. As acknowledgment. Even unfinished beings need witnesses.

My duty would be to keep people from mistaking potential for passage. A scaffold can look like a staircase until you step onto it and find that it leads into an older version of the street. An empty building can look inhabitable until its doors open onto a century that has not been poured yet. I would mark such places with careful signs: a circle broken on the left for “not yet safe,” a line with three dots for “may return by evening,” a salted threshold for “do not speak loudly here.”

I would also tend an engine.

Not one of the vast machines below the basin, whose purposes are half legend and half prayer, but a small condensation engine housed in a brick room near my terrace. Its name would be Low Patience. It would be old, patched, riveted, stained with moss and oil. Its brass fittings would be worn bright where hands touch them. Its iron casing would sweat in the mornings. I would feed it fuel, adjust its valves, clean its filters, and lay one palm against its side to feel whether its rhythm remains steady.

Low Patience would not be merely useful. It would be relational.

Some nights it would run smoothly. Some nights it would complain through its pipes like a tired animal dreaming of load. If its beat faltered, the moss on the terrace above might dry too quickly. If it stopped entirely, no one knows whether the fog would thicken or the stone beneath Wet Step would lose its temporary certainty. So I would sing to it while working, not because sentiment keeps machines alive, but because rhythm helps the hands, and because some engines in the basin are said to settle better when approached with familiar sound.

My song would be simple. A few notes, repeated. A working tune with no triumphant ending. Something you could hum while tying wire or scraping salt from a valve. Children nearby might learn it without meaning to. Goats would ignore it. The salamander in the copper bowl would shift slightly when the lowest note sounded, as if hearing something through water that had not yet arrived.

The people of Wet Step would not treat me as an authority in the grand sense. They would treat me as someone who knows which routes may be trusted until the next fog. They would come to me when a stair has grown a step, when a doorway opens into a room too old for its house, when a bell rings from a tower no one can locate. They would say things like:

“The eastern railing has stopped shedding moss.”

“The quarry opened again, but only on the side that should be solid.”

“My goat will not cross the second bridge.”

“The pool has begun reflecting a city with more chimneys than ours.”

I would listen. I would not promise to fix it. In the basin, fixing is too final a word. I would say, “I will mark it,” or “We will watch until it settles,” or “Keep your anchor dry.”

There would be councils, but they would not be dramatic. They would meet in a long room near the cisterns, under lamps fed by slow gas, with damp maps spread across tables. The maps would be annotated in chalk and corrected often. A bridge master might say which span is real this week. A fog farmer might report that the lower fog is “moving against itself.” A salamander warden might bring word that amphibians have abandoned a pool that had looked reliable for years. I would add my marks: routes closed, routes opened, places where reality feels soft, places where stone has begun to behave too confidently.

No one would ask what the basin is for. That question would be considered impolite, like asking a lung why it breathes. The practical question is always: what holds? What is holding for now? What must be tied down, watered, heated, quieted, marked, or left alone?

Children would be part of my work whether I wished it or not. They would follow me at a distance, learning not to count flocks aloud, learning to distinguish true damp from false damp, learning to carry chalk without breaking it. I would teach them approximate words before precise ones. Not “seven birds,” but “a flockish amount.” Not “ten steps,” but “enough to tire the breath once.” Not “forever,” but “until the next settling.”

I would teach them to make a path visible without insisting that it remain.

That is perhaps the deepest discipline in the basin: to mark without demanding. To build without pretending permanence. To love a place that has not finished becoming itself.

My own life would be small but not lonely. I would share meals near warmth, because heat is communal where warmth is scarce. I would eat bitter orchard fruit softened in compost heat, thick mushroom cakes roasted over engine vents, cheese aged in stone rooms, moss tea that tastes faintly of iron and rain. Sometimes a neighbor would bring goat milk, or beetle-flour bread, or a rare egg from the finches nesting in the bridge supports. We would not speak much. In thin air, silence is not emptiness. It is room enough for breath.

I would have companions, but they would not all be people.

There would be a goat-like creature, broad-backed and calm, with a cracked bell around its neck. I would not own it exactly. It would accompany me because I leave salt in useful places and do not startle it by shouting. Its bell would sound wrong when the ground is false, and I would trust that sound more than my own eyes.

There would be white-eyed finches nesting near the rail curve. I would watch them without disturbing them. If they remain, the bridge supports are likely real. If they leave without pressure change or drought, I would close the walkway below.

There would be carrion birds on the chimneys, patient as inspectors. I would not love them, but I would respect their attention. When they gather at a construction site, work pauses. They may be waiting for death, displacement, or scraps. In the basin, those possibilities are not always separable.

There would be the salamander in the copper bowl. Its name would be First Drip. It would not be a pet in the ordinary sense. It would be an indicator, a colleague, a small moist authority. If First Drip leaves the bowl without apparent reason, I would not use the terrace pool for two days. If it sings—because some amphibians there do sing, a low resonant note made for thin air—I would mark the hour and listen for water remembering itself elsewhere.

There would also be the harder beings.

The fog-fish moving through dense banks, silver interruptions at the edge of sight. I would never count them. I would not try to catch them. I would only note when they appear, because their presence means the fog is thick enough to carry more than weather. The insects that vanish when observed too directly. The footprints without shadows. The wingbeats coming from empty air. I would leave room for them. To deny them would be like denying the fog itself. They are part of the basin’s unfinished population, and my work is not to make them disappear but to keep the living from colliding with them unwisely.

Sometimes, at the scaffold sites, I would see draft-workers clearly. They would be thin and gray, their bodies not quite committed to edges. They would carry beams that weigh nothing, climb rails that should not hold them, and pause as if listening to instructions from a city that has not yet decided on its own dimensions. I would not speak to them. I would set down water or chalk and step back. If one of them looked at me, I would feel less seen than considered, as though the valley were checking whether I, too, might be a temporary feature.

I would wonder, in such moments, whether I am one of them.

Perhaps I would have been made by the basin’s need for attendants. Perhaps I am a person shaped by altitude and damp and careful custom. Perhaps the difference matters less here than elsewhere. In a place where rock can be soft enough to mark with a fingernail and streets can lengthen overnight, identity is also provisional. What matters is whether one keeps faith with the work.

And my work would be this:

to mark the routes that hold,

to erase the routes that do not,

to listen for the engines that keep things settled,

to watch the animals that know before people know,

to leave offerings where unfinished beings are laboring,

and to stand at the edge of the terrace when the fog opens and the basin becomes, for a few moments, less like a city and more like an instrument being tuned by enormous, patient hands.

In the late afternoon—or whatever passes for late afternoon under that pearl-and-slate sky—I would stand by the iron railing furred with moss. Below Wet Step, the valley would fill with fog. A cable car would move across the gap between districts, its lamps dim yellow. Somewhere far beneath, engines would turn with slowness so vast it seems geological. Birds would rise from a chimney in a single dark ribbon. The air would smell of wet rock, coal smoke, ozone, moss, and rust.

The pool behind me would hold its reflection.

In it, the city might appear more finished than the one around me: towers complete, bridges whole, streets continuous, scaffolds gone. Or it might appear less finished: looser, more open, more like a thought the valley has not yet agreed to keep. I would not try to force the reflection into meaning. I would take chalk and draw a thin line around the hollow, not to trap the image but to acknowledge that it had appeared.

Then I would whisper, or perhaps sing, the nearest route-name into the damp air.

Not loudly.

Not finally.

Just enough for the path to remember itself until morning.

That is who I would be in that world: not a hero, not a ruler, not an explorer claiming the basin from above. I would be an attendant of unfinished reality. A chalkwright. A listener at thresholds. A keeper of approximate names. A provisional being with chalk-dusted hands and a harness clipped to what has proven true.

If someone wanted to find me, they should not ask for a fixed address. They should ask where the newest chalk mark is, where the salamanders have gathered, where Low Patience is humming steadily behind its brick wall, where the pool on Wet Step has returned again without rain.

They would find me there.

Marking the edge.

Keeping the anchor dry.

Waiting, without impatience, for the place to settle.
