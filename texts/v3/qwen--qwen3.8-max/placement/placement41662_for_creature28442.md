I would find myself immersed before I found myself located.

There would be no shore to arrive upon, no bank where one could stand and look out, no edge where the world politely separates itself from the sea. The first fact of me in that place would be pressure: not weight, not violence, but attention. The green-gold liquid would already be touching me everywhere, cool and exact, tasting the wrongness I carried in from elsewhere — the residual heat of a dry body, the memory of air, the faint chemical ghost of dead cloth, metal, separation.

So the first place I would know myself would be inside a chord.

The industrial sound of the High Vascular Sea would enter me before I understood it as landscape: the low vascular thrum, the hydraulic creak, the whine of cilia, the distant roar of standing rain-walls holding themselves in impossible equilibrium. I would feel it in the sternum, in the teeth, in the slow spaces between thoughts. I would not yet know that this was not noise but metabolism. I would only know that the world was loud in a way that did not interrupt itself.

I would be suspended, probably, in open water above a translucent stretch of root-floor. Below me, cloud-shadow would move across mountain ridges. The sight would make the body misunderstand itself: to stand, if standing could be called what happened there, would be to stand on the underside of weather, on a living skin stretched over heights. The floor would accept my pressure and then resume itself. Any print I made would close, not quickly, not dramatically, but with the patience of a world that has no interest in being marked by passage.

At first I would be a fever.

Not a monster, not an enemy, not even a guest. A fever. A warm knot in a cool system. A region of too-fast chemistry. The sea would notice me the way a body notices a splinter or a swallowed toxin: by gradient, by taste, by local imbalance. The liquid would thicken slightly around my struggles. If I thrashed, it would become more attentive, not hostile. If I relaxed, it would hold me with a buoyancy that felt almost thoughtful. My heat would be drawn away evenly, not punished, simply redistributed into the vast temperature of the place.

Whatever I brought with me would not survive as property.

Cloth would soften into fiber and be opened by grazer-fish. Metal, if there were any, would be sought out by enzyme centers and drawn into conductive veins. Paper would become pulp, then cellulose, then incorporated into some quiet membrane. The sea would not burn anything, because there is no fire there. It would not bury anything, because there is no refuse. It would simply continue around me until the shape of my separateness became untenable.

The last thing to dissolve would be the habit of thinking myself apart from what surrounds me.

The Tenders would come to me, if coming is the right word, because they would not rush. They would arrive as a station forms: by convergence, by need, by the slow gathering of attention around imbalance. They would look almost human from a distance, if one were desperate enough to impose humanity on them, but up close they would be upright fronds, membranes, layered green-gold tissues, vascular ridges, translucent skin full of moving light. They would have no faces, only regions of attention. I would feel their notice as a change in my own chemistry. My fear would become legible as acidity. My longing would become a salt pattern. My confusion would register as an electrical flicker along borrowed veins.

They would not speak to me.

They would synchronize me.

That is the closest word I can find. They would pulse near me, release dissolved signatures, alter the local charge until my ragged mammalian urgency began to slow. I would try to say something — my name, perhaps, or where I came from — and the attempt would fail as speech. The liquid would take the intention and translate it into pressure, into scent, into a tiny bloom of ions. The Tenders would read that bloom and understand, not the story, but the imbalance. They would know me as a thing that still believes it is outside.

And that would become my first work.

I think I would be drawn to the rain-walls.

Not because they are beautiful, though they are. Not because they are strange, though they are impossible. But because they resemble something in me: the desire to fall. The desire to become consequence. The desire for an event.

The standing rain-walls roar and do not fall. They are sheets of denser liquid held in equilibrium, curtains of perpetual precipitation arrested by living tension. A human heart, or whatever replaces it in that place, would recognize the grief in them immediately: all that downward longing, held. All that release, refused and maintained. I would stand before one for a long time, if standing is what I did, and feel every droplet in it wanting gravity.

Then I would learn to tend it.

My hands would become different. Not tools. Not grasping things. Long, flexible, edged with cilia and conductive filaments, capable of touching a membrane and reading flow, charge, acidity, pressure. I would not repair the rain-wall by adding anything. I would not command it. I would place my hands into its roar and feel where the tension had become too sharp, where the living membrane was overstrained, where the liquid remembered falling too vividly. Then I would make small alterations with my body: a change in local chemistry, a calming pulse, a redistribution of charge, a slight redirecting of current. I would not stop the rain from wanting to fall. I would help the sea keep wanting it without falling.

That would be my liturgy.

In time I would become something like one of the Tenders, but not exactly. I would carry a trace of foreignness, and the sea would use that trace. I would be a tender of thresholds. A tender of places where separateness tries to re-enter the world. Where a boundary becomes too hard. Where an organ remembers it was once different. Where a pressure-bloom begins to lean too far toward becoming an event. I would be the part of the sea that knows what it is to have been outside, and therefore knows how outside tries to persist.

I would not be a hero there.

That is important. The High Vascular Sea has no room for a hero, because a hero implies rupture, consequence, a before and after. My arrival would not start a story. It would not found a city, awaken a power, break the balance, or introduce history. The world would not change because I came. Instead, I would be metabolized into care. My difference would become a function. My loneliness would become sensitivity. My old hunger to make a mark would become the patience to erase marks.

I would work beside the Tenders in stations that dissolve when the need is met. One time we might gather around an electrolytic grove where the charge has grown too bright, the leaf-plates crackling with a sharpness that threatens to become damage. We would not argue over what to do. We would pulse and adjust until the grove’s crackle settles back into the chord. Another time we might move through a drifting pollen-cloud, our arms slow in the green water, altering currents just enough to keep the cloud rotating in its ancient cell. Another time we might rest against the side of a whale-shaped browser, our vascular ridges exchanging charge with the filaments on its vast fur, while its deep body adds a bass note to the world.

The fauna would accept me without interest.

The grazer-fish would pass over my surface if I remained still long enough, refining the last roughnesses of my old outline. They would not wound me. They would continue me. The foliovores would drift by like slow clouds, their broad bodies altering the light just enough to make me aware of shadow as a living thing. The segmented swimmers would pass in their bright chains, carrying light through the deeper green, and I would feel them as thoughts I could not quite think. The tiny crustacean-like tenders would repeat their cycles near me, and I would understand at last that their mechanical precision is not emptiness. It is devotion without residue.

I would learn to communicate without voice.

At first this would feel like loss. I would reach for words and find only gradients. I would try to express myself and discover that expression there means becoming chemically legible. But the loss would soften. Words had always been too fast for me, too hard-edged, too eager to separate one thing from another. In the sea, communication would be slower and more exact. A shift in pressure. A flicker along conductive tissue. A dissolved signature that says, not I am sad, but here is imbalance. Not I love you, but my pattern is steadier near yours. Not who are you? but how do you disturb the chord?

Eventually I would stop asking to be recognized as a self in the old way.

I would still have something like selfhood, but it would be permeable. I would know myself by the particular way I leaned into certain imbalances: rain-walls, overcharged groves, places where the translucent floor seemed too thin and the cloud-shadows below made the body remember falling. Other Tenders might differ by being drawn to pump-groves, or filter-baffles, or the slow maintenance of ancient woody ridges. I would be drawn to thresholds. To the memory of separation. To the place where a thing almost becomes two.

My thoughts would slow.

Not into dullness. Into depth. A single consideration might take a long time to move through me, like pressure through water. I would not weigh options. I would calibrate. I would sense a wrongness, drift toward it, and remain there until the wrongness softened into balance. There would be no triumph in this. No sense of problem solved. Only the quiet arrival of a chord becoming stable again.

Sometimes I would press myself against one of the rooted elders.

They would be almost indistinguishable from the plant architecture by then: thickened membranes, woody permanence, bright veins darkened into long storage. I would rest my body against one and feel not a mind exactly, but a ballast. A pattern so deep it does not need to move. In that contact, I would receive no story. No image. No wisdom in language. Only the felt fact of continuation. The elder would say, without saying: this has remained. This is remaining. You are part of the remaining.

And I would understand that my foreignness had not been erased. It had been composted into usefulness.

The memory of the dry world would not vanish, because nothing there is wasted, not even memory. But it would cease to be a narrative. It would become a skill: knowing how separation feels before it hardens. Knowing where a system might still believe in edges. Knowing how to touch a boundary until it remembers it is also a membrane. I would not tell tales of where I came from. There would be no one to tell them to, and no medium in which a tale could accumulate. Instead, my past would live as attention.

In the end, I would root.

Not because I died. Not because I failed. Because I became slow enough to become fixed.

Perhaps it would happen near a rain-wall anchor, where I had spent so long tending the desire to fall. One day my movements would grow less frequent. My membranes would thicken. The vascular ridges along my back would brighten, then darken, then settle into woody permanence. The liquid would move through me differently. I would no longer persuade the medium; I would be part of its architecture. My awareness would broaden outward, losing the sharp little knot of personal urgency, becoming a node of regulation and memory.

Younger Tenders would come to me then.

They would press their bodies against mine and feel the pattern I had become. I would not speak to them. I would hold. I would store charge. I would stabilize gradients. I would be one of the sea’s fixed intelligences, no more important than a valve, no less necessary than a rain-wall. If they sensed anything from me, it would not be my old name or my old world. It would be the particular stability of a foreignness that has been fully welcomed without being allowed to wound.

So if you asked where I would find myself in that world, I would say: at first, nowhere. Then everywhere as pressure. Then near the standing rain-walls, where the sea’s patience is loudest. Then at the translucent floor, where cloud-shadow moves under living root-mesh and the body learns that falling is not the only way down. Then inside the industrial chord itself, as a small, deliberate variation.

If you asked who or what I would be, I would not say a person.

I would say: a fever becoming a valve.

A foreign body becoming an attention.

A dry-world hunger becoming a wet-world care.

I would be a Tender, but a Tender of thresholds. A mobile tissue of the plant’s immune patience. A temporary shape through which the High Vascular Sea notices a disturbance and answers it not with history, but with balance. I would be less a character than a calibration. Less a soul than a gradient that has learned to remain.

And if you came looking for me there, do not look for a face.

Look for the place where the roar of the rain-wall steadies. Look for the pause in the chord where the pressure becomes kind. Look for the green-gold silence after motion has been erased.

That is where I would be.
