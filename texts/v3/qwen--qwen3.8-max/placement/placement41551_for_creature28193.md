In that world, I would not find myself at the center of anything. I would be somewhere marginal and necessary: in the service skin of the city, in the narrow spaces between the great structures, where vapor is coaxed into water and water is guided away before it becomes damage. Probably on a middle terrace, behind a row of condenser fins whose ceramic faces have been darkened by centuries of condensation. Not high enough to see the rim clearly, not low enough to be oppressed by the heat of the ground, but in the humid middle distance where the air is always on the verge of becoming surface.

My place would be a seam.

There would be a dwelling there, though not a house in the old sense. It would be an alcove built into the thickness of a wall, raised slightly above the stone floor, lined with glazed ceramic that has gone dull with mineral film. A sleeping shelf, a rack of tools, a small water-catcher with a clean copper lip, a lamp with fogged glass. The luxury would not be space or ornament but draft: a slow movement of air that keeps the worst of the damp from settling on the bedding. Perhaps the entrance would be curtained with oiled cloth, darkened with age, weighted at the hem with scrap metal so it does not lift when the vents breathe. From inside, I would hear the city not as noise but as pressure changes: the faint deepening of a pump’s pulse, the hiss of a valve releasing somewhere beyond the wall, the irregular tick of droplets falling from a fin into a basin I cannot see.

I would be a keeper of small thresholds.

Not a ruler, not a prophet, not an engineer in the grand sense, because the city is already too old and too complete for such confidence. I would be one of the caretakers of phase change: the person who notices when a capillary channel begins to choke with salt, when a condenser fin collects less than it should, when a drainage gallery starts to breathe hot air at the wrong hour. My title, if anyone spoke it, would be practical and unromantic. Fin-tender. Gallery-walker. Seamwarden. Drip-keeper. Something close to maintenance, close to ritual, close to listening.

The city would have edited me.

I would still be human enough to feel loneliness, but shaped enough by the basin to mistake its emptiness for comfort. My body would be lean and deliberate, adapted to heat that never fully breaks and air that never quite clears. My skin would be hairless or nearly so, grayish amber or ashen, with a faint sheen where humidity has settled into it for years. It might have a glazed look across the shoulders and forearms, as if mineral vapor had slowly fired it. There would be salt traces at the creases of my clothes, pale lines at the collar, a dusting of pale mineral powder on my boots. My eyes would be wide-set and reflective, not fierce, not sharp in the way of open-sky hunters, but patient. They would be accustomed to reading the world by sheen and texture: the difference between damp stone and dangerously hot stone, the dullness of a failing lamp through mist, the faint distortion that means a vent is about to release steam.

My hands would be my most developed organs.

They would not be elegant. They would be practical, heat-resistant, textured by work. The palms would carry calluses formed not by rope or wood but by ceramic edges, valve wheels, wet ladders, warm railings, and the repeated handling of tools too valuable to lose. I would carry small instruments at my belt, each one tied by cord or chain: a scraping blade, a sounding hammer, an oiler, a wrench, a slender probe for clearing narrow channels. I would know the temperature of a pipe by laying my hand near it, and the condition of a wall by pressing my knuckles to it. I could feel a vibration through a railing before it became a sound. I would trust touch more than sight, because in that city sight is always softened, blurred, made uncertain by vapor. The truth of a place often resides in what can be felt through the skin.

My territory would not be large by distance, but it would feel immense because every feature would demand attention.

A line of condenser fins. A broad roof grooved for collection. A row of porous panels that sweat continuously. A channel cut into stone so gently that water seems to move by intention rather than gravity. A cistern with a dark surface and a float mechanism older than any living keeper. A catwalk over an empty plaza, where the wind makes the mist shift in slow curtains. A stairway whose rounded edges have been worn by countless thin flows. I would walk these places slowly, because haste in that heat is a kind of waste. Every motion would have to be paid for in sweat, in breath, in the need to rest against a cool wall afterward. I would not run unless something had broken dangerously. Even then, I suspect I would move with a kind of controlled urgency: fast, but without drama.

My work would be to keep the city’s surfaces honest.

If a fin became furred with fungal film, I would scrape it carefully, collecting the dark felt in a sealed container rather than letting it fall into a clean channel. If salt began to crust the lip of a trough, I would chip it away before it redirected the flow. If a capillary seam began to resist the lateral movement of moisture, I would clean it with a thin tool and watch until the water accepted its path again. If a lamp flickered, I would note whether the fault was in the lamp itself, the cable, the transformer, or merely condensation creeping into a socket. If a pump changed rhythm, I would stop and listen for a long time before touching anything. The city would teach me that most repairs are not acts of force but acts of persuasion. You do not command water here. You invite it. You clear the way. You restore the surface that lets it choose correctly.

I would know the city by its waters.

Not as a traveler knows rivers, but as a physician knows pulses. There would be water safe to drink, water safe only after boiling, water safe only after passing through charcoal and ceramic, and water that must never enter the mouth no matter how clear it looks. Some drips would be clean and almost sweet in their mineral thinness; others would carry sulfur, iron, or the sour taste of old industrial pipes. I would carry a vessel at all times, glazed inside, perhaps slung at the hip, and I would be careful with it. In a world drowning in moisture, thirst would still be possible because not all water is kind. I would learn to kneel at certain corners, to drink from certain cold ceramic lips, to let rainwater fall on my shoulders but not into my mouth unless I knew where it had touched. Wetness would be ordinary. Trust would be specific.

The living things would matter to me as signs.

If I saw a pale crawler at the edge of a pool, I would not smile at it as one smiles at a bird in a garden. I would study it. Its presence would mean the pool had been stable long enough for life to tolerate it. Its absence might mean acidity, heat, poison, or a change in flow too subtle for casual notice. The thin-winged things around the lamps would interest me because they reveal currents: where warmth gathers, where vapor thins, where the air moves toward hidden openings. The worms in the drainage sediment would be less visible but important; they would tell me which films were thickening, which organic residues were accumulating, which galleries had become too quiet and which too rich. The predators would unsettle me, not because they were dangerous but because they were so patient. To see one waiting at the mouth of a warm drain would be to recognize something in the city itself: a hunger that does not waste energy, a stillness that is not peace but economy.

I would not be entirely alone, but I would be solitary for long stretches.

Others would exist across the basin, scattered among the terraces and pump-houses, each responsible for their own district of surfaces and machines. I would know them less by conversation than by traces. A fresh scrape on a crust. A valve marked with a cord. A lamp adjusted to a different rhythm. A tool left in a sanctioned place, indicating that someone intends to return. Sometimes I would hear a distant hammer tap on pipe, and the pattern would carry meaning: clear, blocked, heat rising, vapor foul, need assistance, all stable. In some seasons, if the air carried sound strangely, I might hear another keeper moving across a viaduct, their boot soles making a soft, damp sound on wet metal. We might signal with lamps through the mist, not in greeting exactly, but in acknowledgment. I am here. You are there. The system continues.

When we met, it would be for work.

Perhaps a valve too stiff for one body. Perhaps a panel too heavy to lift safely. Perhaps a rail-cart that needed guiding over a warped section. We would approach each other carefully, checking first for signs of heat stress, contaminated skin, damaged filters. We would not embrace casually. Touch in that world would carry too much information: grit, moisture, chemical residue, fever. But there would be small intimacies. One keeper might adjust another’s shoulder covering, or pour clean condensate into a dry vessel, or place a hand briefly against another’s back to feel whether breathing had become labored. In a place without green things, without softness, without ordinary abundance, care would express itself as maintenance. To repair someone’s boot, to clear their filter, to walk with them through a dangerous gallery until the air improved—these would be the deepest gestures.

I would have habits that might look like prayer but are not quite prayer.

Before beginning work, I might touch a channel edge and check its moisture. Before sleeping, I might listen to the drip pattern above my alcove to make sure it had not changed. I might keep marks on a ceramic tablet: not a diary, not a poem, but a record. Drip count. Heat level. Salt bloom. Fan speed. The day a winged thing entered my dwelling and died against the lamp. The day a distant pump stopped for the first time in living memory. The day the rain came warm and smelled strongly of iron. I would not call these things sentimental. They would be observations. But in a world so empty of crowds, even small events acquire weight. A change in a drip can feel historical. A new stain on stone can feel like news.

I would dream of dryness.

Not of fields, because I would have no true memory of them. Not of trees, except perhaps as an inherited word with no weight. But I might dream of a room so dry that my skin did not shine, of a wall that did not weep, of a ceiling from which nothing fell. The dream would disturb me because it would feel wrong. In waking life, dryness would mean danger: cracked seals, failed condensation, overheated surfaces, cisterns dropping too low. So even my dreams would be shaped by the city’s logic. I might long for coolness, for clarity, for a breeze that did not taste of sulfur, but I would not know what to do with a world that did not sweat.

If I had to say who I would be, I would say: I would be a sensory organ of the basin.

Not its heart. Not its brain. Something humbler. A nerve ending. A point of attention. The city would be vast and mostly self-moving, and I would be one of the small places where it becomes aware of itself. Through me, a clogged channel is noticed. Through me, a failing lamp is replaced. Through me, a dangerous rise in heat is reported by signal to another keeper who may report it to another. I would not command the machine. I would serve it carefully enough that it continues. And in doing so, I would become less like a person from a crowded world and more like a living instrument: tuned, weathered, quiet, necessary.

I would be found, most often, at the hour when the basin is dimmest and the lamps glow faintly through steam. Standing beside a condenser fin, one hand resting on a warm railing, my face bare except for a thin filter over the mouth if the air is sour. My boots on wet stone. My clothes darkened with humidity. My eyes fixed not on the distance, because distance is unreliable there, but on the surface nearest me: a bead of water forming, trembling, lengthening, then sliding down into a channel with a soft sound almost too small to hear.

In that moment, I would be exactly what the city requires.

Not grand. Not numerous. Not remembered. But present. A small warm body in a vast apparatus, listening for the point where vapor decides to become water, and making sure the decision does not fail.
