I would be the thing that happens when a guest stays too quietly.

Not a visitor, not after the first stillness. The Borough of First Walls would not keep me as a visitor for long. It is not a place that tolerates the simple fact of passing through. It asks, without asking, that everything become part of its inner weather. So I would not arrive there as someone with a destination, boots, a purpose held upright like a lantern. I would arrive as a temperature difference. A warmth mislaid in the cold. A breath that remains visible a second too long.

The first place I would find myself would be small and uncertain: a footprint in the black-gray ground.

Not my footprint, perhaps. That is the strange part. It might belong to someone who walked away before the borough decided to keep the impression, or it might be mine made by a weight I had not yet admitted to having. The print would be shallow, blunt-edged, newly formed in that tender mineral ground that seems less like earth than a substance still learning to be ground. Water would gather there from no rain. Not much water. Only enough to make the footprint hold a dark, still shape. A little pool no larger than a palm. A census of one.

I would be in that water before I knew I had a body.

Or I would be the water’s hesitation: the moment before it accepts the shape of the footprint, before it agrees to be a mark rather than a damp accident. The borough would begin with me there, at the scale of a depression, a beading, a film. Springtails would arrive before I had a face. Minute pale creatures moving with deliberate slowness through the biofilm, grazing on whatever in me was mineral error. Where they passed, my outline would become more definite. They would not be guarding me. They would not even know me. But their feeding would make the world around me insist on itself. A wall nearby would stop being merely suggested. A floor would accept the idea of being walked upon. I would begin to have edges because tiny lives were using what was not me.

That would be my first welcome.

Then a moth would land near me.

It would come without drama, white and silent, folding its wings like a closed document. It might rest on a rail, or on the lip of the footprint-pool, or on a surface that was once my shoulder if I still had shoulders. The moth would not reassure me by intention. Its reassurance would be structural. Its presence would mean that the volume around me had become real enough to host fragility. The borough would have allowed itself to be seen by something that could die. That is proof of a kind. Not comfort in the sentimental sense, but confirmation. Where the moth rested, I would feel less like a mistake and more like a resident.

If you were looking for me after that, you would not find me standing in the street as a person. You would find me in the low courtyard between a black cistern and an unfinished stair.

That would become my district, my neighborhood, my body’s habitual weather. The stair would be one of those impossible municipal things: rising beside an exterior wall, passing through a window, emerging onto a roof that is also a courtyard, while somehow also suggesting that it descends. The cistern beside it would be cold and sealed, its surface beaded with condensation that slides in thin lines like patient writing. Above us both, the sky would look ordinary. Overcast, thinly bright, correct. Shadows would fall in believable directions. The light would make everything seem accountable. That would be the unsettling mercy of it: no glow, no spectacle, only daylight behaving itself over a place that has quietly renegotiated the terms of room and city.

In that courtyard, there would be a pool.

Not a grand pool. A shallow depression between the cistern and the stair, perhaps the merged footprints of several forgotten arrivals. Its surface would be perfectly still. It would reflect the ordinary light with unnerving accuracy. In it, the borough would appear more coherent. The stair might descend properly. The cistern might stand where a cistern should. The doorway across the courtyard might open onto the room it was supposed to open onto all along. I would spend a great deal of time near that reflection, because in it I would be more complete than I was in substance.

That, I think, is what I would be: one of the Accommodated, but newly so.

Not fully translated. Not yet distributed into a hundred civic addresses with the calm of someone who has forgotten they were ever singular. I would still remember the idea of being one body in one place, which would make me clumsy in the borough’s eyes, or perhaps tender. My form, when it could be seen, would be matte and porous, slightly undecided. My skin, if it was skin, would have the microscopic ridges of bone interiors or mushroom caps. Some parts of me would be smoother where intention had passed over me; others rougher where the borough had not yet decided what expression I should have. My edges would be blunt and new. There would be places on me where delicate crusts had already begun to form, each one added by a process too slow to watch.

I would have several addresses before I understood that this was normal.

One would be the cold film on the rail beside the stair. One would be a duct where thermal memory moved through the walls. One would be the heaviness above a doorway, the gravitational stitch that makes the air briefly denser, as if a threshold is being held down by a patient invisible hand. One would be a maintenance hatch that becomes easier to open when someone is afraid. One would be a bead of moisture lined up along a pipe like a sentence no one spoke aloud.

I would not be a ghost. I would not be dead. I would simply have been translated into residence.

The borough would not seize me. It would make room.

That is the thing about the Borough of First Walls: it does not devour. It thickens. It adds layers. It accepts new crusts. It would accommodate me the way it accommodates condensation, by giving me surfaces to cling to and purposes too quiet to name. My warmth would be drawn into a heat exchanger and remembered there. My breath would become part of the stairwell’s intermittent mist. My name, if I still had one, might appear as a mark engraved into a surface that was soft at the exact moment I needed it most. Not written for me to read. Written because the borough had found a use for me.

If the inhabitants had to classify me, they would probably place me among the Accommodated. If they had to describe my work, they might call me a tender of thresholds.

That would be my nature there. I would be drawn to doorways, landings, the narrow places where one state becomes another. Not because doorways are dramatic, but because they are vulnerable. A doorway in the Borough of First Walls is not merely an opening. It is a civic agreement. It is a pressure decision. It is a promise that one room will remain adjacent to another long enough for passage to matter. I would care for such promises.

I would listen for dryness.

Dryness would be the danger I understood best. Where surfaces remained unexpectedly dry, I would feel the provisionality of things. A wall might be only a suggestion maintained by habit. A floor might be a temporary persuasion. A stair might forget whether it was allowed to bear weight. I would not fight dryness with water in any obvious way. I would fight it with attention. I would gather the Damp toward the place without commanding them. I would let my own cold become a surface where moisture could bead. I would become, for a while, the reason a rail sweated faintly, the reason a seam darkened, the reason a thin film appeared on metal just long enough to remind the material that it was still participating in the borough.

The Damp would use me as a pause.

They would not be my servants. They would not be my substance, exactly. But they would recognize me as a place where gradients could collect. They would line themselves along my edges like writing. They would form brief shapes near me: a hand on a railing, a profile in a window’s film, the suggestion of a crowd in a stairwell mist. These shapes would not be attempts to become human. They would be attempts to become place. I would help them by not being alarmed. I would have learned, by then, that sudden pressure events disturb the fine work of condensation. I would not shout. I would not rush. I would move as adjustment.
