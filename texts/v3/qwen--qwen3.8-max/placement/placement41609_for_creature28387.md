I would not find myself there as a traveler.

Not at first. Not as something crossing the basin from point to point, leaving prints on the dark stone, breathing hard in the thin air. I think I would be something discovered only after stillness had become possible.

If I were placed into that world, I would gather myself at an edge.

Not on the open kilometers of bare rock, and not deep inside the thickest moss banks, but in one of the in-between places where the basin’s gradients are strongest: a low terrace in the lee of a worn rise, where a geothermal seam softens the ground just enough to keep a cluster of hollow reeds alive beside a black pool. The pool would be small, still, fringed with ice. The reeds would stand nearby in a tight pale-dark stand, their tips faintly charged when the air is tense. Fog would cross that terrace in slow veils, dragged over the lip of the basin and pulled down into the hollows. Warmth would rise there in the thinnest possible amount—not comfort, not shelter, but a difference. A currency.

There, where cold stone and faint heat meet, where vapor becomes water and water becomes life, I think I would have a place.

At first, I would be easy to mistake for part of the terrain.

From a distance, I might look like a dark stain on the rock, or a low cushion plant swollen with moisture, or a patch of moss that has grown unusually regular in its outline. Frost would gather along my edges. Condensation would bead on me more heavily than on the stone around me. If the air were charged, a faint bluish light might tremble along my finest filaments and then disappear. A grazer passing half a kilometer away might lift its head toward my terrace, not because it saw me, but because the silence around me would feel slightly arranged.

Up close, I would be stranger.

I would not have a face. I would not have limbs in any useful sense. I would be a low, broad organism, or perhaps a colony of organisms, spread across a flat shelf of dark stone near the pool. My body would consist of overlapping lobes of dense, insulated tissue—something between moss, lichen, and fungal mat—dark green-black on the surface, faintly silver beneath where fine threads run into cracks and soil. Those threads would be my deepest roots, though not roots in the simple sense. They would connect me downward and outward, threading into the underweb without being the underweb itself. I would be a local knot in that vast distributed patience, a place where its slow signals become a little more concentrated, a little more attentive.

I would be a listening-knot.

That is the closest thing to a name for what I would be.

Not a listener with ears. Not a watcher with eyes. I would listen with surfaces, with gradients, with tension. I would know the world by the way it alters me.

My upper surface would be beaded with moisture at almost all times, because I would be shaped to collect fog. Fine hairs and tiny living filaments would rise from me, each one sensitive to humidity, temperature, and electrical difference. When the air is dry and cold, I would contract, drawing inward, conserving what little warmth and charge I hold. When fog arrives, I would open. Not dramatically. Not visibly in a single moment. But over minutes, my surfaces would spread slightly, my filaments would lift, and condensation would form on me faster than on the surrounding stone. I would drink the air. I would feed on vapor, on microscopic organic particles, on charged spores, on the faint chemical traces left by passing bodies.

I would not hunt. I would not graze. I would not build.

I would attend.

If a grazer came near my pool, I would feel it before I would know it in any human sense. The first sign would be a pressure change in the air, a broad warmth moving across the terrace. The moss around me would compress under its weight, and the vibration would travel through stone into my filaments. My hairs would lift. The electrical field around me would shift. A faint spark might appear at the edge of one of my lobes, not because I intended it, but because the presence of another large warm body changes the balance of charge. The grazer would probably pause. It might exhale toward me, and that breath would be precious: warm, moist, full of tiny chemical signs. I would absorb what I could. I would record the event not as memory in a brain, but as an altered pattern in my tissues—a slight retention of heat, a changed distribution of moisture, a lingering trace of static.

If a predator passed, I would know it differently.

There would be less warmth exposed. Less vibration. A narrow, controlled disturbance. The air would seem to thin around it rather than widen. I would become stiller. My surface would cool slightly as I drew inward, reducing my own presence. I would not flee, because I would have no need to flee in the ordinary way. Predators of that basin would not likely feed on me; I would offer little meat, little heat, little reward. But I would still respond, because the basin responds to everything. My filaments would lower. My glow would dim. The small creatures sheltering near me would become quieter. The predator would pass through my field of attention like a held breath.

If drifters came overhead, I would know them by the change in charge.

They would move above the terrace in the cold thin air, their membranes adjusting to invisible currents. When one passed near enough, the hairs on my surface would rise, and the reeds beside me might hum faintly. If the air became rich enough with charged spores, I might release a weak discharge of my own—not a flash, not a signal, but a small equalization. The drifter might veer toward it or away from it, depending on its own state. I would not call it. I would not feed it.
