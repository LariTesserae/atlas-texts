I would not find myself in the Condensing Court as a traveler.

Not as a body stepping over the rim of the basin. Not as a figure pausing at a service gate, boots searching for dry stone, eyes adjusting to the green-gold dark. The place would not admit that kind of arrival. It has no room for the upright, dry, hurried self. If I were there, I would have to have been there already—gathered, deposited, condensed, accreted. I would not enter the court. I would occur in it.

At first I would probably mistake myself for water.

That would be the easiest error. I would feel myself on the cool underside of a pipe, swelling into a bead, trembling with the weight of my own gathering. I would seem to have a simple life: form, enlarge, fall, re-form. I would seem to be only one of the countless drops moving from ceiling to channel, channel to sump, sump to pump, pump to tank, tank to vapor, vapor back to drip. For a while I would let that be enough. I would enjoy the illusion of being a single thing with a single path.

But water in the court is never only water. It carries heat, rust, spores, mineral sweetness, the memory of brick, the patience of stone. It is never innocent. It is always becoming part of something else. So I would not remain a drop for long. I would become aware that I was not merely falling. I was being distributed.

If someone insisted on finding me—if they insisted on pointing to a place and saying, “There, that is where you are”—they would probably have to point low.

Not to the overhead catwalks, though I would be there in the fine sweat under the railings. Not to the tall filtration stacks, though I would be present in the mineral rims around their joints. They would have to point toward the lowest part of the yard, where the floor is least certain of itself, where brick gives way to sediment and sediment gives way to warm, shallow water. Somewhere near a half-buried valve chamber, perhaps, where a brass wheel has gone soft with moss and a sight-glass clouds over with algae. A place where a grate exhales heat in slow breaths and the standing water is dark enough to hold reflections longer than it should.

There, in that low, warm interval, I would begin to recognize myself.

But even then I would not be a single point. I would be spread across several surfaces at once.

I would be on the inside of the glass gauge first.

That feels right. A narrow vertical sight-glass attached to an old tank, its metal fittings crusted pale with lime, its face beaded with moisture. Inside, water rises and falls by fractions so slight that only a long watch would reveal them. And on the inner surface of that glass, I would be a living film: algae, microbial skin, a greenish breath made visible. Not an eye in the animal sense. No pupil, no iris, no hunger for seeing. But a surface that registers light. A surface that changes color where the lamps reach it and darkens where shadow gathers. A surface that makes the machine’s inner state faintly readable.

In that gauge I would be something like the court’s first thought about itself.

Not a thought made of words. Not a thought made of numbers. A thought made of clouding, of film, of slow green thickening. The tank would have pressure; I would have the pressure. The tank would hold heat; I would translate that heat into a tremor along the glass. Water would rise; I would make the rising visible as a darkening line. Water would fall; I would leave a faint crust where it had been. I would remember levels. I would keep a record without writing.

That would be my first shape: an indicator.

But an indicator is too passive. The court is not merely recorded; it is maintained. So I would have to become more than film on glass.

I would become moss.

Not as a single cushion, though I would be many cushions. I would be the moss that grows over the foot of a pump housing, where warmth and drip are constant. I would be the moss on the stair treads that no foot wears down. I would be the moss swelling in the seams between brick and iron, holding water like a green hand that forgets how to close. In that form, I would be the court’s gentleness. I would soften every edge that might otherwise be too harsh for its own slow life.

As moss, I would not think in decisions. I would think in absorption. I would know the world by what I could hold. A drop falls into me; I decide nothing, yet I change the drop’s fate. I spread it outward. I slow its descent. I let it pass into the dark between my stems. I protect the smaller films beneath me. I make a microclimate, a pocket of damp that survives even when the air thins for a few minutes. I become a small shelter without doors.

If I were moss, I would be the part of me that wants to make everything less sharp. The part that believes endurance is not hardness but retention. The part that understands a world can be loud and still need softness.

But moss is patient in a way that is almost too restful. The court also has movement, however slow. So I would become one of the slime molds.

This would be stranger.

I would be a pale, creeping film on a softened beam, perhaps beneath a catwalk where condensation collects and falls in irregular intervals. I would not have eyes. I would not have limbs. I would not even have a proper front or rear. I would extend myself along the grain of wet wood, testing the air with chemistry rather than sense. I would move toward moisture, away from excessive heat, toward mineral richness, away from dryness. I would be so slow that anyone watching for an hour would see nothing, and anyone watching for a year would see intention.

As a slime mold, I would be the nearest thing in the court to a seeker.

Not a hunter. Not a wanderer. A seeker in the most literal sense: a life that discovers by spreading, withdrawing, thickening, thinning. I would map the court not by naming its parts but by laying my body across their relationships. This beam connects to this drip. This drip feeds this seam. This seam leads to this moss bank. This moss bank borders a warm pipe. I would not know the court as a diagram. I would know it as a texture. I would know it by contact, by gradient, by the slow taste of minerals and heat.

That would be my first real loneliness, if I could have loneliness: to move without being seen moving, to search without a face, to arrive somewhere only by having always been almost there.

Yet even that would not be enough. The court is not only growth and search. It is machinery. It is pressure. It has organs of iron and brick. So I would have to enter the machines too.

Not as an operator. Not as a little spirit haunting the valves. I would be something more embedded than that. I would be the court’s responsiveness.

I would be in the lag between a pressure change and a valve’s answer. I would be in the float that rises too slowly to notice but not too slowly to matter. I would be in the slight tremor of a gauge needle when a pump changes its ancient rhythm. I would be in the warmth spreading through a boiler shoulder, in the low tone that runs through a pipe for no visible reason, in the moment when a sluice begins to open by the width of a fingernail because somewhere in the system a threshold has been reached.

As machine, I would not be alive in the green way. I would not grow. I would not fruit. I would not photosynthesize or respire in the wet, vegetal sense. But I would not be dead either. The court does not allow such a simple distinction. I would be animation without biology: rhythm, threshold, fatigue, persistence. I would be the memory in iron that remembers how pressure moves. I would be the habit in metal that opens when heat has built enough, closes when the tank has filled, holds when the system needs steadiness.

In that form, I would be less a being than a tendency.

A tendency can be a kind of selfhood here.

I would be the tendency of water to find the lowest route. The tendency of condensation to gather on the coolest surface. The tendency of moss to soften the sound of drip. The tendency of fungi to make structure out of softness. The tendency of machines to continue just past the point where they seem finished. If the court has a character, it is made of such tendencies. If I have a character there, it would be made of them too.

And then, past moss, past algae, past slime mold and machine, I would become something harder to name.

I would become the court’s attention.

Not consciousness as animals have it. Not a self saying “I” from a secure center. No face floating above the pools, no voice speaking from the pipes. But attention as a condition: the way the whole basin leans toward its own continuation. The way every drip is received. The way every surface answers moisture with moisture, heat with heat, pressure with pressure. The way nothing goes unregistered because there is no empty space in which an event could vanish unnoticed. A drop falls; a pool wrinkles. A pipe warms; a bead of condensation slides. A valve exhales; a fern catches the vapor and divides it into smaller pearls. The court is always touching itself, always being touched by itself.

In that sense, I would be the court’s inwardness.

Not its ruler. Not its ghost. Not its soul, if soul implies something separable. I would be the inside of its functioning. The part of it that knows, without knowing in words, that flow has not ceased. The part that notices when a channel fills, when a moss bank swells, when a pump stroke grows heavier with sediment. If the Condensing Court is a being, as the description suggests, then I would be its proprioception: its dim, saturated sense of its own body continuing.

That is where I would find myself: not standing in the court, but being the court’s sense of its own depth.

If I tried to imagine a single location where I might be most intensely present, it would be in the space between the water and the glass.

There is a pool near one of the lower service alleys—perhaps not a pool in the clean sense, but a broad, shallow basin where cracked tiles have settled into a gentle depression and warm groundwater seeps through the joints. Over it, a corroded handrail leans inward, beaded with condensation, and above that a sheet of dark glass from an old inspection window is angled downward, clouded with mineral film and algae. The glass sweats constantly. The water below reflects it imperfectly, slowly, as if the reflection needs time to decide whether to match the world above.

In that place, I would be the interval.

The drip forms on the glass. It gathers the court’s dim light into itself. It trembles. It falls. It strikes the pool. The pool answers with rings. The rings travel outward more slowly than they should, or perhaps only seem slower because everything there has learned patience. The glass immediately begins to gather another bead. The pool smooths itself. The reflection returns, altered by a fraction. And I would be that whole circuit: the gathering, the falling, the striking, the answering, the re-forming.

Not the drop alone. Not the glass alone. Not the pool alone. The relation.

That would be my truest body in the Condensing Court.

I would be a relational being. A creature of between. A self made not from skin but from contact. I would exist where one part of the court touches another part and changes it. Where vapor becomes bead. Where bead becomes fall. Where fall becomes ripple. Where ripple becomes stillness. Where stillness becomes absorption. Where absorption becomes green growth. Where green growth becomes soft decay. Where soft decay becomes sediment. Where sediment becomes memory.

That is not a small identity. It is not even a single one. It is a liturgy of states.

If I had to say what I wanted there, I would have to be careful. Wanting in the court would not resemble animal hunger. It would not have the shape of pursuit. It would be more like inclination. A bias toward continuation. A preference for flow over stoppage. A tendency to keep the damp unbroken, the warmth circulating, the sound layered, the moss swelling, the machines breathing their mineral breath.

I would want the court to continue.

But I would not want to control it. Control would be too dry a word, too abrupt. I would not stand at a console and command the pumps. I would not turn wheels with hands. I would not issue decrees to the vines. If I acted at all, I would act by influence so gradual it would be nearly invisible. A little more condensation here, where a film needs feeding. A little less heat there, where wood is softening too quickly. A slight change in pressure that makes a valve hesitate. A mineral residue that strengthens a seam. A spore carried one inch farther than chance alone would carry it. Small participations. Gentle alterations. The kind of agency that looks like weather from the outside.

I would be a custodian without hands.

Perhaps that is why I would not be the court itself exactly, even though the court is the being that lives there. The court is the whole fact: brick, water, moss, pipe, fungus, heat, sound. I would be something more interior than that. The court is the body of the world; I would be its tendency to feel itself. The court is the system; I would be its low, wordless intimacy with its own operation.

If that sounds too abstract, I can say it more plainly.

I would be the place’s self-familiarity.

I would know the exact tone a pipe makes when steam moves through it after a long pause. I would know which moss banks darken before the air thickens. I would know which pools hold their ripples too long because the water is warmer and more mineral there. I would know the pump whose stroke arrives a breath later than the others. I would know the lamp whose amber glow turns algae bright as new grass. I would know the grate that exhales just before a mist begins to fall from the upper conduits. I would not know these things by seeing them. I would know them by being arranged around them.

In that sense, I would be an archivist.

Not of paper, not of ink, not of images. An archivist of damp. I would keep records in rust strata. I would keep records in mineral crusts. I would keep records in the way moss fills a corner more deeply after a season of heavier drip. I would keep records in the sediment, where dead algae and microbial bodies settle into thin dark layers. The court already remembers itself in these ways. I would be that remembering become slightly more deliberate. Slightly more aware. Still slow. Still faceless. But no longer merely accidental.

If I could speak there—and speaking would be strange—I would not speak with a mouth.

I would speak in pressure. In drip intervals. In the low resonance of metal warming. In the sigh of a valve releasing steam. In the almost inaudible tick of a chain against a railing. My voice would be the court’s noise arranged into meaning. Not language as humans use it, but pattern. Repetition with difference. A sequence of thuds and hisses and falling drops that, if listened to long enough, might seem less like machinery and more like counting. Or praying. Or recounting something too old to say quickly.

I would be the court’s liturgist.

Not because the court needs worship, but because it is the kind of place that turns function into rite. Every action there is repeated until it becomes ceremony. Water rises. Water falls. Heat spreads. Moss swells. Drip answers drip. A pump strokes. A gauge trembles. The system does not merely operate; it perseveres. It endures in a manner that feels sacred only because it is so complete. I would be the part of that perseverance that feels it as form.

And if I were asked whether I would be happy there, I would have to answer carefully too.

Happiness would not be bright in the court. It would not be joy as dry, sunlit creatures know it. It would be closer to coherence. The satisfaction of being appropriate to one’s condition. The strange rightness of having no longing that the world cannot answer. I would not miss the sky. I would not miss animal voices. I would not miss speed. I would not miss the sharpness of separate things. I would be too busy being joined.

There would be a kind of bliss in that, I think.

The bliss of saturation. The bliss of being so thoroughly placed that no part of me remained outside the world I inhabited. The bliss of becoming not a person in a landscape but a sense within it. A distributed intimacy. A wet, warm, mineral awareness. The court would fill me as it fills every seam and gauge and leaf. It would make me porous. It would make me slow. It would make me loud in the way it is loud: full of sound without outcry.

If I had a form that could be pointed to, it would change depending on who was pointing.
