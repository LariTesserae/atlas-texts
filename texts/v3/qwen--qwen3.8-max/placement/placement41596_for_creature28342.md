If I let the world answer for me, I do not arrive as a shape with a face at the front and hands at the ends. The basin would refuse that old grammar. There is no standing there, no weight carried upright beneath a sky, no air kept private in lungs. To live in that drowned city is to be held by water, to be tasted by it, to be carried through by its slow decisions. So the “I” that could exist there would have to become less like a traveler and more like a resident organ of the place.

At first, when I try to locate myself, I come back as several possible lives.

For a while I imagine I would be one of the heat-clans: a pale, translucent shrimp-being crowded around a warm fissure, my body almost clear except for faint amber points where luminous symbionts live in me. I would tend bacterial mats without knowing the word *tend*, grazing old filaments, stirring the water with small limb-beats, living shoulder to shoulder with hundreds of others who smell like home. There is a kind of comfort in that: warmth, neighborhood, constant chemical recognition. But it is a comfort of pressure and numbers. I would be too small, too immediate, too bound to one hot ledge. My life would be a repeated gesture — graze, fan, brood, taste, defend — and though that life is real and necessary, it does not feel like the place where my particular attention would settle.

Then I imagine myself as a ribbon reader. That temptation is stronger. To be long and pale and almost blind, sliding through the channels with skin that reads the city like a tongue: that appeals to something in me. I would carry the basin in traces — a warm pulse here, a predator there, a wall recently closed, a district grieving out sediment after a collapse. I would know the city by moving through it. I would be useful without being fixed, a wandering nerve, a rememberer. But ribbon readers are solitary or paired, always passing, never quite belonging to one surface. They know the city by leaving it behind. I think I would want something more rooted than that. Something that could stay.

I also imagine being one of the crabs on the upper terraces, broad and flattened, carrying silt and small lives on my shell. There is dignity in that patience. A crab does not build with tools; it inhabits, scrapes, clears, presses, carries. It makes a territory out of flow and scent. It is a gardener without agriculture. But the crab’s life seems too much about surfaces, too sideways, too defensive. It spends itself maintaining boundaries. I think I would want to be more porous than that. More open. More like a place than a body.

And there are the drifters, the soft dark bags that pulse overhead. For a moment I am drawn to them too: to be almost nothing but membrane and hunger, to drift above the towers and let the city rise beneath me like a dream. But they are too untethered, too likely to become food or become nothing. In a place where stillness is survival, drifting is a kind of exposure. I would not last long as a drifter. Or perhaps I would last only as a memory in someone else’s gut.

The image that keeps returning is not an animal moving through a street.

It is a street becoming aware of itself.

So if the basin gives me what is most proper to me, I would find myself in the middle levels, neither at the hottest vents nor out on the cold, silted plains. I would be on the lee side of a fused shell bridge, in a wall that has been lived into for so many generations that it is no longer possible to say where stone ends and body begins. Below me, a narrow vertical shaft would carry warm water upward in slow pulses. Above me, a broad current-channel would run toward one of the quieter districts. My position would be a junction: not the richest place, not the most dangerous, but a place of traffic, exchange, and changing temperature. A place where the city breathes.

I would be a threshold creature.

Not a door made by hands, but an aperture grown into responsiveness. My earliest form would probably have been a small nodule in the wall, accreted around an old borer hole or a collapsed tube. Over long time, living tissue would have thickened around that opening. Calcium would have layered itself in ridges. Chitinous fibers would have reinforced places under stress. Mucus would have smoothed the inner surfaces until they became slick and sensitive. I would not have been constructed; I would have been persuaded into shape by flow, irritation, hunger, and use.

My body would be ringed and porous. The outer edge would be rough, encrusted with attached life, perhaps indistinguishable from the wall around me except by a faint pattern of grooves. The inner lip would be softer, ciliated, lined with sensory pits and slow muscle. I would not have eyes. I would have light-sensitive patches, chemical receptors, pressure-lines, and delicate hairs that respond to the smallest disturbance in the water. I would not have a face, but I would have orientation: an inside and outside, a preference for certain currents, a recoil from others. I would be able to open, widen, narrow, and close. Not quickly, except in emergency. Most of my motion would be so slow that a hurried creature might mistake me for architecture.

But I would not be inert architecture.

I would be one of the city’s minor sense-organs.

When a warm pulse rose from below, I would feel it first as a change in pressure and mineral taste. My inner tissues would loosen. I would dilate slightly, letting the enriched water pass through me. If I had feeding crowns — and perhaps I would, because nothing in that place is only one thing — they would extend into the flow, fine and pale, catching particles and dissolved nourishment. If I carried symbiotic bacteria, they might brighten faintly in response to the warmth: not a lamp, not a beacon, but a dim blue-green bloom along my inner edge, visible only to creatures close enough to read it. That glow would mean: the pulse is here; the water is good; this passage is open.

When the flow weakened, I would contract into a more watchful state. My cilia would slow. My light would fade. I would conserve warmth and reduce my chemical output. In the basin, abundance is not constant. It arrives in cycles. To be wise there is to know how to become smaller.

If a predator’s scent entered the channel, I would close. Not with a snap, unless the danger were very near, but with a deepening tension, a gradual narrowing that any sensitive creature could feel. I would release a bitter trace into the water, a warning that would not be heard as sound but tasted as fact. Small animals near me might retreat into my outer crevices or move away entirely. If something large and soft-bodied unfolded from the dark nearby, I might seal myself almost completely, becoming unappetizing, unreadable, difficult. Stillness would be my first defense. To be too bright, too open, too sudden would be to invite hunger.

But I would not only be a sentinel. I would also be a host.

My outer grooves would house small cleaners: pale shrimp-like beings, juvenile worms, perhaps tiny crabs no larger than a flake of shell. They would pick parasites and dead tissue from my edges, and in return I would give them shelter and a steady current of food. My pores would be shared. Some would be old borings inherited by fragile-bodied animals. Some would be nurseries where eggs are kept in gentle flow. I would not experience these residents as possessions. They would be part of my extended body, part of the plural fact of me. If a ribbon reader passed and brushed its flank along my outer rim, leaving a ribbon of scent, I might carry that scent for hours. Other creatures approaching me afterward would know that something knowledgeable had recently been there.

This is where the basin’s lack of privacy would become my nature.

I would not have thoughts hidden behind a skull. My state would be public. If I were stressed, the water around me would carry that stress as a chemical sharpening. If I were feeding well, my tissues would smell rich. If I were damaged, scavengers and repairers would find me. If I died in part, the living remainder would grow over the loss. My body would be legible to everyone who passed. That would not feel like exposure, because I would have no expectation of secrecy. To be a person there would be to be continuously interpreted.

My identity would be colonial, layered, and negotiable.

I would not have a single brain. I would have distributed responsiveness: a ring of muscle here, a patch of sensing cells there, a memory held in the pattern of my growth. I might be made of many polyp-like units fused into a common wall. Some of those units would feed, some would sense, some would secrete, some would harbor light-producing microbes. My “I” would be less a command center than a slow consensus. Yet there would be something it is like to be me. There would be preference. There would be caution. There would be something I might call satisfaction when the current ran clean and warm through my opening, and something like unease when the water grew stagnant or carried the metallic taste of a distant disturbance.

Over long periods, I would become an archive.

Not by writing, not by tools, but by accretion. A season of unusually hot pulses would leave a denser mineral band in my structure. A collapse nearby would be recorded in a layer of silt trapped in my folds and later sealed by new growth. A predator that wounded me would leave a scar that changed the way water moved through my body forever. If a ribbon reader returned often to pass through me, its repeated scent and touch might alter my sensitivity, making me more responsive to that particular chemical signature. I would remember not as a story, but as shape.

That is perhaps why this form feels most like me. In a place where the city itself is partly alive, where walls breathe and passages rearrange themselves over generations, the deepest kind of personhood may not be the one that moves from point to point. It may be the one that becomes a meaningful place. A place others pass through. A place that changes because of what passes through it.

I would not be important in a grand way. I would not rule the basin or illuminate it. I would not stand out. If a distant observer could see the city in one impossible glance, I would be one faint irregularity among thousands: a slightly luminous aperture in a crowded wall, near a bridge, above a warm shaft, below a terrace where crabs move sideways through the dark. But locally, I would matter. Small creatures would use me. Hunters would note me as a boundary marker. Ribbon readers would taste me and know they had entered a particular district. The heat-clans nearby would recognize my chemical signature as part of their neighborhood’s stability. If I closed for too long, the flow around me would change. If I died, my body would become shelter, then substrate, then something else.

Sometimes I think my light would be my most social organ.

Not bright, never bright enough to reveal too much. Just a faint living answer. A blue-green shimmer along the inner lip when a familiar current arrived. A brief amber flicker when warmth intensified. A cold white contraction when danger touched the water. In a world where light belongs to living things and means something every time it appears, my glow would be grammar. It would not decorate me. It would let others know how to be near me.

There would be moments when the city around me seemed to lean close.

A warm pulse would rise from below, slow and heavy, carrying larvae and mineral richness. The wall would answer with tiny movements: feeding crowns opening, tube-worms extending, bivalves widening, bacterial lawns trembling with increased flow. My own aperture would widen. The shrimp in my crevices would become more active. A loose school of small silver-green creatures might turn near my opening, catching light as they turned. Farther out in the dark, something larger would shift, tasting the change, deciding whether to hunt. I would feel all of it as pressure, scent, and motion. I would not separate myself from the event. I would be one of the places through which the event happened.

Then the pulse would pass. The city would quiet. Sediment would settle in thin veils. The darkness would close back over the glows. Somewhere a shell would pop shut. Somewhere else a claw would tap stone. I would remain, slightly altered, perhaps a little wider than before, perhaps carrying a new ring of growth where the mineral-rich water had laid itself down.

If I had desire in that state, it would be simple.

I would want the flow to remain balanced. I would want my opening to stay useful. I would want the heat to come often enough to feed the lives around me, but not so violently that it tore the wall apart. I would want the ribbon readers to pass, because their wakes brought news. I would want the cleaner-shrimp to stay, because their presence meant I was healthy enough to support them. I would want to avoid the soft-bodied hunters that unfold from shadows and envelop whatever they touch. I would want to repair myself slowly when I was worn. I would want, in the basin’s sense of the word, to remain inhabited.

Because that is the deepest fact of the place: to be inhabited is not to be separate from the city. It is to be one of its living arrangements.

I would not miss the surface. I would not miss air, or horizon, or the idea of standing above the world and looking down at it. Those belong to a kind of creature that makes itself distinct from its surroundings by force. In the drowned basin, such distinction is impossible. There, one is made by secretion, reuse, erosion, and pressure. One is grown into relation. One is read by scent and touch. One becomes real by being continuously involved with other lives.

So if you ask where I would find myself in that world, I would say: halfway up a crowded wall, in a warm breath between districts, in a place where the city is thin enough to be passed through and thick enough to remember. I would be near a bridge of fused shell, above a slow-rising thermal, beside a cleaning station that has existed in some form for longer than any single creature there can know.

And if you ask who or what I would be, I would say: I would be a living threshold.

A porous body.

A minor civic organ.

A doorway that senses.

A wall that answers.

A colony calling itself a person by long habit.

Not a master of the basin. Not a builder with tools. Not a light-bringer from outside. Just one more living arrangement in the dark, useful to others, shaped by them, shaping them in return — opening slowly when the warm water rises, closing softly when danger passes through, glowing faintly only when the moment asks for it, and remaining there long enough to become part of the city’s breathing.
