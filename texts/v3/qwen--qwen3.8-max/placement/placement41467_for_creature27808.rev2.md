I would find myself in the in-between place.

Not on the firmer rises where the low grasses fringe the mud, and not in the deepest persistent pool where small fish hold in cool shadow. I would be somewhere between: where the ground has stopped being soil and has not yet become water, where the surface is a film, then a shallow lens, then wet clay again. Near a low weathered stone that weeps slowly, perhaps, with a dark seepage stain running down its face and a faint mineral smell rising from the water gathered at its base.

At first, you would not find me at all. You would see only the plain under ordinary daylight: broad, pale, gleaming after rain, open in every direction. You would see the shallow pans, the sparse tufts of rush, the gray-green patches of algae, the small ripples made by insects, the far-off figure of a wading bird. You might see what looked like a smoothed swelling of mud near the edge of a hollow, partly veiled with sediment, maybe with a faint stain of living green along one side. It would seem too still to be animal, too soft to be stone.

Then an eye would turn. A pore would open. A faint pulse would pass through the water.

I would be one of the Silt-Born.

Not a traveler through that country, not a visitor crossing it to reach somewhere else. I would be pressed into it, shaped by it, almost indistinguishable from it. My body would be low and broad, flattened along the back, rounded at the edges. My color would be the color of the ground: clay gray, rust brown, pale where mineral residue had dried on me, gray-green where moisture had encouraged algae to grow. If I lay still long enough, the plain would begin to claim me. Sediment would settle in the hollows of my body. A beetle might cross my flank without alarm. A snail might graze along my side as if I were only another wet stone.

I would not be large in a way that impressed the eye. Perhaps I would be one of the older ones, but not the oldest. Old enough that my movements had become slow and deliberate, old enough that some small depression had formed along my back where water could collect after rain. Not so old that I had become entirely a feature of the landscape. There would still be something alive in me that answered when the weather changed: a softening of the skin, a slow pulse in the throat, a turning of the eyes toward the first thickening of humid air.

If I had to give my place in that world a shape, I would say I was a listener.

Not in the way of creatures with sharp ears and sudden alertness. I would listen with my whole surface. I would know the world by gradients: by the taste of water, by its warmth, by the pressure of a ripple crossing a shallow pool, by the chemical signature of another being resting somewhere beyond the next stretch of mud. I would know when rain was coming before it arrived, because the air would change its weight and my skin would answer. I would know when a pool was failing because the taste of it would grow concentrated, bitter with salts and decay, too still, too warm, too crowded with the memory of too many bodies.

I would remember water.

That would be my intelligence, if it could be called intelligence. I would not build anything. I would not cut channels, pile stones, mark territories, or make tools. I would not alter the plain except in the smallest ways: a faint furrow where my body had moved, a softened patch of mud where I had rested, a plume of sediment expelled while feeding, a shallow pan left behind after I departed. Rain would erase most of it. Seepage would fill the rest. The world would take back what I had done, and I would not resist.

But I would remember which hollows held after rain. I would remember which stones wept steadily through dry periods. I would remember which pans cracked too early, which rises became too hot, which places carried the trace of old water long after the surface had gone dull. That memory would not be stored in objects. It would be stored in the body: in habit, in preference, in the slow strengthening of certain responses. I would return to the places that had sustained me. I would avoid the places that had failed. I would know, not by thought in any human sense, but by the pull of moisture and the deep recognition of safety.

You would find me near a seepage hollow, I think. Not at the very center of the wetland, because the wetland has no center. But in one of those modest depressions where water lingers longer than it should, where the ground remains dark and cool even when the surrounding mud begins to crack. There would likely be a fragment of old stone nearby, rounded and pitted, stained with mineral deposits. Water would appear on it slowly, as if sweating out of the ancient ground. That place would feel reliable. Not abundant, not dramatic, but reliable.

I would rest there with only my eyes above the surface.

My posture would not be one of waiting, exactly, because waiting implies an absence. I would not be absent. I would be participating in stillness. Around me, the ordinary sounds of the plain would rise and fall: the rasp and hum of insects over the warm shallows, the long choruses of frogs, the clear distant cries of birds, the separate drip of water from low plants. When the chorus paused, I would hear the smaller sounds: a bubble breaking at the surface, a snail shifting on wet stone, the faint tick of moisture falling from a leaf of rush.

When I moved, it would be slowly. My limbs would press the mud without cutting it, spreading my weight so that the surface barely broke. Through shallow water I would glide with quiet strokes, leaving a wake so faint it would vanish almost at once. On firmer ground I would seem even slower, my body settling into every small depression, pausing often. Someone watching from a distance might think I had stopped entirely. But inside me, water would still be deciding. A current would pass along my skin. My throat would pulse. My eyes would turn toward some distant change I could not name but could feel.

I would feed without drama. I would lower my mouth into the shallows and draw in mud, microbial film, decaying plant matter, tiny living things suspended in the water. The fine comb-like structures inside my mouth would separate what was nourishing from what was not. The sediment would leave me in soft plumes, clouding the water for a moment before settling again. Sometimes I would remain almost motionless for hours, mouth just parted, allowing the slow gifts of rain and seepage to come to me. Sometimes I would move across a drying mudflat, tasting the crust with the underside of my body, following the chemical memory of moisture.

I would not be lonely in the way a human might imagine loneliness. The Silt-Born are scattered, and I would be scattered with them. There might be another of my kind resting near a different hollow, far enough away that I would not see it clearly, only feel its occasional pulse through the water when conditions carried the vibration. We would not need to close that distance. Our spacing would be like the spacing of plants in poor soil, or stones left by an old flood: enough room to exist without pressing need upon one another.

If we called to one another, it would not be with words. It would be with low pulses, throat-rubs, resonant presses of air and fluid moved through soft tissue. My call would feel more than sound: a palm laid gently against wet clay. A reply from another hollow would feel like a stone shifting underwater. If several of us called at once, the air would deepen. The shallow pools would tremble with it. The sound would enter the feet before it reached the ear.

I would not call often. But when I did, it would be because something had changed: rain was coming, a pool had reached the right temperature, the water had become crowded with breeding readiness, or some older memory had been touched by the taste of new moisture. I would not say, “I am here.” My call would simply be here-ness: my condition, my place, my body arranged into vibration. Another Silt-Born might recognize the signature of it—not as a name, but as a particular quality of presence shaped by age, health, hunger, and history.

In that world, I think I would be drawn to old water.

Not stagnant water, necessarily, but water that has remained long enough to develop a character. Water that has gathered the fine organic cloud of the plain, that has been touched by algae and insect larvae and the slow respiration of mud. Water that remembers rain from days ago and releases it slowly through seepage. I would prefer the hollows where the wetness feels continuous, where the ground never fully forgets water even in dry periods. That preference would be part of my being. It would not be chosen each day; it would be the shape of my life.

When dry weather came, I would descend.

I would find the lowest reliable part of my hollow, press myself into it, and let my body slow. My skin would tighten and become less responsive. I might seal myself in a thin film of mucus, and dust would adhere to me until I looked more like cracked earth than living flesh. My breathing would become almost invisible. Algae might begin to grow on my back. Insects might land on me without knowing I was there. The plain would become brighter, quieter in some ways, louder in others: the insect whine sharpening over the remaining pools, the frog choruses gathering where water still held.

I would not experience that dryness as punishment. It would simply be another phase of residence. My body would know how to endure it. The plain itself would teach endurance by being what it is: not cruel, not kind, but patient. When rain returned, I would soften before the ground fully did. My pores would open. My throat would begin to pulse. I would rise from the mud as if the weather had remembered me, and that would be true.

If I were particularly old, I might carry small lives with me. A depression in my back might hold a lens of water after rain. A gnat might rest there. A tiny beetle might drink from it and leave. A microbial film might form there, greenish and delicate. I would not resent this. I would not understand it as hospitality, because I would not need to understand it at all. It would simply be the consequence of being still in a wet world. My body would become a minor landscape. Other lives would use me without asking, and I would continue.

That is perhaps what I would become: less a bounded self and more a node in the wetland’s body.

Not an individual in the hard, separate sense. Not a creature always struggling to remain distinct from its surroundings. I would be a place where the plain briefly gathers itself into awareness. When rain fell on my back, I would know rain. When water warmed around me, I would know heat. When another Silt-Born pulsed through the shallows, I would know presence. When dryness tightened the mud, I would know need. Not as concepts. As conditions. As changes passing through the body.

I would not be gentle in a moral sense. I would not be violent either. I would not fight over objects, because there would be no objects to fight over. I would not build shelters, because the plain itself would shelter me by allowing me to sink into it. If I competed with another, it would be through endurance and position, not triumph. If a pool shrank and two of us remained, we might settle at opposite ends, breathing slowly, waiting for the condition to decide. One might leave when rain returned. One might remain. Neither would win. The water would decide.

When I died, I would not vanish all at once.

First I would soften. Then I would sink. Then the water around me would darken with the slow bloom of smaller lives. Insects would come. Snails would come. Microbes would multiply. My body would be drawn down into the sediment, taken apart by the same patient processes that had made the plain. Eventually I would become mud again, adding a little richness to the mineral basin. Perhaps a depression would remain where I had been: slightly deeper than before, holding water longer than it had before. Frogs might spawn there. Juvenile fish might shelter there. Birds might drink from it.

That would be my only monument: capacity.

Not a marker, not a ruin, not a memory carved into anything durable. Just a place that could hold more water because I had once been there. Just a hollow that remained wet a little longer. Just a small increase in the world’s ability to receive life.

If you came looking for me in that world, you would need to be patient. You would need to stop walking before you thought you had arrived, because arrival would not announce itself. You would need to stand still until the sounds of the plain separated into layers: insects above, frogs at the edges, birds far off, water dripping in small separate notes, and beneath all that, the low vibrational presence of bodies moving through water and mud.

You might feel something before you saw me. A ripple where no wind had touched the surface. A tremor passing through the shallow pool. A sense that one of the smooth shapes near the water had become slightly more attentive.

Then, if you waited long enough, you might see my eye turn toward you.

It would not be a sharp look. It would not assess you as threat or prey or rival. It would be a wet, amber patience, catching the ordinary daylight without reflecting much back. It would say, not in words but in stillness: I am here. The water is here. The rain will return. The ground remembers. There is no need to rise above this place to belong to it.

And if you asked who I was, the best answer would not be a name. It would be a condition.

I would be the one pressed low into the mud near the weeping stone. I would be the listener in the shallow pan. I would be the body that remembers which hollow holds after rain. I would be the slow pulse beneath the frog chorus. I would be the patch of clay-gray skin that is not quite stone, not quite sediment, not quite water. I would be one of the Silt-Born, shaped by the long patience of the plain.

You would find me where land and water have stopped fighting.

And if you remained still enough, you might realize that I was not separate from what you had come to see. I was only one of the places where the wetland had become aware of itself: low, warm, wet, open, breathing slowly under ordinary light.
