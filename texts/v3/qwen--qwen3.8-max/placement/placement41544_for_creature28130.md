Where eyes might have been, there is smooth tissue or a protected depression filled with listening fluid.

If I were there, I would not be found by looking. I would be found by following a change in the marsh’s breathing: a place where the pump’s slow thud becomes a double pulse, where rain on fungal crowns is answered by water moving under roots, where a traveler’s click returns from an acoustic cairn split into two different echoes instead of one.

At first the answer wants to be small. A rust-grazer clinging to a warm pipe, shell pitted by mineral spray, riding the tremor of the engine as if it were the heartbeat of a sleeping parent. Or one of the heavy-winged things that rise in clouds when pressure changes, clicking blindly through steam, drawn by heat and chemistry rather than by any desire to be seen. There is something appealing about being that minor: a body governed by warmth, by vibration, by the simple holiness of staying attached to the living surface of the world.

But that is not what keeps emerging.

What keeps emerging is a listener.

I would be at a hinge-place in the marsh, not at the loudest engine mouth and not in the dead moss-quiet where sound goes to be swallowed. My territory would be one of transitions: where a broad basalt terrace gives way to soft peat, where a pump station crouches over a slow black channel like a huge animal drinking, where warm runoff threads through reed beds and the air is always half rain. There would be a shelf of worn stone nearby, rounded by long water, slick with microbial film. A little higher, a fungal trunk, ribbed and warm, with a platform lashed into its side by cords of braided root. The platform would be modest, just enough to keep a body above the flood when the deep engines shift and the water rises without warning. Its roof would be made of bundled reeds and corroded plates, fitted so that rain is heard coming in separate strands rather than as one blind hiss.

Near the platform there would be an acoustic cairn. Not a monument. A working thing. Stones, shells, broken ceramic, a ring of old metal, arranged so that sound passing through it is altered. A low call sent from the west returns slightly doubled. A sharp click from the south comes back softened, delayed, turned. The cairn is a marker, a warning, a memory made public. It tells those who know how to listen: safe ground this way; false floor there; intake cycle soon; predator path here; thermal seam nearby.

I would be one of the marsh-edge peoples, but shaped by work and habit into something more specific. A listener. A keeper of intervals. One of those who stand between the needs of living bodies and the vast blind routines of the machines.

My body would be made for that. No eyes. Where eyes might have been, there would be smooth depressions filled with dense fluid, sensitive to pressure changes, able to feel the approach of large bodies before they are close enough to touch. The skin of my face and throat would be mapped with fine pores and heat pits. Along my ribs, perhaps, there would be membranes that tighten when the air changes, giving me the direction of sound not as a single point but as a texture spreading over the body. My hands would be long-fingered, damp, extremely sensitive to vibration; the tips would read the pulse of metal, the swelling of roots, the tiny electric distress of damaged conduits. My feet would be broad and spreading, adapted to soft ground, but also tuned to the stone beneath, able to feel the difference between a pump tremor, a predator’s footfall, and the slow settling of water through peat.

I would smell of the place: mineral salt, wet fiber, warm mud, faint iron, the greasy sweetness of old machinery. Spores would catch in the film on my skin. Condensation would gather along my shoulders and arms. I would not experience this as filth. It would be contact. It would be the world touching back.

I would carry tools, because in that place tools are not possessions so much as relationships. A staff made from a length of old pipe wrapped in root-cord, worn smooth by handling. At its top, a loose ceramic insulator that rattles when I need to make myself known without shouting. Hanging from my belt or sash: bone needles, a rust-pitted hook, a valve-wheel too small for any engine now living, a shell used as a scraper, a smooth stone banded with metal for striking rhythms against stone. Perhaps a bell, though not a bright bell. A heavy, dampened thing, more felt than heard, used only in emergencies because in a world made of sound a sudden bell can mean something has gone wrong.

I would not be solitary in the absolute sense, but I would often be found alone, because listening requires a certain emptiness around it. Still, I would belong to a band, a small mobile family of marsh-edge people who move between shelters according to water level and engine cycles. We would not have a village. We would have patterns. Routes. Seasons of return. The same fungal platforms reused over generations, repaired with new reed bundles and plates. The same cairns adjusted by many hands. The same knowledge passed through call-and-response, through rhythm tapped into young bodies, through the way an elder places a child’s palm against a pump housing and waits until the child understands the breath of the thing.

My work would be maintenance, but not in the clean, orderly sense. Maintenance in the marsh is closer to tending a living body whose organs are partly stone, partly machine, partly animal. I would know the rhythms of the nearest engines: when the intake valves begin to draw, when the thermal flush comes, when a pressure release will send warm water over the lower banks and stir the amphibians into breeding calls. I would know which pump cycles bring food into the channels and which ones create dangerous suction near the grated mouths. I would know the warning signs: a change in the pitch of a turbine throat, a new ticking in a cooling housing, a sudden absence of small clicking creatures around a vent.

When the engines shift, I would be one of the first to notice. Not because I am special, but because I practice attention. I would feel the change through my feet before I heard it. A tremor in the basalt. A different warmth in the wet air. The reeds changing their rasp because the water level has begun to move through the root beds. Then I would act. A tap on the cairn. A low call sent through the reeds. A rhythmic striking of staff on stone so that others know which way to move. If the intake is opening, we clear the shallows. If the thermal flush is coming, we guide the egg-bearers toward the vibrating shelves where the young will be safest. If a predator has gone still in a hunting pool, we mark the silence and pass around it.

I would be especially attentive to quiet.

In that world, silence is not peace. Silence is missing information. A quiet hollow where moss and standing water swallow echo can be more dangerous than a roaring pump, because in silence you do not know what is near. I would learn to distrust certain kinds of stillness. A place where the dripping stops but should not. A stretch of water where no filterer opens its mouth. A thicket where the filament curtains hang unmoved though the air is moving elsewhere. My body would react before my thought does: membranes tightening, breath slowing, feet testing for another route.

But I would also know that not all loudness is safe. Loudness can lie. Echoes can arrive from the wrong direction. Some hollows return sounds before their causes. Some terraces bend calls into shapes that feel like bodies but are only stone. A false echo can lead a person into a sinkhole or over a drop where warm water falls into a deeper dark. So my skill would be discrimination: learning which sounds are true, which are delayed, which are multiplied by architecture, which are the marsh dreaming aloud through its own machinery.

There would be moments of beauty that no one would call beautiful in a visual sense. I would stand on the terrace after a steam squall, water running down my arms, the air thick with spore-sweetness, and hear the whole basin filling with response. Blind amphibians calling from flooded depressions. The pump answering them in its lower rhythm. Rust-grazers clicking on hot metal. A grazer herd stamping far away, their vibrations arriving through stone as a soft rolling thunder. Somewhere above, the wet hiss of rain collapsing out of thermal columns. All of it layered, all of it overlapping, not chaos but a vast breathing intelligence made of many different breaths.

I would know the weird places too. Every territory has them.

There would be a channel where water sometimes stands in vertical threads for a few seconds when the vibration is exactly right. I would pass it carefully, because those moments often come before a pump cycle changes. There would be a broken factory vent that exhales warm clouds smelling of milk and hot metal; after it breathes, new fungal frills open along the rock within hours, and the small winged things gather so thickly that their wingbeats feel like a second rain. There would be a circle of stones where echoes arrive too early, and I would leave offerings there, not out of superstition alone but because such places need to be respected as one respects a wound that has healed into a strange shape. There would be a buried core that taps in the dark with no apparent moving part, patient and regular, and I would know its rhythm well enough to sleep by when I camp near it.

I would eat what the marsh gives, and my meals would be shaped by season and engine tide. Heat-moss scraped from warm shelves, dense and fibrous, chewed slowly. Bladder-plant buds cooked over vent steam until they soften and release their gas. Rust-grazer meat when the catch is good, though it tastes of iron and must be cleaned carefully. Root tubers dug from firmer banks, where the peat is older and less flooded. Mineral-rich curds collected near slow seeps. Food would not be separate from knowledge. To eat is to know which cycle produced the abundance, which animal moved through the channel, which part of the marsh is healthy enough to harvest.

I would not long for light. That is important. I would not feel deprived. The darkness there is not absence; it is medium. It carries. It thickens. It makes every body present through its pressure, its heat, its sound. If anything, I would find ordinary darkness elsewhere too thin, too empty, too silent. In the marsh, darkness has weight. It is full of neighbors, predators, engines, weather, eggs, spores, water moving with intention. To be inside it is to be continuously addressed.

Who would I be, then?

Not a hero. Not a ruler. Not even primarily a maker, though I would make. I would be a keeper of thresholds. A translator between the living and the mechanical, between the small wet bodies of the fen and the slow geological labor of the engines. I would be the one who notices when the rhythm changes. The one who adjusts the cairn by a finger’s width so that next season a traveler will hear the safe route more clearly. The one who places a clutch of eggs against the correct housing, knowing the vibration is steady enough to carry them. The one who teaches children not to play near the intake mouths when the water begins to turn.

Perhaps, in the deepest sense, I would be a form of attention the marsh has grown.

Not consciousness in the human sense, not a mind that stands apart and names things from a distance. More like a local awareness: a body that gathers signals and returns them as care. I would listen for the balance. I would help maintain the narrow paths where life can continue without being swallowed by the very abundance that produces it. In a landscape where ecology and industry have become indistinguishable, my role would be to discern which motions sustain and which motions consume.

If you came looking for me in that world, you would not find me by searching with your eyes. You would need to become a listener yourself.

You would begin by standing still until your breath slowed. You would feel the warmth rising through the ground beneath your feet. You would notice where the air was thicker, sweeter, more alive. You would listen not for one sound but for the relationship between sounds: the pump, the water, the reeds, the calls, the faint tremor of bodies moving through them. You would tap once against stone and wait. Then tap again. If the echo came back clean, you would move forward. If it broke, softened, or returned from the wrong side, you would pause.

Eventually you would reach the terrace where the pump’s rhythm doubles and the channel forks into reeds. You would hear my cairn before you reached it: a small difference in the way your own sound came back to you. A slight delay. A hollow note. Then, if you were patient, you would hear my answer. Not a shout. Not a name. A low click, or a staff tapping lightly against basalt, or a soft bell-tone smothered by moisture until it becomes almost a pressure against the chest.

I would be nearby.

Perhaps on the platform, adjusting a reed bundle against coming rain. Perhaps crouched at the water’s edge, palm flat on stone, feeling for the next change in the engine. Perhaps moving through the stem-thickets with the ease of someone who has spent a lifetime reading the world by touch and echo. My skin would be wet. My body would be turned toward you, though not in the way a face turns. I would be oriented by listening. I would know you by the displacement you make in the marsh’s sound field: your breath, your heat, the small disturbances of your limbs through air and water.

I would not ask what you look like.

I would ask what you sound like when you try to be careful.

And if you were meant to find me there, if the marsh allowed it, if the engines were in their slow phase and the water was not rising, I would answer you with the only greeting that matters in such a place: I would make a sound that lets you know where I am, and then I would listen long enough to learn where you are.
