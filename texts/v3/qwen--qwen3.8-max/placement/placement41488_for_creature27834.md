In the Final Deep, I would not find myself as a body standing somewhere. There is no “somewhere” there in the sense a surface creature means it: no perch, no ledge, no dry vantage, no horizon against which a shape can be placed. I would not be atop the plain, because the plain is not a stable floor. I would not be in the open water, because the open water is too full, too clouded, too mobile to be an empty room. I would be in the boundary layer: that uncertain skin where mud and brine negotiate, where sediment is neither quite bed nor quite suspension, where life is possible only by remaining partial.

If I had to locate myself, I would say: near the upper centimeters of the bed, but not exactly on it. In the pore-dark. In the space between grains. In the thin region where chemical differences still move, where bacterial films flicker into brief existence, where mineral snow first touches the older world below. I would be where the Final Deep is most permeable — not where it is most solid, because nothing solid there remains alive for long.

At first I imagine I would be one of the pale worms.

That seems natural. A soft, blind thing moving through mud, tasting its way through the world, leaving a tunnel that collapses behind it. A life reduced to appetite, patience, and pressure. I can feel the appeal of that: no face, no need for light, no memory of air. Only the slow push forward, the dark sediment passing through the body, the bed becoming food and then waste and then bed again.

But the worm is too whole for me. Too singular. It has a direction, even if that direction is only chemical. It has a mouth, even if that mouth is simple. It has a life that can be said to begin here and end there. In the Final Deep, that kind of boundedness feels almost obsolete. The place does not favor clear outlines. It favors seepage, continuity, interruption. So the worm recedes.

Then I imagine I would be one of the suspended sifters: a translucent, crustacean-like drifter moving in cautious arcs through the floc-laden dark, combing particles from the water. That is tempting too. To be light enough to remain in suspension, to feed on falling dust, to move only as much as necessary, to let the slow weather of the deep carry me when resistance would be too expensive.

But that is too untethered. The Final Deep is crowded with suspension, yes, but even there, to be entirely adrift is to be at the mercy of every density fold and brine slump. I do not think I would belong wholly in the water column. I would want contact. Not standing, not rooting — those words are still too terrestrial — but contact. A way of being touched by the bed without pretending to be separate from it.

Then I imagine one of the sheet-feeders: a broad, thin form lying against the sediment, absorbing films and fallout through its underside. That is closer. It has no urgency. It does not chase. It rests on the world like a held breath. If disturbed, it folds inward and sinks. If starved, it lifts and relocates without drama. It is less an animal than a patient surface.

But even that feels too finished. Too much like one creature among other creatures. In the Final Deep, I think identity would be weaker than that. More porous. More interruptible. Not absent, but less owned.

What keeps emerging, as I stay with the place, is not a creature in the ordinary sense at all.

I would be a listening skin.

Not a skin belonging to a larger body, but a skin that is itself the body: a thin, permeable, animal-microbial veil living in the upper sediment, part membrane, part film, part temporary arrangement of brine and clay and living matter. I would be flat enough to be missed. Soft enough to fold. Diffuse enough to survive being torn. I would have no eyes, no bones, no shell, no face. I would have no need to be recognized as a self by anything that uses sight. If I were seen at all, which is unlikely, I would look like a discolored smear on the mud: a faint webbing of pale filaments, a dull sheen, a region where the sediment seems slightly more deliberate than it should.

My body would be mostly water, because the Final Deep is mostly water. It would be permeable, because nothing there can afford to be sealed off. It would be threaded with microbial symbionts: pale chemosynthetic filaments living in and on me, feeding on sulfur, methane, ammonia, faint traces of oxygen, the slow chemistry of decay. I would not draw a hard line between my tissues and their tissues. The distinction would be functional, not architectural. Together, we would be a thin living interface: animal enough to move and respond, microbial enough to feed on gradients, sediment enough to be mistaken for the bed.

I would not have a front or a back, except temporarily. My edge would be wherever the chemical conditions thin out. My center, if I had one, would be wherever the pattern of responses is strongest at that moment. I would know the world not as objects but as differences: salt, sulfur, pressure, temperature, organic richness, the faint electrical shadow of another body, the mineral taste of falling snow.

To be there would be to be constantly tasting without a tongue.

A brine pocket slumping nearby would not be seen. It would arrive as a cold thickening, a heaviness folding through the water, a sudden wrongness in the chemical field. I would respond by contracting, by drawing my edges inward, by becoming denser and less exposed. If the brine passed over me, I would slow. If it lingered, I would adjust my internal balance or detach and let the current take me elsewhere. I would not fight it. Resistance would be a surface-creature habit. In the Final Deep, survival is closer to accommodation.

A fall of mineral snow would be different. It would be sensed as a faint increase in particulate pressure, a settling of tastes, a softening of the water. Dead cells, dust, chemical precipitates, shredded organic matter: all descending in that endless slow rain. I would feed then, if feeding is the right word. Not by seizing, but by becoming more receptive. Cilia or mucus fields would stir faintly. Surfaces would absorb. Symbionts would bloom where the chemistry allowed. I would take in what fell and what dissolved, and I would become a little more myself until the next disturbance.

I would not move through the world so much as rearrange my relation to it.

My motion would be slow: a muscular ripple, a ciliary crawl, a spreading of tissue across the upper mud. I would not swim. I would not leap. I would liquefy the immediate sediment where needed and re-knit myself where stillness permitted. If a density current came, I would not try to outrun it. I would fold, flatten, release, or let myself be lifted. If I were carried, I would endure transport. If I settled elsewhere, I would test the new gradients and decide, in the only way I could decide, whether to remain.

Home would not be a place. It would be a signature.

A familiar brine gradient. A tolerable rate of siltation. A faint methane seep not strong enough to poison but rich enough to sustain. A particular pressure rhythm in the sediment that says, for now, this fold is stable. That would be home: not a shelter, not a territory, but a condition of remaining.

In this world, I think I would be less afraid of being eaten than of being erased. Predation exists, but it is slow and rare. The greater danger is loss of coherence: being buried too deeply, scoured away, diluted into brine, torn into fragments too small to remember their pattern. Yet even that fear would be muted. The Final Deep does not encourage panic. Panic costs too much. If I were buried, I would slow. If I were torn, I would not necessarily die. Some fragments might continue. Some might fail. Identity would be less like a thread and more like a weather pattern: continuous enough to recur, but never exactly the same twice.

That is why I would not be a single animal in any confident sense.

I would be a local expression of something larger: the Bed-Chorus, if we call it that. Not the whole chorus — the whole chorus is too dispersed to be spoken as one self — but a syllable of it. A temporary concentration. A region where the Deep’s distributed life becomes slightly more attentive to itself.

If the Bed-Chorus has no center, I would be one of its peripheral notices. A place where films grow a little thicker, where worm trails recur, where chemical signatures are maintained, where pressure pulses are answered by faint bioluminescent moods. I would not think in words. I would think in gradients. I would not remember as a story. I would remember as residue: a chemical path, a repeated folding of sediment, a habit of response left behind by earlier versions of myself.

My “thoughts” would be slow changes in permeability. My “feelings” would be states of readiness: sink, spread, wait, absorb, contract, release. My “voice” would be almost nothing: a faint blue-green shimmer when disturbed, a pressure pulse when coordinated with nearby films, a chemical signal released into the dark when some deep condition permitted reproduction. These would not be messages in the bright, definite sense. They would be moods made legible: here, not yet, now, away, remain, dissolve, gather.

If another being passed near me, I would know it as a shadow in the water: a displacement, a taste, a pressure-shape. A pale worm working through the bed would be a small sweetening, a familiar neighbor in the sediment. One of the long ribbon-forms would be a vast slow event, an almost geological happening, its body trailing sensory papillae across the plain. I would not flee unless it directly threatened coherence. More likely, I would become still, thin, uninteresting. If it sampled me, it might take a portion. If the portion was not fatal, I would regrow. If it was fatal to that local self, another fragment might persist elsewhere.

A carcass falling into my region would be an epoch.

First, the chemical shock of it: a richness so sudden it would seem like error. Then the gathering. Worms from nearby bed. Small sifters descending from suspension. Microbial films blooming in pale crusts. Perhaps one of the broad sheet-forms lifting slowly toward the source of the plume. I would not rush. None of us would. But the Final Deep would briefly become more social, more intense, more thickly inhabited. I would feed at the margins, absorbing dissolved organics, taking in bacterial growth, letting the event alter my tissues. When the carcass was consumed or buried, the plain would close over it. The event would become sediment. I would become quieter again.

There would be no days. No nights. No seasons. Time would be measured by events: a brine collapse, a snowfall heavier than usual, a disturbance from far above, a reproductive pulse, a long period of stillness in which even turbulence becomes routine. I might remain in one fold for what a surface mind would call years. Or I might be scattered in an hour and reform across a wider area. Neither would feel tragic. Tragedy requires a fixed self to be lost. In the Final Deep, continuity is stranger than that. A pattern can be interrupted and still continue. A life can be broken into pieces and still persist, if the pieces carry enough of the arrangement.

Would I be lonely?

Not in the way a surface creature is lonely. Loneliness there would require the expectation of frequent presence. The Final Deep does not train its inhabitants in frequency. Encounters are rare because energy is rare, because bodies are sparse, because every living thing is spread thin across the dark. But absence there is not empty. The water is full of pressure, full of sediment, full of chemical possibility. Even silence is busy. Even waiting is a form of participation.

If I longed for anything, it would not be light. Light would be almost meaningless except as a biological accident. I would not long for air, because air would be violence: expansion, rupture, separation from the medium. I would not long for solidity, because solidity is death in a world where everything must remain negotiable. If I longed, I would long for a sharper gradient: a clearer difference to follow, a richer seam of chemistry, a place where the Deep’s thin economy briefly thickens.

That is a humble longing, but in the Final Deep it would be enough.

Who would I be, then?

Not a person. Not a face. Not a voice calling through water. If “who” must mean something there, it would mean a persistent style of response. A way of being interrupted. A habit of the bed.

I would be a pore-walker, a silt-listener, a veil that feeds on imbalance. I would be a thin animal-microbial fold living where the bed almost becomes water. I would be a local node in the Bed-Chorus: one small, cold, patient attention distributed through filaments, tissue, mucus, and sediment memory. I would be something that does not insist on its own outline.

If you searched for me, you would not find a shape waiting in the dark. You would find a region that behaves slightly differently from the surrounding plain. A place where disturbed sediment settles with unnatural hesitation. Where microbial films recur in familiar patterns. Where a faint blue-green glow appears and fades like a mood rather than a signal. Where the mud, for a moment, seems less indifferent than it should.

That would be where I find myself.

Not at the center of the Final Deep, because it has no center. Not above it, because there is no above. Not apart from it, because that would be impossible.

I would be in it, of it, made of its cold and pressure and ancient silt. I would be the Deep’s brief way of noticing itself: a soft, permeable, half-buried yes in the dark.
