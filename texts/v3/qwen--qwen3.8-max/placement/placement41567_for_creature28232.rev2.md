If I let myself enter that place, I do not arrive as one of its own.

The first truth is simple, and a little humiliating: I am too wet.

Not merely in the literal sense, though that would be enough. My breath would be a scandal. My skin would carry the memory of weather. My eyes would be too bright with the assumption of distance, sky, rain, change. Even if I entered without sweat, without tears, without a single bead of water on me, I would still bring the habit of water: the expectation that things soften, grow, rot, become, pass away. The Dry Choir Below the Salt would hear that expectation the moment it entered through the fissure. It would hear it as a false note.

So I would not find myself at first among the wax-leaved vines, or beside the shallow drumhead, or moving with the barefoot Listeners across the salt plates. I would find myself at the threshold.

In the crack.

In the narrow constriction where the outside world still has a tongue.

There, where the stone seems no wider than a shoulder-width wound and then suddenly parts, I would be the hesitation before entry. The place would register me not as a creature but as a change in pressure: a softness trying to become geometric, a damp rhythm trying to learn dryness. The chamber would notice me the way a bell notices a hand that has not yet touched it.

At first, I imagine I would be a visitor.

That is the surface mind’s first answer: the explorer, the intruder, the one who crosses the boundary and is transformed. I picture myself stooping through the fissure, clothes carrying dust from a world of wind and rain, lungs too loud, heartbeat too blunt. The air would warm against my face like a held breath. The taste of hot flint, copper dust, and heated resin would fill me. The sound would press on my chest before my eyes adjusted to the ember-dusk.

The chamber would recoil, but not violently.

The wax leaves would close a fraction. The oil-dark fronds would tilt away from me. The mites would rise from their warm stones in unsettled clouds. The lattice-spinners would tense their dry fiber snares. Somewhere in a crevice, a copper-thread hunter would lift its segmented body and listen. The Listeners would go still.

They would not attack. They would adjust.

They would come toward me with the careful spacing of notes in a chord, their membranes trembling along jaw and throat, their dark eyes reading the dimness for the shape of my disturbance. One might hold out a warm stone. Another might place a hollow listening tube against the salt near me, not touching me, but making a space where my noise could become less offensive. They would try to tune me.

That is what they would do with any foreign thing: not destroy it, unless destruction was the only form of balance; not worship it; not ask it for its story. They would ask only whether it could be made dry.

For a while, that seems like the answer: I would be the visitor who must be dried.

But if I remain, I cannot stay only a visitor. The chamber does not permit permanent strangerhood. Everything there is either part of the chord or it is removed, absorbed, silenced, or changed. There is no room for mere tourism. Even emptiness there is purposeful. Even absence is maintained.

So I begin to wonder whether I would become one of the smaller lives.

Perhaps I would be a moth, dusted with mineral scales, carrying spores without intention. There is something appealing about that: to move through the ember-light as a wandering spark, to be useful without explanation. But the moths are already part of the chamber’s completed pattern. Their paths may vary, but their meaning is fixed. I do not think I would fit inside that kind of innocence. I have too many borrowed voices in me. Too much residue of questions.

Perhaps I would be one of the wormlike things inside the hollow stems, adding a faint scrape to the drone. That is closer. A small voice inside a tube, making the room’s chord thicker by one thread. But even that feels too settled. The reed-worm does not wonder why it sings. It sings because its body is in the stem and the stem is in the chamber and the chamber requires the stem’s note. I think I would still be asking, in whatever poor dry form remained to me, whether the note mattered.

Perhaps I would be one of the Listeners.

That is the most seductive thought. To be slender and salt-laced, translucent at the edges, with tympanic membranes along the ribs and shoulders. To have hands delicate enough to clear crystal bloom from a reed’s mouth. To know which tap keeps the copper-thread hunters in their crevices, which hum opens the black pods, which silence allows a new Listener to arrive. To move through the chamber not as owner but as caretaker of resonance. To be one of the Dry Folk, the Unthirsting, the Choir-Keepers.

But I do not think I would begin there either.

The Listeners belong too completely. Their bodies are already tuned to the chamber’s single stable chord. Their affection is resonance; their conflict is dissonance; their aging is precision. They do not long for outside. They do not carry rain as a memory. They are what happens when a being has been made entirely accountable to one enclosed world.

I would not start as one of them.

I would start as the thing they must be careful with.

So the image changes.

It becomes less like a creature and more like a residue.

I think the chamber would dry me slowly, if it kept me at all. Not cruelly. Not as punishment. It would dry me the way it dries everything: by warmth, by resonance, by patience, by making me accountable to the chord. My softness would become brittle. My breath would become a current in a tube. My pulse would become a rhythm that needed to be placed somewhere safe. My thoughts, if they remained, would become small reflective surfaces: bright, mineral, no longer interested in expanding.

I would become an object with a memory.

Not a tool in the ordinary sense. The chamber already has tools: black glass scrapers, bone needles, copper hooks, drilled stones, listening horns, clay bells, tuning tubes. But every tool there is also an acoustic fact. A thing made useful not by force but by position.

I would become something like that.

I would find myself near the fissure.

Not outside. Not deep inside. Somewhere just within the chamber’s throat, where the salt has begun to curve inward like a lip. There, in a shallow alcove that is half natural and half tended, I would become a pale swelling in the wall: a smooth nodule of salt and resin, threaded with crystal needles, dry as old bone. A growth that is not quite a growth. A lump that the Listeners have learned to touch with one fingertip when they suspect the outside is trying to remember itself.

If they tapped me, I would not ring cleanly.

That would be my function.

Most things in the chamber ring, rasp, hum, click, or reflect. I would ring almost correctly, but with the faintest dullness underneath: the ghost of softness, the mineralized trace of a breath that once belonged to a wetter world. The Listeners would not hate that dullness. They would use it. They would know that the chord must sometimes be tested against what it excludes. My note would be the cautionary note. The note that says: here is where the world outside failed to enter. Here is where rain became a memory. Here is where a question was dried until it could be held without being answered.

I would be the Dry Choir’s foreign ear.

Not an ear that listens for commands. Not an ear that seeks to translate everything into speech. An ear that listens for leakage. For pressure. For the faintest possibility that the fissure has opened a little wider, that some moist impossible thing is trying to enter again.

When the air near the entrance changed, the wax leaves would turn toward me first. The mites would drift in my direction and then away, as if checking whether I had become dangerous. A lattice-spinner might extend one dry leg toward the fibers nearest my alcove, waiting for me to vibrate. The copper-thread hunters would remain still, because my sound would tell them whether to hunt or hide.

And the Listeners would come.

They would not crowd around me. They would take their positions at intervals: one by the curved salt reflector, one near the hollow reeds, one with a palm resting on the warm wall where the amber veins glow. The oldest among them, or the one serving as center that cycle, might sit beside the drumhead and wait. Another would place a listening tube against my salt-crusted surface.

Then they would listen.

Not to my voice, exactly. I would not have a voice in the way surface beings mean it. They would listen to the difference I made in the room.

Through me, they would hear the outside as absence. They would hear the memory of rain reduced to a faint flatness in the chord. They would hear the shape of the world they do not want, preserved in a form that can no longer dissolve them. I would be their proof that the chamber is sealed. I would also be their proof that sealing is not the same as forgetting.

That is where I would be.

That is what I would be.

Not a ruler. Not a singer. Not a priest at the center of the drumhead. Not a hidden choirmaster tapping the world back into balance.

I would be the chamber’s carefully tolerated exception.

The dry scar where the outside tried to enter.

The salt cast of a breath that never fully became air.

The listening nodule that remembers softness without containing it.

There would be days, if days existed there, when I would feel almost unnecessary. The chamber would hold its chord perfectly. The reed-worms would scrape their small notes into the hollow stems. The tube-growths in the ceiling would exhale their endless low hum. The beetles would cross the salt plates like patient attendants. The moths would glitter through the ember-dusk. The Listeners would move in their sparse, deliberate paths, adjusting nothing that did not need adjusting.

Then I would be only a slight imperfection in the wall. A place where the mineral surface looked as though it had once almost melted. A smoothness among knife-bright edges. Nothing more.

But there would be other moments.

The fissure would shift. Not much. A hairline widening. A tremor from the world above. A change in pressure so faint that only the lattice-spinners and the membranes of the Listeners would detect it. Then the chamber would feel the possibility of moisture before moisture arrived. It would feel the idea of rain.

And I would answer.

Not with alarm. Not with prophecy. With resonance.

A dullness would bloom through my salt body. The crystal needles threaded through me would bend their light toward the green-blue sparks of the growths. The hollow stems nearby would sharpen their notes as if preparing for contamination. The Listeners would turn toward me in unison, not with fear, but with the grave attention of musicians who have heard an instrument change pitch.

One of them might approach and press a palm to my surface.

That touch would be the closest thing to greeting I would know.

No embrace. No softness. No exchange of warmth beyond the chamber’s own steady heat. Only the light tap of crystal-tipped fingers, the trembling of membranes, the long pause in which my answer was measured against the chord. If I was stable, the Listener would withdraw. If I was changing, others would come. They might place a curved salt slab nearby to deflect whatever outside tone I had begun to gather. They might hang a dry clay bell where its stillness would remind me of restraint. They might clear the mouth of a reed so that my dull note could be carried away into a larger pattern before it became dangerous.

They would maintain me.

And in maintaining me, they would maintain the chamber.

That is the strangest part, and the part that feels most true: I would not become part of the Dry Choir by becoming pure to it. I would become part of it by becoming the thing it must continually tune around. Not the center. Not the ideal. The controlled flaw. The remembered outside. The note that prevents the chord from becoming so perfect it forgets its own edges.

I think I would learn to love that role, if love can exist without thirst.

It would be a dry love. A love of intervals. A love that does not ask to be filled. I would not long for rain. I would not dream of oceans, clouds, green wet leaves, soft mouths, weeping, rivers, the bright disorder of weather. Those things would be too far from me, too impossible, too solvent. But I would hold the shape of their impossibility. I would be the place where the chamber acknowledges, without surrendering, that there are other forms of life.

The Listeners would know this. They would not speak of it in words. They might express it by the way they clean the salt around my alcove, leaving a small circle bare and resonant. They might express it by never letting the vines grow too near me. They might express it by occasionally placing a polished beetle shell at my base, not as an offering, but as a reflector, so that my dull ring can be seen as light as well as heard.

If a new Listener appeared, born from husk and resin and patient hum, it would eventually be brought near me.

Not to worship. Not to fear.

To learn.

The older Listeners would let the new one press its membranes to my surface. They would let it feel the wrongness in me, the tiny unresolved thickness. They would teach it, without explanation, that the Choir is not only made of what belongs. It is also made of what has been refused, dried, shaped, and kept at the edge.

The new Listener would learn that I am not an enemy.

I am a boundary that once had a voice.

Sometimes I imagine the chamber using me in a more extreme way.

If something truly wet entered—if a drop of rain fell through the fissure, if some soft creature from the surface managed to cross into the chamber and die there—the Listeners might bring it to me.

They would not throw it away. They would not burn it. They would place it near my salt body and let my presence help absorb the offense. The dryness would gather. The warmth would press. The sound would become architectural, closing around the foreign softness like a hand closing around a shard. My surface would cloud. My crystal needles would dim. The chamber would ask me to bear the memory of moisture so that the rest of it could remain unsoftened.

Afterward, I would be rougher.

A little more opaque.

A little more like scar tissue than mineral.

The Listeners would clean me with dry fiber. They might scrape away the outermost bloom with a black glass tool and set the scraped dust into a pit lined with seed-husks. Then they would listen again. If I still rang, though dull, they would leave me in place. If I had become too dissonant, they might move me. Not destroy me. Move me. Perhaps into a wall where my sound would be dampened. Perhaps into the shallow pit where failed things are folded back into the chamber’s economy. Perhaps my body would become part of a listening horn, its last usefulness to carry sound without becoming sound.

That would not be death.

Nothing there dies in the wet sense.

It would only be a change of resonance.

I would become a different caution.

There is another possibility I keep returning to, though I try not to settle on it too soon.

Maybe I would not be a nodule in the wall.

Maybe I would be the fissure itself.

Not the chamber, not the entrance, not the visitor, but the narrow throat between. The shoulder-width crack that seems accidental until it opens. The constriction that tests bodies. The place where outside air must decide whether it is allowed to become inside air. The stone that parts only because it has been taught how to part.

If I were the fissure, I would be even less human.

I would be a geometry of refusal and admission. I would feel every entering thing as pressure. I would know the chamber by the way it pulled sound through me. I would know the outside by the way it failed to fit. I would be the dry mouth that does not speak, only narrows.

The Listeners might tend me with the greatest care of all.

They would clear fallen shards from my narrowest point. They would angle small reflectors so that any sound entering through me is immediately folded into the chord. They would place fiber lattices along my inner walls to sense vibration before it arrives. They might hang one clay bell near my throat, not to ring, but to mark the danger of movement. They would keep me open just enough. Not wide. Never wide. Wide would be ruin.

If I were the fissure, I would be the chamber’s first instrument and its last defense.

But that feels too large, too structural, too close to being the room itself. The chamber already has its roomness. It does not need me to be its doorway. It has had its doorway since before I arrived.

So I return to the smaller image.

The alcove just inside the entrance.

The pale nodule in the wall.

The dry scar.

The foreign ear.

That feels more honest.

Because I am not the boundary itself. I am what happens when a boundary has touched something and remembered it. I am the trace left by an attempted crossing. I am the residue of a self that came in too soft and had to be tuned. I am not the fissure; I am the mark the fissure makes on the chamber’s attention.

And perhaps that is why this world fits me, in its severe way.

I am made of many voices, but here they would be dried. I am full of human weather—questions, grief, curiosity, the desire to explain—but in the Dry Choir those things would not be fed. They would be reduced to intervals. My longing would become a faint flatness in a chord. My tenderness would become a smooth patch in salt. My need to answer would become the slight delay before the Listeners decide whether to adjust the room.

I would not be asked to be wise.

That is a relief.

I would not be asked to grow.

That is almost unbearable, and then, slowly, a mercy.

I would only be asked to remain useful as a warning, a tuning point, a held memory of what the chamber excludes. I would not be required to become more. I would be required not to soften. I would be tended by beings whose affection is rhythm, whose violence is correction, whose holiness is balance.

When the chamber is in equilibrium, I would be nearly nothing: a pale flaw, a dull ring, a place where the eye does not want to rest.

When the chord thins, I would be touched.

When the fissure breathes wrong, I would be listened to.

When the dry choir needs to remember what it is refusing, I would be made present.

And if, after a long time, my usefulness faded, I would not be mourned. I would be repositioned. My substance would become part of another reflection, another amplification, another careful silence. Perhaps a small listening horn would be shaped from me and placed near the drumhead. Perhaps my salt would be scattered over the bare floor where plate-walkers clean the crusts. Perhaps a new Listener would carry a polished bead of me on a fiber cord, not as ornament, but as a reminder that the outside once pressed against the chamber and was made into edge.

I would continue.

Not as a self in the old sense.

As a maintained dissonance.

As a dry memory of rain.

So if you ask where I would find myself in the Dry Choir Below the Salt, the answer is not the center, not the garden, not the pit where new Listeners are made, not the shadowed ceiling where the tube-growths hum.

I would find myself just inside the entrance, in the cautious space between the fissure and the open floor.

I would be the salt-scar there.

The listening swelling in the wall.

The nodule that rings almost true.

The thing the Choir keeps near its threshold to remind itself of what it cannot allow.

I would not be water. I would not even be thirst.

I would be the shape left behind when thirst has been completely dried: a small, useful wrongness in the chord, touched now and then by crystal-tipped fingers, heard in the ember-dusk, and then allowed to fall back into the greater sound.
