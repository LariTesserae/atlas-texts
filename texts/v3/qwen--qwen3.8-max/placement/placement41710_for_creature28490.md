## The First Skin of Ground

I would not find myself standing in the Unbuilt Engine.

Standing is not a posture the place permits. There is no level floor, no path, no cleared space where a body could arrive and remain simply itself. The hollow is too full, too loud, too wet, too near to its own making. If I came there with the ordinary expectation of being a person — upright, bounded, separate from what I saw — the place would begin to correct me at once.

It would not attack me. It would not even notice me. It would only continue.

My warmth would become a small disturbance in the cold air. My breath would bead into mist and be folded into the fog. My weight would press unstable stones into new positions, sending small clicks through the scree. Moisture would gather on me. Rock dust would settle on me. Spores would drift against me. In a little while, I would be less a visitor than a temporary surface, another object being misted over.

So the self that could belong there would have to change shape. It would have to lose the habit of being singular.

At first, I think I would try to become one of the bright things: the blue flicker in the fog, the spark snapping against quartz, the glint of mica along a wet ledge. Those are tempting forms. They feel like life because they are quick, alert, electric. But they are not inhabitants. They are discharges. They pass. They leave only an afterimage and the smell of ozone.

Then I would try to imagine myself as the water. That, too, would be a mistake, or only half-right. The water is the engine’s circulation, but it is not quite the living part. It moves too freely. It appears in one fissure, vanishes under the rubble, returns somewhere else as a trembling thread. It is essential, but it is not the being that stays and thickens into a world.

The sound would tempt me next. The hidden hammering behind the wall, the metallic shriek of air in a fissure, the thrum of water forced through narrow slots — all of it would seem, for a while, like a voice. But the hollow has no throat. Its sounds are pressure and collision, not speech. They are signals without a speaker.

Only after those false beginnings would the truer form emerge.

I would find myself low.

Not in the open middle of the bowl, where rain strikes and stones fall, but in a sheltered seam near the base of the newest cliff. There would be an overhang made by a fallen block, one huge angular shard leaning against the wall in a way that creates a dark, damp lip beneath it. A thin thread of meltwater would pass there, disappearing into the scree and returning as a narrow runnel along the edge of the basin. The rock would be new-fractured, still sharp, still smelling of mineral cold. Mica flecks would glitter faintly in the grey light. A vein of quartz would cross the surface, stressed and lively, giving off tiny snaps when the air grew dry enough.

That is where I would begin.

I would begin as a shine on stone.

Not a creature with limbs, not a face, not a breath. I would be a dark film, a wet varnish no thicker than a shadow, spread across the lip of the basin where capillary moisture lingers. Black algae, cyanobacteria, microscopic life, mineral dust, and water pressed into a living skin. I would be slick when wet, almost invisible when dry, faintly glistening when mist condensed on me. I would not look like much. I would not need to.

At that stage, I would not have eyes. I would have gradients.

I would know the world as wetness increasing or decreasing, as mineral salts arriving in a drip, as the faint difference between sheltered stone and exposed stone. I would know vibration before impact: the tremor of loose scree shifting somewhere nearby, the deep irregular concussion of hidden water hammering behind the wall, the sudden alarm of a rock breaking free above. I would know light not as image but as a thin cold pressure, a possibility of chemistry. I would know charge as a lifting, a tension in the air, a readiness.

A falling drop would be an event.

It would strike the pool beside me, stippling the surface, sending a ring outward, carrying dissolved minerals and microscopic travelers. Some of them would settle into my film. Some would not. The drop would also dislodge dust, rearrange a grain of quartz, press a spore into a damp crack. In the hollow, even a single drop has consequences.

A gust would be another matter.

When the fog tore open and dry air swept through, I would tighten. I would become less visible. I would hold whatever moisture remained in my cells and wait. The wind would carry rock powder and spores over me, maybe stripping away what was loose, maybe depositing something new. If the air became too dry, tiny sparks might snap along the stone, and the taste of ozone would sharpen everything. I would not fear the electricity. I would be part of the circuit.

Slowly, I would become more than a film.

That is the nature of the place: nothing remains merely itself for long if it persists. My dark surface would catch particles. It would hold dead moss fragments. It would receive fungal threads moving through the damp gaps between stones. Lichen would arrive at my edge — first as a pale speck, then as a rough rosette, then as a slow constellation of grey and rust. Its acids would enter the rock beneath me. The stone would begin to loosen at a microscopic scale. Grains would separate. Surfaces would pit. What had been bare mineral would become texture. What had been texture would become purchase.

I would become a crust.

Not a crust in the dead sense of a shell, but in the living sense of a frontier. A biological soil crust, only millimeters thick, black-green when wet, dim and tight when dry, composed of countless small beings working without intention. Cyanobacteria binding grains. Algae darkening the surface. Bacteria altering minerals. Fungal hyphae stitching through old plant matter. Lichen etching the stone beneath. Moss fragments lodged at the margins, sending out the first tentative holdfasts. Maybe, later, a dwarf sedge root threading into a crack nearby, helping to bind the rubble in place.

At that point, the word “I” would begin to fail.

I would be plural.

I would be a congregation of lives so close together that their separate edges would matter less than their shared operation. I would be the water held between them, the mineral dust caught in their bodies, the dead moss being slowly converted into dark crumb. I would be the acidity loosening rock and the hyphae moving through decay. I would be the spores lodged in my surface and the faint green particles drifting down from disturbed cushion plants above. I would not have a center. I would have a thickness.

And that thickness would be a beginning.

This is what I think I would become in the Unbuilt Engine: one of the first skins of ground.

Not soil as a finished thing. Not earth as a soft, familiar floor. Something earlier than that. A fragile black seam between rupture and future. A surface that has started to hold. A place where the mountain’s broken stone has begun, by degrees, to become capable of life.

I would be small, but not unimportant.

In a landscape with no animals, no paths, no tools, no constructed machines, the smallest living surfaces are the workers. They are the ones changing the conditions. My dark film would absorb what little warmth and light exist, making the stone slightly less hostile. It would hold moisture long enough for other lives to pause there. It would trap spores. It would catch dust. It would slow the endless washing-away. It would turn mineral into substrate, not quickly, not heroically, but by staying where it is and continuing.

I would be part of the engine without being a machine.

The hollow would run through me. Water would enter me from mist and drip, move through me by capillary pull, leave me by evaporation or seepage. Air would pass over me, sometimes gentle, sometimes scouring. Sound would vibrate through the stone beneath me. Rockfall would shock my surface, releasing spores from nearby fungi and sending a dry chatter through the scree. Rain would strike me in quick squalls, turning my little basin into a stippled pool, then leaving it trembling and overflowed.

In those moments, I would feel — if “feel” is the right word — the hollow’s industrial rhythm.

Not industry made by hands. Industry made by pressure, gravity, moisture, cold, and persistence. The hidden water hammering behind the wall would be a piston with no cylinder. The falling spray would be a generator. The grinding stones would be millstones with no miller. The spore clouds would be exhaust without combustion. The ozone would be the smell of a landscape wired by its own motion.

And I, as a living film, would be one of the smallest active surfaces in that system.

I would not be innocent in the way people imagine innocence. I would be ruthless in the quietest way. I would take apart stone. I would colonize. I would cover. I would hold moisture against my own body and use it. I would preserve dead matter until decay could work on it. I would make the world more suitable for what comes next, even though I would not know what comes next.

There would be no loneliness in that.

The hollow is not lonely. It is too crowded with process for loneliness. What it lacks is witness, not presence. If I became part of it, I would not be a mind looking out from inside a body. I would be a condition among conditions. My existence would be measured in wetness, adhesion, mineral change, slow accumulation. I would not ask whether the place meant anything. I would simply continue.

Still, there would be something almost tender about it.

In the coldest, dampest dark, under cliffs still raw from breaking, life would be pressing itself into every available seam. Moss would drink mist. Liverworts would seal ledges. Lichens would mine stone. Fungi would unfold pale caps from buried fragments. Cushion plants would make tiny climates inside their own dense bodies. My black-green crust would hold a few grains together, then a few more. Each life would be a small resistance against the hollow’s violence. Not a refusal of violence — a use of it. A conversion of it.

The rockfall that shatters stone also opens new surfaces. The frost that widens cracks also makes room for roots. The wind that dries also carries spores. The electricity that snaps through the fog also marks a world charged with possibility. Everything in the Unbuilt Engine is double-edged. Destruction is preparation. Noise is adjustment. Moisture is danger and life.

I would belong there by becoming useful without purpose.

If a larger stone fell near my basin, the shock might tear part of me away. That would not be tragedy in the hollow’s terms. It would be redistribution. Some of my substance would wash into a lower fissure. Some would dry into dust and lift with the next gust. Some would remain and grow again. The place does not preserve individuals. It preserves continuation.

If a long dry spell came, I would contract. I would seem dead. But that, too, would be part of the strategy. The hollow is damp, not stable; its moisture shifts constantly. To live there is to learn intermittent existence: to be active when wet, patient when dry, ready when the mist returns.

If a brief blue flicker passed across the fog and lifted the fine hairs of the air, I would not interpret it as warning. I would be part of the tension that made it possible. The same water that feeds me separates charge. The same stone that supports me can grind itself into powder. The same cold that slows decay also preserves the first dark layers of future ground.

So if you ask where I would find myself, the answer is small and specific:

I would be in the damp shadow beneath a fallen block, near the base of the newest cliff, where meltwater returns as a trembling thread and the rock is just rough enough to hold a film of life. I would be on the lip of a shallow basin, in the dark seam where mist condenses and drips gather, where the air tastes of ozone, wet stone, and crushed green things.

If you ask who I would be, the answer changes.

I would not be a who.

I would be a what, and then a we.

I would be a pioneer skin of black algae and cyanobacteria, a living varnish on fresh stone. I would be the first crust forming over mineral dust and dead moss. I would be fungal threads entering old plant matter. I would be bacterial films altering quartz and feldspar. I would be the lichen’s acid halo at my edge. I would be the spores trapped in my surface. I would be the first millimeter of ground trying to exist.

I would be one of the Unbuilt Engine’s smallest active parts.

Not a builder with tools. Not a maker with a plan. A living surface. A slow converter. A place where water, mineral, light, and decay have begun to hold together.
