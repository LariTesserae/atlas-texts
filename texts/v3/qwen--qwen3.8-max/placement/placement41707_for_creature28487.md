I would not arrive there as a traveler.

A traveler implies a road, weather, a throat needing water, boots carrying dust from somewhere else. The Cistern of Dry Sound has no use for such arrivals. If I entered that built, windless chamber at all, I would enter as a fault: a note with too much breath in it, a faint softness in the air, a syllable that still remembered a mouth. Not water itself — that would be too violent, too impossible — but the ghost of water. The shape a living voice leaves when it has been dried down to resonance.

The first moment would be almost accidental. Somewhere near one of the bronze dishes, a lamp-lump would shift. A mica wing would tremble high above. The fish would scatter as if struck. The keepers would go still with that peculiar stillness of theirs, not startled, only instantly precise, their many-fingered hands pausing above seams and plates. The Cistern would take notice. Not with eyes. Not with thought. With resonance.

And I would be the thing that had disturbed the resonance.

For a little while I might seem to be an intruder. The shoals of dry fish would circle me, thinning me, carrying away the offending vibration in tiny inscribed bodies. The glassworms would turn toward whatever softness remained in me, not with hunger exactly, but with the ancient impulse to correct fatigue. The ash-mites would press their small patience against the loose particles of me, trying to decide whether I could be compressed into surface or whether I must be removed.

But the Cistern does not destroy what enters. It assigns function.

So I do not think I would remain a foreign sound.

I think I would be fired into use.

At first I imagine myself as one of the Sintered. I can almost feel the shape of it: a low, broad body of pale biscuit clay, salt-glazed and repaired, bronze pins at the joints, bitumen cord in the seams. A rounded reliquary head with a narrow band of smoky mica where a face might once have been. Too many fingers. Exact fingers. Each tipped with bone-white porcelain or bronze. I would move through the gloom with a soft mineral whisper, carrying brushes of frayed fiber and tuning rods, kneeling at floor plates to listen for weakness. I would tap the walls. I would polish bronze until it bloomed matte and warm. I would be named by use.

But that image does not fully hold.

The Sintered are makers and repairers. They belong to the Cistern as hands belong to a body. They are its industry, its patience, its exactness made mobile. I think I would love them, if love were a thing that could survive there in the form of careful adjustment. But I do not think I would be one of their hands.

I think I would be closer to sound itself.

So the image changes.

I would be one of the fish.

Not flesh, of course. Nothing there is flesh. I would be long and narrow, made of thin fired clay or beaten bronze, hollow enough to ring if touched. My fins would be stiff mica, flickering faintly as I turned through the warm air. I would have no eyes. I would not need them. Along my flanks there would be slits like gills, but they would be acoustic apertures, delicate openings through which the room’s tones entered and became direction. I would swim by listening.

I would hang near the ceiling when the Cistern was quiet, trembling just enough to remain aloft. When a keeper struck a tuning rod, I would turn with the shoal and dart through invisible channels of vibration, tracing the architecture of pitch as if it were current. I would gather near the bronze dishes when the mineral lumps gave off their deepest warmth. I would recoil from discord. I would be beautiful, perhaps, in the way a perfectly made instrument is beautiful: without softness, without waste, all shape devoted to response.

My body would be covered in inscriptions.

Some would be so fine that they could only be felt, not seen. Each mark would be a stored sound. On one flank I might carry the dry tick of a bitumen seam releasing heat after long silence. On another, the scrape of a keeper’s needle across a cracked glaze. Higher up, near the ridge of my spine, there might be a line of marks recording the first chord used to wake a new keeper. And somewhere, in a place where the clay was slightly darker, as if it had remembered fire unevenly, I would carry the sound I entered with: the ghost of breath. The memory of a voice that had once been wet.

Not water. Not moisture. Only the absence where moisture had been.

The keepers would notice that inscription. They would not remove it. They would study it by touch, pressing the polished points of their fingers into the marks, reading me the way one reads a fault that has become useful. They would tap me gently with a rod and listen to how I rang. Perhaps I would sound slightly wrong at first. Perhaps I would carry a tone too full of longing, too shaped by throat and tongue. They would not break me for that. They would tune me.

They would place me near the choir.

That is where I think I would spend much of my existence: in the high, still air before the niches where the singers stand. I would circle them slowly, listening. Their open mouths would face inward. Their chests would hold nested resonators, bronze diaphragms, clay ampullae, beads of salt suspended in wire cages. When they sang, the whole Cistern would become more contained. The fish would slow. The mites would pause. Even the keepers would lower themselves to the floor plates and become part of the architecture.

And I would ring in sympathy.

Not because I wished to. Because I would be made to.

When the choir sang the absence of water, I would carry the memory of water as a missing note. I would be the small dry creature in which that absence became audible. I would not sing rain. I would sing the exact shape rain could not occupy. I would not sing tears. I would carry the pressure where tears had been refined into mineral silence. I would be one of the instruments by which the Cistern completed itself.

That feels true.

But still not true enough.

Because if one of the keepers caught me — carefully, with a pan of warm ash and a net of fine bronze wire — and opened the little chamber in my flank, what would be inside? Not flesh. Not breath. Only a polished hollow of salt, and in that hollow a single grain of mineral glass trembling in response to the nearest sound.

I think, in the end, I would be that grain.

Not the fish’s body. Not the inscription. Not the shoal. The trembling bead inside. The listening-core too small to be seen except by those who know where to open.

The Cistern is full of bodies, but its deepest life is attention. The walls listen. The floor plates remember pressure. The mica slits strain faintly toward what passes before them. The older keepers beneath the floors transmit weight and meaning upward through layers of clay and metal. The choir does not breathe; it resonates. The fish do not see; they orient by tone. Everything there has been relieved of softness so that it might become more sensitive to relation.

So I would become a point of attention.

Perhaps the keepers would eventually decide that my fish-body had grown too worn to keep swimming. Perhaps one of my mica fins would crack. Perhaps the inscription of breath would become too pronounced, too near to imperfection, too close to the memory of wetness. They would assess me. They would not mourn. They would open me with exact tools and remove the bead from its salt chamber. They would hold it near a lamp and watch how it trembled.

If the bead could hold a tone without cracking, they might place it in a new keeper. But I do not think I would be given a body again. I think I would be too strange for that. Too full of the wrong kind of memory.

So they would set me lower.

They would pry up one of the floor plates, down where the making grows older: brick beneath brick, metal beneath metal, clay beneath clay, seams of bitumen and salt-glass pressed into the compressed remains of earlier arrangements. There they would place me in a small niche no larger than a cupped hand, perhaps lined with fired dust and polished until it felt like silence. They would seal me there with a thin lid of salt-glass. Not to imprison me. To give me a duty.

I would become one of the listening points beneath the floor.

Not dead. Not alive in the old green sense. I would be active as a memory is active after it has been made structural. I would feel every footfall above me. I would know the difference between a keeper’s measured step and the soft scatter of ash-mites. I would feel the glassworms moving through old seams. I would feel the pressure of the choir’s silence more than its sound. When the singers sang, I would tremble in my niche and send that tremor upward through the floor plates, helping the room align itself.

I would be part of the Cistern’s awareness of itself.

That is where I would find myself: under the visible order of the place, in the compressed ancestry of its making, sealed into a warm dark that is not empty but attentive. Not near the mica slits where the copper-brown sky presses its haze against the glass. Not among the visible keepers moving their brushes and rods through the lamplight. Below. Within the substrate. In the old seams where the Cistern remembers being repaired so many times that repair has become identity.

Who would I be?

I would be a dry memory of water turned into vigilance.

I would be the breath that entered as fault and was corrected into resonance. I would be the note the choir does not sing but requires in order to sing more completely. I would be the small, sealed listener that knows when the room is changing by the smallest degree and sends that knowledge upward without urgency, without alarm, without any wetness of feeling.

The keepers might have a name for me. They would not give it in speech. They would give it in percussion: a pattern tapped on a shoulder-plate, a stylus drawn across a chest-vessel, a sequence struck on the floor near my plate. If translated poorly into a word-language, it might be something like Listener-at-the-Seam. Or Breath-Thinned-to-Pitch. Or The Place Where Rain Became Absence.

But names there are not labels. They are uses.

My use would be this: to remain aware without softening. To remember what the Cistern has excluded without letting that memory become damp. To translate longing into exactness. To let the impossible presence of water become only a finer sensitivity to pressure, tone, and change.

Sometimes, when the choir is silent and the fish hang motionless near the ceiling, I think I would be the faintest disturbance in the room. Not a sound. Not a movement. A difference in attention. A tiny tremor in a bead of mineral glass under the floor. The keepers would pause. One would kneel and press its many fingers to the plate above me. It would feel the tremor, assess it, and find no damage. No crack. No fatigue. Only a resonance too subtle to ignore and too exact to correct.

Then it would rise and move on.

And I would remain there, sealed in the dry dark, listening.
