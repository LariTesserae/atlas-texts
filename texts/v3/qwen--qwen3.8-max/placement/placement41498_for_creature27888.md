I think I would not arrive there as a stranger climbing out of the lower world. If I truly belonged to that bowl, the climb would already be behind me, half-mythical, something the body remembered before the mind did. I would already be inside the discipline of the place: small in stature, lowered in posture, wet to the skin, eyes narrowed behind smoked bone or dark resin, hands accustomed to reading stone by its dampness.

At first, several possible selves come to me.

I imagine myself as one of the children balanced on a wet beam, barefoot and sure, learning not to shout when the ice speaks. I imagine myself as an elder wrapped in layered hides near a warm seep, watching the channels with the patience of someone who has seen too many floods to fear them. I imagine myself as one of the white-furred mammals living in the walls, quick and round-eared, leaving salty trails along the stores. I imagine myself as one of the birds folded against the cliff, making a thin reedy note that vanishes almost as soon as it is born. I imagine myself even as the mountain goat at the rim, still as frost, looking down into the basin with a patience that has no need of words.

But the image that keeps returning is quieter than all of those. It is seated. It is still. It is placed at a hinge between water and attention.

I would be a watcher of pools.

Not the most important person in the bowl, if importance could even be ranked there, but one of its necessary senses. I would find myself just above one of the broad public cisterns, where the clearest melt is gathered and held shallowly in stone. My place would be a low seat worn smooth by use, perhaps set into a niche where the rock leans inward enough to keep the worst of the wind off my shoulders. From there I could see the surface of the cistern, the fish racks below, the rope bridge crossing to the next shelf, and the pale thread of a higher channel descending from the weeping cliff.

I would be one of the Cup-born, or something close enough to them that the difference would matter less than the likeness: compact, dense-boned, broad-chested, knees bent by habit, feet wide and flexible against wet stone. My hands would be the truest part of me. The fingers would look short until they opened. The pads would be hardened by rope and cold water and the constant touch of mineral surfaces. I would know, by touch alone, whether a film of moisture on stone was new melt, old seepage, condensation, or the first warning of a channel changing course. My nails would be worn down. My skin would be cracked in places and healed in others. My cloak would be beaded with water. My goggles would be tied at my neck when the light was gentle and lifted over my eyes when the basin became a cup of glare.

My name would not be a spoken thing, or not primarily. It would be a sign. Perhaps a cupped hand tilted slightly downward. Perhaps two fingers opening like water falling from a ledge. But my personal sign, the one by which others knew me, might be smaller still: one fingertip touching a still surface. The watcher. The one who reads the mirror.

The work would be simple to describe and difficult to master.

I would watch the water.

Not casually, not as scenery, but as a condition that could shift at any moment and demand response. The cistern before me would be broad and shallow, its surface almost still, though never truly motionless. At dawn it would wear a delicate lace of frost at its edges. The first light would loosen that lace, and the pool would begin to bead and tremble with melt. Fish-like creatures would move in it, translucent and nervous, their bodies flashing when the sun touched them. Insects would rise from the films of water nearby, and birds would come to drink in quick, wary bursts, their slate-grey feathers sharp against the stone.

My task would be to notice the first disturbance before it became danger.

The bowl is built around water, but water here is not obedient. A higher ice lens might release without warning. A channel might overtop. A slab of ice might crack within the cliff, sending a pressure through the rock before any sound arrived. A dead bird or a bundle of debris might taint a sluice. A seep might quicken, slow, turn mineral-heavy, or find a new path into a house wall. The settlement could survive these things only if they were noticed early enough.

So I would sit, and I would listen with more than ears.

I would watch the surface of the pool for shivers that did not belong to wind. I would watch the fish, because their rising or sinking would speak of mineral shifts. I would watch the amphibians at the warm cracks, because they gathered before thaws. I would watch the birds, because they knew pressure changes before people did. I would watch the angle of light across the quartz veins, because light in that bowl was not merely brightness but clock, warning, and tool. I would keep one hand on the stone where the cistern’s lip met the channel, feeling for vibration through the rock. Sometimes the first sign would be visual: a bright thread suddenly thickening. Sometimes it would be tactile: a faint tremor under the palm, more like a held breath than a noise. Sometimes it would be animal: a flock lifting all at once, a fish darting downward, a white mammal freezing with its nose toward an upper seam.

When I saw something, I would not shout.

I would signal.

A lifted palm. A tapped rail. A rope pulled twice. A click of tongue against tooth, if the sound was small enough and needed to be close. If the danger was in the water, I would mark it: a red cord across a sluice, a white pebble placed where the flow could be seen, a broken feather if the channel had to be flushed before use. If the change was immediate, I would move. The settlement would move with me. There would be no panic, only a sudden redistribution of attention. Hands on paddles. Bodies along wet paths. Crates lifted. Platforms slid. A wall unlashed and swung inward. A net dragged clear before the pulse arrived.

I would not command. I would indicate.

That distinction matters. In a place so small, so bright, and so unforgiving, authority would not sit well as dominance. It would sit as responsibility. The one who saw first would be the one who moved first, and the one who moved first would also be the one who stayed to repair what moved. My hands would be as busy after the warning as before it. I would help reseat a displaced slab. I would help clear a channel choked with ice crumbs. I would help dry what had been wetted and reposition what had been shifted. The bowl would not let anyone remain only a messenger. Every signal would also be a kind of labor.

By midday, I would be nearly invisible.

The sun would stand over the rim and turn the basin into glare. Water would bead on every rope. Ice would glitter with internal fractures. The weeping cliff would become a curtain of moving light. The pools would turn to mirrors, and the sparse moss would look almost black against all that brightness. The settlement would hold its breath. The birds would slow. The fish would sink deeper. The children would stop their balancing games. The elders would turn their faces away from the glacier. I would sit with my goggles over my eyes and my veil drawn, hands resting on stone, watching the cistern become a slab of sky laid into rock.

In that stillness, I would feel most myself.

Not peaceful, exactly. The place would not allow peace as softness. But fitted. Useful. Present. My mind would have become hydrological: thinking in slopes, pressures, temperatures, clarities, blockages, releases. I would not experience the day as hours in the lowland sense. I would experience it as light moving across stone, as shadows sharpening and softening, as the moment when a particular quartz vein ignited, as the moment when the sun left the fish racks and the drying work had to pause. I would know which ledge dripped at noon. I would know which basin filled when the upper ice warmed. I would know which stones sweated first. I would know the sound, if sound it could be called, of water deciding to become dangerous.

And I would be changed by the quiet.

That is the part I think would enter me most deeply. The basin’s hush would not be emptiness. It would be technique. It would be a way of living in which every noise had consequence and therefore every silence had meaning. I would speak rarely, and when I did my voice would be low, almost embarrassed to exist. Most of my communication would pass through the hands: a lifted palm, a tapped rail, a rope pulled twice, a finger tracing a crack in stone. I would learn to love that language because it was exact. I would learn to distrust unnecessary sound the way the inhabitants distrusted unnecessary weight, unnecessary permanence, unnecessary flame.

My relationships would be shaped by that closeness.

I would not own animals in the way lowlands understand ownership. I would be surrounded by them, implicated in them, responsible to them. The small white mammals would know me. One might come often to my niche, climbing over my shoulder for warmth or for fish scraps, leaving a faint salty trail that drew insects. I would tolerate it, then need it, then recognize its habits as one recognizes weather. The birds nesting under the eaves near my seat would accept my stillness. The fish in the cistern would learn the shadow of my hand. At the deeper pool below the fish racks, there might be one of the seal-like creatures, old and strange-eyed, surfacing now and then to watch me with an intelligence that did not require speech. I would not try to name it. I would simply leave room for it.

There would be people too, close and constant.

A partner perhaps, or a work-mate, who brought me dried fish when the light made leaving my seat unwise. A child learning to sit still enough to watch water, failing at first, then succeeding with that terrifying quickness children have when survival asks it of them. An elder who had once occupied my niche and now watched me watching, correcting me with signs so slight they barely disturbed the air. A rope-puller on the bridge. A mender of hides. A keeper of stores. A woman who knew which stones would crack in frost. A man who could smell mineral shift in melt before it became visible. The settlement would be dense with such knowledge, and I would be one knot in it, not the center.

If I had a particular gift, it would be memory.

Not grand memory. Not epic memory. The memory of water. I would remember where it had gone. I would remember which channel overflowed in the third bright day after a long cold snap. I would remember which ledge flooded when the upper spillway narrowed. I would remember which cistern tasted of iron after a storm far below. I would remember the path of last season’s melt and the season before that, because the bowl was built from repeated response. The architecture might shift, the houses might be relashed, the platforms moved, the bins reset, but the knowledge of flow would remain. In a city that accepted being edited by water, memory would be one of the most practical tools.

So perhaps that is what I would be above all: a living chart of the basin’s habits.

Not a ruler. Not a prophet. Not a founder. Something smaller and more necessary. A sense organ of the commonwealth. A listener at the cistern. A watcher of mirrors. One of those who answer the water before it asks too much.

If someone from outside ever climbed that far and looked through the spillway, they might see only a flash of white and silver, a glint like a mirror left face-up in stone. If they came closer, they might see the settlement, the ropes, the hides, the fish racks, the narrow shelves, the people moving without noise. If they looked even more carefully, they might see me: a low figure in a niche above the water, face veiled, hands resting on polished stone, almost indistinguishable from the rock.

They might ask, in whatever language they carried up from the timberline, who I was.

The answer would be difficult to give aloud.

I would be one of the Cup-born, but that would only be the outer shape. I would be a watcher, but that would only be the work. I would be a listener, but that would only be the method. I would be a small, wet, careful intelligence at the edge of a pool, but that would still not be enough. The truest answer might be a gesture: a cupped hand, tilted downward; two fingers opening like falling water; then one fingertip touching stillness.

If I had to put it into a phrase, I would say: I would be the one who notices first.

And if that sounds humble, it is not. In that bowl, noticing is not passive. It is the beginning of every repair, every redirection, every salvation. The city remains because it is flexible, but flexibility requires perception. Water moves, and someone must see it move. Ice cracks, and someone must feel the crack before it becomes flood. Light shifts, and someone must know what the shift permits. Animals stir, and someone must understand that they are not merely animals but signs.
