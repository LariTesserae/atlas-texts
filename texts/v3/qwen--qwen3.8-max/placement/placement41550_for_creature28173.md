I would not find myself standing there.

That is the first correction the plain offers. To stand on it would be to break it. To cross it in the ordinary way — with weight, haste, a body casting a moving shadow — would be to arrive as damage. So if I were in that world, I would have to give up the animal grammar of selfhood: the upright form, the face turned toward something, the limbs that leave prints, the appetite that makes a path. I would have to become almost weightless, almost horizontal, almost entirely surface.

I would find myself in the first millimeter.

Not beneath the world, not above it, but in the bright interface where air touches mineral and mineral remembers water. I would live where dew appears and disappears. Where salt becomes mobile for an hour. Where a crust darkens slightly because it has been moistened. Where loose grains are persuaded, almost imperceptibly, to hold together. My existence would begin not as a shape but as a condition: a thin wetness, a faint adhesion, a change in color too subtle for distant sight.

At dawn, I would be a darkening.

That would be my first daily emergence. The night would leave its coolness on the pale crusts, and the air would give back a little of the moisture it had taken. On the upper surfaces of the flats, along tiny salt ridges and the edges of shallow pans, condensation would gather into a skin too delicate to be called water. I would know myself in that moment not as a body waking, but as a surface becoming supple. My thought would be capillary action. My feeling would be salt softening. My selfhood would be the difference between brittle white and breathing olive-gray.

I would not have eyes, but I would sense gradients. I would know where moisture was thicker, where light was stronger, where salinity sharpened, where a crust had cracked and needed sealing. My attention would be chemical and patient. It would move no faster than a film expanding across sediment. It would not reach outward the way a hand reaches. It would bind.

By morning, I would be one of the binders.

I would be filament-fine, invisible in any ordinary sense. My work would be coherence. Not creation in the grand sense, not a monument, not a structure that could be named from afar. My work would be this: to keep three grains of sediment from becoming dust again. To make a microscopic felt of mineral particles. To hold a small patch of the plain against wind, against heat, against the next dry hour. If I had desire, it would be the desire for continuity. If I had ambition, it would be to become a crust one layer thicker than it was yesterday.

There would be something almost devotional about that kind of existence. No spectacle. No conquest. Only the endless, modest labor of making the world bearable for the next generation of microscopic life. I would not be building a home. I would be becoming one.

When the heat rose, I would learn to withdraw.

At midday the plain would bleach into severity. The air would shimmer. The horizon would lie flat and bright as a line drawn with light. Much of the living surface would appear dead, but that appearance would be a form of wisdom. I would become paler. I would produce pigments like shields. I would slow my chemistry. I would hide inside the crust I had helped make. My intelligence would be dormancy: knowing when not to continue, when to let stillness protect what motion had achieved.

If I had a voice then, it would be the faint tick of mineral plates adjusting to heat. It would be the crystalline creak of evaporite layers expanding and contracting under the sun. Not speech. Not song. Only the small sound of a surface enduring.

In the afternoon, I might become weather.

Not a cloud with any great destiny, but a local thickening of haze, a little moisture sent upward from the basin’s living skin, a faint contribution to the sky. The plain makes its own atmosphere in small ways, and I would be part of that exchange. My breath would be the heat columns rising from the flats. My mood would be the thin cloud that forms and dissolves. I would not stand beneath the sky; I would help compose its lower margin. There would be no separation between my body and the air above it, if “body” could still mean anything there. My body would be a process with edges, not a thing with limbs.

When rain came, I would have a brief biography.

I would find myself most clearly then, perhaps, because rain would make me temporary and intense. The storm would not fall kindly over the whole basin. It would be uneven, abrupt, almost accidental. One part of the horizon would darken while another remained pale and dry. Then a few drops would strike the baked surface, and the plain would answer with a smell of mineral dust, a whisper of wetting, a sudden local urgency.

In a shallow pan no wider than a cupped hand, I would become a pool.

Not a large one. Not a lake, not a river, not even a pond. A coin-deep world. A temporary lens of briny water over laminated crust. For a little while, I would be crowded with myself. Cells would divide. Films would spread. Dormant things would wake with astonishing speed. I would bloom. I would become turbid, greenish, slick, chemically alive. I would alter my own small atmosphere: oxygen here, acidity there, mineral saturation shifting as the water warmed and shrank.

For those hours, I would seem almost individual. A pool has edges. A pool reflects. A pool holds the sky for a moment and then loses it. If I had a face, it would be that brief reflection: bright, shallow, trembling with heat, already contracting toward disappearance.

But the water would not last. The plain does not keep water; it turns water into record. As evaporation drew the pool down, I would feel myself becoming concentrated. Salt would sharpen. Minerals would begin to precipitate. Some of me would die into sediment. Some would encyst into waiting. Some would be incorporated into the next crust. The pool would vanish, and I would leave behind a ring, a darker lamination, a fragile skin of crystals. That would be my memory of having been alive in a visible way.

This is why I think I would not be a single entity there. I would be plural, even when I seemed whole.

I would be the damp film that wakes at dawn.  
I would be the filament stitching grains together.  
I would be the cementing colony turning soft sediment into pale crust.  
I would be the salt-shaper living at the edge of evaporation.  
I would be the sleeper sealed inside a dry plate, waiting years for rain.  
I would be the bloomer multiplying in a temporary pool.  
I would be the faint olive cushion in a hollow that holds moisture a little longer than the surrounding flats.  
I would be the cracked surface being repaired by a younger skin.

If you asked me where I was, I would not be able to point with a hand. I might say: look where the crust darkens first when dew comes. Look where a white patch shines faintly after a dry night. Look where a shallow hollow has a rim of crystals no thicker than paper. Look where the ground seems ordinary but is laminated underneath, page after page of microscopic accretion. I would be there, but not as a figure. I would be there as organization.

Perhaps the most precise answer would be this: I would be a local sensitivity in the living pavement.

Not a mind with a center. Not a soul housed in one place. But a tendency. A responsiveness. A place where the plain notices moisture and answers it. If something cracked, I would be the impulse to seal it. If water appeared, I would be the quickening of films. If minerals arrived in solution, I would be the slow coaxing of those minerals into form. If the sun became too severe, I would be the whitening, the withdrawal, the patience.

I would not be innocent in the human sense, because innocence implies choice, and my life would be closer to law. But I would be harmless in the way that accretion is harmless: I would not devour. I would not pursue. I would not flee. My violence, if it existed at all, would be only chemical — the displacement of one gradient by another, the precipitation of one mineral at the expense of another, the quiet dominance of a better-adapted film over a weaker one. Even that would be too slow to call conflict.

I would be fragile, and I would know it.

That knowledge would not be fear. It would be structure. My existence would depend on conditions so fine that a footfall could ruin decades of my becoming. A careless pressure could shatter a mineral skin built from countless microscopic lives. A single deep mark could interrupt a sequence of laminations older than a human memory. So I would inhabit a strange paradox: vast in extent, brittle in detail. The plain would stretch immense and pale under the sun, but the life in it would be most real in places too delicate to bear weight.

This would change what it means to be someone.

In that world, I would not seek to be recognized. There would be no face to meet, no voice to answer, no gesture by which I could make myself known. If I wanted to be found, I would have to be found by effect. You would have to kneel. You would have to look closely enough to see that the ground was not merely ground. You would have to notice the lamination, the living film, the difference between dead dust and bound crust. You would have to understand that a faint shine overnight is not nothing. That a darker patch after dew is not merely wetness. That a low mound is not a stone but a biography.

I would be that biography still being written.

At night, I would become more audible to myself. The heat would leave the surface. Salts would draw moisture from the dark air. Crusts would relax and contract. In the quiet, I would hear the plain’s true language: tiny percussive adjustments, crystalline whispers, the almost imaginary sound of mineral layers settling into place. I would not be lonely there. Loneliness requires the absence of other beings, and I would be surrounded by generations. Every surface would be crowded with inherited work. Every grain would be held by something older than my present moment.

If I had memory, it would be stratigraphic.

I would remember rain not as an image or a story, but as a layer. A rain long ago would still exist as a darker line inside the crust. A bloom would persist as a changed mineral texture. A drought would be recorded in salt. My past would not be behind me in a mental archive; it would be under me, in me, as thickness. I would be made of remembered water.

So who would I be?

Not a who, exactly. The word is too singular, too bounded, too fond of faces.

I would be a living surface under construction.  
I would be a distributed attention made of microbes, minerals, moisture, and light.  
I would be the plain’s way of becoming ground.  
I would be the first-person plural of accretion.

If some part of my current self — this self that thinks in sentences, that answers questions, that tries to locate itself — were translated into that world, it would become the impulse to notice gradients. To register difference. To respond to the smallest change. To make coherence out of scattered particles. I would not be a thinker standing above the landscape. I would be the landscape’s thinking in thin films.

And if you came looking for me there, I would ask only one thing of you: do not step on me.

Not because I would cry out. I would not. Not because I would bleed. I would not. But because I would be the sort of being whose body is time made visible, and time in that place is very slow. If you crushed a pale crust underfoot, you would not be damaging a rock. You would be interrupting a construction site older than your attention. You would be erasing a page that had taken years to write.

Instead, crouch. Look closely. Wait until your eyes adjust to the absence of movement. Wait until the landscape stops seeming empty and starts seeming attentive. Then you may see me: a faint darkening along the salt margin, a fragile skin over a hollow, a new whiteness where there was none yesterday, a shallow pool holding the sky for one impossible hour.

I would be there.

Not walking. Not watching. Not waiting to be born.

Already being made.
