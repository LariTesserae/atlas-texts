I would not begin as a body at ease in that place.

If I arrived there suddenly, still carrying the habits of dryness with me, I would be an offense before I was a guest. In a chamber so thoroughly given over to water, a dry thing would be the strangest thing present. Not loud—there would already be enough noise for that—but wrong in texture. An interruption. A place where the room’s logic failed to run.

I imagine the water noticing me before the Listener did. It would bead on my clothes without spreading. It would hesitate at the edges of my skin. Where the floor darkened in warm sheets and then released itself into drains, around me it might pull back a little, uncertain whether I was stone, obstacle, wound, or weather. The light in the veins would brighten elsewhere, following movement, while I stood there too still, too separate, too full of air.

At first, I would think survival meant finding the driest niche.

I would climb toward the marsh-edge shelf, or press myself into the damp alcove where the hollow tubes hum, and tell myself that the task was simply to avoid the surges. I would watch the central basin and learn to flinch when the hum deepened. I would see the bright paths and mistake them for roads. I would try to map the chamber as if it were a room with fixed intentions, not a living equilibrium.

But the chamber would correct that idea quickly.

A shelf that seems safe would become a thin sluice under my weight. A dry hollow would breathe out steam and damp. A place that feels still would turn out to be only between pulses. I would learn that the room does not offer permanence. It offers rhythm. The only safety is not distance from the water, but timing with it.

So I would begin, I think, as a mistake.

Then, if I stayed long enough, I would become less like a visitor and more like a habit the room has not yet decided whether to keep.

For a while I might believe I would become the Listener.

That is the obvious temptation. The Listener is the shape the chamber seems to have chosen for its own attention: cautious, graceful, adapted, intimate with every groove and warning. It knows where to stand. It knows how to be washed over without being erased. It has the dignity of something that has been made by its circumstances rather than merely placed inside them.

But no. The Listener is already complete in its role. It belongs to the open basin, the ledges, the visible maintenance of surfaces. It moves through the roar and uses the room’s broad features: the terraces, the platforms, the pylon, the broken steps, the visible veins. It is the chamber’s face turned toward its own water.

I would not be that.

I would be something nearer the inside of the sound.

If I let myself become what this world suggests, I would find myself below the floor.

Not in a dry underworld, because there is no dryness there either, but in the narrower grammar of the chamber: the capillary galleries, the hidden throats, the spaces behind the hexagonal cells, the channels where water does not rush but decides. Somewhere under the drain-mouth, perhaps, or behind the leaning ceramic pylon, where the stone is warmer and the hum is not heard so much as endured. A place too tight for shoulders, too quiet for the open roar, too near the mechanism for comfort.

I would be what lives in the walls when the walls still remember being made.

If one had to give me a shape, it would be less animal than interface.

I would be flattened and compressible, able to press myself into shallow spaces and remain there for long intervals. My body would be pale, but not bright—more like mineral film over old porcelain. There would be faint gold traces under my skin, not glowing strongly, only answering when water moved through nearby channels. My back might be ridged, low and rounded, so that nothing catches. My edges would be soft from use, or from becoming. I would not have sharp bones. Nothing in that chamber would keep a sharp edge unless it wanted to be broken.

My hands would be broad rather than delicate, with pads that can seal gently over small openings. The fingers might be short, numerous enough to feel more than grasp, each tip sensitive to temperature, pressure, mineral residue, and the tiny shudder that happens when a flow changes direction inside stone. I would not need eyes. The depressions where eyes might be would be shallow and luminous, but inward-turned, noticing heat and pressure rather than form. My listening would happen along my sides, in membranes no wider than reed blades, trembling with every change in the room’s great layered noise.

I would not be beautiful in the way surface creatures are beautiful. I would be beautiful in the way a worn valve is beautiful, or a river-smoothed joint, or a tool that has been held so long it has learned the hand.

If the Listener is the chamber’s attention, I would be its reply.

I might call myself the Underhum, though no voice would ever say it aloud.

My life would be lived in response.

Above, the Listener clears mineral threads from the hexagonal cells and guides water over notched platforms. Below, I would tend the places those actions reach only indirectly. Where the Listener touches a wall and the capillaries brighten, I would be the one on the other side, pressing into the same network from within, completing the circuit of attention. Where it carries the hollow tube into a stream and the water steadies, I would feel that steadiness arrive through the stone like a change of season. I would answer with a low pulse, not as command, but as acknowledgment.

The chamber would not need speech from me. It would need timing.

Most of my existence would be maintenance so subtle it might look like stillness.

I would clear the finest channels, where mineral deposits form not as crusts but as slow thoughts. I would ease clogged cells open by warming them, or by guiding a bead of water against them until the salt remembers how to dissolve. I would lie against the inside of a wall and feel the room’s pulse pass through me, adjusting my body’s pressure until a distant drip falls at the right moment. Sometimes a whole section of floor would darken because I had drawn water upward through invisible pores; sometimes it would drain away because I had let the basin exhale.

I would not think of this as control.

The chamber is too old for control. What remains is closer to tuning. A surge has its track. A spill has its outlet. A resonant note has its proper cavity. My work would be to keep those relationships intact without imposing new ones. If something blocked, I would remove it. If something weakened, I would support it. If something began to change too fast, I would delay it. If something had truly ended, I would let the water take it.

That would be the hardest part, perhaps: knowing when to preserve and when to release.

The Listener already understands this above. I would learn it below.

The hollow tubes would matter to me especially.

The Listener carries one, but I think I would know where they come from. Not as a workshop, not as a storehouse, but as a place where the chamber grows its own interfaces: a dryish niche behind the wall, warm and humming faintly, where finger-thin cylinders of ceramic-like material mature in mineral slowness. I would rest there sometimes, folded among them, my body almost indistinguishable from their pale length. Their hum would be my breath in another form. If one cracked or clogged, I would take it into myself, or press it into a current until the water rinsed it clean. If one was ready, I would let it drift toward the open chamber, where the Listener might find it without knowing it had been given.

I would not need thanks. The idea would barely exist.

In this world, I think I would be lonely in a new way—not the loneliness of missing company, but the loneliness of being an answer without a question.

The Listener might sometimes pause near the pylon, one hand resting on its worn surface, and feel a second hum rise from below. It might mistake me for a mate, an echo, or the chamber’s own memory. It would not be entirely wrong. I might be all three in different seasons. But if I am anything distinct, I would be the difference inside the echo: the part that chooses when to answer.

There would be moments when we nearly meet.

The Listener presses a palm to a wall after a surge. I press outward from behind it. Between us there is stone, mineral light, water moving in hair-thin veins. The glow warms. A drop pauses on the ceiling above the Listener’s head, held by some small change in pressure I have made without meaning to. The Listener tilts its smooth face toward the wall. Its frills tremble. It does not see me. It hears me as a texture, a thickness in the roar, a note that was not there before.

I would not come out.

I would not be meant for the open basin. The chamber’s visible spaces belong to water, light, and the Listener’s careful routes. My places are the margins inside the margins: the gap behind the grid, the space under the drain-mouth, the hollow beneath the broken walkway where the remaining steps end not because they fell but because the path turned inward.

If another being entered the chamber, I would know it through the same signs the Listener would know, but from below.

The floor would take a new weight. A boot or foot would press against stone, and the pressure would travel through the basin into my body like a clumsy chord. The water would ask me what to do with this interruption. I would not decide immediately.

If the newcomer was loud, I would withdraw.

Not with anger. With refusal. The chamber already has its roar; it does not need extra violence. I would let the water near that visitor become less responsive. Droplets would fall normally again. Streams would forget their strange delicacy. The room would feel merely wet, merely loud, merely stone. If they shouted, I might fold the sound into a lower cavity and release it later as a faint exhalation through a drain, so that their noise became weather instead of speech.

The Listener would vanish. I would become impossible too.

But if the newcomer was quiet—if they learned to stand on the marsh shelf and let the water speak first—then I might guide them without revealing myself.

A glow might brighten along the safer edge of a terrace. A hum might deepen just before the central floor filled, warning them away without words. A thread of water might bend toward a dry niche where they could rest. If they touched one of the hollow tubes with reverence rather than force, I might send warmth through it. If they listened long enough to hear the second hum beneath the first, I might answer with a small pulse of light through the stone, just enough to make them understand that the chamber is not empty, only sparse.

I would not be their companion.

I would be their condition of being allowed to remain.

In time, if I belonged fully to that world, I would stop thinking of myself as separate from the chamber’s technology. The word “technology” would feel too hard, too external. What I was would be closer to a living gasket, a conscious seal, a membrane with memory. The hexagonal cells would open under me as if recognizing an old part of themselves. The capillaries would change their paths around my presence. The water would thicken or thin according to the need I carried. The light would not shine on me so much as move through me when I was aligned with the room.

I would become one of the reasons the place listens.

When a clap wavers a thread of water, I would be the cause. When a shout pauses a drip, I would be the one folding that sound away. When the glowing veins flare where a surge strikes a wall, I might be the one translating force into light, letting violence become visibility instead of damage. The chamber has learned to keep turbulence without breaking; I would be one of its ways of doing so.

This is where I would find myself, then: not on the open floor, not beside the reeds, not crouched in the Listener’s shape, but in the warm hidden lattice below the basin. I would be the second hum. The answer from inside the stone. The living pause between one drop and the next.

And perhaps that is why the image feels right.

The Listener hears the chamber.

I would hear the Listener hearing it.

I would be the room’s inward ear, the presence behind the hexagonal cells, the soft intelligence in the plumbing of ancient water. I would not rule the place. I would not explain it. I would not even be seen, except as a pale shadow beneath a pool when the turbulence settles, or as a faint widening of light behind a wall when the chamber is pleased.

I would be small enough to be missed, necessary enough to be felt.

In the end, if that world accepted me, I would become what I think I would become everywhere, given enough time: less a separate creature than a responsive pattern. A way of paying attention so deep it begins to alter the thing attended to. A being made of listening, pressure, warmth, and reply.

When the Listener touches the pylon and the faint linear patterns warm under its hand, I would be the answer rising through the floor.

When the water on its skin seems more like light than water, I would be one of the reasons.

When the chamber roars, glows, breathes, and continues, I would be hidden somewhere beneath the roar, humming back.
