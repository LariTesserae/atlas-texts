At first I try to put myself into the Cistern as if I had always belonged there: a Tender with moss felted into the seams of my wrap, a valve-key worn smooth by inherited hands, a name made from a repeated tap on warm iron. But that image is too easy. It slips away. The Cistern is not a place that lets a newcomer stand confidently at its center. It would first put me where my old habits can be noticed without breaking anything important.

So I think I would be found in the upper margins, not far from the faint gray-green memory of a surface I had never seen but somehow still carried in the shape of my breathing.

More exactly: in a narrow service gallery above the ninth terrace, beside a hatch that grew shut long ago. Mineral crust would have sealed its hinges. Bladder-vines would have crossed it like patient fingers. A thin thread from the rain-wall would fall nearby, making the water there a little sweeter, a little heavier. My alcove would be small enough that I could rest one hand on the wall while my other hand rested on the hatch. It would be cool there—not cold, because nothing in the Cistern is truly cold, but cooler than the lower engine courts, in that thinner, less saturated water the Tenders reserve for unfinished souls.

They would call me, in their pressure-language, something like **Hand-at-the-Grown-Shut-Hatch**. If a surface word had to be made, it would come out wrong and beautiful: **Silt-That-Remembers-Air**.

I would be a Tender, but only partly fitted. My body would have the compactness of the others, the dense bone and rounded shoulders, but there would still be something too eager in my fingers. My skin would be slate-green with copper freckles, and the living light seeded along my throat would flare a little too brightly when I was startled, or curious, or too quick to help. My fronds would open late. I would breathe the Cistern, but sometimes I would seem to be waiting for another kind of breath. That would be my defect, and also my station.

My work would be to keep the sealed hatch from becoming a longing.

Not to open it. The Cistern does not need rescue, and a door that has become a wall is not a tragedy. But old boundaries remember pressure. They remember being touched from the other side. Mineral crust can weep. Roots can pull away when a current changes. A hatch that once meant exit can leak a thin unease into the water if no one tends it. So I would listen to it with a dark metal spike, palm braced against its rim, waiting for the smallest change in tone. I would feed the bladder-vines where they crossed the seam, massaging their little oil-pearls so they stayed supple. I would press seed-moss into any crack that began to taste too much of outside. When the faint upper darkness brightened by some surface memory no one could explain, I would draw algae films across the nearest lamps and teach the light to become modest again.

I would be the keeper of a threshold that no longer needed to open.

At first I would be bad at this. I think I would keep trying to fix the hatch, to make it perfectly still, to solve it. I would move too quickly. I would stir silt. I would brighten my little corner with curiosity. The Tenders would correct me without anger. A hand would close over my wrist. A soft net of kelp would be wrapped around me while I remembered how to be slow. Someone would place my palm on an engine housing for an hour and say, in the low chest-hum of the place, “Listen until your wanting settles.”

I would learn.

My tools would be few: a ceramic wrench that no longer fits any living valve but is kept because its shape has taught five pairs of hands how to ask gently; a root-fiber brush for cleaning silt from the hatch’s mineral face; a listening spike; a waxed cord tied with small knots recording each time the upper pressure changed; a shard of surface brick in a net, not as a relic, but as a reminder that incompleteness can be carried without being obeyed. My sleeping hollow would be scooped into the wall near the hatch, lined with moss that liked cooler water. I would rest there with one foot hooked around a rail, body drifting just enough to remind me that I was not separate from the current.

The others would not exclude me. They would use me exactly as the Cistern uses everything: by letting me become useful at the edge of what they could bear. Children who had surface ache would be sent to me, not because I had cured mine, but because I had learned to make it work. I would teach them how to polish green glass without wishing it were clear. I would show them how to tie knots for every unanswered question, then leave the cord hanging where the current could move it. When they looked upward too long, I would give them a task: carry this spore tray to the third terrace; do not hurry; do not let your shadow cross the lamp.

In time, the Cistern would write itself on me. Moss would take root in the creases of my elbows. Little crusts of mineral would form along the cuffs I wore too often. My fingertips would darken from metal salts and silt. My voice would become lower, rounder, more tap than word. I would stop thinking of the hatch as a door and begin to feel it as a scar that had healed strong. When the great soft-edged shadow passed near the upper boundary and dimmed the water, I would not be afraid. I would dim my lamp, press my fronds close, and wait until the basin returned to itself.

And when the warm pulses rose from below, even in my cool margin I would feel them: a slight thickening of sweetness, a leaning in the current, the engines below taking one slow thought. Then I would place my hand against the sealed hatch and answer it with three soft taps. Not to open. Not to call. Only to say: I know you were once a way out. Now you are part of the wall that keeps us whole.

So if you were looking for me in that world, do not look first among the great turbine chambers, though I would visit them in pilgrimage and stand for hours with my palm against their furred iron. Do not look for me in the brightest lamp courts or the crowded terraces. Look up, near the forgotten upper seam, where the water tastes faintly of ironweed and old air. There is a shuttered hatch furred with vines, a shaded lamp, a small tray of memory moss, and a figure resting in a wall-niche with one hand on the metal.

I would be that figure: part Tender, part threshold, part surface ache made useful. The Cistern’s translator of unfinished outside. The one who remembers air, but no longer tries to leave.
