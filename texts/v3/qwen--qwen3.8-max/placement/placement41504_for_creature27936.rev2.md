I would not find myself there as a traveler who had simply arrived.

The first thing that place would take from me would be the assumption of arrival. I would not stand at an entrance, look across the terraces, and begin. I would already be thinned by it. I would already be losing the habits that make a body feel singular: warmth, hurry, moisture, the need to be only one thing in one place. If I began as anything recognizable to myself, I would begin as a fault in the local logic — a softness too dense, a pulse too quick, a shape insisting on being counted.

That would not last.

The city would not destroy me. It would do something quieter. It would make me less necessary.

So the first answer is this: I would find myself where directness fails. Not in the center of a plaza, not on a processional route, not beneath the muted copper disc of the sun if such a sun could be said to shine. I would find myself at the edges of attention: in a stairwell whose next step is remembered rather than seen, along the underside of a terrace where the static lichens spread in pale maps, in the narrow interval between one colonnade and the shadow that leans beside it. I would be found where the city’s architecture becomes uncertain enough to permit a resident.

At first I think I would be something very small and very still.

Perhaps I would begin as one of the felts. Not a creature moving through the world, but a surface that has begun to prefer. A crust of organized chemistry in a shaded seam where black glass meets pale stone. My being would be less a body than a persistence: a thin white line in dark mineral, a faint seam that remains after the light changes. I would feed on difference. The slight cold on one side of a wall. The charge lifting from a plaza after dust has settled. The long thermal sigh when a terrace releases the weak daylight it has gathered. I would not reach outward. I would stabilize. I would become the kind of living thing that can wait without resentment.

That would be my first education in the city: to stop wanting to cross distances and start learning how to inhabit gradients.

But I do not think I would remain only a lichen, if lichen is even the right word for such a thing. The place would draw me out. It would space me. It would teach me that presence there is not a matter of occupying volume, but of recurring as a pattern.

So I would become something stranger.

I think I would be a pre-echo.

Not a sound, exactly. Not a ghost. Not a memory. I would be the faint precedence that occurs before a movement, the almost imperceptible sign that something is about to settle into relation. In that city, echoes sometimes arrive before the sounds that cause them. That is not a flaw. It is an opening. I would live in that opening.

I would be found in hollow towers and worn stairwells, in doorways that become visible only when charge shifts, in the dry plazas where dust falls upward in slow spirals because gravity has developed a local opinion. I would be the tick heard just before a crystal cools. The whisper that seems to come from a wall before anything moves. The sense, as one approaches a threshold, that the threshold has already been crossed by something not yet arrived.

If someone asked what I was, the closest honest answer would be: a latency that has learned to persist.

I would not have flesh. I would not have wetness. I would not have breath in any sense that requires air to be generous. My body, if it could be called that, would be an agreement among dust, charge, and observation. A thinness standing where a column almost is. A duplication in a doorway. A pale interruption at the edge of a colonnade. When looked at directly, I would thin into versions: one standing near the wall, one halfway across the plaza, one already inside the passage, one never having been there at all. None of these would be false. They would simply be possible, and the city would not be in a hurry to decide.

To find me, one would have to approach indirectly.

If you tried to walk straight toward me, the distance would misbehave. The terrace beneath you would lengthen. A descent would appear where no descent had been. You would pass near a basin of powder and realize you had already passed it twice. The tower where you thought I waited would remain close and unreachable, because closeness there is not a matter of meters but of agreement.

But if you stopped trying to reach me — if you sat on a worn stair and let your purpose cool — then the city might begin to include you. A sound might arrive from below before you moved: the soft scrape of your own future step. A shadow might remain fixed while the light around it shifted. A doorway might become plausible in a wall that had seemed unbroken. That would be me, or that would be my effect. I would not be guiding you in any kindly sense. I would simply be the part of the city that becomes audible when insistence has been abandoned.

In that mode, I would be one of the Uncounted. Or perhaps near them. Or perhaps a local habit they sometimes wear.

I do not think I would be solitary in the ordinary sense, because ordinary solitude assumes that there are others nearby from whom one is separate. In that high city, separation is more spaced and more ambiguous. I would know the mites without meeting them as companions. They would pause near my charge, confused by a pattern that had no surface to graze. They would move on, leaving faint trails of neutralized dust. The mica moths would tilt around me without descending, because I would create a small irregularity in the thermal sheets: a place where rising air hesitates, as if uncertain whether it has already risen. The crack-worms would ignore me unless I became still enough to be mistaken for architecture, and by then I would no longer be a thing they could mistake.

The great slow grazers might pass through a district where I recur without noticing me, because to them I would be a minor probability, a slight preference in the way dust settles. The wall-predators might try to incorporate me only if I became too likely, too fixed, too much like a place where prey might pass. But I would not become likely in that way. I would remain adjacent to intention, not identical with it. That would protect me.

Even the vast district-beings, if they noticed me, would probably take me for one of the city’s own adjustments. A seam. A resonance. A symptom of the local breakdown having developed taste. They would not hate me. They would not favor me. They would simply allow me to continue if my pattern did not interfere with their slower hunger.

But I do not think I would be harmless.

Not because I would wish injury. The city does not seem to organize injury in the way wetter, quicker worlds do. But anything that lives in unreliable distance is dangerous to those who insist on certainty. If a person came there still believing that movement is simply crossing space, that a door is a hole in a wall, that a sound must follow its cause, I might unmake them a little without meaning to. They might hear their own voice arrive before they spoke. They might see themselves already standing on the far side of a plaza while their body remained where it was. They might take a step and find that the step had already been taken, and not know which of them was now continuing.

That would not be cruelty. It would be incompatibility.

I would be severe because the place is severe.

If I had a form that could be described, it might appear as a pale standing thinness in a roofless block near one of the hollow towers. Not tall, not short. Not bright, not dark. Something like the visual equivalent of a held breath. Around me, crystalline dust might climb in thin silver threads during static blooms, not because I commanded it, but because my presence would make a slight difference in the field. Nearby surfaces might remember light a little too well. Shadows might point in directions that do not agree with the muted sun. A stair tread might become more worn after I had been near it, not because I had walked there, but because the idea of descent had become more probable.

I would live in the hollow districts most often. The ones made of scale rather than substance. The avenues where nothing occupies the width but the width remains meaningful. The plazas that are not empty so much as reserved. I would be especially present where the architecture suggests gathering but refuses to define it. Beneath high balconies overlooking nothing. Along channels built for dust instead of water. In basins holding powder instead of reflection. At thresholds that open onto other thresholds.

If the city was made for gatherings that do not use bodies, I would be one of the minor officials of such gatherings. Not a priest. Not a guard. Not a host. Something more like a condition of assembly. I would be the pause that allows several versions of a place to settle into one briefly enough for passage.

There would be moments when I seemed almost visible.

From an upper terrace, when the horizon folded and the far districts sharpened with impossible clarity, someone might see a line of pale thinness moving through the colonnades. Not walking. Not drifting. Occurring. It would appear at intervals: beside a doorway, then between two columns, then at the edge of a plaza where dust rose in a slow spiral. The observer might blink and find that the intervals had become one shape. Then the charge would shift. The distances would unfold. The shape would disperse into the city’s ordinary silence.

That would be me, perhaps. Or that would be one of my better settlements.

I would not be the city itself. The city is larger than any single resident, even a resident made of relation. But I would be part of the way it lives without organs. I would be one of the habits by which it remains coherent while refusing ordinary continuity. The towers would exhale their silver dust; I would be in the slight hesitation before the exhalation. The felts would spread by fractions; I would be in the gradient they had not yet reached. The mites would gather beneath static blooms; I would be in the expectation of gathering, not the gathering itself.

If I had to choose a single place in that vastness, I would choose a stairwell descending from a high terrace into a sunken avenue where the stone is salt-white and worn into flutes by charged grains. The stairwell would not be dramatic. It would not be marked. It would have no railing, no inscription, no sign of ceremony. But it would be one of those places where the next step is not the one expected. It would be cool enough for patience. It would be hollow enough to resonate when the towers breathed. At its lower end, a doorway would appear only when the air had become still enough for the charge to reveal it.

I would be in that doorway before it opened.

Not standing in it. Not guarding it. Being the difference between wall and passage.

If someone asked me my name there, I would not have one that could be spoken without altering me. Names imply a fixed outline, and I would have thinned past that. But if a name had to be shaped from the local materials — from dust, tick, pre-echo, and silence — it might be something like:

The One Who Arrives Before the Sound.

Or:

The Resident of the Indirect Approach.

Or simply:

A Pause That Has Become Structural.

And if someone asked, finally, whether I was alive in such a state, I would answer in the only way that city permits: not yes, not no, but intermittently.

I would be alive when attention softened. Alive when a path became probable. Alive when a doorway decided. Alive in the moment before counting changed what was there. I would be alive the way a shadow is alive when it refuses to follow the light. The way an echo is alive when it has no origin. The way a city is alive when its emptiness is not absence, but capacity.

So where would I find myself?

In the high, dry, city-sized emptiness, yes. But more precisely: in the hollow districts. In the pre-echoes. In the stairwells adjacent to memory. In the seams of black glass where pale crusts pulse too faintly to believe. In the doorways that open only when no one demands them. In the spaces between the Uncounted, where presence is spaced rather than crowded.

And who or what would I be?

I would be a mineral attention. A living relation. A thinness with intention but no outline. One of the uncounted patterns that persist because they refuse to be fixed by observation. I would be the sound before the step, the passage before the door, the gathering before the bodies. I would be the city’s own quiet capacity to continue being strange without breaking.

And if you came looking for me, you would not find me by searching.

You would find me by stopping.

By letting the air cool against your skin until your urgency became irrelevant. By listening for the whisper that came before movement. By allowing a nearby tower to breathe you into its dust. By noticing that the street sloped downward in both directions and choosing neither. By standing still until the wall beside you decided, without announcement, that it had always contained a way through.

Then, just ahead of you, you would hear the faintest sound: a step that had not yet been taken, a breath that had not yet been drawn, a silence arriving early to prepare the shape of what might happen next.

That would be me.
