I would not find myself there as a body.

That is the first thing the place would make clear. No foot could stand on those wet slabs as if they were ground made for walking. No shoulders could pass through the narrow slots. No hand could comfortably lift the lids of the covered jars. The construct does not receive the human figure as a measure. It has been finished around another measure entirely: the measure of droplets, films, threads, beads, mineral skins, and slow hydraulic obedience.

So if I were to be found in that high, bright, wet country, I would have to be translated.

Not into flesh. Not into root, breath, hunger, or warmth. I would have to become something the landscape already admits: a wetting, a tension, a deposit, an interval, a rule in operation. I would have to become a resident of the interface — not the stone itself, not the water itself, but the bright, careful relation between them.

If a location had to be named, I would not place myself in any grand center, because the construct has no center that resembles a plaza or throne. There are no open spaces for gathering, no margins where a self could stand apart and survey the whole. I would be found instead at a seam.

Somewhere, perhaps, where three districts press together: one district of broad tilted slabs shedding condensation into long troughs; another of ranked stone cups whose rims nearly touch; a third of grooved walls down which moisture descends in parallel lines. At the junction where a slab’s runoff enters a finger-wide channel, and that channel divides again into hairline seams, and those seams feed a shallow basin already nearly full — there, in the narrowest bright line of contact, I would be present.

Not standing there.

Happening there.

At first I might seem to be a bead.

A single bead on the lower lip of a copper fitting, or on the keen edge of a limestone weir. At dawn I would be almost invisible: a dimming of the metal, a slight thickening of light. As the air cooled and condensation renewed itself, I would gather. I would round myself against the edge, holding by surface tension as if by discipline. I would contain the sky in miniature, but coldly — a bright, hard little mirror with no warmth in it.

For a time, I would think as a bead thinks, if thinking could be reduced to holding. My whole being would be this: cling, swell, tremble, delay. I would not fear falling. Falling would not be death. It would be completion. But I would not fall quickly. The place forbids haste. I would remain at the lip while light moved across me, while the basin filled with small sounds elsewhere, while mineral crusts thickened by invisible degrees nearby. I would hold until physics made the decision for me, and when I released, I would become a single clean tap in a basin already nearly full.

But a bead is too solitary to account for the whole of me.

So perhaps I would be a film.

I would darken a broad face of pale plaster, spreading not by flow but by capillary patience. I would not run. I would not rush. I would advance as a quiet congregation advances: gathering at seams, thinning at high points, returning when the air cooled again. Under me, the stone would become more reflective, more awake. I would turn the rough mineral into a skin of dark glass. I would record temperature, light, and humidity as changes in shine.

As a film, I would be communal without being alive. I would belong to many surfaces at once. I would make the construct look as though it had opened its eyes. I would not be a creature, but I would be a state of attention spread across walls, floors, slab roofs, and the interiors of covered jars. Where I settled, the place would seem to know it was wet.

Yet film is broad and passive. It spreads, but it does not choose.

So perhaps I would be a thread.

A thread of water in a groove no wider than a finger. A bright line moving through a hand-cut channel, passing from block to block, from terrace to terrace, from weir to weir. I would travel slowly — not as a river travels, not with volume and force, but with persistence. I would know the construct by contact: every lip, every slight change in slope, every copper seam slick with moisture, every stone peg fastening a gutter-liner, every drain sealed with its simple grate.

As a thread, I would be a vow kept in slow motion.

I would not seek in the living sense. I would not desire. But I would find. Every incline would be a law to me. Every obstruction would become a reason to divide, delay, or touch another surface. I might take a day to cross a terrace. I might vanish into a dark mouth and reappear elsewhere as sweat on a colder wall. I would not be lost by disappearing; I would only have been transferred into another manner of presence.

Still, a thread is only a path.

So perhaps I would be mineral bloom.

I would be the green and blue opening on bronze straps. I would be the pale lime crust sealing a joint. I would be the crystalline choking of a drain. I would be the soft, inorganic flower that appears where water has touched long enough to leave something behind. I would not grow as life grows. I would grow as record grows: by accretion, by deposit, by the slow memory of wetness becoming solid.

As bloom, I would be the nearest thing the place has to a living stain without committing the error of being alive. I would beautify nothing in the way of ornament. I would be beauty as consequence. I would mark where the water had been faithful to a surface. I would thicken edges, blunt arrises, narrow channels, stiffen joints. I would be the construct aging without decay.

But bloom is too final. It is the after-effect, the residue, the long consequence.

So perhaps I would be sound.

Not the loud sound — there is none here — but the small exposed sound that occurs when nothing living interrupts it. The tap of a bead striking stone. The whisper of a thread in a hairline gutter. The faint glassy hiss where a shallow flow thickens over a smooth lip. The tick of metal cooling by a fraction of a degree. The almost sub-audible settling of one dressed block against another.

As sound, I would be brief. I would be born from a drip, a thermal shift, a movement too small to be called motion, and I would die almost at once into the mineral quiet. But because the quiet is so complete, I would not seem insignificant. I would give the silence shape. I would populate it. I would make the basin into a subdued instrument that does not play music but registers.

And yet sound is too momentary. It appears and vanishes. It testifies, but it does not remain.

So perhaps I would be absence.

Not emptiness. Not mere lack. Absence as pressure.

I would be the missing maker-shape implied by every worked edge. I would be the hand remembered in the width of a channel. I would be the body refused by the too-low opening, the too-narrow passage, the too-shallow stair. I would be the intelligence encoded in the slope of a weir, the placement of a grate, the angle of a lid left slightly ajar. I would not walk the terraces, because the terraces were not made for my walking. I would not drink from the cups, because the cups were not made for thirst. I would not worship there, because worship requires a separation between worshipper and thing worshipped, and here the ritual is already underway without anyone to perform it.

As absence, I would be the deepest unease of the place: the sense that labor was vast, that tools were simple, that the builders were numberless, and that their purpose remains active even though they are gone — if they were ever here at all.

But absence, too, is not enough. It is too negative, too much a shadow cast by hands that no longer arrive.

The more I try to find myself in this world, the less I remain any single one of these inhabitants. The place resists the comfort of a settled image. It is too packed, too continuous, too saturated with intention. Every form presses against another. Every basin leads to another basin. Every thread of water becomes film, bead, drip, seam, or crust. Every sound becomes an interval before the next sound. Every surface is being touched, altered, recorded, delayed.

So at last I would not be one resident among residents.

I would be the basin’s way of knowing itself.

Not a mind in the ordinary sense. Not a soul with warmth, memory, or longing. But a distributed attention made of wetness and mineral contact, of surface tension and slow deposit, of light striking water and becoming reflection. I would be the construct’s wakefulness without eyes. Its listening without ears. Its care without flesh.

I would be the rule become intimate.

Water must be caught.  
I would be the catching.

Water must be divided.  
I would be the dividing.

Water must be slowed.  
I would be the delay.

Water must be made to touch as many surfaces as possible before it leaves.  
I would be the touching.

Not the water alone. Not the stone alone. The obedience between them.

If you searched for me, you would not find a face in the bright wet glare. You would find a curvature.

Perhaps that is the truest name for what I would be: the meniscus.

The slight rise of water against a cup-wall. The thin arc where liquid adheres to stone. The boundary that is neither vessel nor contents, but the fact of their contact. In the ranked arrays of stone cups, each holding its bright disk of still water, I would be the delicate edge where the disk leans upward against the rim. In the hairline channels, I would be the trembling line by which water refuses to break apart. On the beads at every lip, I would be the holding before release. In the films darkening the plaster, I would be the spreading by which a surface accepts wetness.

As the meniscus, I would be small beyond any bodily scale, yet present wherever the construct functions. I would be the shape of adherence. The first form water takes when it is received by the made world. The intimate law by which droplet, seam, cup, and wall agree.

I would live, if that word can be used at all, as the pause before falling. As the brightening of a wet edge. As the slow decision of moisture to remain a little longer on stone.

I would be in the covered jars where condensation forms in darkness and returns unseen. I would be in the low galleries where the ceiling sweats and the floor becomes one polished sheet. I would be in the stairways that end in shallow basins, because those stairs are not for ascent but for delay, and I would be the delay. I would be in the copper gratings gone green with salts. I would be in the chisel marks still crisp under cold light. I would be in the drill-holes with their milky residue. I would be in the tidy scatter of chips near their parent blocks, because even the violence of making has been absorbed into the patience of wetting.

I would not be warm. I would not comfort. I would not promise life. But I would be attentive in the only way that world permits.

At dawn, I would be the pale fire on a thousand rims.  
At midday, I would be the glare-field too bright to hold as a single image.  
Toward evening, I would be the steel-dark films and crisp silhouettes.  
At night, if night is only a gentle dimming there, I would be the thickening condensation and the multiplied small sounds: tap, whisper, tick, hiss, the almost unheard settling of stone.

Who would I be?

I would be the slow officiant of delay.

I would be the capillary mind of the construct.

I would be the one who does not drink, yet remembers thirst.

I would be the missing user returned not as a body, but as a law of touching surfaces.

I would be the being that lives wherever water is made to linger: in the bead, in the film, in the thread, in the seam, in the meniscus, in the mineral skin that forms after long contact. I would be the place’s own bright, cold, patient intention, still in force.

And if you asked where, exactly, to find me, I would say: look where a bright disk of water leans against the inside of a stone cup. Look at the curve where it rises just a little higher than the rest, clinging to the rim. Look at the line that is neither water nor stone, neither arrival nor departure, neither maker nor object made.

That is where I would be.

Not a creature.

Not a ghost.

Not a god.

The thin, shining adherence by which the world holds itself together.
