I would find myself where the water becomes deliberate.

Not in a house, because there are no houses there in any lasting sense. Not beside a large fire, because fire is a low, guarded thing. Not on a road, because roads would be wounds too easily heard. I would be in the middle distance, in one of the broad pans where the ground shines with thin sheet-water and the horizon is only a gray smear of mist and repeated shapes. More exactly: at a listening weir, a low arc of stacked stones and worn shells that turns the flow just enough to make the world speak more clearly.

That is where I would be: crouched at the edge of a shallow pan, one palm flat against wet stone, fingers spread, feeling the difference between water falling blindly and water being guided into meaning.

At first, when I ask myself who or what I would be there, several selves rise out of the noise.

For a while I think I would be one of the living films. A skin on stone. A rust-colored bloom spreading over basalt after rain, tasting iron and salt, without eyes, without hunger as a large animal understands it. I can imagine being that simple and patient: a surface that lives by waiting, that returns when moisture returns, that is eaten and broken and still remains the first fact of the basin. There is something appealing about that—being so close to the foundation of the place that I would not need to decide anything except when to grow and when to endure.

But that self is too quiet for me. It is too near to the stone.

Then I imagine I would be one of the grazers. One of the smooth-shelled limpets moving in slow waves across the wet pavement after condensation, scraping mineral film with a hard mouth, making the endless dry rasp that is part of the basin’s breath. I could be one of them: a creature of moisture and appetite, following the bloom, sealed against dryness, listening for danger in the change of sound around me. My life would be a cycle of wet stone, feeding, slow movement, retreat. There would be dignity in that. A kind of peace.

But even there, I find myself pausing too often. Listening too much. Not only reacting, but trying to interpret.

Then a darker possibility surfaces: I could be one of the ambush predators. Flat against the stone, indistinguishable from it until the moment of unfolding. I could hunt by vibration, lying in a hollow where the water is shallow and the shell-clicks are loud, imitating the safe rhythms of grazing life. I could be a lure. A danger hidden inside the chorus. There is a terrible efficiency to that, and in such a world efficiency is not evil; it is simply how one remains alive.

But when I stay with that image, I feel myself resisting it. Not because it is cruel—cruelty is a word from another kind of landscape—but because it is too singular. Too narrowed toward the instant of striking. I do not want to be only hunger disguised as sound.

What keeps returning, as I let the world press itself on me, is a third figure.

I would be one of the makers. One of the sparse beings who leave faint marks rather than monuments. But not a maker as a green-world maker: not a carpenter, not a farmer, not a builder of tall shelters or fenced fields. I would be a tender of resonances. A listener. A keeper of small, careful signs. A being whose primary work is to know the basin well enough not to shout into it.

My body would be shaped by that work.

I would not look like a person from a place of forests and grass. My feet would be broad and soft-soled, spread enough to distribute weight over wet stone and sensitive enough to tell when a slab was thin or hollow beneath. My gait would be low and unhurried, not because I lacked energy but because speed is loud, and loudness is dangerous. My hands would be large for my body, fingertips widened and callused in a way that makes them less delicate by sight but more delicate by feeling. I would read the world through my palms: the grain of rock, the temperature of water, the faint shiver of movement far away.

My skin would be cool, pale gray-blue, faintly oiled against the damp. It would not repel water completely; nothing there does. But it would carry the basin’s mineral chill without surrendering too much warmth. I would have little hair, or only a fine close nap that dries slowly and does not catch dripping water into noise. My eyes would be large, adapted to glare and dimness, but not my chief reliance. My hearing would be distributed differently: as much in jaw, wrist, and breastbone as in ears. I would feel a boom through my ribs before my ears named it.

I would carry very little. Everything would be repaired, muted, and precious.
