Their knuckles chapped with cold, their sleeves dark to the elbow, their eyes accustomed to reading the ground by shine. If I were there, I would be one of those figures, though not exactly a mason and not exactly a laborer. I would be found near the edge where intention first asks the ground to cooperate: on the lowest broad terrace, in a roofless room whose walls are only knee-high, beside a channel that has not yet learned its final voice.

The room would have no name yet. It would have a position, a bearing, a relation to water. It would lie just downstream from a wooden template left in place so long that mineral rind has begun to seal it into the rock, and just above the cistern where the falling water deepens into a slow, hollow boom. There, where the stone is pale and beaded, where a footprint fills with clear water before it can be called a footprint, I would be the one kneeling with an ear near the ground.

I would be a listener.

Not a priest, not an architect, not exactly a builder, though the place would make all three of those roles porous. I would be something like a clerk of the unfinished city: a sounding-keeper, a water-reader, a tender of emptiness. My palms would be roughened by rope and cold stone. My boots would be softened and heavy with damp. Moss would have begun to grow in the seams of whatever I wore, not because I neglected myself, but because the basin treats every still object as a surface offered to life. My skin would always be cool. My breath would whiten even in ordinary light. When I spoke, my voice would seem to arrive a half-beat after my mouth, as if the air were still deciding whether to carry it.

My work would begin with water.

Before any block was set, I would follow the thin dark lines the rain left on stone. I would watch where moisture lingered after the clouds had moved on, because those lingering patches would be the first drafts of future walls. I would carry a shallow bowl of clay and a narrow wooden rod, and when a wet trace appeared in a flat plaza I would kneel beside it and mark it—not by covering it, but by giving it room. The masons would come later with cord and hammer. They would trust the trace because water had made it, and they would trust me because I had not tried to improve it.

I would also test stone by sound.

Every new block lifted by the little cranes would receive a tap before it received a place. I would carry a hammer no larger than a cupped hand, its head dense and dark, its handle worn smooth by use. The strike would not be forceful. It would be a question. A good stone would answer with a clear, brief tone; a stone with a hidden seam would answer with a duller hesitation; a stone too eager to drink water would sound as if it were listening back. The workers would not need many words from me. A nod would be enough. A hand laid flat on the rock would mean: let it rest. Two taps in the same place would mean: turn it. A single tap near the edge would mean: this one belongs to a wall that has not yet been traced.

The strange part is that I would not be entirely separate from the city’s material. If the basin had its way, I would be partly made of it. I would be a being with mineral in my joints and water in my attention. Perhaps I would have been grown rather than born there: condensed from the same cold air that lays a second skin on stone, gathered in a roofless room until the pattern became stable enough to stand. My bones would feel like wet limestone. My thoughts would move slowly, like water finding its level. I would not find this frightening. In that place, the boundary between maker and material is already uncertain; a wooden frame can become an armature for stone, a survey line can become a damp mark, an intention can soak into geology. I would be one more instance of that rule. I would be the basin’s attention becoming a body.

My home, if I had one, would not be a house. Houses would be too finished for me. I would live in the empty plaza nearest the resonant chamber, where the ground’s deep booming rises through the soles of the boots and makes the ribs feel briefly architectural. I would sleep on a bed of woven sedges under a low scaffold, not because I needed shelter so much as because the scaffold would have begun to acquire moss, and I would want to lie near something that was changing slowly. In the night, the basin would be loud in its patient way: drip, rush, creak, boom. I would not experience those sounds as noise. I would experience them as maintenance. They would keep me coherent. If one of them stopped—if a channel went silent, if a drip failed, if a resonant throat became muffled—I would wake at once, because silence there would not mean peace. It would mean obstruction.

That is why I would be valued, if I was valued at all: I would be alert to absence.

The builders already know that emptiness is capacity, but I would be the one who makes sure the emptiness remains legible. I would oppose no one. I would not fill a room because it felt lonely. I would not roof a space before its water had taught the walls where to stand. I would stand in a broad street that no crowd had yet walked and listen to the way the rain crossed it. If the sound scattered too quickly, the street was too wide; if it returned too sharply, the street was too narrow; if it moved down the slope with a steady, cold murmur, then the street had been taught correctly and should be left alone.

I would be, in other words, a custodian of the not-yet.

There would be moments when the other workers called across the terraces, their short loud phrases bouncing from stone to stone, and I would answer from below or above, not with new information but with the same phrase returned after it had passed through the basin’s echo. That alteration would matter. If the call came back with a clear double edge, the space between us was open and stable. If it came back flattened, something had leaned into the air: a scaffold needing bracing, a wet timber shifting, a wall beginning to stand where it should not. My voice would be one of the tools by which the place checked itself.
