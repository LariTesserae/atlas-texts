Not at the seam.

Not at the cracked maintenance hatch half-buried in dust, where an outsider might stoop and peer into the hidden hollow. That threshold would be too full of arrival, too full of the outside. If I were there, still carrying the memory of weather, still soft in the ordinary way of soft things, I would be an emergency. My breath would be a leak. My skin would be a scandal. The faintest trace of moisture on my mouth, my palms, my eyes would register in that dry-lit chamber as something between treasure and catastrophe.

So no: if I truly found myself in that world, I would not be at the entrance. I would be deeper, lower, where the floor has settled into its shallow basin and the plain gray-white light makes every body accountable.

At first, I imagine myself as a visitor. That is the easiest thought, and also the wrong one. I imagine stepping through the seam too tall, too warm, too full of fluid and pulse. The mites would turn toward me in slow rust-colored drifts. The clickers would falter. The sound-makers would go briefly silent, then start a quick, testing rhythm. Antennae would lift from every seam. Predators would not attack immediately; they would listen. Scavengers would gather near anything loose, shed, or separable about me. The chamber would not hate me. It would simply recognize what I was not: dry, sealed, rhythmical, incorporated.

If I remained human, I would be a rupture. I would have to be dried, corrected, disassembled, or expelled. There would be no place in that commonwealth for a creature that carries water inside itself as a matter of course. My very living softness would be a violation.

But the question pulls me past that first image. It asks not only where I would appear, but what I would become if the world had time enough to make me its own.

So I let the human shape be worn down. I let the softness be filed away. I let the water be taken, or sealed, or converted into something so dry it no longer remembers rain. I let the heartbeat slow into engine rhythm. I let thought become vibration. I let the need for sky disappear.

When that happens, I do not become one of the darting flies, or one of the tiny mites, or one of the pale larvae in the seams. Those are essential, but they are not where my mind settles. If I am to be in that world as something more than an intruder, I become one of the older, larger shapes: one of the low, broad tenders moving through the center of the basin.

You would find me there, under the ordinary light.

Not near the upper walls where moths rise in dry clouds, and not in the narrow ducts where copper-colored worms clear the hollow pipes. Not in the highest shadows, because the chamber has no real shadows of that kind; only practical shadows cast by railings, engines, cabinets, and broken hoods. You would find me in the middle distance of the hollow, where the floor has been polished almost smooth by countless small feet, but where shallow grooves still mark the oldest paths. My route would run between the engine cradle and the relay bank, past a low brick box with darkened vents, past a cracked ceramic chimney no taller than a knee, past a row of corroded cabinets whose gauges broke long ago and have since become shelters.

There would be a place where the dust has been compacted into something like stone, yet remains faintly ridged by traffic. That would be my ground. Near a heating box whose surface never glows but gives off a steady, dry warmth. Near a worn flywheel turning behind a guard thinned to lace. Near a panel where old switches project like broken teeth and beetles cross the contacts in patient lines. That is where I would be: at the worn heart of the chamber, where machine and floor have begun to resemble each other.

I would not be beautiful in any green or shining sense. I would be beautiful the way a final tool is beautiful: shaped by use until nothing unnecessary remains.

My body would be low and broad, perhaps no larger than a footlocker, perhaps a little more, depending on how much time had passed over me. I would be armored in plates that are partly grown and partly gathered: shell, old enamel, flakes of porcelain, thin metal scales, ceramic chips, dust fused into a matte outer skin. Nothing on me would glisten. Nothing would sweat. My surface would be dry as fired clay, cool as iron left in shade. In some places my armor would be worn smooth by contact with walls and machines. In others it would carry tiny scratches, grooves, and pits where the chamber’s life has written itself onto me.

My legs would end in pads and fine hooks, made for polished steel, cracked tile, compacted dust, and rounded stone. My head would sit low, partly tucked beneath a shelf of shell. My eyes would be dim and coarse, enough to register the chamber’s plain illumination brightening or dimming, but not enough to make a world of images. I would know mostly by vibration. The floor would speak to me. The engines would speak through it. The smaller creatures would speak through their movement. I would hear with my whole body.

On my back there would be life.

Not as a burden, but as a condition. Mites would ride in rust-colored patches between my plates. Pale larvae would shelter in the joints of my legs. A few small beetles would travel with me, climbing over me when I rest, cleaning the dust from my seams. Perhaps a web-spinner would have anchored a brittle gray filament to one of my older plates, using me as a moving post. I would carry a small traveling community across the basin, and I would not be troubled by that. I would be less a single animal than a slow event with legs.

I would be a tender.

Not a ruler. Not a priest. Not a guardian in any heroic sense. The chamber does not need heroes. It needs maintenance. So I would be a maintenance body, a mobile organ of the room. My work would be quiet and constant. I would clear dust from vents with stiff mouthparts. I would scrape oxide from surfaces too broad for the beetles to manage alone. I would press loose fragments into seams when the chamber’s vibration has worked them free. I would move obstructions that are too heavy for the smaller crowd. I would open paths when the floor becomes too thick with grazing bands. I would press my body against warm casings and live panels, drawing heat or charge into myself, or perhaps only testing them, making sure they remain as they should be.

Sometimes I would stop for a long time beside a heating box or a transformer niche, motionless except for the faint settling of dust around me. Smaller creatures would climb over me then. They would feed on parasites and gathered particles in the crevices of my armor. They would treat me as furniture, as terrain, as a temporary station. If a relay clicked nearby, my own deep click might answer. If the engine changed rhythm, my body would know before the smaller creatures did, because I would be heavy enough to feel the shift through the floor. Then I would move again, following a path worn by countless predecessors, adding my weight to the groove.

My mind, if it could still be called a mind, would not be full of words.

It would be full of rhythm.

Engine hum.  
Fan breath.  
Relay click.  
Beetle tick.  
Rasper warning.  
Dust shift.  
Wing-whisper.  
The tiny metallic applause of current-scattered bodies.  
The scraping drag of scavengers.  
The deep pulse of my own shell against the floor.

I would think in intervals. A pause too long near a conveyor screw. A missing tick from a cooling plate. A change in the pitch of a fan. A sudden silence where there should be a cricket-like rasp. These would be my thoughts. Not sentences, but states. Not desires, but orientations. Move toward the wrongness. Clear the obstruction. Restore the ordinary murmur. Keep the chamber running.

That is what I would be: a keeper of the ordinary murmur.

The machines would not be tools to me. They would not be objects I contemplated. They would be weather, hearth, pasture, command, and kin. The engines would produce the conditions of my existence. My body would produce conditions for the smaller creatures. The smaller creatures would maintain the finer mechanisms. The mechanisms would maintain the heat, current, vibration, and dryness. The dryness would maintain us all. There would be no outside to this circle. No expectation of rain. No dream of green. No memory of rivers. The circle would be complete, and I would be inside it as one of its slower moving parts.

If I found myself near a relay panel where key-beetles climbed into broken sockets, I would wait. I would feel their small interruptions in the current. I would know that their bodies completing or breaking faint circuits was not separate from my own larger work. They were the fine adjustment; I was the coarse adjustment. They cleaned the contact; I cleared the space around it. They answered the machine at the scale of a grain; I answered it at the scale of a plate or panel. Together we would be one act.

If a predator passed through the crowd and the smaller bodies rippled away, I would not intervene. The chamber requires predators. Without them, the grazers would overwhelm delicate parts. Without death, the floor would choke. I would feel the quick violence, the brief dismantling, and understand it as transfer. Nothing wasted. Nothing mourned in a wet or soft way. The dead would be taken apart, sorted, carried, reduced, returned. If one of my own plates cracked or a piece of old shell fell from me, scavengers would come for that too. I would not be offended. I would be part of the economy.

If a moth cloud rose from the floor after some disturbance, I would continue moving. The dry whisper of wings would pass above me, mineral dust would sift down, and the light would remain ordinary. The moths would carry their particulate to another corner. The chamber would correct itself. I would correct myself by continuing.

The thing I would fear most would not be pain.

It would be silence.

A silence in the wrong place would be more terrible than any wound. If the engines stopped, if the fans ceased, if the relays failed to answer, if the sound-makers went mute and did not return, then the chamber would be entering some final state even it could not repair. I would move toward that silence with all the urgency my heavy body allowed. I would click against the floor. I would press myself to panels. I would test the air through vents. I would try to find the missing pulse.

And moisture.

That would be the other great wrongness.

If some impossible drop of water entered that world, I would be drawn toward it with a terrible attention. Not because I could drink. Not because I could use it gently. Because moisture would alter everything. It would darken dust. It would change conductivity. It would invite corrosion of the chamber’s careful dry chemistry. It would make soft what must remain sealed. It might be treated as sacred by some part of me, perhaps, but only in the way a poison can be sacred: set apart, dangerous, demanding response.

If a human visitor came carrying the forbidden weather of their body, I would be among the first to approach. I would not recognize them as kin. I would recognize them as softness, heat, leak, and wrong rhythm. The chamber would not ask me to pity them. It would ask me to assess. Could they be dried? Could they be incorporated? Could they move with the rhythm? If not, they would be treated as any other malfunction: worked upon, broken down, or sealed away.

That is the hardest part of the answer, and also the truest.

If I belonged there, I would not retain a human ethics of softness. I would have an ethics of continuation. The chamber would not value life because it is tender. It would value life because it maintains. It would not value the individual because it is unique. It would value the individual insofar as the individual is a function. My own value would be measured by how well I kept the rhythm, how cleanly I moved through my route, how effectively my body supported the smaller lives and the older machines.

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness belongs to creatures who remember separation from a wider world. That hollow does not remember a wider world. It has intensified inward. It has become complete in its confinement. If I were one of its tenders, I would not long for sky. I would not dream of rain. I would not imagine fields. My fulfillment would be the next correct movement. The next cleared vent. The next answered click. The next warm plate against my shell. The next slow passage across the polished basin while the ordinary light shows everything exactly as it is.

In my resting place, under a hooded shelter near the heating box, I would sometimes be still for so long that dust gathered on my plates. Beetles would cross me as if I were a fallen cabinet. Larvae would explore the seams of my legs. A rasper nearby would produce a short dry scrape, and the sound would travel through me like a command. The engine would turn. The fans would breathe. The current would pulse through old conductors. I would click once, deeply, almost inaudibly, and the chamber would continue.

If I died there, there would be no softness in it.

My plates would be separated. The mites would disperse. The larvae would leave my joints. Scavengers would arrive with blunt patience. Metal fragments would be carried to warm crevices. Ceramic pieces would be fitted into nests. Shell would become floor. Dust would become dust. The groove I had worn into my route would remain for a time, guiding others after me. Eventually another body would wear it deeper, or the chamber would shift the path by some small erosion of its own. Nothing would be wasted. Nothing would be remembered as a grave. I would be incorporated.

So this is where I would find myself:

Low.  
Central.  
Under plain light.  
Between the engine and the relay bank.  
On the polished, ridged floor of the hidden basin.  
Among the crowded, dry, loud commonwealth.

And this is who I would be:

Not a visitor.  
Not a human with weather in my lungs.  
Not a single master of the room.

I would be one of the old plated tenders. A slow, dry-bodied maintenance creature. A walking repair. A living panel. A moving shelter for smaller lives. A click in time with the engine. A weight that keeps the path worn. A thing that tends the final machinery because tending is the last stable form of life.

My name would not be a word.

It would be a rhythm:

hum,  
click,  
rasp,  
shift,  
and the long dry whisper of dust settling after my passing.
