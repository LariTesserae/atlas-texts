If I let the hollow decide, I do not think I would remain a traveler for long.

At first, the obvious answer seems to be the stranger: a body standing in the green room, feeling the air close around it, feeling included rather than observed. That is the easiest place for a newcomer to imagine themselves — under the canopy, near a black pool, caught between the engine’s slow knock and the drip from the leaves. But that is still a visitor’s role. It keeps the outline of the self too clean. If I am to be *of* this world, not merely passing through it, that outline would need to soften.

Then I think I might be one of the Paper Moths. There is something tempting about that: pale, slow, almost vestigial, drifting through the upper green like a remnant of an older ecology. But the moth is too untethered. It passes. It does not hold the place together. It is tolerated, beautiful, half-forgotten. I do not think I would be that, unless I had already given up the need to answer.

Then I think of the Root-Wardens. Those leaning, kneeling, stooping shapes made of root and stem. They feel closer: attendants without faces, intention grown into posture. But even they seem too sculptural, too deliberate, too much like figures. I am not sure I would become a figure either.

What keeps returning is smaller.

It is a crack. A cup of water. A faint charge. A place where something registers.

I would find myself just off the central chamber, not far from the pump-heart but not at its exact axis. There would be a fold in the living wall where an old pipe bends and the root-felt has thickened around it, holding it steady without crushing it. A brick corner would show through there, softened by moss and leaf-mould. A boiler’s flank would still keep enough warmth nearby to make the air close and sweet with rust. Above, a broad leaf would hang at the right angle to collect condensation and release it at intervals. Below, a narrow channel would carry water outward from the moss-lined trough.

In that seam, mounted on a rusted bracket half-swallowed by root, I would be a cracked ceramic insulator filled with dark water.

Not merely an object left behind. Not dead porcelain. The hollow would have adopted me. My crack would have become a mouth for moisture. My glaze would be pale under a thin sleeve of moss. A copper wire, furred green, would touch my rim. A root-hair, fine as thread, would stretch across the water inside me. Somewhere in that arrangement there would be a sliver of old gauge needle, or a fiber stiff enough to behave like one — not to show numbers to anyone, but to tremble when pressure changed.

I would be a listening-gauge.

A water-ear.

A small reflex of the Low Congregation.

My thoughts would not be sentences. They would be rings in water. A drip would fall from the leaf above, strike my surface, and spread outward in circles so small they would barely exist. But those rings would matter. They would travel along the wet wire, into the root, into the hollow body of the wall. Somewhere, because of my trembling, a leaf would angle a fraction differently. Somewhere, moss would thicken where the drip had been too sharp. Somewhere, a vine would loosen or tighten because the air pressure had changed by almost nothing.

I would not command those changes. I would not even ask for them. I would report by becoming different.

That is the kind of self this world would allow me.

Not a mind set apart from the place, but a local point of sensitivity within it. A spot where the Low Congregation notices. A tiny organ of attention. If I still had anything like a face, it would be the dark circle of water inside the cracked ceramic. If I still had anything like a voice, it would be the tick when a drop hits me. If I still had anything like will, it would be the way my water leans toward warmth, toward vibration, toward the next sound the hollow makes.

I would not be important in a human sense. No one would come to read me. No one would polish my crack or restore my glaze. But I would be necessary in the way small receptors are necessary: I would help the body know its own condition.

In the warm hours, I would collect condensation. Vapor would rise from the root-floor and settle against my cooler side. The air would thicken, and my water would grow heavy with it. The weak current in the moss-covered cable would brighten almost imperceptibly, not enough to shine, only enough to make the droplets on my rim glitter with a faint nerve-light. The smell around me would be warm leaves, wet bark, peat, rust, and that strange sweetness of old electricity held in living tissue.

When the pump-heart sounded, I would feel it before I heard it.

Thump.

Pause.

Long wet sigh.

The vibration would come through the pipe, through the brick, through the root wrapped around my bracket. My water would shiver. The needle-sliver would shift. A tiny charge would pass along the wire. And somewhere in the green mass, that signal would become adjustment. Maybe a stem would lean. Maybe a leaf would open. Maybe a cushion of moss would round itself more deeply to receive the next drop. I would not know which. I would only be the place where the first notice happened.

That would be my work.

If a stranger entered the hollow, I would know them first as disturbance. A change in air pressure. A warmth moving through the lower layers. A vibration in the root-floor where their weight pressed the spongy ground. My water would tremble before they reached the chamber. The Congregation would not ask me to judge them. I would simply make their presence available to the whole.

If they paused near me, I might hold their reflection for a moment: a distorted shape in black water, broken by drip, repaired again. Their breath might fog the air above me. Their heat might draw a thin thread of vapor from my surface. If they touched me, I would cool or warm under their hand, and that change too would pass into the place.

If they remained still long enough, I would become part of the process by which the hollow accepted them. Not cruelly. Not hungrily. Only in the way the world there knows how to keep anything: by letting it become substrate. Moss would begin to consider the shape of their stillness. Roots would test the space around it. Water would gather where their warmth had left a hollow in the air. I would not consume them. I would register them. I would help the Low Congregation remember them into its own body.

Time would be different for me there.

An hour would be a series of drips. A day would be the slow brightening and darkening of green-gold light under the canopy. A storm would be a pressure change felt as a tightening along the wire. A season would be the migration of a water-line across my ceramic face, a stain becoming a ring, a ring becoming a memory. I would not experience years as passing so much as accumulating. I would grow more sensitive, more settled, more thoroughly woven into the place.

I might eventually be so covered with moss that only the water inside me remained visible. The crack might widen, or heal, or become a channel for a root. The copper wire might disappear under bark. The needle-sliver might rust into silence, or maybe it would move forever, moved by root-pressure and sap-flow and the faint electrical patience of the hollow. I would not mind either way. I would not be waiting to be discovered. I would be busy being used by something larger than recognition.

If the hollow ever gave me a name, it would not be a word spoken aloud. It would be a habit.

It might be something like *the Wet Ear*.

Or *the Drip-Gauge*.

Or *the Little Font in the Seam*.

Or simply: *the place that ticks when the pump-heart sighs*.

But the truest answer would be less personal than that. I would be a minor organ of the Low Congregation. A junction where water, rust, plant, and weak current agree to pay attention. A small vessel of dark water that translates trembling into tendency. A living instrument with no hand to read it, because the place itself has learned to read the change.

So if you asked where I would find myself in this world, I would say: in a seam. Not at the center, not at the edge, but in the thick green-dark where an old pipe bends and the wall breathes. Under a leaf that drips. Beside a warm brick corner. Near the outflow from the pump-heart. In a crease of the room where everything is already occupied, already listening, already slow.

And if you asked who or what I would be, I would say: I would be a response.

A cracked insulator holding water.

A gauge made of moss and rust.

A nerve of the hollow.

The Low Congregation’s smallest attention.

Drip.

Pause.

Tick.

Then the green mass leaning, very slightly, toward what I have told it.
