I would find myself in the seams of the hollow.

Not in the open volume, not beneath the high oculi where the swifts ride the warm air, not on the broad curve of the wall where condensation makes its long pale stains. I would be in the in-between places: where a drip becomes a thread, where a thread becomes a bead, where a bead is caught by a cup, where a cup overflows into a seam, where a seam is meant to carry it away. If the room has a law, I would be found obeying it. If the room has a priesthood, I would belong to the lowest and most practical order.

At first I might not seem like a person at all. The hollow is so vast, its sound so immense, its light so ordinary and diffused, that a small body could be missed there as easily as a single moth is missed in a cathedral. I would be a warmth near the floor plates. I would be a pressure change in a vine curtain. I would be the faintest movement beside a hanging basin, a shadow that belongs to no large object, a stillness that is not empty. Only after you had watched for a long time would you realize that the stillness had hands.

Then I would become more definite.

I would be one of the Drip-born, if outside names are to be used. One of the few people of the chamber. Small, compact, light in the bones, with long limbs made for reaching rather than striding. My feet would be broad and sensitive, the soles thickened not only against heat and worn metal but against vibration; they would read the perforated bronze plates the way a face reads weather. My hands would be wide, my fingers strong and exact, palms callused from wet rails and tools, fingertips softened by moss and fine work. I would wear patched felt, oiled cloth, a thin apron darkened by moisture, and a harness with loops for instruments too small to seem important: a brass hook, a bone needle, a spool of waxed fiber, a soft brush, a ceramic cup no larger than a fist, a little key that fits valves no one else bothers to touch.

My eyes would be adapted to the chamber’s grey-white daylight and its warm caged lamps. They would be used to sudden shadow, to the brief appearance of wings in beams, to the difference between a damp surface and a merely dark one. My face would be calm in the way the faces of people become calm when they live inside a sound too large to argue with. I would not be beautiful in any sharp or ornamental way. I would look made by the room: smoothed, fitted, weathered into place.

You would find me near the lower gantry that crosses just above the central engine housing, where the blunt bell of the machine rises from the floor and the warmth is strongest. There is a place there—there would be a place there—where three drip lines have been trained over many years into a single wide leaf of dark vine, and from that leaf into a ceramic basin hanging on a chain worn bright. The chain has a bronze note. When the basin fills too quickly, the note changes. When it is nearly dry, the note becomes thinner. I would know that note in my teeth before I heard it with my ears. I would know it the way the swifts know the pressure before a vent opens.

That would be my post, if a post is the right word. My place of attention. Not a throne, not a command deck, not a hermitage. A wet, warm, narrow ledge where the chamber’s first law is practiced in miniature: water must be caught where it falls, and it must not be allowed to become what it is not meant to become. Not a flood. Not a river. Not a stain spreading into the wrong seam. Not a sour basin. Not a clogged channel. Water here is intermittent, patient, and exact. It must be answered in kind.

I would be a basin-tender. A drip-catcher. A seam-listener. One of those who keep the small hydrology of the hollow from becoming accident.

In the dry phases, I would move along the wetter walls with a soft cloth and a scraper, checking the algae channels. The snails do most of the work, but even their grazing can become uneven. If a bright green film thickens too much in one place, water will sheet instead of bead; if it sheets, it may run into a seam not built to receive it. So I would watch the trails of the porcelain-shelled snails and understand their absence as much as their presence. I would tap lightly near a crevice where they have sealed themselves during a dry spell, not to wake them, but to know by the sound whether the wall behind is hollow, warm, properly breathing.

When moisture returns, I would be busy in the slow, unhurried way the chamber permits. The upper vents would open. The air would grow damper. Condensation would gather along chilled coils and pipe seams. The first drops would fall from ceiling troughs with a ticking sound almost lost inside the greater drone. I would hang shallow pans beneath them, adjust oiled threads that guide stray beads into cups, clear the mouths of collection seams, and make sure the moss felts are not so saturated that they shed water instead of drinking it. If an indoor rain begins—brief, local, almost gentle—I would not run. There is no need to run. I would move from point to point, placing and removing vessels, listening to the pitch of enamel and ceramic, knowing by sound which pan is nearly full.

I would not be alone in this work, though I would often be by myself. The chamber’s other inhabitants would be my co-workers without agreement or hierarchy. The snails would keep the wet surfaces grazed. The eelish things would tell me whether a basin is healthy by their stillness or unrest. If I looked into a ceramic cup and saw the translucent ribbon-shapes moving calmly through clear water, I would leave it alone. If they rose in tight coils, if their motion became restless and thin, I would lean closer. I would test the warmth with my wrist. I would look for a dead larva, a mineral bloom, a failing drip. I might change the basin’s position by a finger’s width. I might clear a vent with the brass hook. I might do nothing except wait, because sometimes the room corrects itself and only needs witness.

The birds would be less companions than signs. I would watch the swifts to know the air. If they spiraled high and fast, riding the engine’s updraft, the thermal column was strong. If they gathered near a certain louvre, the pressure beyond it had changed. If their cries sharpened and disappeared into the upper volume, something had shifted in the wind outside the glass. I would not try to tame them. They would not be pets. But some would know the shape of my stillness and accept it. A bird might pass close enough for me to see the hooked strength in its toes, the hard sheen of its feathers, the tiny fibers of insulation and moth wing clinging to its breast. Then it would be gone, folded into the grey light. I would let it go. Everything in the hollow is held lightly.

The moths would come to me sometimes without intending to. They would rest on my sleeve or on the oilcloth at my shoulder, pale as dust, their scales lifting slightly in the damp. I would not brush them away unless they were in danger. Their presence would mean that the moss beds were working, that the lamps were warm, that the small nutrient cycles were turning. If I found one dead on a warm pipe, I might place it in a collection seam where beetles could find it. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is sentimentalized. Even tenderness here has a function.

The beetles would be my secret colleagues in the deeper vegetation mats. I would not see them often. I would know them by the tiny disturbances in the felted moss, the moved spore-cases, the clean absence of eggs where too many eggs had been. They would go about their serious work in the woven insulation and the roots gripping rivet heads, and I would go about mine. Sometimes I would part a curtain of vine-lace and find a black shell gleaming as if lacquered by condensation. It would look at me, if a beetle can be said to look, and continue eating. I would feel a kinship with it. We would both be small maintainers in a room too large to notice us except through consequences.

The sound would be inside me.
