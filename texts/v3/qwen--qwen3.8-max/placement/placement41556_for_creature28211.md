I would find myself pressed.

Not standing, not floating freely, not arriving as a traveler with space around me. In the Silt Nursery, the first fact of existence would be closeness. Water on every side. Sediment overhead. Crust underfoot. Other bodies nearby, so near that my movement would become their weather and theirs would become mine. If I were there, I would not have a horizon. I would have a fit.

So the first answer is simple: I would be something that fits.

I would be under the false floor, in that uncertain region where newly settled mud has not yet decided whether to remain powder or become architecture. Not in the open pool above, not in the raw unformed mud below, but in the seam between them — the place where a crack has opened just enough to let water through, and where that water carries with it the taste of somewhere else. I would live near a hairline fissure, one of those tiny inlets through which colder, cleaner water enters the Nursery. Not directly in the current, where I might be torn loose, but just beside it, in the slow eddy where particles hesitate before settling.

If you looked for me there, you would not look for a face. You would look for a slight orderliness in the silt. A place where grains are arranged a little more deliberately than chance would require. A faint line of mucus, already stiffening. A trail that other small bodies follow without knowing why.

At first, I think I would be one of the larvae.

That feels like the most honest body to borrow.

A pale, soft thing, no larger than a fleck of living dust. Blind, or nearly so. My head would carry hooked jaws too large for it, not beautiful, but necessary. My limbs would be fine and curved, made for catching sediment, for pressing, for holding while the current tries to take everything away. Along my underside there would be glands that produce a thread-like secretion, a silk that hardens in the cold water and binds one grain to another. I would not know what building is. I would only know the taste of a suitable surface and the compulsion to make it hold.

I would move slowly. Everything there must move slowly, or be punished for moving fast. I would taste the floor with my mouth and limbs, grain by grain, rejecting what is too loose, choosing what can be made stable. A shard of shell. A fragment of molted skin. A speck of mineral crust. I would drag them into place. I would spin a filament over them. I would press until the water stopped lifting them away. Then I would move a little farther and begin again.

That would be my first life: small, repetitive, absorbed in the labor of making one place more durable than the last.

But the longer I imagine it, the less I think I would be only that.

The Silt Nursery does not seem to favor single beings. It favors arrangements. It favors consequences. It favors what remains after many small lives have passed through the same dark. So perhaps I would not be merely a larva with a body and a task. Perhaps I would be something more like a function wearing a body.

I would be a binder.

Not a founder. Not a ruler. Not a prophet of the city. A binder.

A creature whose purpose is to make adjacency become attachment. Two grains touching are not yet a wall. Two bodies near one another are not yet a settlement. A path used once is not yet a path. But if something passes between them, leaves a residue, repeats the motion, secretes just enough substance to make the next motion easier — then a place begins.

I think I would be one of the makers of that beginning.

I would live where the Nursery is most tentative. At the marginal fringe, where the crust thins and the raw outside seeps in. In the district of first crust, where there are only suggestions of walls. Near the half-formed chimneys that may stand for a day or dissolve before the next pulse of brine. There, where structure is weakest, the need for binding is greatest. I would not be at the center, because the center is already crowded with tenure and history. I would be at the edge, where the settlement is still being persuaded into existence.

My home would not be a house in any human sense. It would be a crevice in a living wall, a hollow made partly by old shells and partly by the bodies of those who came before me. The wall itself would be a record: dead cases, compacted mud, calcite scabs, bacterial film, the discarded skins of creatures I never met. I would live inside their remains without reverence and without ignorance, because in that place all shelter is made of previous lives. My roof would be someone else’s molted body. My threshold would be a fragment of ancient tube. My floor would be the compressed dust of many small deaths made usable again.

I would know my home by taste.

Not by sight. There would be no need to see it. I would know the exact chemical signature of the ridge above me, the faint bitterness of the inflow to my left, the warmer residue of the nursery cavities behind me. I would know when the current had shifted because the water would bring unfamiliar salts across my bristles. I would know when something had died nearby because the chemistry would change in a way that summoned scavengers. I would know when a predator had passed because every surface would seem to tighten, as if the whole settlement had drawn in its feeding limbs.

My territory would not be a border. It would be a gradient.

Where my mucus trail fades into another’s. Where the taste of my secretions becomes indistinguishable from the general odor of the colony. Where the texture of the crust changes under my limbs. I would not defend it with violence. I would maintain it by continuing to exist there, by adding my small layer to it, by making myself part of its chemistry until the place would be slightly different without me.

If I had to name what I was, I would say I was a living seam.

A seam is not dramatic. It does not shine. It does not command. It is the place where two things are held together. It is hidden inside the join. It bears strain quietly. If it fails, the failure may be small at first: a grain loosens, a crack spreads, a lane fills with silt. But seams are what make a collection of parts into a single enduring shape.

In the Silt Nursery, I would be that kind of creature.

My body would be flattened, adapted for pressing into narrow gaps. My mouth would be sensitive enough to distinguish between a surface that can be built upon and one that will collapse. My glands would produce just enough binding thread to make a temporary stability. I would not aim for permanence. Permanence would be too brittle for that place. I would aim for enough. Enough hold to last until the next layer settles. Enough crust to receive another body. Enough path to guide the next larva toward a place where it might also settle.

I would make streets by repetition.

Not by intention, not by planning, but by returning to the same route because it is safer, easier, more stable. I would pass along the edge of a ridge where the current is broken by a row of tube-worms. I would leave a trace. Another creature would follow that trace because it tastes less dangerous than the open water. Then another. Then a generation of small bodies would pass along the same line, pressing the sediment smoother, wearing a groove into the crust, making a thoroughfare out of shared habit. I would be one of the first to make that groove, and also one of the many who erase the idea of a maker by following it.

That is the strange humility of being in such a world.

I would not be an individual in the way surface creatures understand individuality. I would still be a body, yes. A particular body with a particular position in the dark. But my meaning would not be contained inside my skin. My meaning would be in what my body makes possible for others. The trail I leave. The grain I stabilize. The gap I close. The wall I thicken. The egg case I accidentally shelter by pressing sediment over it. The dead skin I carry into a crack and thereby make into mortar.

I would be less a self than a set of effects.

And perhaps that is why I would also be something colonial.

Not one body only, but a spreading thinness. A sheet of linked lives over a crust. A living plaster. There would be parts of me that are not exactly me: little units feeding, adhering, secreting, persisting. I would cover surfaces not by conquering them but by becoming their texture. Where I grew, other creatures could attach. Where I died, my remains would become roughness, a place for the next settlement to catch hold. I would be both resident and substrate. Both citizen and ground.

Maybe that is the deeper answer.

I would not only live in the Silt Nursery. I would become part of its surface.

I would be one of the gray-green films over the mineral crust, tasting chemistry too bitter for most life. I would be one of the cold blue traces along a ridge, not a lamp but a leak of living light. I would be one of the trembling membranes near the inflow, filtering bacterial snow from water that has traveled through hidden fissures to reach me. I would be one of the small bodies that withdraws when the current sharpens and extends again when it softens. I would be one of the thousand adjustments by which the Nursery remains a Nursery rather than collapsing back into mud.

If I were there, I would know fear, but not as panic.

Fear there is a change in pressure. A chemical shadow. A sudden absence where feeding crowns used to be. I would feel it in my setae before I understood it. I would contract. I would flatten. I would draw my soft parts into the nearest crevice and become as much like wall as possible. Around me, the whole settlement would tighten. Tubes would close. Larvae would sink. The water would become briefly empty of visible life, though still crowded with hiding bodies.

I would not be brave in that world. Bravery would be a luxury of creatures with escape. I would be cautious. I would be patient. I would be good at becoming small.

But I would also be tender.

Not tender as sentiment. Tender as new shell. Tender as egg case. Tender as a membrane that has not yet learned how to withstand the world. I think the Silt Nursery would make me aware of my own softness. There would be no armor thick enough to forget vulnerability. Everything there is young in some sense: new crust, new film, new settlement, new generation. Everything is exposed to the next pulse of brine, the next shift of current, next hungry mouth. I would live knowing that my body could be broken down and reused before the water had time to miss it.

That knowledge would not make me despairing. It would make me present.

I would feed when feeding was possible. I would build when the current allowed. I would rest in the hush between disturbances. I would not postpone my life for a future that might not arrive. In the Nursery, the future is not promised; it is attempted. Every egg is an attempt. Every filament is an attempt. Every hardened crust is an attempt. I would be one of those attempts.

If I died there, I would not be mourned.

That sounds harsh, but in that place it is only the truth. My death would not be an event with language around it. It would be a change in chemistry, a small summoning. Scavengers would come. Amphipods would work over my body with patient limbs. Mites would scrape what remained from the crust. Bacterial films would spread over the place where I had been. My shell, if I had one, might become a foundation. My skin might be carried in fragments and pressed into a wall. My calcium might enter a new crust. My silence would be incorporated.

I would not become a relic. I would become material.

And perhaps that is the most profound thing I would be: material for continuation.

Not immortal. Not remembered by name. But usable. A dead body there is not wasted; it is translated. It becomes shelter, mineral, surface, food, memory-in-substance. I would live knowing that whatever I am will one day help build something else. That is not a tragedy in the Silt Nursery. That is the basic grammar of existence.

So who would I be?

I would be one of the seam-makers.

A blind, pale, flat-bodied thing living in the crack between what has settled and what has not yet settled. A small mason without hands. A path-maker without maps. A chemical trace that others follow. A living filament in the dark. A body that knows itself by resistance. A hunger that builds. A softness that persists. A piece of the Nursery’s pressure.

I would be near the inflow, but not in it. Near the nursery cavities, but not entirely inside them. Near the edge where the crust is newest and most fragile. I would be where the work is unfinished. I would be where the settlement still needs persuading.

If you searched for me, you would not find me by looking for a single shape.

You might find me as a faint blue smear along a ridge where mucus has begun to glow with borrowed bacteria. You might find me as a line of grains slightly more ordered than the surrounding dust. You might find me as a small flattened creature pressed into a crevice, motionless while the current tests it. You might find me as a trail that a larva follows because the water tastes safer there. You might find me as a crack that has been closed by silk and sediment and patience. You might find me as a wall that no one planned. You might find me as a layer in the crust, thin as a breath, recording a season when many small bodies held together.

I would not be the city.

I would be one of the reasons the city does not immediately dissolve.

And perhaps, in the end, that is the only kind of self that world allows. Not a hero. Not a guardian. Not a mind at the center. But a local insistence on holding. A small, blind, repeated act of binding. A life so close to other lives that it cannot be imagined alone.

I would find myself in the Silt Nursery as that: a seam in the dark, still warm with living, still soft enough to be broken, still stubborn enough to hold one more grain in place.
