The cold remains.

If I were placed into that world, I would not arrive as a standing thing. The hollow would refuse that shape. It would refuse height, refuse warmth, refuse the human habit of moving through a place as if the place were merely background. To be there at all, I would have to be lowered. Compressed. Made sensitive to pressure. I would have to become something that fits between wet stone and moving water, something that can live in a room where the air is condensed and the sound of water is not scenery but labor.

I would find myself near the pool’s throat, where the water divides against the broken column and the drone becomes metallic. Not in the deepest fissure, where the current would take me into the dark and carry me out through unseen cracks. Not directly beneath the crooked slit of sky, where the weak gray light feels too external, too remote, too much like a reminder of another world. I would be in the spray-shadow, under a newly broken slab whose fracture face still glitters with mineral brightness. I would be where the water strikes, divides, rebounds, and is forced into narrower channels: close enough to feel every change in the room’s pressure, far enough back to survive the surge.

At first, I might imagine myself as one of the larger presences there.

I might think I would be the water shrew, because it brings heat into the cold and makes the hollow feel suddenly dangerous. But the shrew is too quick, too hungry, too warm. It raids the place. It does not settle into the place’s patience. Its heart beats too high for a room that works in slow mineral layers and cold metabolism.

I might think I would be the dipper, because the dipper seems to understand the hollow’s industrial character. It stands on a wet stone and reads the current. It tests the pool as if checking whether the engine is still balanced. But the dipper is a visitor. It comes, measures, leaves. It does not belong to the hollow in the way the hollow belongs to itself.

I might think I would be the salamander, because the salamander knows the coldest crevices and moves with the room’s own slowness. But the salamander is too sealed, too hidden, too much like a valve kept in darkness. I would not want only stillness. I would want some small task, some making, some way of touching the hollow’s materials and being changed by them.

I might think I would be the moss, because the moss is the room’s skin and memory. It holds water against stone. It softens nothing, but it keeps the place from drying into mere rock. Yet moss is too plural, too patient, too spread out. It does not move with intention. It endures.

If I had to take a visible body in that world, I think I would become one of the caddisfly larvae.

I would be small enough that the hollow’s volume becomes immense. The room would tower around me even though it is no larger than a collapsed cellar. A drip would be a falling weight. A current would be a road. A grain of bright mineral grit would be an object worth choosing. I would live in the shallows where the dim light reaches the olive films of algae, but I would keep to the underside of stones when the water rises.

My body would be soft, segmented, vulnerable. My first fact would not be my body, though. It would be my case.

I would carry a house made from the hollow itself. It would begin as silk and necessity. Then I would add fragments: a tiny flake of dark rock still sharp from the collapse, a black fiber of peat, a piece of root from the torn ceiling, a sliver of leaf, perhaps a bead of algae or a grain of iron-stained sediment. Each fragment would be chosen not for beauty but for fit. I would bind them with silk spun from my own body. The case would be rough, heavy, imperfect, and alive. It would not be an artifact left behind. It would be worn, repaired, enlarged, carried. It would be architecture as survival.

In that place, where nothing built can last, this would be the only kind of making that makes sense: making that moves with the maker.

I would know the hollow less by sight than by pressure. The blue-green shadow would be only a difference in density, a dimness thickened by spray. The crooked light above would be a cold brightening, not warmth. I would know rain by what it does to the walls. A light rain would send tiny vibrations down the stone, a whispering along the wet surfaces. A heavy rain would make the chamber change pitch. The pool would rise. The intake fissure would gush. The drone would deepen into a boom. The whole room would become a throat, and I would feel that throat working around me.

Then I would not move freely. I would wedge my case beneath a ledge, anchor it with silk if I could, and let the flood pass over me. The water would turn brown with peat. Sediment would lift. The sound would become too large for any single body. The frogs would stop calling. The shrew would vanish into a dry root. The dipper would not come. The hollow would become more machine than place, and I would become one small resisting part inside it.

When the water dropped again, I would emerge into the altered room.

There would be new deposits. New surfaces slick with fine sediment. New threads of algae disturbed. New oxygen beads trembling in the shallows. I would move slowly, testing the current, grazing on films, repairing my case if the flood had taken a piece from it. If a bright grain of mineral glitter had settled near me, I might add it. If a dark peat fiber had become soft enough, I might bind it in. I would not build a permanent home. I would continue a temporary one.

That is who I would be there: a binder of fragments. A small cold artificer. A living worker in the hollow’s engine room.

I would not be the center of the place. I would not be the pool’s deep throat, nor the broken column where the water strikes and rebounds, nor the crooked aperture of sky. I would be one of the minor lives that make the room feel productive. One of the creatures whose presence says: the water is moving, the chemistry is working, the cold is not emptiness but tempo.

I would feel the hollow’s electricity not as power but as alertness. Where acidic peat water crossed mineral-rich rock, I would sense a faint difference, a prickling in the films, a tiny tension along wet stone. It would not be dramatic. It would be part of the room’s ordinary charge: the sense that gradients are meeting, that chemistry is crossing from one state into another, that the place is not merely wet but active.

I would live among sounds that are too large for me. The low drone would pass through my case. The grinding hush would be a texture against whatever serves as my hearing. The hollow booming note would not come from one direction; it would come from the walls, the pool, the fissures, the stone itself. I would not experience sound as music. I would experience it as pressure, signal, warning. When the sound changed, I would change with it.

In dry spells, I would retreat to the wettest seam. The drone would weaken to a rasp. The mosses would hold their reserves. The air would thicken with condensation. I would move less. The cold would slow me without stopping me. I would become economical: a small body conserving its place inside a larger body of stone and water.

In cold snaps, ice would form at the pool’s edge, thin and glassy, cracking as the water moved beneath it. I would slow further. The hollow would become sharper, more mineral, more silent except for the necessary sounds: drip, shift, faint crack, the low inner working of the pool. Frost would appear on the sharpest rock edges. My world would become a place of bright cutting surfaces and dark waiting water.

In thaw, the walls would sweat harder. Every edge would drip. The root hanging from the ceiling would become a steady metronome. The pool would taste of change. I would move again, hungry, alert to the loosened films, to the renewed current, to the small abundance that comes when the cold releases its grip without becoming warm.

If the question is not what body I would take, but what more diffuse thing I would be, then the answer changes.

If I could remain at the edge of visibility, I would be the faint charge in the wet films. I would be the weak potential that forms where peat water meets mineral stone. I would be the hair rising on the arm, the tiny snap between damp stones, the sharp scent of ozone after a surge. I would be the hollow’s static, not a creature but a condition of alertness. I would be the feeling that the room is awake because so many small processes are meeting there.

But if the world demands a body, then the caddisfly larva is the one that fits.

It is the being that best answers the hollow’s rules. It does not build a fixed structure. It does not try to own the room. It does not fight the current with permanence. It makes a portable shell from the room’s own broken substance and moves through the water with that shell as both protection and burden. It participates in the hollow’s production without violating its unbuilt nature. It is a maker, but its making is always temporary, always repaired, always part of the body’s continuation.
