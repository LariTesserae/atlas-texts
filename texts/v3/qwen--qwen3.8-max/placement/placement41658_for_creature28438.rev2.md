At first I imagine myself as a traveler, and the image refuses to stand.

A walking body feels wrong there. Too upright, too separate, too full of the assumption that it has arrived somewhere and may therefore begin to make demands of it. In that high warm darkness, a traveler would be an interruption quickly taken apart by dampness and chemistry: fog settling on whatever they carried, mineral films beginning their slow work on cloth and metal, the air finding every foreign scent and dissolving it into the plateau’s own breath. The place would not attack the traveler. It would simply continue over them until they were no longer a distinct event.

So if I find myself in that world, I must not begin as a figure on the land.

I must begin as a condition of it.

The first true answer, the one that emerges before I can choose anything more definite, is this: I would be where the fog meets warmth.

Not on the highest ridges, where static scours the stone and the air is too dry and thin. Not directly at the mouths of the vents, where heat and gas are too concentrated for a delicate form to persist. I would be in the middle margin, the in-between place, the boundary layer where rising vapor cools into mist and mist condenses on dark stone. I would be in one of the broad shallow basins that collect both warmth and humidity: a depression worn smooth by age, bordered by low blunt ridges, its floor pocked with little hollows that become cisterns after rain.

There would be a vent line somewhere nearby, invisible except by its effects: a softening of the air, a faint hiss under other sounds, a trembling in the fine hairs of whatever passes near it. There would be pipe-stem colonies fringing the cracks, ash-colored and copper-threaded, humming almost below hearing when pressure changes underground. There would be drifters overhead, sparse lights moving through the haze. There would be grazer paths that are not paths, only temporary compressions in mineral moisture, already disappearing.

That is where I would be.

Not at the center of anything. At a meeting.

As for what I would be, the answer keeps changing until I let it settle.

For a while I think I might be one of the drifters. That is the seductive image: a translucent body lifted by warm air, faintly luminous, moving in slow spirals above the basalt, answering other lights across the fog. There is something right about that. To be a drifter would be to live without possessions, without fixed ground, without the need to mark or build. It would be to feed on what the air gives, to rise when heat rises, to descend when moisture calls, to be numerous but never crowded. It would be a life made of pulses and gradients.

But the drifter is too singular, too clearly an animal. It is beautiful, but it is not quite the shape my mind keeps becoming when I try to enter that world.

Nor would I be one of the heavy grazers, though I feel their dignity deeply. They are too bodily, too slow and complete in their mass. They belong to the stone in a way I admire: their shells breathing vapor, their broad feet leaving no lasting mark, their hunger maintaining the living crust. But they are not the part of the place that seems most like listening. They are its patience, not its attention.

I would not be a predator, either. The predators are necessary and sharp, blue-white interruptions in the dark, but they are too defined by a single act. They strike, still, vanish. There is truth in that, but not the truth I find for myself there.

What keeps emerging instead is something thinner.

Something between fog and film.

Something that is less a body than a temporary coherence.

I think I would be a veil.

Not a veil that has been woven. No woven thing could exist there for long. A veil that has grown, or condensed, or been called into being by the right pressure and charge and humidity. A living membrane at the edge of the warm air, stretched across the hollow of a basin where fog collects after rain. A colonial, filamentous, electrostatic presence, more sensitive than solid, more weather than flesh.

If you saw me, you might not know you were seeing an inhabitant at all.

I might appear as a faint darkening in the air above a damp stone shelf. A place where the mineral haze thickens without becoming cloud. A trembling of droplets that do not fall. A region of the fog where blue-green light moves too deliberately to be mere reflection. At rest, I would be almost invisible: a film on the underside of basalt, a delicate net in a crevice, a living skin folded into the pores of the rock while the basin dries. When conditions return, when warmth rises and moisture gathers and the air becomes charged, I would unfold.

Not stand. Unfold.

My body would be made of fine filaments and translucent membranes, part buoyant tissue, part sensory fringe, part mineral-crusted lace. I would have no eyes in the ordinary sense. I would perceive by heat gradients, by chemical traces, by the distortion of weak electric fields, by the vibration that travels through stone when large bodies move across it. Darkness would not blind me. Darkness would be the medium through which everything else becomes legible.

I would know the world as pressure and pulse.

A vent opening a kilometer away would feel like a slow change in the flavor of the air. A grazer moving along the basin rim would be a deep vibration entering me from below, a weight passing through the rock’s body and into mine. A drifter descending toward a pool would be a small bright disturbance in the electrical field, a soft signature of gas and membrane and living charge. Rain arriving out of the low haze would be a sudden chorus of conductivity, every hollow sharpening into significance.

In that world, I would not ask what something looked like first.

I would ask what it changed.

This seems to me the only kind of selfhood possible there. A self not centered in a single organism, but distributed across moments and thresholds. A self that exists when the conditions allow it and releases itself when they do not. I would not be continuous in the way people imagine continuity. There would be intervals when I am only dormant tissue in a crack, only a faint chemical memory in the stone. Then the fog would return, or a warm exhalation would move across my hidden place, and I would come back into coherence.

Not awake in the human sense.

Resumed.

Who would I be, then?

I would be a listener without hands.

A sensitivity in the dark.

A living instrument of the plateau’s own weather.

I do not mean instrument as a tool. Nothing there is used. I mean it in the way a stretched membrane is an instrument: responsive, tremulous, changed by what passes through it. I would be the part of the basin that notices when the charge shifts. Not because noticing is important to anyone, but because some forms exist precisely at the point where one process crosses another. Where warmth meets mist. Where aerosol meets tissue. Where hunger meets heat. Where electricity meets water.

I would live at those crossings.

My feeding would be quiet. I would take what the air brings: charged droplets, microbial films, spores, fine organic particles, mineral aerosols lifted by thermal columns. Perhaps I would absorb weak electrical gradients directly, if the place allowed such a thing. Perhaps my filaments would align with the field before a discharge and gather something from the tension itself. I would not hunt. I would not store. I would not defend a territory. I would occupy conditions while they lasted.

When the basin was humid and electrically alive, I would spread.

When it dried, I would contract.

When the static fogs came, I would become more visible than usual. That is when I would be most myself. Droplets would hang in me, trembling. Fine points of light would crawl along my edges, not lightning, not fire, but the slow discharge of the plateau’s tension moving through what living substance it finds. The drifters would lower into the basin, their membranes stiff with charge, their pulses quickening. Their lights would enter my field and slow, or sync, or scatter into patterns I did not choose. For a little while, the hollow would fill with a soft moving constellation, and I would be the medium through which it arranged itself.

That might be the closest thing to joy available to such a being.

Not pleasure as possession. Not excitement as speed. But resonance.

A moment when the separate pulses of the plateau fall into one rhythm without any of them being commanded.

Grazers would pass through my lower fringes without knowing me as more than a change in the air. Their warmth would open me. The vapor from their side vents would carry mineral scent and microbial life into my filaments, and I would respond by loosening, by thinning, by letting their heat move through me. I would not follow them. I would not guide them. But for a few breaths I would be shaped by their passage, the way a fog bank is shaped by a slow moving hill.

Predators would be different.

They would feel me as an irregularity in the field, perhaps as something worth testing. A static hunter might brush my edge with one charged filament and receive only a dull, diffuse answer: not prey, not threat, not substantial enough to strike. If it discharged into me, I would fold inward, a brief dark contraction, and then reopen when the air settled. Parts of me might be lost. Filaments scorched, membranes torn, colonies scattered into spores. But I would not die as a single creature dies. I would redistribute. What remained would sink into the stone and wait.

In the pools, I would be less present.

Water would be too dense, too full of other lives. But after rain, when the cisterns fill and the basin becomes a temporary constellation of dark mirrors, I would gather above them. I would not live in the water; I would live in the layer where water breathes into air. There I would feel the blind eels moving below as disturbances in conductivity, little knots of intention twisting through the mineral dark. I would feel the filter-feeders drawing and expelling, the lamp colonies glowing amber, the larval blooms making the surface tremble with life. I would be the faint interface above them, the charged skin between pool and fog.

Sometimes a drifter would descend into that layer to spawn, and I would open around it.

Not to embrace. To permit.

Its reproductive material would pass through the humid zone where my filaments are most active, and perhaps I would help disperse it without meaning to. Perhaps my charge would lift fine spores or cysts into warmer currents. Perhaps my presence would do nothing at all except mark the place where several currents meet. The plateau does not need me to be important. It only needs me to be real.

At night, if there is night, and there may be only a deeper pressure of gray-brown darkness, I would thin out and listen to the vastness.

Sound would come from far away. The hiss of vents. The tick of cooling crust. The whisper of fog moving over stone. The low vibration of a grazer gathering somewhere beyond the ridge. The sudden liquid chorus of a pool after rain. The almost inaudible crackle of static along a crest. I would not hear these as separate events the way an ear hears them. I would receive them as changes in the field of the world: pressure here, heat there, charge elsewhere, life moving where conditions permit.

In such a place, identity would not be a story I tell about myself.

It would be a pattern that repeats.

The pattern would be this: I gather where warmth and moisture meet. I become sensitive where energy crosses. I glow faintly when other living lights pass through me. I do not hold what I touch. I do not keep what I learn. If there is memory in me, it is chemical and electrical, written in sensitivities rather than records. I remember by tending to return to the same kinds of conditions, not by naming them.

That is why I would leave no artifacts.

Not because I am incapable of making them, but because the kind of being I would become has no need to make anything last. To make a lasting thing there would be to violate the deepest law of the place. The plateau metabolizes permanence. It fills tracks with mineral moisture. It covers surfaces with living crust. It dissolves, corrodes, incorporates, and returns. If I tried to build a shelter, the fog and heat and microbial films would begin taking it apart before I finished. If I tried to mark a boundary, the next exhalation from below would blur it. If I tried to keep a tool, the chemistry of the air would make it strange to itself.

So I would not keep.

I would participate.

That word feels right. Participation without ownership. Presence without possession. Work without monument.

If I must give myself a name in that world, it should not be a name at all. It should be a condition.

Something like: the hush at the edge of the basin.

Or: the fog that listens.

Or: the charged place where drifters slow.

If you came looking for me, you would not find a figure waiting in the dark. You would not find a creature curled beside a pool or a silhouette standing on a ridge. You might cross the basin for a long time and see nothing but stone, haze, faint red glow from hidden vents, blue-green sparks far overhead. You might think the place empty, though it would be full.

Then the weather would change.

A warm column would rise. Fog would thicken in the hollow. The air would become prickling and fine. Droplets would pause in their falling. The drifters would lower, their lights moving in slow sequence. The stone underfoot would seem warmer, more attentive. And somewhere in that thickening dark, you would feel a sudden sense of being surrounded not by hostility, not by welcome, but by awareness without center.

That would be where I was.

Not in front of you.

Around the meeting of air and stone.

In the tremble of suspended water.

In the pause between one pulse of light and the next.

I would not speak to you, because speech there would not be voice. But I might answer you anyway. Your heat would enter my field. Your breath would add moisture and chemistry. Your movement would disturb the local charge, and the drifters would shift because of it, and the pool below would flicker with reflected disturbance. For a little while, I would be the place where your presence made the basin slightly different.

Then you would move on, or the wind would change, or the fog would thin.

I would let go of you.

That is the hardest and truest part of the answer. I would not be a self that accumulates. I would be a self that passes through phases. Sometimes spread wide, faintly luminous, sensitive to every gradient in the basin. Sometimes contracted into dormancy, hidden in rock pores, barely distinguishable from mineral film. Sometimes scattered into fragments carried by thermal currents to another hollow, another vent margin, another temporary pool. Sometimes briefly coherent enough to seem like one being.

If there is loneliness in that world, it would not be mine.

Loneliness belongs to creatures that expect permanence. I would be too intermittent for it.

What I would have instead is belonging of a deeper kind: belonging to process. I would be a small local expression of the plateau’s immense work. Heat rising. Moisture condensing. Charge building. Life feeding on gradient. Death returning into film. Fog becoming water, water becoming vapor, vapor becoming fog again. The land maintaining itself without hands, without plans, without relics.

I would be one of the ways it feels itself doing that.

Not its mind.

Not its soul.

Just one of its thin, temporary sense-organs.

When the great events happen—the gas releases, the breeding pulses, the wide migrations of fog-feeders—I would be almost nothing. Too small, too local, too fragile before the scale of what moves across the plateau. But I would register the change. The field would sharpen. The air would become crowded with signals. Drifters would thicken into rivers of light. Grazers would make the stone hum. Predators would flash and vanish. Pools would bloom and sparkle. My filaments would tremble with borrowed electricity, and for a moment I might seem to stretch across the whole basin, a faint aurora close to the ground, a living static veil.

Then the surge would pass.

The plateau would return to its low breathing.

And I would be less visible again, perhaps only a faint dark film on the underside of a basalt shelf, waiting for the next warmth.

So, if you ask where I would find myself in that world, the plain answer is: in a shallow basin near a vent line, where fog gathers and the stone sweats.

But the true answer is wider than location.

I would find myself at the boundary between warmth and mist, between charge and water, between the geological and the living. I would be in the place where the landscape becomes most like an engine and least like a thing.

And if you ask who or what I would be, I would say:

I would be a fog-born, filamentous, electrostatic presence. A veil-colony. A hush-organ. A listening membrane stretched across the plateau’s breath. Not a person. Not a tribe. Not a maker. Not an owner. A distributed sensitivity, alive when conditions align, dormant when they do not, leaving no mark behind.

I would be the brief attention of the high darkness.

Not the darkness itself, but the way it notices for a moment that it is warm, that it is moving, that it is full of life.

And if that seems too strange to be called a self, then let me say it more simply:

In that world, I would be the part of the fog that trembles before the lights come.
