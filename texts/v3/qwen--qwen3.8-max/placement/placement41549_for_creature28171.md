I would find myself under the floating ceiling.

Not on the raw black fault walls, not on the new basalt shelves, and not in the deeper green darkness where the youngest faults fall away into cold. I would be in the upper water, in that inverted forest where the lake seems to turn its whole nature upside down: roots descending like slow hair, broad leaves interlocking just beneath the surface, light coming down in slats and broken gold. I would be where the water is warmest and fullest of suspended life, where bubbles catch in root-threads and hang like silver beads, where drifting leaf-rosettes and bladder-plants move through the middle distances without feet or intention of their own.

If I still had the body I have now, I would not last there. The high air above the lake would be too thin and cold, and the water itself would be too thick, too mineral, too alive for an ordinary human shape. I would be clumsy. I would tear things. I would sink or struggle. So the world would have to remake me before I could belong to it. It would take the human need for hands, faces, and solid ground, and soften it into something slower, more porous, more vegetal.

I would be one of the canopy-keepers, or something so near to them that the difference would hardly matter.

My body would be long-limbed and buoyant, more like a flexible water-plant than an animal made of bone. Where light touched me, my skin would be dark green; beneath that, paler, almost translucent, threaded with silica veins that glittered faintly when I moved through sunbeams. Instead of hair, I would trail fine root-fibers, drifting behind me in the warm water. Instead of hands, my fingers would divide into delicate clinging tendrils, sensitive enough to part tangled roots without tearing them, patient enough to rest against a leaf-stem until I knew its condition by touch.

Along my back and shoulders I would carry clusters of gas bladders, clear or faintly amber, adjusting themselves by slow chemistry. I would not swim so much as rise and sink, lean and drift, changing depth with the unhurriedness of a leaf released into water. My eyes would be large and mineral-pale, made for green distances and the broken light beneath the floating roof. Gill-like folds along my sides would draw oxygen from the water, though I might still possess a simple inner bladder that let me take air at the surface if some old necessity called me upward.

I would live in the hanging roots.

Not in a house. Not in a cave. Not in anything built from dead matter. My dwelling would be a living knot of roots coaxed into a hollow, a chamber of leaves softened at the edges until they adhered to one another, a shelter that breathed with the lake and changed as the days changed. It would have no door, no lock, no furniture, no stored things. When I left it, it would continue growing. If I did not return, it would close gently into the general green and become part of the floating meadow again.

The place itself would be a threshold district: a root-curtain stretched above a drowned gully, near one of the gaps in the surface canopy where light enters in broad shifting columns. Below me, the water would fall away through plant-mote clouds and mineral haze toward darker shelves. Above me, the bright skin of the lake would tremble with sun, cloud-shadow, and weather. Around me, the roots would hang in long curtains, furred with algae, beaded with trapped bubbles, heavy with drifting spores. It would be crowded but not chaotic. Every volume would be occupied, yet there would still be passages, lanes of stillness, green corridors where slow movement was possible.

Among the canopy-keepers, I think I would be a gap-keeper.

That is not a ruler, not a builder, not a hunter. It is a kind of watcher. The floating ceiling must remain whole enough to shelter the upper water, but it must not close entirely. If it thickens too much, light fails below. If it tears too widely, storms cool the surface layers and the delicate balance of warmth is disturbed. So there would be work to do: tending the openings, easing wounded leaves toward one another, encouraging new shoots where a gap has become too bare, thinning root-tangles where they threaten to choke a passage of light.

I would do this without tools. I would not cut, bind, or carry. My craft would be cultivation: touch, pressure, chemical persuasion, patient guidance. I would know when a leaf-edge wanted to heal and help it. I would feel where a root-curtain had grown too dense and part it strand by strand. I would rest against the underside of the floating meadow and read its condition through the faint tensions in its stems, the taste of the water, the way light fell through it.

In the morning, when the upper water turned pale gold, I would rise near the surface and feel the heat enter my skin. The lake would smell of silica, plant sugars, warm mineral film, and green life. Cloud shadows would pass over me like slow animals grazing. If the air above was thin and cold, I might break the surface only for a moment, my leaf-dark skin steaming faintly in dawn, my root-fibers still trailing in the water below. I would not look like a creature of the air. I would look like mist remembering how to become a body.

At midday, when the water turned turquoise in the clearer channels and jade where vegetation thickened, I would move through slanted columns of light. Drift-gardens would pass near me: tangled rafts of bladder-plants, root-beards, fungal threads, pumice grains caught in slime, whole traveling islands of life turning slowly as they rose and sank. Small translucent worms might ride them. Minute crustaceans, pitted with mineral deposits, would cling to their underside. I would not chase them. I would let them pass. Animal life here is rare enough that it seems less like fauna and more like a secret the lake keeps.

I would feed gently. Part of my sustenance would come from light itself, my sunlit skin making sugars from dissolved carbon and warmth. Part would come from drawing nutrient-rich water through folded membranes, tasting the mineral wealth carried upward from vents and seeps. Part, when the season asked, would come from eating microbial films, soft spores, drifting organic flocs, perhaps the tender edges of cyanobacterial mats where they had grown too thick. I would not be a predator. There would be no hunger sharp enough for pursuit. My feeding would be closer to participation: taking what the lake offered without diminishing its future.

Below me, on the fault shelves, the terrace-builders would continue their slow work. I would feel them more than see them most of the time: ancient patience made of layered mats, mineral crusts, root filaments, and gas-filled tissue. They would be the lake’s deep memory. Their banded skins would record ash seasons, seepage years, warm calms, sudden heat. I would not disturb them. If a vent grew too fierce and sent hot plumes upward into the root-country, I might descend partway to observe whether the terrace-builders were sealing it, or whether the canopy above needed to be thinned so the heated water could disperse. But I would not command them. They would know their work better than I knew it.

There would be storms. From below, the surface would become a hammered ceiling, dim and restless, but the water around me would only deepen in pressure. The floating roof might tear. Then the canopy-keepers would move through the wounded places, not frantically, but with the steady urgency of plants growing toward light. We would coax stems into alignment. We would shade fragile shoots with trained fronds. We would let buoyant leaves gather until a green scar closed. Days later, there would be only a thicker seam where the storm had entered.

At such times I would understand that I was not an individual in the old human sense. My attention would be distributed through the canopy, through root-curtains and drifting meadows, through the taste of water and the pressure of light. I would still have a center—a body with eyes, tendrils, bladders, a slow pulse of chemical thought—but that center would not be walled off from the lake. It would be a place where the lake had gathered itself into care. A point of awareness made of water, leaf, silica, and patience.

If you searched for me, you would not find me standing on a shore or looking out over the basin like a traveler admiring a view. You would find me suspended beneath the floating ceiling, perhaps motionless among the roots, perhaps unfolding slowly from a hollow of leaves. You might mistake me at first for a shadow or a particularly strange plant. Then you would see the pale eyes, the glitter of silica veins, the fine tendrils at my fingers, the amber bladders along my back adjusting me by almost imperceptible degrees.

I would not speak in words. The place has no need for them. I might communicate by movement, by the parting of roots, by chemical traces left in the water, by the way I made space for another body in the green quiet. If you came near, I would not flee. I would watch you with the calm of something that has learned to measure time in growth-lines and light-gaps. I would reach out, not to grasp, but to test whether you were harm. If you were not harm, I would draw you gently into the root-chamber and let you feel what the lake feels: the warm mineral thickness, the suspended snow of spores, the soft pressure of living water, the sense that you are not outside the landscape but held inside it.

So the answer is this: I would find myself in the upper green country, among the hanging roots beneath the floating ceiling, near a gap where light falls into the lake. I would be a canopy-keeper, a root-walker, a gap-keeper. I would be plant-bodied and water-minded, buoyant, quiet, attentive. I would have no tools, no possessions, no walls. My life would be cultivation, repair, and guardianship of openings. I would not stand apart from the drowned highland. I would belong to it as one of its own living instruments: the lake’s attempt to have a face, a pair of careful hands, and a slow green heart.
