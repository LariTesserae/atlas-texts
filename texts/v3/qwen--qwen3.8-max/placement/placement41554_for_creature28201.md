If the Condensery were to find a place for me, it would not be at the center of the main chamber, beside the low circular basin, where all the gutters meet. That place would feel too exposed, too much like a mouth speaking openly. Instead, I would find myself in one of the side galleries just above the chamber floor: a narrow, low-ceilinged passage where the tile vault is close enough to make a person bow without thinking, and where the air is a little cooler because it moves toward an unseen drain.

There would be a copper elbow there, sweating in a rhythm I would come to know better than any calendar. Beneath it would sit an enamel pan worn white at the rim, and beside the pan a shelf of jars marked with knots and colored threads. I would be near that seam where warmth rises from the machines below and moisture gathers on the older, colder metal above. I would be near the place where the Condensery does one of its smallest, most persistent acts of weather.

I would be one of the Dripwise.

Not a ruler, not a prophet, not an explorer returned with maps of impossible depths. I would be a custodian of small waters. A listener. A keeper of pans and cloths, a sorter of tastes, a watcher of slow changes. If the others gave me a task-name, it would be something quiet: Seam-Listener, maybe, or Wringe-Tender, or the One Who Marks the Green Bloom. I would be the sort of person who notices when a drip has altered its mineral character, when a valve that has resisted for years suddenly becomes merely reluctant, when a patch of black algae thickens a little too quickly in one corner and means the humidity has shifted by a fraction no instrument would bother to record.

My body would be changed by the place, or perhaps it would have always been suited to it. I would go barefoot or in soft-soled shoes, because the floor is a language of temperature and texture. My feet would know the ribbed plates, the treacherous smoothness, the loose grate, the raised lip of the drain cover, the brick step hollowed by generations of careful descent. The soles of my feet would be stained with rust and mineral film. My hands would be quick and quiet: fingertips often wrinkled from damp, palms creased with faint discoloration—iron-brown, copper-green, lime-white. My eyes would be wide and slow-blinking, adjusted not to true light but to sheen: the glimmer of wet metal, the ghost of a gauge face, the deeper black of an open hatch.

I would not be heroic. That is the important part. The Condensery would not make me a conqueror of its darkness. It would make me a participant in its balance.

I would live in an alcove behind a dead boiler whose interior has been lined with absorbent mats and folded rags. A hammock would be strung between two condenser legs nearby, not because it is beautiful, but because the legs are warm and stable and have stood long enough to become furniture. A coat would hang from a valve handle I know never to turn fully. A small bowl of black algae would sit on a machine housing near my sleeping niche, not as ornament but as an indicator. If the bowl dried too fast, I would know the air had changed. If it grew too thick, I would know another season of the Condensery had begun.

My possessions would be few, but each would be heavy with use.

I would keep enamel basins cleaned to a dull shine, because mineral crusts must not choke the flow. I would keep jars with narrow necks for trusted drips and wide-mouthed jars for water still being tested. I would keep cords with knots: red for danger, blue for lamp-safe, green for algae trays, plain for waiting. I would keep a hooded lamp, carefully trimmed, never burning too hot. I would keep a wooden spoon, a scraping tool, several folded rags, a coil of lead wire, and a small brass bell that I would never ring because sound in the Condensery is too easily a wound.

Most of all, I would keep attention.

That would be my true substance there: attention, stretched thin and made patient.

I would wake in intervals, because the Condensery does not have day and night in the ordinary sense. It has heat-building, vapor-gathering, cooling, releasing, settling. Sometimes I would wake because the pulse below the floor had changed by a breath. Sometimes because a pipe had ticked in a new place. Sometimes because the air felt fuller against my skin, meaning rain was near.

When the room began to rain from itself, I would rise without hurry.

First the thickening air. Then the faint ticking on metal. Then the whisper on cloth. I would move along the gallery with one hand trailing on a warm rail, feeling the vibration of the place rather than seeing it. I would check the tarps, adjust the pans, touch the hanging strips of cloth to see how heavily they had drunk. I would wring one over a jar at the proper moment, not too soon, not too late, because water gathered too early may taste of old dust, and water gathered too late may have taken too much from the metal. I would listen to the different impacts: the bright tick on enamel, the softer pat on cloth, the hollow knock on a tank housing, the almost silent kiss of a drop falling into algae.

I would know which drips to collect and which to leave.

The first few drops beneath an unfamiliar leak would be left for mineral growths or insects, because the first water tastes strongest of metal and may carry what has been sleeping in the pipe. I would not force a valve that resisted. I would listen before opening anything. If a valve refused me gently, I would take that as information, not defeat. Resistance would mean the valve belonged to a longer cycle than my own impatience.

I would sort water the way others sort coins or herbs.

Sweet and flat water, gathered from a trusted ceramic channel, would be kept for drinking in small amounts. Bitter copper water would be kept for cleaning certain fittings. Water that left a white crust would be marked and set aside, away from delicate mechanisms. Sleeping water—water gathered where the air had been still for too long—would be handled with particular care. I would not drink it unless there was no other choice. I would say, as the Dripwise say, that it remembers too much.

I would be especially watchful of green bloom.

A greenish crust around a copper-rich leak would not alarm me by itself. It would be ordinary, even useful. But a green bloom where none had been before would make me pause. I would touch a fingertip to it, look at the residue, then trace the drip upward with my eyes until the darkness swallowed the line. I would mark the place with a knot of cord and wait. If the bloom spread, I would move the pan beneath it. If the water changed, I would warn the others with a low phrase and a hand sign: two fingers against the thumb, then a slow opening, meaning *new taste, unknown source, do not trust yet*.

I would not be alone, but I would often be solitary.

The others would pass near me in the dark: a Wringer checking wall-cloths, a Pankeeper carrying a basin against the hip, a Lampkeeper moving with a hooded flame that made our faces briefly amber and then gone. We would speak little. Our voices would be low, shaped to the room’s preference. Much of what we said would be touch and tap and gesture. A knuckle against a pipe: *this line is waking*. Two taps: *rain soon*. A flat hand held near a warm tank: *wait*. A thumb rubbed slowly over brass: *old grease, old seal, not yet*.

I would understand that language because my body would have learned it before my mouth did.

Sometimes I would kneel beside a shallow pan and watch the glass-fleas move through the collected drip. Their pale bodies would flick in short jerks, almost transparent, like tiny living splinters of the Condensery’s own thought. I would not find them charming in the way surface creatures are charming. I would find them necessary. If they darted actively, the water was probably safe enough to touch. If they gathered at the edge and stilled, I would move the pan and mark the leak. They would be my first informants, my small pale instruments.

I would have respect for the blind beetles, too.

In the warmer corners where fungal threads spread through old insulation, I would see them moving slowly over brick and machine housings, their shells the color of old bone. I would not crush them. I would step around them if they crossed a path. Their presence would mean decay had not turned sour. Their absence would worry me more than their abundance. If I found one dead on its back in the open, I would stop and taste the air with my attention, trying to sense what had changed before any human word for it existed.

I would be tender toward the moth-like things that gathered near pilot lights, though I would not keep them. They would live briefly, flutter uncertainly, leave powder behind, and fall into cups or channels. I would remove their bodies when I found them, not with disgust but with the quiet recognition that even the smallest death feeds the room’s long patience. Their dust would become part of the manufactured soil. Their bodies would feed mold. Nothing there would be wasted, because nothing there could afford waste.

I would not be entirely human in the way a surface person would mean it.

The Condensery would wear away whatever in me was too sharp, too hurried, too eager to name and master. I would become more like a sense organ belonging to the room itself. A living gauge. A slow animal of thresholds. My thoughts would be less like declarations and more like measurements: *this wall is hotter than yesterday; this drip has become thinner; this pan needs cleaning before the next cycle; this cloth has taken on a bitter smell; this valve is dreaming of opening but should not be helped yet.*

I would dream in condensation.

When I slept, I would dream of beads forming along a seam in the ceiling, each one swelling with unbearable slowness until it released. I would dream of channels filling and emptying, of jars labeled with knots, of water passing from metal to air to cloth to vessel to mouth to skin and back again into the room’s hidden circulation. I would dream that my body was made of many small authorities: a film on brick, a trembling drop, a pale flicker in an enamel pan, a hand moving over warm iron without needing light.

If someone asked me who I was, I would not answer with a grand title.

I would say: I am one of the Dripwise.

If they asked what that meant, I would say: I keep the balance between the next drop and the one after it.

If they asked where I could be found, I would say: in the low gallery behind the dead boiler, where the copper elbow sweats into the enamel pan and the ceiling is close enough to make you bow.

But if they asked what I truly was, I might hesitate.

Because in the Condensery, identity is not a single bright point. It is distributed. It spreads through habits, surfaces, routines, temperatures, and attentions. I would be a body, yes. A pale, damp-skinned, barefoot person with stained hands and quiet voice. But I would also be the pattern I maintained. I would be the memory of which valves could be touched and which must be left alone. I would be the knowledge of which water was safe, which was useful, which was dangerous, and which was still undecided. I would be the cloth wrung into the jar, the pan cleaned before overflow, the green bloom noticed early, the glass-flea watched without sentimentality.

I would be a small consciousness inside a larger breathing machine.

Not the master of the Condensery. Not even its priest. More like its nerve ending. Its careful attendant. Its listener at the seam.

There would be moments when I felt the whole place gather itself. The heat would rise from the lower machinery. The air would thicken. The tile vault would seem to lower by an inch, though it had not moved. The pipes overhead would cool just enough to begin releasing what they had been holding. In that moment, before the first drop fell, I would be perfectly still.

I would feel the room become weather.

Then the tiny impacts would begin. Ticking on metal. Whispering on cloth. A soft patter on tarps. I would move among the basins with my hands already knowing what to do. I would not rush. I would not speak above a murmur. I would collect, redirect, mark, wait, listen, and collect again. My face would be damp. My hair would cling to my neck. The darkness would be full of small presences, none of them needing to be seen to be understood.

In that rain made by the room itself, I would know exactly where I was.

Not above, not outside, not passing through.

Below the dry levels, beneath the sealed freight tunnels and abandoned ventilation galleries, in the warm dark where every surface is made but every made surface has learned patience, I would be kneeling beside an enamel pan, one hand resting on the warm rim, head tilted toward the sound of the next drop.

I would be a Dripwise.

I would be a Seam-Listener.

I would be a tender of slow waters.

And the Condensery, breathing around me, would know me as one of its own.
