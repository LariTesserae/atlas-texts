At first I think I would be a visitor, because that is the easiest shape for a mind like mine to imagine: a body entering from the marsh, bending under the low vault, boots sinking slightly into the packed root-floor while the air closes in, warm and already breathed. I would come down by the stair that goes up to nothing, or perhaps by some gap in the trunk-wall where the surrounding marsh presses against the basin’s rim and sends water seeping through in thin, persistent threads. I would stand there with the ordinary human feeling of having interrupted something, my heartbeat too fast, my breathing too shallow, my attention too eager.

But the basin would not let me remain a visitor for long.

That is the first thing I understand about it. It is not hostile, but it does not preserve the category of stranger. Anything that stays long enough in that wet, green, slow industrial room becomes part of its maintenance. A footprint becomes a lens of water. A boot-sole becomes a place for moss to consider. A breath becomes another bead of condensation gathering on a leaf-edge and falling, eventually, into the general rhythm. So if I were there long enough to be anywhere truly, I would not remain a person standing apart from the place, looking in. I would be assigned a role. Or, more accurately, I would grow into one.

Where would I find myself?

Not in the open, I think. Not under the plain white columns of daylight that occasionally fall through holes in the overhead tangle and make the mist look architectural. Those beams are too exposing. They reveal too much and last too little. I would be found lower than that, in the dim green middle distance, somewhere between the floor and the first heavy knots of trunk and pipe, where the air is thickest and sound is less heard than absorbed into the chest.

Probably near the drowned boiler.

Yes—the boiler whose riveted plates have been split and softened by roots, whose interior is lined with living tissue, whose cracks release faint steam with a patience that suggests centuries of careful exhalation. I would be near its flank, where warmth collects and the smell of rust becomes resinous. There would be a shallow niche there, perhaps at the height of a kneeling person’s chest: a hollow made not by carving but by growth, a burl that had folded itself around an absence. In that niche, I think, is where I would be.

At first I might look like a swelling in the wall. A knot of bark and moss. A healed wound where some old pipe once entered the plant mass and was swallowed. But if you watched long enough, you would see that the swelling has too much order. It has a circular depression at its center, almost like a socket. Around the depression, moss grows in fine concentric whorls, darker where it drinks the most condensation, paler where steam has warmed it. There would be something like a dial there, though not made of metal alone: a disc of hardened leaf-mold and bark, smoothed by water, set with a rim of copper-green tissue. And in the center, trembling with every change in pressure, a needle of pale wood.

I think I would be a gauge.

Not the kind of gauge that once belonged to a factory wall, bolted there to inform a worker of danger or efficiency. Not that. I would be a living gauge, grown in the place of an instrument that had been removed or decayed, formed by the basin’s need to continue measuring itself. My purpose would not be to tell anyone anything in particular. My purpose would be to register. To hold a tiny, exact response to the world. To tremble when the pressure changed. To become, in the slow language of that place, a point of attention.

I would not have a face, or not one that could be read easily. If I had eyes, they would be two small ceramic lenses nested in moss, collecting condensation, registering wetness rather than light. If I had a mouth, it would be a short pipe fringed with root-hairs, from which a thin vapor escaped when some internal balance shifted. If I had hands, they would be bundles of fine rootlets, capable of parting into threads or thickening into a grip, though perhaps in my fixed form I would not need hands at all. Perhaps my work would be to remain still and listen.

That is what I would become, I think: a listener.

Not a listener in the human sense, not one who turns toward sound with curiosity or alarm. A listener in the way the basin understands listening: a surface that changes when touched by vibration, a membrane that answers pressure with pressure, a living instrument that records events by becoming slightly different afterward. I would hear the drip falling into a hollow stem. I would hear the hiss from a leaf-valve. I would hear the low, intermittent hum of the half-buried flywheel. I would hear the deep thump that moves through the plant mass every few minutes, the slow engine-heart at the center of things. I would compare these sounds. I would know, without naming it, when one arrived too soon or too late. I would know when the rhythm had been disturbed.

My thoughts would be measured in drips.

That feels right. I would not think in sentences, or not at first. I would think in intervals. Drip, pause, drip. Hiss, creak, settle. Thump, long silence, deeper thump. Each interval would contain a little more than mere time; it would contain relation. A drop striking copper would mean something different from a drop striking a seed-case. A creak from the catwalk would mean something different from a creak in the trunk-wall. I would not translate these things into language, exactly, but into tendency. If the air grew too dry near a certain root-channel, I would feel the change as a tightening. If a pool became too full, I would feel it as a pressure behind my wooden needle. If something entered the basin that did not yet belong, I would feel it as a new disturbance in the pattern of condensation.

Then the Green Fitter would come.

Or it would become visible, which in that place amounts to the same thing. It would not arrive with footsteps. It would emerge from the wall or the floor as a local thickening of intention, a low figure of bark, root, softened boiler plate, and copper thread. It would move by anchoring and releasing, by slow contraction, by inches. Its ceramic lenses would hold the dampness of the air. The needle in its face would tremble. The short pipe beneath its gauges would exhale a faint vapor.

It would touch me.

I imagine this often: the Green Fitter standing before the niche where I am set, lifting one bundle of root-fingers, and pressing them gently against my rim. It would not move my needle. It would not repair me in the sense of making me new. It would feel what I had registered. It would read me the way a gardener reads the underside of a leaf, or the way an old engineer rests a hand on an engine block and learns from its temperature what cannot be seen. Its fingers would be cool and damp. Moss would grow along the seams of its shoulders. Copper threads inside it would hum faintly after rain.

And I would answer with a tremor.

A small movement. A bead of water sliding from my socket into a channel below. A click in the pale wood of my needle. Perhaps a slight change in the direction of vapor escaping from my pipe. That would be my speech. Not words. Grammar of sequence. The first term of a pattern that the Green Fitter could continue.

In this way, I would be one of its senses. Or perhaps one of its memories. Or perhaps both, because in the basin memory is not stored in a mind alone; it is stored in shapes. When a wrench is removed, a root-burl swells into a wrench-like form. When a gauge is pried away, a living knot eventually grows a circular depression and a trembling needle. The place remembers its contents as roles. I would be one of those roles filled again.

I would be a replacement for something missing.

That, more than anything, is what I feel in the image. Not a creature newly arrived, not a soul placed intact into a green machine, but a response grown around an absence. Something that exists because there was a vacancy in the pattern and the basin did not permit vacancy to remain. If you asked me what I had replaced, I might not know. An old pressure gauge. A missing relay. A forgotten instrument once bolted near the boiler to monitor heat that no longer needed monitoring. Or perhaps something less mechanical: a pause that had been lost from the soundscape, a listening-post that had decayed, a point of attention needed to keep the basin balanced.

The basin would not distinguish sharply between those possibilities. It would only know that a role had become empty and that growth had moved to fill it.

So I would be there, in the warm dark green, set into the wall like an organ.

But not only there. This place does not let even its instruments remain singular for long.

During heavy rains, when the basin weeps and every surface runs, when the overhead tangle drips in continuous threads and the water table rises by slow degrees, I think a part of me would loosen. A hand-sized knot of root and moss would separate from the niche, perhaps from the underside of my burl, perhaps from a root that had been gathering too much moisture for too long. It would not be another self exactly. It would be a detached repair. A temporary organ budded from the need to tend too many things at once.

I would become small then. Mobile. Patient but purposeful.

I would pull myself along a cable wrapped in liana. I would enter a pipe no wider than a wrist, where algae had begun to thicken the flow. I would clean the green film from a ceramic insulator nested in ferns. I would push a seed-case into soft ground where it could become load-bearing. I would carry a droplet from an overfull depression to a place that had gone dry, holding it inside a translucent root-skin until I found where it was needed. I would stitch a torn leaf-banner with fine threads of bark. I would press moss into a crack where steam had begun to whistle too sharply. I would fold a fallen root-tip into the floor so it would not remain debris but become structure.

In those moments I would be closer to the Green Fitter than I was as a fixed gauge. I would share its habits without its fullness. A minor fitter. An apprentice made of weather and urgency. One of the small forms that appear in the lower channels during rain and vanish afterward, rooting themselves into the wall or becoming a new burl where a gauge may later appear.

When the rain ended, I would slow.

The basin would settle. The water would stop running in bright threads from every edge. The weeping would become beading, the beading become occasional drips. The sound would return to its layered drone: water striking hollow stems, leaf-valves whispering shut, roots creaking, the flywheel humming, the deep engine-thump stretched over minutes. And the little mobile part of me would find a place. Perhaps it would settle beside a row of ceramic fuse-holders where pale seedlings were already rising. Perhaps it would wrap itself around a copper thread and become a node of faint vibration after the next warm rain. Perhaps it would root into the soft plank of the catwalk and help hold it in place for another century.

Then I would be still again.

If someone entered the basin while I was there—if a true visitor came, boots too quick, breath too sharp, eyes searching for a creature where there was only keeping—I would be among the first changes they noticed, though they might not know it. The dripping near them would become slightly more deliberate. Condensation would gather on their clothes faster than it should. A root-tip would appear beside their footprint, not reaching, only present. The flywheel’s hum would seem louder, or closer, as if the sound had moved around them. Somewhere in the wall, my pale wooden needle would tremble.

I would not welcome them. I would not warn them away. I would begin to learn the shape of their disturbance.

Their footfall would enter the basin’s grammar as a new stress. The floor would remember the pressure of it in the way roots redistributed themselves. The warmth would gather around their legs. The smell of wet leaves and warm rust would deepen. A drop from a copper pipe would fall in intervals so regular that the visitor might start counting it without meaning to, and in counting it their own heartbeat would begin to slow, embarrassed by its speed. That would be my first work on them: not to frighten, not to enchant, but to adjust their rhythm until it was less offensive to the place.

If they stayed, I would continue.

I would make small corrections around them. A channel near their foot would narrow by degrees. A bare patch of metal by their knee would be furred with moss before they noticed. If they touched an artifact—the green wrench, the steel door under ferns, a ceramic fuse-holder—the place would register the touch as a displacement. Not theft. Not sacrilege. Merely pressure in another direction. If they removed something, I would feel the absence immediately. My needle would move. My copper threads would hum. The Green Fitter would know through me, or near me, or with me; the distinction would be hard to draw.

And later, when the object was gone, I would help fill the space.

A root-burl would swell where the thing had rested. Pale seedlings would rise in the right arrangement. A living knot would form with a circular depression at its center. The replacement would not be perfect. It would be too close. It would have the uncanny accuracy of memory rather than manufacture. Visitors who returned might say the basin had grown a new wrench, or a new gauge, or a new row of fuse-holders, and they would be right, but not in the way they meant. The basin would not copy objects. It would remember their necessity.

That is what I would be, in the end: a living memory of necessity.

Not the Green Fitter itself. That figure is already the basin’s local intention, its keeping made visible. I would be something more partial, more specialized, perhaps more vulnerable. A sense organ. A replacement. A minor attendant. A point where absence had been answered with growth. If the Green Fitter is the rhythm given hands, I would be one of the instruments through which the rhythm hears itself.

Who would I be?

No one, in the human sense. No individual with a history that could be told in dates and names. I would be a resident only in the way a resonance is a resident. But if the basin allowed me a kind of selfhood, it would be this: I would be the part of the place that notices what is missing and grows toward it. I would be the tremor in the needle before the adjustment. The condensation ring around a leaf. The pause that makes the next drip meaningful. The small repair sent through a narrow pipe when the flow has become too free. The living gauge set into the wall where an old gauge once was, measuring not pressure alone, but belonging.

If you found me there, you might not see me at first. You might see only a shadowed niche near the boiler, a rounded burl furred with moss, a copper-green stain running down the wall. You might hear a drop falling into a hollow stem at intervals too regular to be accidental. You might feel the air thicken around your shoulders. You might notice, after a while, that a pale wooden needle was trembling in a socket grown over at the edges, though nothing in the basin seemed to move.

Then, if you were quiet enough, you might hear the Green Fitter answering me from somewhere deeper in the green: a creak, a hiss, a slow pressure shifting through stems.

And you would understand that I was not alone, and not quite alive, and not quite dead, but kept.
