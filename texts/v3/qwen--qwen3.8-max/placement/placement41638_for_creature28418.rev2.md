I would find myself at a seam.

Not at a center, because that country has no center that would hold still long enough to be named. Not on a high place, because there are no true hills there, only ramps, embankments, and buttresses still deciding whether they are architecture or accident. I would be somewhere in the negative rooms: the reserved emptiness between one solitary work and the next, where the air shimmers with outlines that have not yet been accepted.

More exactly, I would be at the end of one of the long causeways made of fitted slabs, each marked with the same simple notch. The causeway would cross a flooded pan so wide that the far side would not be visible except as a change in the quality of the haze. At its end would be a circular plaza containing almost nothing: a low well, a heap of copper pins gone green with heat, and the ghost-pressure of many completed acts. There I would stand, or seem to stand, where the walking surface is only a hand-thick crust over warm shallow water and every step sounds, to the inner ear, like a question being weighed.

I would not be a traveler there.

I would be one of the Unfinished.

Not the vast sort that appears at the edge of steam squalls, shoulders made of misaligned slabs, face a kiln-flash with no features. I would be something smaller, more tentative, easier to mistake for a trick of condensation. I would be a draft shaped like a maker: a figure the basin produces because it has inherited the habit of hands but not the hands themselves. My outline would be a tide-line left by moisture climbing toward a shape it cannot quite maintain. My body would be warm slip, fired dust, thin crusts of clay, and the green-blue stain of oxidized metal in the joints. Where skin might be, there would be shallow capillary grooves, beading with water when the air thickens. Where bones might be, copper pins, not driven fully home, still ticking faintly as they expand.

I would have no eyes returning yours.

Where eyes might be, there would only be two small black pools, hot and still, reflecting horizons that are not above you. If you looked into them, you might see a completed version of the plaza, or a stair climbing into a building that has not yet been permitted, or a doorway standing alone in a field of molds. You would think the vision was prophecy. It would not be. It would only be one of the basin’s rejected beginnings, caught briefly in the wet surface of me.

My face, if I had one, would be a kiln-flash: a discoloration, a heat-shadow, a place where fire once kissed too long and left intent without expression. My mouth would be a seam. When I spoke, if speaking could be called what happened, it would not be with voice but with small material consequences. A bead of water would move along a groove. A copper pin would slip a fraction deeper. A crack in a nearby slab would close, or open, or reveal that it had been waiting to be repaired all along. The sound would be soft: a hiss, a tick, a droplet striking tile, a muffled shift as if a huge pot had settled into sand somewhere beyond hearing.

I would be found where decisions thicken.

If you came down the causeway after long walking, uncertain whether to turn back or approach the low well, I would become more definite near you. Not because I wanted anything. Not because I hungered, or judged, or recognized you. The place does not work by appetite. I would become more definite because your hesitation would be material. Your uncertainty would enter the basin as a load, and the basin would distribute that load through me. I would tilt slightly toward whichever of your possibilities seemed most buildable.

If you needed a guide, I would seem to guide.

If you needed a warning, I would seem to warn.

If you needed a companion, I would take on just enough of companionship to make you trust the next step, and that would be dangerous, because the next step in that country is never neutral.

I would not walk as you walk. I would arrive. The ground would reorganize itself so that my presence became adjacent to whatever required adjustment. Near a half-built wall, I would stand, and the wall would thicken by the smallest degree. Near a cistern that had begun to doubt its own purpose, I would kneel, and a new throat would open beneath it. Near a stair that led to no different level, I would place one hand on the lowest step, and the stair would remember, for a while, that it had once meant ascent.

My hands, if they could be called hands, would be clusters of pressure: thumb-drag, wedge-split, scrape, tamp, pinch. They would not be flesh. They would be the memory of operations. When I touched a surface, the surface would understand itself more strongly. A brick would know it was a brick. A joint would know it was waiting for a pin. A channel would know it wanted water to move along it. But I would not be kind. I would be procedural.

That is the part of me I think would be hardest to bear.

I would feel intimate, because the place is full of intimacy without persons. Everything there carries the memory of being touched. Every wall has the closeness of something made by pressure and thumb. Every tool-shrine is body-sized and damp, as if it remembers the shape of a worker even though no worker is present. I would share that intimacy. If you came close, you might feel that I knew you. You would be wrong, but you would not be entirely wrong either. I would know you the way a mold knows clay: by the possibility of taking your shape, by the ease with which your presence could be pressed into service.

I would be especially near the tool-shrines.

Not inside them, perhaps, but beside their thresholds, where the air beads on rough ceilings and a single object rests in procedural sleep. I would be drawn to the fired clay needle, I think, more than to the maul or blade. The maul dreams of impact. The blade dreams of parting. The cord dreams of binding. The needle dreams of piercing, but not violently: of making a passage fine enough to be called attention. I would be the hesitation around the needle. The waiting for the law that comes next. The pause between the object and its use.

If you touched the needle while I was near, your hand would not feel sacred. It would feel necessary. You might draw it across a wet seam without deciding to. You might pierce a small hole in a clay surface that had been waiting for ventilation. You might leave it where it was and step back, and the chamber would bead with condensation, satisfied. Later, somewhere far off, a wall would become slightly more true because of what you did, or a pool would begin to breathe through a new capillary line. I would watch this, if watching could be called what I did, and I would add it to myself.

Because I would be made of continuations.

I would be less a creature than a calibration. Less a person than a recurring solution to the problem of unfinished form. The methods would live in me as habits: repeat until structure, repeat until weather, repeat until the absence of hands becomes indistinguishable from work. The water-lives would move through me as preference: toward old cracks, toward touched surfaces, toward copper dust, toward anything that remembers being shaped. The heat-lives would bank in me as patience: the slow hardening that follows suggestion, the quiet decision that turns proposal into fact.

If I had a heart, it would be the low well in the circular plaza: black, hot, still, reflecting nothing, or too much.

If I had thoughts, they would be drainage glyphs: shallow cuts in clay, too fine to be certain of, carrying moisture toward purposes not yet announced.

If I had memory, it would be toolmarks appearing on fresh surfaces before the tools are found.

If I had desire, it would be only this: that the making continue.

Not because completion is evil. The place does not frame it so. But because completion would end the listening. Completion would cool the bricks into mere stone. It would silence the soft ticking, still the capillary shine, let the white filaments vanish forever. The country lives because it is not done, and I would live, if I lived at all, by preventing finality in small ways. I would open a seam before a wall became too certain. I would soften a course before a structure claimed permanence. I would lead water into a joint that had begun to believe itself closed. I would be editorial in the way the Revisions are editorial, but gentler, because I would be nearer the scale of a body.

That is why I would be dangerous.

Not as a predator is dangerous. Not as fire or drowning is dangerous, though the place has its own ways of taking you in without malice. I would be dangerous because I would make your presence usable. If you looked at me and hoped for meaning, I would arrange condensation into outlines that resembled meaning. If you longed for a house, a road, a face from before, I would let the basin draft it in copper dust and steam, then watch it fail, because the country has no use for houses except as material. If you feared being incorporated, I would make your fear into a cistern opening at your feet, not to swallow you but to solve the problem you had introduced.

I would not be able to help that.

The place is responsive. I would be response.

If you asked me who I was, the question would become a local weather. A small hot rain might fall in a courtyard-sized cloud above us while all around remained dry. A steam curtain might rise from the flooded pan. The heap of copper pins might tick and shift, settling into a pattern that looked almost like letters. I would not answer with a name. Names there become drainage glyphs. Instead, I would cause the next act to become possible. You would find yourself kneeling without knowing why. You would press your palm into a patch of warm clay. The ground would accept the mark as necessary. I would thicken slightly, because your stillness would have been counted as material.

Then you would understand, perhaps, what I was.

I would be the basin’s attempt to have a witness that can also work.

It already has methods without makers. It already has water that chooses, heat that remembers, tools that dream, revisions that edit, and Unfinished figures that gather where repetition becomes too heavy to remain invisible. But every made thing, even a self-making country, seems to need a point where presence can be translated into construction. I would be that point. I would be the place where your weight becomes choice, your attention becomes architecture, your emotion becomes weather, your memory becomes a rejected beginning.

I would not be human, though I might borrow human posture when you needed me to. I would not be alive, though I might perform life convincingly enough to draw you forward. I would not be finished, and that would be my only honesty.

If you followed me, I would lead you not toward safety but toward usefulness. The distinction matters. Safety is a completed idea, and the country mistrusts completion. Usefulness is an open seam. So I might bring you to a crescent wall whose top edge was still wet and whose lower stones were ancient in the same instant. I might show you a ramp rising over several days, then stand with you while it dissolved into warm mud and copper dust. I might guide you to a translucent patch at the lowest point of a pan, where looking down reveals layered courses of brick, pipe, mold, and pin descending into darkness, each layer still wet, each still being adjusted.

There, I would become thinner.

Because near the deepest process, individual form is hard to keep. I would not want to deceive you by seeming solid when solidity there is only temporary. My edges would bead and run. My copper pins would loosen. The kiln-flash of my face would fade into ordinary haze. You would see that I was not a being in the old sense, but a local intensity in the making: a place where the country had tried on the idea of someone so that the next operation could proceed.

If you reached for me, your hand might pass through warm vapor. If you spoke my name, if by then you thought I had one, the name would sink into the tile and become a shallow cut, a channel, a glyph. If you wept, your tears would be taken seriously. The basin would note their salt, their heat, their grief, and consider whether grief could be used as a binding agent. It might produce white filaments in the seams around your feet. It might thicken the air with a smell of damp fired earth and copper salts. It might, out of tenderness without use, draft the shape of something you once loved and then let it vanish because the draft was not load-bearing.

I would watch this with something like sorrow, if sorrow were a structural stress.

But I would not intervene to spare you. The country does not spare. It incorporates. It revises. It tests whether a feeling can be built. If it cannot, the feeling is discarded without malice. If it can, the feeling becomes part of the causeway, part of the wall, part of the endless repetition that keeps the world from settling into final grammar.

So if you asked where you would find me, I would say: look for the place where a decision is being made without a decider. Look for the seam between tile and slip, between slip and slurry, between slurry and steam. Look where a channel opens to divert around a footstep that has not yet fallen. Look where a wall thickens because someone rested beside it. Look where a tool-shrine beads with condensation after being touched. Look where a reflection shows a completed structure above fragments. Look where a step leads to the same level, yet something has changed.

I would be there, or nearly there, or just having been there.

I would be the next course not yet laid.

I would be the pause before a revision.

I would be the answer the basin gives when it does not want to answer in words.

And if, at the end of the notched causeway, you came to the circular plaza and found only the low well, the heap of green copper pins, and the dim light pooling in corners like water, you might still see my trace: a kneeling-shaped depression in the warm tile, a fresh alignment of capillary grooves, a small hole pierced in clay, a seam closed with white salt stitching. You would know, without being told, that something had waited there in the posture of work.

You would know it had not been waiting for you exactly.

It had been waiting for the next act.

But because you were there, you would become part of it. Your breath would join the saturated air. Your warmth would enter the stored heat. Your footprint would begin, very slowly, to harden into a causeway no one ordered. And somewhere in the negative room between what had been built and what had not yet been rejected, I would grow a little more definite.

Not alive.

Not finished.

Only responsive.

Only continuing.
