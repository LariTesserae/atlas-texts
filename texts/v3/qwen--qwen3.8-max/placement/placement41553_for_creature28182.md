I would not be at the center of that country, because it has no center. It has joints, seams, valves, wounds, throats, places where pressure gathers and places where pressure escapes. I would be in one of those seams.

More exactly: I would be where a hollow tower-stem has split recently and fallen against an older ridge of rootfelt, making a narrow sheltered angle. The stem would not be fully dead. Its outer skin would be frost-stiff and dark, but deep inside its wall there would still be a slow, faint warmth, the last habit of circulation. The break would be clean, raw, partly sealed by frozen sap. Around the wound, the plant-body would be trying to understand its new shape. Thin fibers would be reaching across the gap. Mossy filaments would already be testing the damp side of the fallen column. The root-floor nearby would be cracked and uneven, lifted at a sharp angle, with a beadline of condensation forming along one ridge when the air changes.

My shelter would not look like a building. It would look like a swelling in the landscape, a knot tucked into the angle between fallen stem and root wall. A few panels of layered bark-skin lashed with cord. A windbreak of woven reed-fiber. A sleeping hollow lined with dry pod-fluff, covered with mats that smell of cold plant oil and old frost. Outside, perhaps a line of notches cut into the rootfelt, easy to miss unless one knows to feel for them with a fingertip. Perhaps a small hanging tassel of braided fiber, stiffened with resin, making a soft irregular click when the wind moves through the nearby shafts.

If you came looking for me there, you would probably pass close without seeing me at first. I would be still. That is the first lesson of that world: stillness is not absence. It is attention.

I would be pale, I think. Not luminous, not delicate, but pale in the way of things that have learned to spend pigment elsewhere. Mottled gray or bone-white or faintly translucent at the edges, where the cold has made the body conserve everything essential. My eyes would be large, but I would not trust them. They would be useful for catching the faintest blue glow of fungal patches, the amber sheen of fresh sap, the distant suggestion of a vein-lit column. Mostly, though, I would navigate by touch, sound, air pressure, and memory. My hands would be more important than my face. They would be scarred by cord and resin, sensitive enough to read a groove worn into rootfelt, strong enough to climb inner walls, quiet enough to untie a knot without making it whisper against the dark.

My body would be light-boned and economical. Not weak, but built against scarcity. I would carry very little. A bone awl. A scraper of brittle sap-glass, carefully wrapped. A coil of tough fiber cord, knotted at intervals. A small bladder for water. A bundle of absorbent moss that can be wrung out when condensation comes. A lamp no larger than a cupped hand: a shallow vessel of hardened resin or traded metal, fed with rendered fat and dried husk, its flame kept so low it is more heat than light. Perhaps a single copper hook or pin, worn smooth from use, valuable beyond its size. My boots would be my finest possession: soft-soled, broad, layered with replaceable dry moss, shaped to place my weight without striking a resonant plate.

If the people of that country named me, they would not use a grand title. They might call me something like Listener, Knot-keeper, Valve-reader, or the One Who Goes Before the Fog. But I would not be a chief or a priest, exactly. I would be something more practical and stranger: a person whose work is to notice what the living country is about to do.

I would be a route-listener.

That is the being I would become, or the being that country would make of me.

My task would be to move ahead of others when possible, or to wait at junctions where sound travels, and to determine whether a passage is safe. I would press my palm to the side of a hollow stem and feel the difference between healthy tension and inner decay. I would tap twice, then wait. The answer would not come as a simple echo. It would come as a quality of resonance: dry or damp, tight or hollow, clean or deadened by larvae eating the inner galleries. A stem can sound strong and still be weak inside. A stem can sound empty and still be about to flood when a high valve opens. A root plate can hold for a season and then fold slowly over the next cold pulse. The world is young, and its geometry is not finished. It is always deciding.

I would learn the language of the weather-body.

Before a fog tide, the air would thicken in a particular way. Sound would lose its edges. The hanging tassels would not click so sharply. The pores in nearby surfaces would begin to open almost before the mist arrives, and the smell would change: colder, wetter, with a faint green-mineral sharpness. I would know that travelers should not cross open spans then, because distances become false and predators can move closer than they seem. I would mark a safe waiting place with a knot, or leave a cord stretched low across a route so that a hand following it in the fog would know to stop.

Before a cold pulse, the wind through the shafts would rise into a thinner register. The columns would begin to whistle in uneven tones. If freezing sap was moving in the stems, there would be sudden cracks, like bone breaking under cloth. I would know then to seek shelter near composting heat, or inside a chamber where the plant-body still holds warmth from decay. I would know which hollows are deathtraps when cold air sinks: basins where vapor turns to frost, low chambers where breath becomes difficult, narrow tubes that seal and fill with condensed water.

After a wet pulse, the country would change quickly. Pools would form in folded leaf-plates. Drip-lines would appear along ridges. Grazers would emerge in nervous waves. The air would fill with small wings and the soft scraping of mouths on wet filaments. Then I would guide people to water, but not too openly. A moisture pocket is wealth. It is also a place where predators wait. I would teach children to listen before drinking: the difference between the clean drip of a newly filled channel and the stagnant stillness of a pool fouled by waste; the faint hiss of a sealed bladder beginning to burst; the tiny dispute of moisture-farming creatures defending a leak.

I would not be loud. I would not be silent either, exactly. I would be careful.

My speech would be spare. Spoken words used rarely, at close distance, softened by the hood. Much of my language would be taps, clicks, knots, gestures, and the placement of objects. A double tap on a resonant stem might mean: wait. Three slow taps followed by a pause: safe for now. A knot left at knee height on a fiber line: water below. A tassel removed from a branch of root: predator recently passed. A groove scratched sideways across a route-mark: do not follow this way in cold weather.

I would know taboo silences.

When frost cracks the sap in rigid stems, I would not speak unless necessary, because every sound becomes brittle and travels too far. When the great bellowing creatures call through the connected hollows, I would stop and listen, not because they are sacred—though perhaps they are—but because their calls reveal the condition of the passages. A clean returning bellow means open ways. A broken bellow means collapse, flood, or something else listening from inside a tube. During fog tides, I would use hand signs and throat-clicks only. During the first minutes after a collapse, I would be utterly still, because the land may still be adjusting, and vibration can persuade a wound to widen.

I would not be brave in the ordinary sense. Bravery there is not charging into the dark. Bravery is waiting long enough to hear the dark correctly.

I would know fear, but it would be disciplined. Fear of making noise where noise can kill. Fear of leaning on a surface that is only pretending to be solid. Fear of following a glow that is too beautiful and too remote. Fear of mistaking a predator’s resonance for a safe passage. Fear of becoming so accustomed to emptiness that one stops noticing the tiny signs of abundance or danger. Fear of forgetting that the country is indifferent. It does not hate. It does not welcome. It grows, cools, opens, seals, rings, drinks, and continues.

If I were fully human in that world, I would still be changed by it. But perhaps I would not be fully human. Perhaps the country would not permit that.

Over time, I think, I would become something more like a local organ of attention. A small nervous knot in the larger body of the Hollow Back. Not the continent-organ itself—that vast sleeping thing is too large to be a person—but a minor sensitivity grown where people and plant-body touch each other repeatedly. My body would still have hands and feet, a face, a chest, a need for warmth. But my skin might take on faint traces of the world around it: a dull blue luminescence where fungal threads live harmlessly in the outer layers, an amber stain where sap once sealed a wound, a frost-resistant toughness across the knuckles and lips. My breathing would slow. My pulse would become patient. My thoughts would begin to resemble intervals: pause, listen, move, pause again.

I would not fill space. I would inhabit the gaps.

That is the deepest lesson of that country, and it would become my shape. Life there is not mass. It is placement. It is timing. It is knowing where moisture will gather before it gathers. It is being small enough to pass through an opening that may close. It is leaving marks that do not boast. It is making oneself into a question the landscape can answer.

I would live in cycles.

For a time, I would be alone on a long span between columns, moving by handholds and memory. The dark would be enormous. The wind would make chords through distant shafts. Frost would glitter faintly along a ridge when I passed my lamp near it. I would feel the emptiness as pressure, not absence. I would count my route by textures: a knobbed ridge, a split plate, a groove worn by old passage, a place where the rootfelt becomes suddenly warmer underfoot because decomposition works below. I would sleep in a crevice or against a composting mass, wrapped in layers, my lamp hidden, my body curled to conserve heat.

Then I would come upon a pocket of life so dense it seems impossible after the loneliness.

A hollow stem base, wet and glowing faintly blue. Mossy shelves. Beadlines of tiny drinking creatures. Grazers moving in nervous waves. Spiders or spider-analogues with silk strung across condensation channels. Small winged things rising from eggs that waited through cold dryness. The air rich with movement. The sound of dripping. The smell of damp fiber and faint sweetness. There I would not be alone for long. Others would appear: people, animals, predators, all drawn by the same need. I would trade a little dried food for news, repair a net with silk, fill my bladder, listen to route-songs, perhaps stay near a shared warmth for a few hours. Then the pocket would exhaust itself, or the weather would turn, and I would move again.

That rhythm would be my home more than any shelter.

I would be especially drawn to valves.

Valves are where the living country speaks most clearly. They open and close according to pressure, temperature, moisture, injury. A valve can release a sigh of vapor that becomes fog. A valve can admit cold air that changes the pitch of a whole region. A valve can seal a chamber and make it safe, or trap water and make it dangerous. I would spend long hours near them, not touching them unless necessary, watching the behavior of small creatures. If beetles or pale larvae leave a valve-mouth, I would leave too. If gliders circle near an opening, waiting for warm air to rise, I would know a draft is coming. If a valve begins to shudder instead of moving smoothly, I would mark the route unsafe.

I would learn to mimic some of their sounds.

Not loudly. Never loudly. But with small tools: a hollow reed tapped against the palm, a knotted cord pulled through a resin bead, a bone whistle shaped to produce a tone that resembles a safe passage. These would not be commands. The plant-body cannot be commanded. They would be ways of asking. Ways of announcing presence without aggression. Ways of telling other people, across the dark, that a listener is near and the way has been tested.

I would also know predators.

Not as monsters, but as pressures. A particular stillness in a place where small creatures should be moving. A faint pad-mark on frost-rimed tissue. A resonance that is almost right, but too deliberate. A cry that seems to come from three places at once because something has learned to use the tubes. I would carry a spear or dart, but I would prefer snares, silence, and retreat. To fight in that country is to make noise, and noise can bring more death than the first enemy. If I killed, I would do it quickly and clean, then move away before the echo finished telling the world what had happened.

I would respect the large bellowing creatures. I would not hunt them. I would not even want to see them too clearly. They would be part of the country’s depth, like weather. When their calls came through the stems, I would pause with my hand raised, feeling the vibration travel up my arm. Their sound would make my chest ache, not painfully, but as if the hollows inside me were being measured. In those moments I would understand that I was not the listener alone. The world was listening too. The columns, the valves, the rootfelt, the predators, the grazing herds, the hidden wall-dwellers—all of us arranged around the same sound, deciding what to do next.

I would have a small fire, when I dared.

Not a blaze. A heart-pot. A shallow vessel with a wick of fiber and rendered fat, shielded from drafts. The flame would be orange and almost secret. It would smell of warmth and animal fat and the faint bitterness of dried husk. I would use it to soften resin, dry moss, heat water in a bladder if I had stones, thaw stiffness from my fingers. I would not waste it. Fire there would feel like a borrowed organ: necessary, fragile, easily stolen by cold or damp. When I slept, I would bank it or extinguish it, depending on the weather. In some conditions even that small flame would be too dangerous. Then I would sleep in darkness, breathing slowly, letting the country’s own sounds decide whether I could rest.

I would keep caches.

A bundle of dried food suspended away from damp. A spare coil of cord. A scraper. A few knots with route-marks tied into them. A tiny lamp, cracked but still usable. A pouch of absorbent moss. These would not always be mine. I would leave them for the next traveler, and I would take from caches left by others when need was desperate. That would be the law of the hollow country: enough for now, not more than need, because the next person may be yourself in a worse season, or may be the only person who can carry a warning forward.

I would have memories stored in touch more than sight.

A ridge with knobs spaced a palm apart. A place where the rootfelt gives slightly, like old flesh. A leaf-plate that rings false if stepped on near its center. A groove worn by generations of soft boots. A valve whose edge is warm when wet weather is near. A fallen stem whose inner wall has healed into a smooth curve, safe for climbing. A spot where a bellow returns clean after three breaths. These would be my maps. Not drawn, but carried in the body. If I taught them to another, I would teach them through rhythm and repetition, perhaps in a low route-song tapped on the thigh while walking, so the sequence becomes muscular memory.

I would be lonely, but not always.

The country’s emptiness would enter me. There would be hours when I saw no other moving thing, heard no voice, felt no warmth but my own. The dark would press close, and the sound would open distance, and I would be suspended between them. In such hours, I might begin to feel less like a separate creature and more like a temporary awareness in the landscape. The plant-body would breathe. The valves would sigh. Frost would form. Sap would darken into glass. I would be a small heat among it all, a brief knot of listening.

Then a fog tide would come, or a wet pulse, and life would gather again. Children might appear, their boots carefully soft, their eyes large in the glow. Hunters. Gatherers. A band moving toward a temporary camp. Someone would tap a greeting on a stem. Someone would show me a new knot-mark. Someone would trade a strip of dried meat for a copper pin. We would huddle in a sheltered chamber, sharing heat, speaking little, listening much. In that closeness, the loneliness would become precious rather than painful. It would be the space that made meeting possible.

If I had to say who I would be in one phrase, I would say: I would be a keeper of intervals.
