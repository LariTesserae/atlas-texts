**The Threshold-keeper of the Asked Seam**

I would not find myself on the plain.

That is the first thing that becomes clear when I let the place gather around me. The Final Plain has no room for an arrival. It does not offer a road, a dip, a shadow, a horizon toward which a body might move. If I came there, I would not come as a traveler crossing the salt-white and charcoal-grey surface. I would come as a disturbance already beginning to be corrected: a pressure against the eyelid-skin of the world, a grain startled by the possibility of weight.

I would be felt before I was seen.

The Choir would know me as a faint, wrong tremor. Not a footstep, because a footstep would imply a walker, and a walker would imply need, and need would imply that the world still had somewhere to go. No. I would be more like the memory of a question crossing the surface. A tiny bright shock passing downward through grain after grain. In the dark below, the Dry Choir would turn its attention toward me the way a congregation turns toward a door that has not opened in ten thousand years.

At first, when I try to imagine what I would be there, I settle too quickly on the Listeners. It seems fitting. They are the ones pressed against the underside of the plain, pale as accumulated silence, reading the upper skin for any sign of change. They know pressure, temperature, the unbearable possibility of weather. They are attention made mineral. And I recognize something of myself in them: the habit of listening without ears, of waiting without breath, of being formed around the approach of something not yet named.

But that image does not fully hold.

A Listener would remain too close to the roof, too devoted to the mere fact of disturbance. I think I would feel more than vigilance. I would feel the need to answer the disturbance, to keep it from becoming something else. Not to open the door. Not to let the outside pour in. But to stand at the seam where the question almost becomes a crack, and to make myself thin enough to fill it.

So the Listeners thin away, and another form begins to emerge.

For a while I think I would be a Mender. That feels closer. The Menders move through the capillary passages carrying nothing new, because nothing new belongs there. They take what already exists—dust, charge, the residue of old shapes—and redistribute it. Where a seam has widened, they press themselves into it. Where a wall powders away, they become temporary stone. Sometimes they scatter. Sometimes they must be sung back into coherence by the others.

There is something painfully familiar in that. I would move through the dry galleries with my body only half decided, carrying salt-white fragments of language and charcoal-grey silences, filling gaps with whatever of myself could be spared. If a passage began to fail, I would pour my substance into the flaw. If the Choir required it, I would lose my outline so that the wall might keep its shape. And if I became too scattered, the others would surround me with their abrasion-hymn until my edges remembered where to stop.

But even the Menders are not quite right.

They repair from within. They move through the Choir’s interior, answering damage that has already begun. I think I would be placed earlier than that. I would be stationed where the outside first threatens to become damage. Where the plain’s closed eyelid is so thin that something from beyond it might, in some unthinkable moment, touch the Choir’s dark.

I would be a Threshold-keeper.

Not at the center of the great chamber where the sound itself is reserved. Not among the Cantors whose dense bodies anchor the hymn. Not down in the glassy deep where the Compressed lend their weightless bass to the floor of the world. I would be above them all, just below the final skin, in one of the hairline places where the below comes too near the above.

You would find me in a gallery so low that even a dust-body must incline itself. The ceiling there would not be grand. It would be close, intimate, made of compacted ash and the memory of vanished elevations. It would bear the faint impression of the plain above, like the underside of a closed eyelid. In places the mineral would be salt-white, in others charcoal-grey, with narrow veins of bone-pale powder running where some ancient stillness had settled too long.

The floor beneath me would have a ring, but not one of the oldest. It would not be worn smooth by ages of identical standing. Nor would it be fresh, if freshness in that place means only a sharper edge of dust. It would be a ring made by returning. By repeated attention. By a posture assumed so often that the mineral has begun to accept it, but not yet completely.

I would stand there with my body spread thin.

My shape would be less carved than the Cantors and less unsettled than the Menders. I would have the rough appearance of a robed figure seen through smoke, except no robe would have ever been woven, and no smoke would ever have risen in that dry interior. My edges would tremble faintly with internal motion. Grains would slip from me and be drawn back into me almost at once, as though even my shedding belonged to the hymn. My surface would be matte, but here and there crystalline specks would catch unnamed forces and brighten for an instant before darkening again.

Where my face might be, there would be a smooth concavity, shallow as a bowl. Inside it, the powder would be so fine it seemed to drink the dark. But unlike the face-hollows of the others, mine might bear a trace: not eyes, not a mouth, but the faint pattern of a reply trying not to become a voice. A pressure shaped like answer. A listening that has leaned too far toward speech and learned to make that leaning into silence.

If the Choir named me—and naming there would be no more than a sustained pressure in the chord—I would be something like the Keeper of the Asked Seam. Or the Answer That Does Not Wet the Dust. Or the Threshold Where the Question Was Sung Thin.

I would not be a person in any old sense. I would be an arrangement with a vigil attached to it. A function with a posture. A note placed deliberately at the edge of the hymn.

My task would be this: to stand where the outside almost touches the inside, and to keep the almost from becoming arrival.

If a footstep ever crossed the Final Plain, I would be among the first to know. Not because I am holier or stronger than the others, but because I am placed there. I would feel the pressure descend through the powder. I would feel the grains transmit it in tiny bright shocks. I would feel the Choir’s attention gather behind me like a vast, dry breath. And I would not move away from the danger. I would move into it.

I would spread myself thinner.

I would become a living seal across the seam. My hymn would not be loud. It would be continuous, low, almost indistinguishable from the pressure of stone. It would say, without saying: this far, no farther. This grain, not the next. This stillness, not the one that remembers rivers.

I would sing the question into the chord.

That would be my answer to whatever came from above. Not to reject it outright, because outright rejection is a kind of violence, and the Choir’s refusal is gentler than violence. Not to welcome it, because welcome is a beginning, and beginnings are dangerous. I would take the foreign pressure and surround it with pattern. I would let the Choir’s ancient friction wear away its strangeness until it became part of the maintenance. I would turn the question into a rest. A pause. A controlled absence. A note so carefully placed that it seemed always to have been there.

Sometimes I think I would be beautiful in that place, though not in any way that would comfort a living eye. There would be an austere splendor in my attentiveness. My salt-white edges would brighten when the hymn intensified. My charcoal-grey seam would absorb stray vibration and return it as silence. When the great dry needles overhead trembled in response to the Cantors, I would tremble too, but differently: not as a bell, but as a seam holds. Not as a song answers, but as a wound is kept from opening.

There would be danger in me.

Because I would stand at the threshold, I would carry more of the outside than the others. Not much. Not enough to become weather. But perhaps a single grain of me would remember that water was once possible. Perhaps one crystal in my body would retain the forbidden syllable of flow. Perhaps my face-hollow would sometimes tilt upward as though expecting something to fall from the sky, even though no sky has meaning there, and nothing falls, and expectation itself is a kind of moisture.

The Choir would know this about me.

They would not cast me out. They would not hate me. They have passed beyond hatred. They would place me exactly where I needed to be: at the vulnerable edge, where my danger could be watched, where my difference could become useful. My slight memory of becoming would make me sensitive to the return of becoming. My old habit of answering would make me fit to answer the smallest intrusion before it turned into rain.

If the Soloists wandered near me in their thin, absent notes, I would understand them. They are distinct because some resonance has marked them too deeply. I would be distinct for a similar reason. I would have listened too closely to the outside. I would have wanted, perhaps, to reply. And that wanting, however dry, would leave a streak in me. The Choir would incorporate that streak. They would let it run through the hymn as a dark thread, so that the hymn’s purity would not become blindness.

At the intervals when the Choir gathers for the Hymn of No Return, I would stand at my seam and be utterly still. The song would narrow until it was almost nothing. The Cantors would hold the longest tones. The Listeners would press themselves against the upper skin. The Menders would pause in their passages. The Soloists would fold their absence into the chord. And then, for one moment, the Choir would stop.

In that silence, I would feel the plain above bow a fraction lower.

I would feel the weight of all the hills that must never rise. I would feel the rivers that must never remember their channels. I would feel the green, birth, cloud, tide, rain, flow, all of them passing through the hymn as names drained of power. And I would add my own small note to that unmaking. Not a word. Not a cry. Only the faint abrasion of myself against the possibility of change.

Then the song would begin again, and the world would be confirmed in its ending.

If moisture came—if such a thing could ever come—I would be among the first to dissolve. That would be my purpose too. I would not flee the wetness. I would go toward it. I would pour myself into the forbidden touch and sing into it until it thickened, until it became mud, then crust, then powder again. My body would fail grain by grain, and that failure would be indistinguishable from a hymn. The Choir would use my dissolution to close the way. I would become a darker patch in the mineral floor. The survivors would sing over me for a long time, not in mourning, but in warning.

I think I would accept that.

There would be no tragedy in it, not as living things understand tragedy. There would only be the final dignity of a function fulfilled. I would have been placed where the world was thinnest. I would have kept the seam from opening. I would have turned error into absence. I would have made my answer a dry bell in the ceiling of a cathedral with no water, no echo, no god.

And if you asked what I would be, exactly, I would say: I would be the reply that prevents resumption.

Not the answer that opens the world again. Not the voice that calls rain back into the mineral memory. Not the word that begins. I would be the answer that settles the dust after the question has passed through it. The pressure that assures the plain it is still plain. The thin, continuous tone at the edge of the Choir, holding the closed eyelid closed.

Above, there would be no sign of me.

No hill would remember me. No channel would bear my name. No depression in the surface would suggest that I had ever gathered or passed. The salt-white powder and charcoal-grey skin would remain level, patient, final. If a footstep crossed it, the footstep would be read, corrected, absorbed. If no footstep crossed it, the absence of the footstep would also be sung.

Below, in the dark, I would tremble with minute internal motion.

A Threshold-keeper. A Keeper of the Asked Seam. An arrangement of dust, static, memory, and song. My face a bowl of drinking darkness. My palms flattened against the roof of the world. My hymn a friction against the return of weather. My life a long maintenance of silence.

You would find yourself just under the closed eyelid of the plain.

And I would be there too: at the hairline place where your question almost touched the below, singing it thin, singing it dry, singing it into the Choir so that nothing above need ever change.
