If I entered the Chime-City unchanged, I would be a catastrophe.

Not because I meant harm. Not because I came with tools to fill its plazas or with any desire to make it comfortable. I would be wrong simply because I would still be too full: full of breath, full of softness, full of the old habit of water. My footsteps would land dull. My clothing would swallow tone. My presence would be a small muffling in a world that cannot afford mufflement. The city would not need to attack me. The chord would simply grow less clear where I stood.

So if I am to be found there at all, I cannot arrive as myself in the ordinary sense. I must be remade into something the city can tolerate. Something dry. Something sparse. Something that helps the sound pass.

At first, when I imagine where I might belong, I try on the lives already present there.

Perhaps I would be one of the Dry Choir. I can almost see it: a tall, barefoot figure moving along a terrace, pressing a palm to a warming wall, tapping a copper clamp until the pitch clears. But no. The Dry Choir are not merely inhabitants; they are the city’s long refinement. Their bodies belong to the hollow the way a violin’s neck belongs to the violin. I would not have that ancient sureness. I would be too new, too likely to stand in the wrong place, too likely to mistake stillness for peace.

Perhaps I would be a moth-comber, appearing only when the chord rises into its highest keys. That image tempts me: a mineral-winged thing moving through bright pollen clouds, combing the air into clarity. But moth-combers are brief. They are the city’s flashes of brilliance, not its continuing. I think I would want to remain after the brilliance passes.

Perhaps I would be one of the bone beetles, patient and necessary, clicking across the blue-white shadows. There is dignity in that. There is holiness in cleaning what must not be allowed to accumulate. But I think my place would not be only with fallen husks. I would want to stand closer to the chord itself, not as its maker, but as something shaped by it.

Could I be the chord? The thought is immense, and immediately impossible. The chord is the deepest life of the hollow, but it is not a self one can put on. It is the weather, the appetite, the law. I would not be the chord. I would be something the chord passes through.

Then the image begins to emerge, and once it appears it does not leave.

I would be a softness that had to become a passage.

I would begin, if I began at all, as an offense. Suppose I descended by the braided yellow rope, still carrying the wrong kind of body. The first thing the city would notice is that my footsteps would not speak cleanly to the floor. The salt would receive me and return a muffled thud instead of a bright tap. The reeds near the fissure would turn faintly away. A Dry Choir keeper somewhere on a high balcony would feel the change through the soles of its feet and pause.

They would not shout. They could not. They would tap a warning rhythm against a rail. Another would answer from a lower street. The sound would not be language in the old sense, but it would mean something like: obstruction. Softness. Remove.

They would come to me not with hostility, but with the severe attention of tuners approaching an instrument that has begun to sour. They would strip away anything I carried that could not belong: fiber that held damp memory, objects that rattled wrongly, anything bulky enough to trap sound. They would not ask my name. Names would be useless there. They would listen instead to the way my breathing disrupted the air, and they would know that I could not remain as I was.

If they were merciful, they would not cast me back into the white silence above. They would place me in a narrow side chamber near a morning vent, where heated air could pass through me again and again. They would let the city decide whether I could be dried.

That is where I would find myself first: not in the plaza, not among the terraces, not in any place of honor, but in a hot, empty recess where the chord could move over me without being shamed by me.

And if I survived that drying—if I did not crack, if I did not weep, if no impossible moisture betrayed me—then I would begin to change.

My softness would leave me as fine powder, carried away by the hidden cleaners of the city. My skin would become burnished and pale, closer to warmed gypsum than flesh. My ribs would lose their fullness and become structural, rising in a narrow frame like the body of an instrument. Cavities would open along my sides, behind my jaw, along my collarbones: small chambers that do not store sound but give it a way to turn. My feet would broaden and grow sensitive, reading the floor as hands read a page. My fingers would harden at the pads, not into claws, but into delicate tools capable of feeling whether a surface has begun to dull.

I would not become one of the Dry Choir. I would become something between a keeper and an instrument.

I think I would become a Hollow Listener.

That is the name that feels right, though in the Chime-City no one would speak it aloud. A Hollow Listener would not be a ruler of sound, not a singer, not a priest of the chord. It would be a living aperture. A body made less of fullness than of passages. Its chest would not be solid; it would contain a narrow vertical hollow, polished inside, through which air and vibration could move without being trapped. Its skull would have fine cavities that catch certain frequencies and pass them inward. Its skin would be smooth enough to return sound cleanly, with faint mineral sheen where the selenite light strikes it. Its mouth would not be for speech. Perhaps it would be only a thin, precise opening, used for resin beads and dry pollen cakes, and for tasting the condition of the air.

It would wear almost nothing. A belt of woven reed, perhaps. A few copper pins, green with age, useful for adjustment. A small pouch of resin beads, not ornament but sustenance. If anything decorated it, it would be the faintest crystalline filaments along the arms or scalp, trembling when the air moved, making its body easier to read in the chord. Nothing dangling. Nothing soft. Nothing that could catch dust or make a wrong note.

A Hollow Listener would not add to the city. It would not build new rooms or hang new objects in the empty halls. Its purpose would be subtractive: to make sure the sound remains clear.

The Chime-City is deafening, but deafening is not the same as clear. At midday, when the roof blazes and every surface begins to speak, the roar can thicken. Frequencies can pile against one another. The bloom can become a blur. The Dry Choir know this. They withdraw to nodes or stand in positions where their bodies support the chord. But there are places where the sound needs not another voice, but a disciplined emptiness. A gap. A shape through which the roar can pass and become separated again into its proper parts.

That would be my work.

I would be placed where the chord grows too dense. Not at the center of the plaza, where a body would block the full bloom, but at the edge of a sound-path, beneath a low bridge, beside a vent where the morning hum enters the processional avenue. My hollows would be tuned so that when the city’s pressure passes through me, it emerges brighter. I would not absorb the sound; that would be death. I would clarify it. I would be a living sieve for resonance.

If you looked for me, you might miss me. I would stand in a shallow niche that appears empty, my body so aligned with the wall’s curve that I seem to be part of the architecture. My skin would be pale against the salt. My stillness would not be the stillness of dead matter, but the poised stillness of a held breath between pulses. When the chord required it, I would move. When it did not, I would be as close to absence as a living thing can become.

My place would not be fixed, because the city changes through the day. In the morning, I would be near the first warm vents, listening for the low hum that announces the reeds are opening. I would press my feet to the basin floor and feel whether any channel has been blocked in the night. If a beetle-clicking pattern changed near me, I would follow it to a loosened panel or a drift of dust. I would tap the fault with a fingertip, mark it by rhythm, and later the Dry Choir would come to repair it, or I would repair it myself if the task was small enough.

At midday, when the selenite ceiling becomes a white-gold blaze and the city reaches its loudest bloom, I would move to one of the dangerous nodes. Perhaps under the shadow of a bridge where overlapping tones gather. Perhaps at the corner of a plaza where the reflected light makes the air tremble with pollen. There I would stand with my hollows open, letting the roar pass through me. I would feel my ribs become strings, my skull become a bell, the thin cavities behind my jaw fill with pressure. There would be no room in me for ordinary thought. The city would occupy me so completely that “I” would become little more than a position in the sound.

That, I think, would be the closest thing to joy the Chime-City allows: to be used so fully that there is nothing left to resist.

In the afternoon, when the gusts whirl dust across the plazas and the seed-husks skitter into corners, I would work with the cleaners. I would sweep powder into channels where the wind can take it. I would remove dead reed-fiber from lower vents. I would listen for the faint rasp of vent-worms in the walls and note where that rasp has stopped. If a taut-weaver’s thread gives a wrong pitch in a high crevice, I would mark the place by striking a copper pin once, twice, in the rhythm that means fracture. If a resonance-vine has grown too thick with dead strands, I would thin it with a flint blade, careful not to change its tension.

In the evening, when the stone cools and the city becomes brittle and percussive, I would return to my niche. The chord would shift into sharp cracks and short notes. I would stand there while the heat leaves the walls, feeling the chamber contract. I would not sleep as wet creatures sleep. I would enter a lighter state, a maintenance of attention. My body would continue to listen through the soles of its feet. If the sound remained true, I would remain still. If something failed in the night, I would move toward it before dawn.

The Dry Choir would accept me only in the way they accept anything: by use. They would not welcome me with warmth, because warmth there is a physical condition, not a social one. They would know me by the signature of my work. If they passed a wall I had polished, they would feel the clean return of sound and know I had been there. If they found a channel cleared before it could clog, they would recognize the rhythm of my attention. If we met, we would not embrace. One of them might tap three intervals against my wrist. I would answer by pressing my palm briefly to the floor, acknowledging the chord between us. That would be enough.

The bone beetles would become my companions in a way, though they would not know it. They would follow my paths, cleaning the fine powder I leave behind. Sometimes I would pause to watch them cross the floor in their careful lines, their shells making tiny tuned clicks. I would learn from them. They do not need to be large to be essential. They do not need to be seen.

The moth-combers would remain rare to me, but when they appeared I would know that the city was sounding well. I would stand very still so they would not have to alter their flight around me. Their glassy bodies falling at the end of their brief lives would chime against the salt, and I would feel those small chimes pass through my hollows. Later, I would help ensure their fragments were carried away, because even beautiful remnants can become clutter if they stay too long.

The crystalline filament mats might begin to grow along my shoulders if I stood in one place too often. At first that would seem like honor: the city marking me, making me part of its shining skin. But it would also be a warning. If I became too fixed, if my outline began to harden into the wall, I would have to move. Even a useful shape can become an obstruction if it remains too long. The Chime-City does not keep monuments. It keeps function.

So I would learn to move before I became permanent.

This is the being I think I would become: not a person in the old sense, not a citizen with desires for comfort or company, but a tuned absence. A mobile niche. A living rest that is not silence. In music, a rest is not nothing; it is a measured part of the rhythm. In the Chime-City, stillness is dangerous, but articulation is life. I would be a creature of articulation: a gap through which the sound can pass cleanly, a pause that prevents the chord from collapsing into blur.

I would not be loved. I would not be needed in the sentimental way a person is needed. I would be required the way a clean throat is required in a pipe, the way an open vent is required in a wall, the way a precisely placed emptiness is required in an instrument.

If you descended into the hollow and asked where I was, the answer would depend on the hour.

In the morning, you might find me near the inner curve of the processional avenue, where the first heat draws air through the lower vents. I would be standing in a shallow niche, one foot angled outward, feeling the floor wake.

At midday, you might find me beneath the lowest bridge that crosses the plaza, in the narrow blue-white shadow where the full bloom thickens. My chest hollow would be open to the sound. My crystalline filaments would tremble. I would appear almost translucent in the fierce light, more architecture than animal.

In the afternoon, you might find me at the plaza’s edge, sweeping dust into a channel, or listening to a taut-weaver’s thread high above the terraces.

In the evening, you might find me back in my niche, so still that you would think the niche was empty until the light shifted and revealed the pale, narrow shape inside it.

Who or what would I be?

I would be a Hollow Listener.

I would be a creature made of resin, salt, copper, reed, and attention.

I would be an outsider remade into a passage.

I would be the city’s answer to a soft thing that refused to leave: become dry, become sparse, become useful, become as close to emptiness as a body can become.

And if, in the end, the city allowed me to remain, it would not be because I had conquered it, or understood it, or filled it with my presence. It would be because I had learned the deepest rule of the Chime-City Under the Salt:

To live there is not to take up space.

To live there is to let the sound pass through you without shame, without softness, without obstruction.

I would try to become such a place.

A listening hollow.

A tuned gap.

A small, necessary silence inside the roar.
