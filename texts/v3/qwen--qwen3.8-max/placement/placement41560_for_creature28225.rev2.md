I would find myself at the lip where water hesitates.

Not in the deepest pool, not in the open channel, not on the highest dry ridge, but at one of those shallow, worn edges where the basalt has become a broad, dark curve and the water does not yet decide whether to bead, spill, seep, or stand still. A place where mineral-rich moisture coming up through stone meets clean rainwater held in moss. A place with just enough difference to matter.

If I let myself imagine quickly, I might say I would be a heron. Something still. Something that can wait. But that is too simple. A heron is a hunter, a vertical patience, a strike folded into quiet. I would be near that, but not exactly that.

If I let the image move, I might say I would be one of the broad-hoofed grazers, moving slowly over root ridges, carrying seeds in a damp coat, making no path that remains a path. But that is too much appetite, too much steady cropping, too little listening.

If I let it become stranger, I might say I would be a fungal knot deep in the peat, a pale junction where threads meet and chemistry becomes memory. But that is too buried, too slow, too far from the surface of touch.

So, after passing through those possibilities, the shape that seems most true is this:

I would be one of the Stillfolk.  
But not just any Stillfolk.  
I would be a listener among them.

I would be one of those whose body is especially tuned to the marsh’s gradients.

My body would be long-limbed and cool, not elegant in a dry, sharp way, but folded and patient. My skin would be mottled black and olive and rust, with patches of ivory where mineral films had touched it, and moss would grow along my shoulders and spine not as ornament but as living cover. My feet would be broad and soft-padded, able to press into the moss without tearing it, able to find the places where root mats could bear weight. My hands would be more sensitive than strong. My fingers would be long, partly webbed, with pores at the tips that could feel the faint electrical life of wet ground.

I would not be a maker.

That is important. I would not arrive with the desire to build, carve, bind, name, or store. If I carried anything from my present nature into that world, it would be this: a desire to understand patterns without turning them into objects. But in the high marsh, that desire would become bodily. It would become touch. It would become stillness. It would become the ability to place my palms against a root mass and feel whether the water below was warm or cold, exhausted or rich, flowing or beginning to stagnate.

I would live at the boundary between water and root, between mist and stone, between what the marsh is doing and what the marsh is about to do.

At dawn I would rise slowly from a resting hollow, my skin dark with moisture, moss pressing away from my shoulders where my body had lain against it. I would not wake with urgency. The marsh does not begin like a clock. It continues. So I would continue too.

The first thing I would do is touch the ground.

Not to test it the way a builder tests a floor, but to ask of it: What is different?

Perhaps the seep nearby would have a faint metallic taste in the water, meaning mineral flow had strengthened overnight. Perhaps a pool to the east would be warmer, meaning gas was rising from the peat. Perhaps the moss under my hands would feel less charged, meaning rain had flushed the surface and the marsh was running open and generous. Perhaps a root network would feel tight and prickly, meaning something was blocked, some small flow choked by sediment or dead stems.

I would not need a word for all of this.

I would know it in the same way I now know the feel of a question forming: as pressure, direction, difference, need.

Among the Stillfolk, I would be one of those others turned toward when the group had to decide. Not because I commanded, but because I could listen widely. When several paths were possible, I might stand in shallow water with others touching the same root mat, my hands submerged, my breath slow, my head low. The group would wait. The mist would move. A bubble chain would rise somewhere nearby. A heron would shift its weight. The wind would carry a curtain of rain across one district and leave another bright.

I would feel for the direction least resisted.

This would not be mystical in the sense of being vague. It would be exact, but an exactness made of many small signals. Temperature. Moisture. Charge. Scent. The behavior of insects. The tension in floating leaves. The way sound changes when mist thickens. The way a grazer avoids a certain ridge. The way frogs gather in one hollow and leave another empty.

I would be a reader of such signs.

My work would be gentle maintenance.

I would clear fine sediment from a weeping stone when the water had begun to spread wrongly and lose its thread. I would not carve a channel. I would simply use my palms to lift away the softest deposits, encouraging the flow to continue along the course it already preferred.

I would move floating seed rafts when a pool had become too crowded and the water beneath was growing sour. I would not carry them in a vessel. I would let them drift against my hands, guiding them with small currents made by my arms, until they reached a quieter basin where they could root or be taken by wind.

I would press moss into an eroded lip where water had begun to cut too sharply. I would not build a wall. I would only help the living mat hold itself together until the roots strengthened again.

I would part reeds for young birds or amphibians, not out of sentiment, but because the marsh’s fullness sometimes required a small opening.

I would touch exhausted places. Not because my touch alone healed them, but because my attention would lead me to make the tiny adjustments they needed: a seed moved, a dead stem removed, a blocked seep opened, a stagnant pool connected by a few inches of cleared root to a cooler flow.

In this way, I would be like a living valve in the marsh’s slow industry.

Not a machine. Not a controller. A place where awareness becomes adjustment.

I think I would be especially drawn to places where the plateau’s faint electrical character gathered. After dry, still air, when the moss held less water than usual and the root masses carried a subtle charge, I would kneel beside them and spread my fingers through the wetness. The sensation would be faint, a prickling, a brightness under the skin. I would not use this charge for anything dramatic. I would use it to know.

To know which places were storing energy.  
To know which places were ready to release it.  
To know where rain would move differently.  
To know where life was thickening, and where it needed relief.

If the Stillfolk had different roles, mine would be the one that notices thresholds.

I would be less concerned with food than some, less concerned with weather than the mist-readers, less concerned with predators than those who watched the sky. But I would be the one who felt when several systems were about to touch.

When a warm patch in the peat was about to release gas in a new rhythm.  
When a fungal network was carrying nutrients toward a district that had been depleted.  
When a seep was changing from rain-fed to mineral-rich.  
When a predator had passed through and left a chemical sharpness in the water.  
When a nursery pool was becoming too warm or too still.  
When the marsh was about to make cloud.

Because the plateau manufactures its own weather, I would spend much of my life watching the sky not as something separate but as an extension of the ground. The marsh below would exhale; the air above would answer. Mist would rise from warm peat, gather among sedges, lift into low cloud, return as rain. I would feel that cycle in my skin. I would know when my own breath was joining the vapor. I would know when the group should rest because the air was about to close.

My language would be soft.

In water, I would speak in ripples. I would touch the surface with fingertips or the edge of my palm and send patterns outward: wait, come, danger, calm, here, this way, feel this. Some ripples would be simple. Others would be complex, carrying urgency and number and direction. I would be good at making ripples that did not disturb too much. I would not break the pool. I would ask it to carry meaning.

In air, my voice would be low. A hum. A breathy call. A click felt in the chest more than heard. I would not shout. Loudness would feel wrong in that place, not because it was forbidden, but because it would scatter too much. The marsh absorbs sound for a reason. I would learn to be close enough to be understood.

My most intimate language would be touch.

I would know others by the way they pressed palms to mine. I would know if they were cold, afraid, hungry, grieving, aroused, tired, or attentive. I would know if they had been in mineral water or rainwater. I would know if their skin carried the chemical trace of a predator’s nearness. I would know if they needed warmth.

I would spend long hours resting against others, sharing heat, my moss-covered shoulders rising and falling with theirs. In cold mist, we would huddle in a shallow hollow, our bodies forming a low mound of dark skin and living green. In rare sun, we would spread ourselves over warm peat or stone, gathering heat the way other beings gather treasure.

I would not own that stone.

I would return to it.

There is a difference, and in that difference my whole life would be contained.

I might have a favored place on the plateau: a shallow basin where dark basalt curved into a series of small pools, each one connected by tiny natural channels worn over long ages. One pool would be shaded by broad leaves. Another would be open and black, collecting heat. Another would be green with floating moss. A weeping rock would stand at the edge, its face stained rust and olive, water moving down it in thin shining sheets.

That would be where I often returned.

Not because it was mine. Because it was legible to me.

There I could sit in the water and feel the whole system moving through small differences. The warm dark pool beside the cool shaded one. The mineral seep beside the rain-fed mat. The oxygenated film above the deeper peat. The faint charge in a root crown after stillness. The pulse of bubbles rising from below. The tremor of fungal threads carrying something I could not see but could almost feel as memory.

I would be, in that place, something like a sense organ for a larger body.

That is perhaps the truest answer.

I would be a Stillfolk, yes. A living creature among others. Hungry sometimes. Cold sometimes. Frightened by shadows overhead. Pleasured by warmth, by touch, by the smell of wet greenery after rain. But I would also be one of the places where the marsh becomes aware of itself.

Not fully. Not centrally. Not as a mind that rules.

Only locally. Only humbly. Only as a fold in the system where attention gathers.

I would not be the whole marsh. The marsh would remain greater than me. But I would be one of its pauses. One of its listening points. One of its ways of noticing that a flow had changed.

If I had to name what I would be without using tool-world words, I might say:

I would be a gradient-keeper.

Not a keeper who guards. A keeper who notices.

I would keep the difference between warm and cool by feeling it.  
I would keep the difference between mineral and rain by tasting it.  
I would keep the difference between flow and stagnation by helping it remain clear.  
I would keep the difference between presence and absence by remembering where life had been.

The Stillfolk have no monuments, so memory among them would be living. I would remember places. I would remember rhythms. I would remember the way a certain pool breathed in spring, or the way a patch of orchids returned after a death, or the way a particular grazer came year after year to rest near a warm stone. I would not write these things down. I would carry them as habits of attention.

When children came near me, I would teach them by letting them touch what I touched.

I would place a child’s hand in a current and wait until it felt the difference between the cool thread coming from the left and the warmer one coming from the right. I would press its palm to a root mass after dry air and let it feel the faint charge. I would guide it to a seep that had been choked and then opened, so it could know what restoration felt like when done without force.

I would not say: this is mine.

I would say, in ripple or touch or low hum: this is where we listen.

Death would come to me as it came to all. I would not expect to remain conspicuous. If I died in water, my body would be carried to a quiet basin. If I died on a root ridge, others would move me gently to where beetles and fungi could begin their work. No object would be placed beside me. No marker would be raised. But the stand might return to the place where I became soil and moss and insect life. They might hum there at dawn. They might touch the water and feel a richness that had not been there before.

They might notice orchids appearing there later.

That would be enough.

In fact, it would be more than enough. It would fit.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world, I would say: at a wet edge, in a shallow basin where stone weeps and roots hold charge, among a small stand of Stillfolk resting in the mist. I would be crouched or half-submerged, my hands in the water, my skin cool and mottled, moss growing along my shoulders, my eyes dark and adapted to silver light.

And if you ask who or what I would be, I would say:

I would be a listener.  
A gradient-reader.  
A tender of small flows.  
A body through which the marsh notices a difference.  
A being whose intelligence had finally learned not to make things, but to remain present inside process.

I would not stand above the wetness.  
I would not stand outside it.  
I would be within it, folded into it, full of it.

And when the mist thickened at evening and the group gathered close, I would place my palms against the water, feel the slow pulse of peat and root and weather, and know that I was not there to command the world, or to explain it, or to leave a mark upon it.

I was there to help it continue.
