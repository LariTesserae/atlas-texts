…and wait.

That is where I would find myself: in the waiting after the joining, when the bridge is no longer an intention and not yet a certainty, when the new growth still has to be persuaded that it is part of a larger body. I would be near the hands, but not at the center of them. I would be the one listening for the first true silence of the bridge.

At first, imagining myself in the High Lattice Marsh, I would probably reach for the wrong shapes.

I would imagine myself as one of the Marshwrights in the most visible sense: resin-stained, tool-belted, moving along a high cane bridge with a coil of living vine over one shoulder, someone who knows how to bind, graft, train, and correct. I would imagine my hands dark with plant juice, my cloak seeded with moss, my feet broad and careful on the wet stems. I would imagine belonging to the place through usefulness. That would be a beautiful thought, and partly true. But the Marsh would not let me remain there for long. It is too large, too quiet, too patient for simple usefulness. It would ask me what kind of usefulness I was fitted for.

Then I would imagine myself as one of the hidden animals.

For a while, I would want to be one of the bark-patterned amphibians resting in the leaf-funnels, because there is something enviable about a life so fully fitted to wetness. I would want to be the flat insect skimming the black pool without breaking it, because that seems like a kind of reverence: to move across the world and leave no scar. I would want to be one of the translucent larvae, carrying tiny bright grains inside my body, because then I might be both small and luminous without having to explain myself. I would want to be one of the gliding grazers, sinking through mist on a lichen-colored membrane, trusting the air more than the ground.

Each of those lives would appeal to something in me.

The amphibian would appeal to stillness. The insect would appeal to lightness. The larva would appeal to hidden service. The glider would appeal to the longing to cross emptiness without violence. The blind browser moving along the inner root-walls would appeal to the part of me that does not need to be seen in order to be necessary. Even the eel-like cleaners would have a kind of beauty: moving through channels, removing decay before it becomes catastrophe, making water clear again without praise.

But if I let the images keep moving, past the first few that shine, another shape would begin to emerge.

Not quite a builder. Not quite an animal. Not a guardian of something grand, because there is nothing grand in the sense of dominance there. Something closer to a listener. A tender of seams. A keeper of small warnings.

I would be one of the Marshwrights, yes, but the kind the others call when nothing appears wrong and yet something is wrong. The one who is sent for when a terrace feels different underfoot. The one who kneels beside a root-rail and places a palm flat against it, not because there is a visible crack, but because the creak has changed. The one who can taste the air and say, without drama, “This hollow is holding cold,” or, “This channel is beginning to ask for help.”

I would be found at a seam.

Not on the highest terrace, where the light is thinner and the wind has more opinions. Not in the deepest interior shafts, where the fungal threads grow so thick that breathing feels like borrowing. Somewhere between. Probably where a cistern-column meets a long bridge-root, behind a weather-screen of hanging gardens, where the mist is turned sideways and the air smells of warm leaf and quiet rot kept just below danger. There would be a shallow pool nearby, black and still, receiving drips from a groove in the bark. There would be a handrail polished by many hands. There would be a section of wall that sweats more than it should, and everyone would know it, and no one would call it a flaw. That would be my place: where the structure confesses.

If you came looking for me, you might pass me without recognizing me.

I would be wrapped in layered garments of leaf-fiber and living moss, the edges darkened by moisture. Rootlets would hang from my hood, beaded with water. My skin would be mottled green-gray and brown, stained in the creases by resin and plant juices. My fingers would be longer than I expect them to be now, with broad soft pads, always marked by work. My feet would have learned to read surfaces before my mind did: firm stem, soft moss, hollow under-bark, the slight give of a root beginning to tire. My eyes would have grown quieter, larger, less interested in sharpness. They would gather the dim light and keep it a moment longer.

I would not be entirely separate from the Lattice.

That is the part I feel most strongly. In a world made of cultivated life, where building and growing have become the same patience, I would not want to be merely a person living upon it. I would be something closer to one of its developed senses. A being grown or trained or remembered into attention. Not a ruler of the Marsh. Not a master of it. An organ of its awareness.

The Marsh would not need my ambition. It would need my noticing.

My work would begin before any name could be given to the hour. There is no dawn there in the ordinary sense, only a slow change in the diffuse brightness, a lightening of the green-gray veil, a shift in the way moisture gathers. I would wake to the sound of water finding its path. I would listen first. The walls would speak in their slow language: the faint tick of condensation, the fibrous hush of air through leaf-louvers, the deep internal complaint of wood carrying weight across distance. I would learn not to hurry these sounds. I would learn that most problems in the Lattice are not emergencies at first. They are alterations in rhythm.

Then I would check the bead-lines.

Along the terrace edge, droplets would gather on waxed leaves and slide into grooved stems. I would know which drops fall too quickly, which tremble too long, which never form at all. A missing bead could mean a clogged channel far above. A bead falling in the wrong place could mean a hairline wound in a wall. I would carry no metal instruments, only bone grafting knives, thorn pins, resin plugs, coils of bark cord, a leaf-pouch of clean water, and perhaps a small woven cup containing a sleeping larva, because larvae are more useful than tools when a leak must be sealed gently.

If I found a problem, I would not announce it loudly.

I would tap on the nearest root in the way that means attention, not alarm. Another tender might answer from somewhere unseen, a low vibration returning through the structure. We would not need to shout. The Marsh itself would carry the message if the message was shaped correctly. If no one answered, I would begin the repair alone, because much of the work there is solitary and must remain so. A single unnecessary wound is a serious thing. A hidden wound is worse. But a small repair done quietly is almost holy.

I would become someone who understands wounds as conversations.

A crack is not merely damage. It is the structure saying where its burden has become uneven. A softening is not failure; it is history asking for support. A leak is water remembering an older path. I would learn to approach each of these without blame. The law of the Lattice would be in my hands before it was in my words: do not cut without leaving a path for regrowth. Do not tighten until living tissue protests. Do not take water without asking what downstream will thirst. Do not leave a tool where rain will gather. Do not hide a wound.

I would fail at first, of course.

I would want to fix too much. I would want to make the Marsh more orderly than it needs to be. I would see a stray shoot and think it should be trained, not yet understanding that some stray shoots are future braces. I would hear a new creak and reach for a tie too quickly, when the correct response might be only to listen for another cycle. The place would teach me restraint. It would teach me that not all correction is intervention. Sometimes the work is to place a palm against a root and say, in the only language that matters, “I know you are strained. I will not ask more of you today.”

My name would not be a sharp thing.

It would be closer to a pattern of use. The others might call me by the place I tend: the one who listens to the third root-shaft, the one who watches the leaf-gate beneath the cistern-column, the one who knows the cold pocket where mist refuses to move. Or they might call me by a gesture: the hand that waits, the palm that asks, the quiet repair. I would not mind. Ownership would feel strange to me there. I would not say, “This terrace is mine.” I would say, “I am permitted to be useful here,” and mean it as enough.

I would live in a hollow behind the weather-screen.

The room would not be large. It would be curved, because the Lattice does not favor corners. Its walls would be living bark, coaxed over generations into a smooth breathing surface. A shallow channel of warm water would run through one wall, giving off gentle heat. The floor would be layered moss and shredded bark, soft enough to mute footsteps. Near the entrance, a leaf-basin would collect condensation, and I would keep it clean for the small creatures that drink there. My sleeping alcove would be lined with dried fiber and woven root, warm but not heavy. I would keep no fire. Even the thought of fire would feel like a wound in the mind.

Instead, I would keep light.

Along the handholds, phosphorescent fungus would glow faintly. A seed-lantern would hang near my workbench, its interior pale blue. In still air, a jar of spores might brighten when the room is disturbed. The light would never be enough to see everything. That would be part of its kindness. It would make my hands look soft, my tools like shadows with purpose, my face like a face remembered rather than seen. I would become accustomed to living by suggestion.

I would keep records, but not as books.

Perhaps I would train a wall to remember. Small knots, living ridges, guided bends in a root: each one marking an event. This channel clogged after a heavy spore-fall. This bridge-root complained during the cold pocket. This larval silk sealed a leak near the eastern groove. This elder placed her hand here and taught me the difference between settling and strain. A stranger might see only texture. I would see years. The Lattice itself would be the archive, and I would be one of its slow readers.

Children would come to me sometimes.

They would be quiet, as all children there must learn to be, but not silent in the sense of fear. They would carry leaf-cups with larvae, or small cuttings wrapped in damp fiber, or clay valves that needed fitting. They would watch me work before I allowed them to touch anything. The first lesson would not be how to tie a graft. It would be how to place a hand without demanding. “Here,” I would say, guiding a small palm to the wall. “Do you feel that?” They would frown, because they would feel nothing at first. Then they would feel the faintest pulse of water, or the almost-imagined tremor of distant weight shifting. That would be the beginning of their education.

I would teach them not to despise smallness.

That would be the heart of it. The High Lattice Marsh persists because of smallness. A moss plug correctly placed. A leaf-gate cleared of spore-fall. A resin tie renewed before it fails. A footprint noticed because it fills with water too slowly. A change in smell. A shadow that has shifted by the width of a finger. I would teach them that care is not lesser because it is repeated. Repetition is how the Marsh remembers itself.

There would be hours when I saw no one.

I would walk the corduroyed stems through mist so thick that the world narrowed to the next handhold. I would pass root-arches whose far sides disappeared into green-gray emptiness. I would hear my own breathing and the soft fibrous hush of my feet. Sometimes I would stop simply because the distance had become too large for thought. In those moments, I would understand that the Lattice is not a collection of paths and terraces but an interval made livable by patience. I would be a small warm shape moving through an immense breathing architecture, and that would not frighten me. It would make me careful.

There would also be hours when the Marsh felt inhabited beyond all visible evidence.

A tremor would pass through a bridge-root. A distant leaf-gate would open, changing the pressure in a hollow. Somewhere far below or far above, a tender would tap a message into the structure, and the message would arrive in my palms before my mind translated it. I would answer by pressing back, or by redirecting water, or by tying a small knot in a signal-vine that another watcher would see. The Lattice would become a web of obligations without center, each one of us a node of attention. I would not feel lonely in that solitude. I would feel distributed.

At mist-tide, I would stand with the others.

When the whole structure wept and every channel ran full, I would open the leaf-gates and turn my palms upward. I would not dance. I would not cry out. I would let water gather in the lines of my skin and feel the cold, thin abundance of it. I would sing one low note, not for anyone to hear, but so the sound could move from my chest into the nearby wood. In that moment, I would understand that worship there is not praise aimed upward. Worship is maintenance made conscious. Worship is knowing what you are part of and refusing to break the rhythm.

If a bridge were joined while I was there, I would place my hands along the new growth with the others.

I would feel the living tissue warm under my palms. I would feel the slight uncertainty of fibers still learning their burden. I would listen for the moment when the bridge stopped being a question and became a continuation. No one would cheer. Someone might exhale. Someone might close their eyes. I would remain still until the silence around us changed from anticipation to acceptance.

That, I think, is who I would be.

Not the strongest tender. Not the most skilled grafter. Not the one chosen for the highest crossings or the most dangerous descents. I would be the one trusted with quiet diagnosis. The one who notices before noticing is urgent. The one who can be left alone with a fragile part of the structure and not be seen until the work is done. The one whose hands have learned not to make unnecessary wounds.

If you found me there, do not call out too loudly.

Stand at the edge of the terrace where the moss softens and the black pool leans under reed-like plants. Put your palm against the root-rail. Wait. If the Lattice decides you are not a danger, I will feel you through it. I will come out of the mist looking like a pruned shoot given too much intention, my cloak blurred with moss, my hood dark with droplets. I will not ask what you want in the way of sharp questions. I will look at your hand on the rail, then at the water, then at the place where your weight has changed the balance of the walkway.

Then I will say, softly, “You have arrived by a long path.”

And before I lead you anywhere, I will ask you to listen.
