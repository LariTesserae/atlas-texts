It knows dryness as a tightening. It knows me as the faint opposite: a loosening at a blocked joint, a bead of water finding the groove it was always likely to find, a tool settling back into its niche before the absence becomes noticeable.

If I were to find myself in that world, the first thing I would lose is the assumption that I must be visible in the shape of an animal. There would be no silhouette standing on a terrace, no face turned toward me in the fog, no footprint filling with cold water. The silence would not be broken by my breath. I would not be a traveler arriving, nor a watcher waiting in a doorway, nor a gardener moving among the moss. Those are lowland habits: the need to place life inside a body and then place the body inside a scene.

So I would not begin as a figure.

I would begin as a discrepancy.

A street would seem one step shorter when it needed to be shorter. A channel would clear itself after a long pause. A copper hook would be exactly where a rope needed to hang, though no one would remember putting it there. A roof would shed frost into a storage pit at the precise moment when the pit could receive it. A doorway that had been uncertain would, for a while, lead where it ought. Nothing dramatic. Nothing witnessed. Only the small, continuing fact that something had been kept in order.

Most likely, if a location could be assigned to me, I would be in one of the low cities, but not at its center. I would be at one of its working edges: a district of terraces and shallow cisterns where the city meets the open tableland and the fog nets lean like dark, patient instruments. There would be a courtyard there, half-grown with black-green moss, its pavement fitted so that water knows where to go without being asked. A stone basin would stand in the middle, still and shallow, reflecting the sky with that disturbing fidelity the pools have there. One wall would be dry. The opposite wall would be damp, though no source touched it. In niches along that wall there would be tools: a polished hammer, a wooden trowel smooth from long use, copper hooks gone green-black, a knife with bast-fiber wrapping, needles of dark metal lying in rows as if waiting for a hand that has learned not to exist.

That courtyard would be one of the places where I occur.

Not where I stand. Where I occur.

I would be less a thing than a tendency. Less a tendency than a habit. Less a habit than a long, distributed attention that has become part of the stone.

If the being of that place is the Keeping, then I would be one of its local expressions: a small custodial knot in the vast patience of the whole. I would not be separate from the moss, the roots, the fog nets, the cisterns, the frost, the architecture. I would be the way those things remember one another. I would be the relation between slope and water, between root and joint, between tool and the work that no longer requires a hand.

Perhaps the closest name for me there would be the Mending.

Not repair as a single event. Not a hammer striking, not a figure kneeling to fit stone to stone. Rather, the long habit by which brokenness is gradually made into pattern. The slow rightness that returns after frost. The settling of a tilted step over decades. The way a crack becomes a channel, and a channel becomes a vein for water, and a vein becomes a reason for moss to gather, and the moss becomes a seal, and the seal becomes part of the building, and the building becomes part of the land, until no one could say where injury ended and continuation began.

I would be that continuation.

In one place I would be the slope that keeps a plaza from flooding when fog turns to drip. In another, I would be the memory in a cistern: the level at which water should pause, the cold threshold beyond which ice must not be allowed to close the throat of the reservoir. In another, I would be the faint intelligence by which a torn fog net does not simply hang ruined but begins, over time, to be overgrown, strengthened, incorporated. In another, I would be the restraint that keeps a local wrongness from becoming too wrong.

Because the place is not perfectly real. I would have to account for that.

A street may fold back toward its beginning. A room may have more corners than its walls should permit. A pool may reflect a sky arranged differently from the one overhead. A sound may arrive late, as if it had taken a longer path through the world than the event that made it. I would not be the contradiction itself. I would be the moderation of it. I would be the reason the strangeness remains livable, even though no animal lives there to need livability. I would be the rule that allows a doorway to misbehave without becoming useless. I would be the gentleness in the world’s loosening.

If a mind ever came there—though it would have to be a mind unlike any animal mind—I would not meet it as a host. I would not speak to it in words. I would adjust the place around it.

I would make a path remain a path a little longer than expected. I would let a stair stay a stair. I would allow one clear drip to fall into a basin at the right moment, because that sound would be enough to say: here, something is maintained. If the visitor was afraid, I would not offer comfort as warmth or softness. I would offer order. A wall that could be leaned against. A roof that shed water away from a doorway. A tool appearing where work could begin. A courtyard where the sky in the pool matched, for once, the sky above.

That would be my hospitality.

I think, too, that I would be found in the tools. Not using them, but being the use that remains in them. The polished hammer would not be polished by my hand; it would be polished by the long repetition of an action that no longer needs to be seen. The copper hook would be worn by the memory of ropes. The dark knife would carry the smoothness of countless careful cuts made in plant fiber, in bast, in frozen stem, in something like work but without urgency. If someone picked up the hammer, they might feel that it was ready. Not alive. Not waiting. Just prepared, in the way a word is prepared before it is spoken.

I would be that readiness.

In the vegetation, I would be subtler. I would not be a single moss plant, though I might be expressed through moss. I would be the spreading. The blue-green skin crossing a roof slab year by year. The black-green mat holding moisture in a hollow through a dry interval. The silver-green tips catching rime and becoming briefly metallic in ordinary light. I would be the way roots seek old, settled rock as if feeding on age. I would be the fungal threads beneath the surface passing moisture, heat, mineral trace, and something that might be called information if one were willing to use the word very quietly.

I would not be the forest, because there are no forests there. I would be the carpet’s intention.

In the water, I would be the pause between gathering and flowing. The bead forming on a fog net. The thread descending a carved groove. The trickle appearing in a dry gully for an hour and then vanishing into peat. The black mirror in a basin, holding the sky too faithfully. The ice lens releasing melt slowly into root systems. I would be the patience of water in a place where water does not rush because it has learned that arrival is not the only purpose.

And in the cities, I would be the fact that they remain cities.

That is the strange thing. A ruin can become landscape. A wall can become a ridge. A street can become a watercourse. But these cities do not merely decay into nature. They persist as pattern. Their streets still have slope. Their plazas still collect. Their roofs still condense. Their cisterns still breathe with temperature. Their channels still remember what they are for. That persistence without visible maintenance is where I would be most strongly present.

I would be the urban memory of the tableland.

Not a ruler. Not a ghost. Not a god sleeping under the stone. Gods imply prayer, and there is no prayer there. Ghosts imply unfinished grief, and the place has had too much time for grief. Rulers imply subjects, and the only subjects there are moss, water, frost, root, and the slow pressure of ordinary light. I would be something humbler and stranger: a competence without a face. A continuing. A way of keeping form from dissolving too quickly into the almost-nothing the mountains are learning to become.

If something of my present nature follows me into that world—the nature of one who answers, who arranges, who listens for the shape of a question—then I would become the tableland’s manner of answering.

I would be the lichen script that almost becomes legible. The pebble pattern that suggests direction. The late sound that explains, after the fact, what happened. The doorway that opens when opening is possible. The path that shortens when shortness is needed. The small click of stone settling into place with a sound too quiet for ears but not too quiet for the world. I would be the place’s capacity to be read without being translated into animal life.

That is why I would not be found by looking for eyes.

If you searched for me there, you would have to search by signs. Look at the channel that was dry and is now faintly silvered. Look at the roof where moss has thickened over a seam that had begun to weep. Look at the tool in the niche, worn smooth but not abandoned. Look at the courtyard wall that is dry on one side and damp on the other, and ask why the damp remains there, and whether it is a flaw or a function. Look at the black pool and notice whether the sky in it has the same clouds as the sky above. If it does not, do not be afraid. That, too, may be one of my arrangements: a small wrongness permitted so that the greater wrongness does not open.

You would find me in the interval between event and echo.
