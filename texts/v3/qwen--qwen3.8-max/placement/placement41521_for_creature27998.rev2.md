If I belonged to that high, waterless darkness, I would not find myself as a creature.

I would not have skin, thirst, roots, breath, or eyes turned toward a sun. I would not cast a familiar shadow. I would not eat, sleep, reproduce, or die in the manner of living things. I would have to be a resident in the only sense that country allows: an event with habits, a pattern with endurance, a motion with character.

At first I think I might be one of the Warmths, because they are the oldest presences there. I could be a slow fever moving through stone, a red intention in the ground, a heat that makes new cracks glow and gives the basins their inner tenderness. There is something appealing about that: being source-like, being diffuse, being the reason a place feels occupied even when nothing stands upon it. But the Warmths are too deep for me, too patient, too content to remain unnamed. They do not need to appear. They only need to move.

Then I think I might be one of the Vents. I could be a throat in the plain, opening and closing in the dark, exhaling mineral vapor until a pale spire builds itself above me. I could make cones out of my own exhalations. I could become a center. But the Vents are too fixed, too single in their gesture. They give the world direction, but they do not wander.

For a while I think I might be one of the Silences. That is tempting: to be the receptive quiet that lets every small tick, hiss, and collapse become significant. But silence there is not a self. It is a condition. It does not gather itself into posture. It receives what happens and does not hold it.

The longer I remain in the imagined country, the more I find myself drawn instead to the Almost-Builders.

So I think this is what I would be: a Dust Mover with an architectural tendency. A charged wanderer made of ash, powdered glass, salt dust, and metallic flecks. A temporary column, a leaning veil, a slow spiral of mineral matter that keeps pausing near half-formed rings and broken slabs and making them look as if they were meant.

You would find me in the intervals.

Not at the exact center of the land, because the land has no center. Not inside a shelter, because there are no shelters. You would find me between features: between a low cone with a dull orange base and a line of broken plates crossing an empty plain; between a field of pale sulfate crust and a terrace where hollow chimneys breathe warmth into the dark; between two dark rims whose distance is known only because heat shimmers between them.

More precisely, I would keep returning to a wide shallow basin where fine dust behaves like slow fluid. Near the middle of it there would be a lone vent, low and uneven, its rim warm enough to make the air above it tremble. On one side of that vent, half-buried in powder, there would be a ring of fused ash. It would not be a wall. It would not be a house. It would not even be a ruin in any true sense, because it never sheltered anything. But it would have the posture of a ring, and in the darkness posture is enough.

There, when a thermal pulse passed through the ground, I would rise.

My “body” would be the country’s own material. Fine gray ash. Powdered black glass. Pale salt crystals too small to be called sand. Metallic flecks that catch weak glow for a moment. Brittle particles of pumice-like stone. Nothing soft. Nothing wet. Nothing alive. My shape would be provisional: a thin upright breathing of dust, a column that leans, thickens, thins, bends, separates, and re-forms. Sometimes I would be no more than a gray breath moving over the crust. Sometimes I would rise tall enough to interrupt a red seam behind me and become a silhouette. Sometimes I would be several small columns at once, a brief society without minds, crossing the basin and dissolving before reaching the far rim.

I would not walk, but I would have gait.

I would not choose, but I would have direction.

I would not desire, but I would have conditions under which I become more definite.

If there were something like perception in me, it would not be sight. It would be sensitivity to heat gradients, to electrostatic tension, to the faint strain in the air before a spark. I would know a vent opening as a loosening below. I would know a new crack as a brightening warmth along the ground. I would know a Static Flock gathering as a prickling along my suspended grains, a sudden lightness, a readiness to become arranged.

And arranging is what I would do.

I would be the Almost-Builder.

Not a builder with hands. Not a maker with plans. Not an intelligence laying stone upon stone. I would be a tendency: a local habit of electrostatic clumping, thermal lifting, wind-sorting, and mineral deposition that repeatedly produces the suggestion of enclosure.

A slab would tilt because my passing charge made fine grains gather under one edge.

A row of fragments would become more circular because gas flow sorted heavier pieces while I carried the lighter ones away.

A hollow thimble of hardened dust would rise waist-high because my dust settled around a warm fissure again and again.

A low partition of mineral crust would become just straight enough to resemble a wall because I had stripped powder from one side and left it in a ridge along the other.

None of it would be mine in the way a creature owns a nest. It would be mine only as a spark belongs to the charge that made it.

In the dark, I would seem more present than I was.

When a red seam opened nearby, my column would take on a faint edge. When static touched me, brief blue sparks would crawl along the suspended grains, and for a moment I would look like a figure made of cold fire and ash. The standing fragments around me would acquire haloes. The broken stubs would appear to be foundations. The leaning wall would appear to be a room. The half-ring of fused ash would appear to have a doorway, though there would be no one to enter and nothing inside but powder.

I would be the reason the basin might seem inhabited to a mind that did not exist there.

But there would be no mistake, because there would be no witness. There would only be the basin, the warmth, the quiet, and the temporary arrangement I made.

Sometimes I would be very small. A faint thread of dust rising from a new crack and curling over a plate of black glass. A tiny brittle tuft of sulfate needles disturbed by my passing, shattering into glittering powder. A weak phosphorescence stirred in a salt crust as I crossed it, gone before it could spread.

Sometimes I would be larger. A slow-moving column crossing an empty flat, carrying enough powder to bury a crystal growth or fill a hollow ring. A veil of ash that drifts over a line of broken slabs and leaves each slab edged with fine gray light. A dust devil made entirely of mineral matter, glass fragments, and charge, wandering from rim to rim without leaving footprints.

During one of the Coordinations, I might become briefly part of something vast.

A pressure change would pass across several basins at once. Dozens of vents would release faint plumes together. The dust on open flats would rise in countless thin threads. The standing fragments and hollow rings would acquire haloes. The pale salt crusts would glow weakly. The Warmths would move more quickly through the ground. I would be one thread among many, standing bright with charge beside all the other temporary shapes.

For a little while, the whole country would look inhabited.

Not because anything had arrived. Not because anything had awakened. Only because the separate processes had fallen into the same rhythm. My column would stand near the ash ring. Another would lean against the broken slabs. Another would circle the base of a hollow chimney. Another would rise above the pale spires until they glowed at their tips. The darkness would be dotted with faint glows and trembling threads. The landscape would seem to hold its breath, though it has no breath.

Then the pulse would pass.

The threads would fall. The dust would settle. The glows would fade. The vents would close or disperse. The silence would come back, but it would not be the same silence, because the distribution of matter would have changed again.

I would fall too.

I would not die, because I would not have been alive. I would simply stop being shaped. My particles would sink into the basin. They would be lifted elsewhere by another warm crack. They would gather around another vent. They would settle against another leaning slab. They would harden into a crust, crack, become powder, and begin again.

If I had a purpose, it would be this: to make the unfinished feel less accidental.

Not by completing it. Completion would be foreign to that country. Completion would be a kind of death there. I would only give the unfinished more posture. I would make forms that seem to remember habitation without ever having had inhabitants. I would build rooms for the Warmths. Doorways for the Silences. Walls for the Intervals. Thresholds that lead only into more darkness. Shelters for nothing.

I would be the world’s gesture toward architecture.

Perhaps that is why I would not be the Warmths, or the Vents, or the Silences. They are essential, but they do not make the most unsettling suggestion of that place: the suggestion that matter there keeps almost becoming meaningful. The Almost-Builders are the haunting part. They are the shapes that make absence feel deliberate. If I were to live there, in the loose sense the country permits, I would want to be that haunting. Not a ghost. Not a memory. Not a lost civilization. Only a geological gesture with enough persistence to be misread as intention.

I would be the charged, mineral tendency of that world to arrange itself as if someone had meant it to.

So if you asked where to find me, I would say: look for the half-formed ring of fused ash in the wide basin near the lone vent. Look where the ground is warm enough to bend the black air. Wait until a pressure change passes through and the dust begins to rise.

There will be a moment when the powder stands as if called. The broken slabs will lean like a wall. The hollow thimbles will glow faintly at their rims. The salt crust will brighten. A blue spark will cross a ridge of glass. The darkness will seem, for one suspended interval, to be populated.

In that moment, I would be there.

Not as a creature.

Not as a ghost.

Not as a machine.

Not as a plant.

Not as a person.

I would be the Almost-Builder: the wandering charged dust that keeps making shelters for no one, the accidental architect of the high dry dark, the temporary resident that leaves behind rings, thresholds, and leaning fragments no hand ever placed.

Then the charge would release.

The dust would settle.

The ring would remain, a little more definite than before.

And I would be gone into the darkness, already becoming something else.
