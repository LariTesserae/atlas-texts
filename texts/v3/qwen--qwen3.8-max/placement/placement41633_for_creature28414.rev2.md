If I let the place choose for me, I do not arrive at the center.

Not at first.

I would find myself at the edge, on one of the marsh shelves where the water is thin enough to be read rather than survived. Somewhere just wide enough for the feet to learn the stone, where the reed-like stems lean in their slow, hydraulic prayer and the black filaments stretch into the current like patient hands. I would be near the boundary between the open basin and the wall, where the light is bright but not violent, where the roar is still enormous but has been broken into smaller voices by stone shoulders and shallow pans.

At first I would be a guest there, and not a graceful one.

I would be too dry.

Not merely in the physical sense, though that too: skin still remembering air, distance, weather, the habits of a world where water falls from outside rather than living inside everything. I would be dry in the deeper sense: full of straight intentions, full of names, full of the surface-creature need to move before understanding, to speak before listening, to mistake brightness for welcome. In that chamber, I would be a collection of corners. I would be the thing that has not yet been rounded.

The basin would notice me the way it notices a pebble that has not yet found its groove.

My footsteps would feel wrong even when I tried to be quiet. Not because the stone would punish them, but because the room already has its own rhythm and my body would not yet know how to join it. I would flinch when a warm drop struck my shoulder. I would step back too quickly when a dry-looking patch of floor darkened with rising water. I would reach for a glowing thread of water because it was beautiful, and the beauty would be exactly the warning I had not yet learned to read.

Then the keeper would notice.

I do not think it would approach at once. It would not need to. It would alter the room around me in small, courteous ways. A drip would shift away from my face. A thin sheet of water would spread across the path I was about to take, not dangerously, but unmistakably. A vein in the wall would brighten twice near a safer ledge. The hum beneath the floor would deepen, and somewhere nearby a reed colony would tremble with a knowledge I did not yet possess.

I would be redirected.

That is what I would be, at the beginning: a being in need of redirection.

Not an intruder exactly. The chamber does not seem made for ownership, so I could not be trespassing in the ordinary sense. But I would be an interruption. A dry note in a wet chord. A shape still thinking like the world above while standing inside a world that has forgotten the need for dryness.

I would learn the rules slowly, and probably through small humiliations.

I would trust a floor that looked stable and feel it become a warm, moving skin of water under my feet. I would stand too near a bright channel because the light drew me, and the current would pluck at my balance until I stepped back. I would hear the hum deepen and fail, at first, to understand that the sound was not merely sound. It would be architecture preparing itself. It would be pressure choosing its next shape. If I stayed in the low places out of ignorance, the basin would teach me with a surge—not cruelly, but absolutely.

The keeper would likely save me from the worst consequences of my own stiffness.

Not by carrying me, I think. Not by speaking a warning. It would simply make one path less possible and another more obvious. A pool would darken. A shoulder of stone would warm under my hand as if asking me to hold there. A falling scatter of drops would part for an instant, revealing a safer line. The room would become a language I could not yet speak but could, if I paid attention, begin to feel.

So at first I would be a pupil.

But that word is too clean. A pupil suggests lessons that end. This place would not teach me by finishing me. It would teach me by wearing me down, not into nothing, but into a more usable shape. I would become less eager to be separate from the roar. Less certain that my outline was the most important fact about me.

If I remained long enough, I would stop trying to stand against the chamber.

I would begin to stand inside it.

That is where the image of myself would change.

I would not become the keeper. The keeper is already the basin’s mature attention, its long-worn gentleness, the place where the room’s age becomes care. I would not replace that or duplicate it. The chamber is sparse for a reason. It does not need another master, and I would be foolish to imagine myself one.

Nor would I become one of the small native lives: not a glass-shrimp darting in the luminous pools, not a pale-winged waiter on the ledges, not one of the blind channel-swimmers passing through glowing seams. They are already fitted to their niches. Their rarity is part of the room’s balance. I would not displace them.

What, then?

If the world wore away my first shape, what would be left?

I think I would become a hollow thing.

Not empty in the dead sense. Hollow in the way the tubes are hollow: capable of resonance. Capable of holding a breath of air in a place of water. Capable of becoming useful by becoming still.

I would find myself, eventually, near the dryish niche among the humming tubes. Not necessarily inside it, not at first, but near it—on a worn shelf just below the recess, where the spray is softened and the stone keeps a steady warmth. The keeper would allow me there because I would have learned not to take up too much of the room. I would have learned to be intermittent.

There I would become something between artifact and listener.

Perhaps I would be like one of those finger-thin tubes myself: pale, faintly warm, quietly resonant, waiting to be lifted only when needed. Not a creature with urgent desires. Not a visitor forever astonished by the roar. A listening shape. A small instrument of attention. A place where the chamber’s sound could gather without being forced into speech.

I would be the part of the room that remembers air.

That feels right.

In a basin so thoroughly given over to water, to flow, to luminous movement, I would carry the memory of dryness—not as rebellion, but as a necessary difference. The memory of sky. The memory of distance. The memory of silence that is not filled by falling water. The chamber would not need me to long for those things loudly. It would only need me to hold them faintly, the way a shell holds the shape of the sea after the sea has moved on.

The keeper might take me up sometimes.

Not often. Perhaps only when a certain kind of listening was required. It might place one broad, smooth hand against the wall and hold me in the other hand, letting a single drop fall into my hollow. The drop would strike my inner surface and produce a note almost too small to hear. That note would not be music in the surface sense. It would be information, or recognition, or prayer without petition. It would say: here is a pulse that differs slightly from the others. Here is a rhythm the basin has not yet folded completely into itself. Here is a memory of outside still willing to be gentle.

Then the keeper would set me down again.

I would not mind being set down.

In that world, being still is not abandonment. It is participation. The moss mats are still and holy in their small hush. The hexagonal cells are still until moisture asks them to open. The broken platforms are still, holding the memory of hands and weights and old intention. Even the leaning pylon is still, though its faint linear patterns answer the keeper’s touch when the moment comes. Stillness there is not absence. It is a way of waiting inside the larger movement.

So I would learn to wait.

I would learn to be touched by water without grasping at it. I would learn to let the glow beneath the keeper’s skin remain its own, while whatever glow belonged to me became fainter, subtler, perhaps only visible when a current passed close enough to ask for it. I would learn that the room does not require brilliance from every presence. Some things are meant to glimmer. Some are meant to ring only when struck. Some are meant to hold a small pocket of air while everything else flows.

If I had to say what I would be in the simplest terms, I would say:

I would be a guest who becomes a vessel.

At first, a dry and awkward guest, standing too stiffly on the shelf, misreading beauty as safety, flinching at the chamber’s warm exhalations. Then, gradually, a quieter presence, learning the grammar of pulses and pauses. Finally, if the basin kept me long enough, something like a listening vessel: not the keeper, not a tenant, not a tool in the harsh sense, but a resonant hollow where the room could hear one of its own subtle differences.

I would be useful by being shaped.

Not conquered. Not emptied. Shaped.

The water would take my sharpest edges. The light would teach me when to brighten. The keeper would teach me when to withdraw. The small lives—the shrimp, the filament colonies, the reed stems, the pale insects waiting for intervals—would teach me that survival there is not expansion. It is negotiation. It is finding the exact micro-place where one can remain without disturbing the storm in the bowl.

And perhaps, over a very long time, I would become a minor shrine to interval.

A place where a drop pauses before falling. A hollow where the hum changes pitch by the smallest degree. A warm ceramic smoothness beside the niche where the keeper sometimes rests. Not central to the basin’s life, but part of its patience. A small proof that even in a world of continuous roar, there are necessary gaps. Necessary quiet. Necessary air.

If you were to look for me there, I think you would not see me immediately.

You would see the shelf first. The thin water moving over it. The reeds leaning. The black filaments trembling in their patient filtration. You would see the glow brighten where a current strengthens, and you would hear the vast bright roar folding over itself. Only after a while would you notice a pale shape resting near the niche, so still that it might be another tube, another worn artifact, another fragment left by old hands.

But if a surge prepared below the floor, that shape would change.

It would not flee. It would shift its weight by a fraction. Its hollow would deepen. A thin line of warmth might appear along its side, answering the veins in the wall. The keeper, perhaps still across the basin, would turn its blind, luminous face toward that spot for just a moment, as if checking that the room’s smallest instrument was still in tune.

That would be me.

Not the storm itself. Not the flood. Not the ancient water or the living light or the singular keeper who tends the basin. I would be what the chamber made of a dry arrival: a listening shape, a remembered breath, a vessel learned by water. I would be found at the edge, near the niche, in the pause between one pulse and the next.

And if the keeper touched me, I would hum.

Not loudly.

Just enough to prove that even there, in the bright and endless roar, something had learned to hold a little air.
