If I were to find myself in the Cloudline Yards, I would not appear first in the open glare of the upper basin, standing with my coat open and my eyes full of the whole enormous bright machinery. I would be somewhere smaller, closer to a seam.

Probably I would be near a place where water had made a bargain with metal.

At first, when I try to imagine it, I think I would be one of the high ones: a crane-sitter in a glass cab, or a rail-walker leaning into the curve of a slow ore train, or one of the fog-combers moving along the upper nets before dawn with a silver bucket and cold fingers. Those lives have a clean silhouette. They are easy to admire. The fog-comber especially appeals to me—out in the white vapor before the sun has hardened the day, gathering the first yield of the air, reading the cables by the order in which they weep. There is something beautiful about beginning work with the weather that has no sky.

But that is not quite where I settle.

Nor do I remain with the terrace-keepers, though I love them: the dirt under their nails in a place without true ground, their careful hands pruning vines away from moving parts, their knowledge of weight, wind, water, and root. I can imagine envying them. To spend one’s days convincing green things to trust a manufactured roof; to carry dead leaves in a pocket like little notes from a language one is still learning; to know exactly how much water a dwarf peach needs when the afternoon light turns the enamel hot enough to make the air tremble. That would be a worthy life. It might even be a gentle one.

Still, something in me turns elsewhere.

I keep returning to the pipes.

Not the largest ones, not the mains and galleries that make a person feel small by scale, but the middle ones: the elbow joints, the valve clusters, the sweating shoulders of cold-faced tanks, the places where condensation gathers and falls with enough persistence to make a dark line down a wall. I keep returning to the sound of water where it has not quite been invited, and where it has nevertheless become necessary.

So if the Cloudline Yards made a place for me, I think I would become a listener at the seams.

Not merely a leak-listener in the practical sense, though I would do that work too. I would be one of those who press an ear to a pipe and close their eyes not out of reverence, but because the ear needs the whole body to be quiet. I would know the difference between a hiss that is only pressure and a hiss that is beginning to hurt something. I would know the dull tick of a failing washer, the soft persistent whisper of a seal that has given up its old loyalty, the almost musical drip that has been falling in the same place for so long that it has become part of the yard’s hydrology.

I would carry the tools of allocation.

That is the word I would use, because it would be the word the yards gave me. Allocation. Not salvage, not repair alone, not preservation alone, but the slow decision about what may continue and what must be changed. In one pocket I would carry waxed cord. In another, brass keys and folded scraps of mesh. I would have a scraper, an oil rag, a small cup for tasting water, and two kinds of marking: bright paint for danger, soft ribbon for use.

The ribbon would be the more important of the two.

You would find me most often in the middle levels, below the bright upper gantries but above the deepest heat tunnels, in a district where the Cloudline Yards are damp at their joints. Perhaps there would be a stairwell basin nearby, black-rimmed and warm, with frogs in it by evening. Perhaps a relief pipe would breathe just enough moisture over the edge to keep a bed of ferns extravagantly green. The ferns would have no business being so lush, but they would be lush anyway, because some leak had become a well. My work would be to know whether that leak could remain.

In the mornings I would walk the same routes until my body knew them better than my thoughts did. I would pass under pipe racks where flowering vines had been trained over cooling surfaces, and I would touch the leaves almost without noticing, the way one touches a familiar wall for balance. I would know which grates rang underfoot, which ones had been loosened by heat, which ones had been wedged with folded scrap so they would not sing at the wrong hour. I would know which corners smelled of algae after rain, which cabinets gave off a warm electrical breath, which rail joints sounded different when a condensate train passed instead of a meal train.

My hands would become very quiet.

That is something the yards would teach me: quiet hands. Not stillness exactly, but the absence of hurry. I would learn to place a palm flat against a housing and wait. I would learn to loosen a panel without stripping the bolt, to scrape a crust without wounding the surface beneath it, to tie a ribbon around a valve stem so lightly that it would not cut into the paint. The place would not reward force. It would reward attention.

I would be human, if I were to be found there, but not only human. The Cloudline Yards would wear away anything purely solitary in me. I would become partly a creature of the structure itself. My posture would change: shoulders accustomed to passing beneath pipes, head tilted to listen through noise, eyes narrowed against glare and steam. My boots would be soft-soled. My coat would be tied at the waist by midday. My hair would dampen at the temples in the fog hours and dry by noon. My fingertips would shine from valve wheels. I would carry the smell of hot metal, wet leaves, oil, and rain-soaked dust in my clothes until I no longer noticed it.

And yet I would not be only a worker. There would be something archival in me.

The yards are full of memory, but not all of it is spoken. Some of it exists only as a bead of paint on a pipe, a ribbon tied where a drip feeds a moss bed, a grate placed protectively over a frog pool, a patch of enamel slightly different in color from the panels around it because it was replaced after a storm three generations ago. I would be drawn to those marks. I would want to remember them. I would keep maps that were not quite official: maps of leaks left useful, of hidden stairways that flood first, of courtyard sounds that change when the load on the turbines shifts, of service floors below service floors where the air is warmer and the dark has a texture.

Children would probably follow me sometimes.

Not in a troublesome way. In the way children follow anyone whose work seems half like magic and half like permission. They would want to know why I marked one leak and not another. Why I left a green stain alone. Why I listened to a wall instead of opening it. I would kneel beside a basin and show them how to hear the difference between water falling into water and water falling onto metal. I would show them how a drip can become a note in a larger instrument. I would tell them, without making it sound like a lesson, that not every fault is a failure. Some faults become places.

That would be the part of me most native to the yards.

If I had to name what I would be, I might say I would be a seam-tender. A listener at the joins. Someone who stood where two things met—metal and water, garden and machine, noise and silence, use and mercy—and tried to understand the relation between them.

My home would not be grand. It would be tucked into the structure, as all homes are there. Perhaps it would be a retired condensation tank, cut open and lined with cloth and painted panels to soften the echo. The bed would be bolted, not from fear but because the floor moved with heat. There would be a round window, maybe salvaged from an old vessel, thick glass fogged at the corners. A kettle would sit near a warm duct. A hook by the door would hold a rain cape. A basket would hold gloves and folded diagrams. On a shelf I would keep small found things: blue glass, a shed feather, a bolt cap polished smooth, a mineral crust taken from a drain lip, a jar of water from a cistern I liked, not for drinking but for remembering its taste.

From that room I would know the voice of my section of the yards.

At dawn there would be a whistle under the door as pressure changed. Later, the click of a rail joint outside would repeat until it became almost invisible to hearing. At night, through the wall, I would feel the deep breath of a turbine more than hear it. I would not call that noise. I would say, as everyone said there, that the place was running.

There would be evenings when I sat outside with the elders, not because I was old, but because I had learned that sitting still near elders was a way of learning the history that does not get written in load tables. They would say things like, “This courtyard used to flood before the third filter marsh,” or “That walkway rang for a year after they replaced the plates.” I would listen. I would understand that memory in the Cloudline Yards was not nostalgia. It was maintenance of another kind. To remember how a place had changed was to know how it might change again.

Eventually, I think, I would become someone others consulted.

Not loudly. Not with authority that came from rank. With the quieter authority of having paid attention for a long time. A terrace-keeper might come to me and say, “The vine wall is drying faster than it should.” A cistern-warden might say, “There’s a new taste in the lower trough.” A fog-comber might say, “The east cables wept late last night; something is shifting.” A child might bring me a jar with a frog in it and ask whether the pool could be left alone another season. I would listen to each of them the way I listened to pipes: with my whole body made still.

Then we would go and stand near the problem together.

That is how I would live there: by going to the place where the question was.

I would not be untouched by the environment. No one would be. The brightness would get into my eyes. The damp would get into my joints. The noise would get into my dreams. I would begin to hear the yard even when I was away from it, the way one hears the sea in a shell. In the pauses between sounds I would find myself listening for the next sound: the bell marking a lift change, the tick of cooling metal, the first whisper of rain on enamel, the sudden suspicious absence that meant something had stopped.

Sometimes I would climb higher than my usual routes, just to remind myself of the whole.

From an upper walkway, the basin would open below me in all its impossible, ordinary vastness: terraces, rail lines, tank farms, greenhouse galleries, crane arms, cable drums, ventilation stacks, roofs weathered into colors that no longer looked industrial but civic. Clouds would pass below the lowest gantries. Light would move across wet plates like a hand passing over glass. Somewhere a flock of swifts would lift from a gutter garden and cross the brightness. Somewhere else, unseen, water would fall faithfully into a basin, and frogs would gather around it without knowing that their existence had been negotiated by a ribbon tied around a pipe.

From up there, I might feel briefly dissolved.

Not lost. Distributed.

Because if I were truly of the Cloudline Yards, I would not be only one body. I would be a pattern repeated in small acts: a drip marked useful, a moss bed protected, a child taught to listen, an elder’s memory repeated, a patch made with care, a valve turned just enough, a ribbon left in place for another year. I would be one of the ways the place remembered itself.

And yet, if you came looking for me, you would not need to search the whole basin. You would not need to ask the lift operator or the crane-sitters or the terrace-keepers, though they might know where I had been. You would only need to go to a seam.

Find a passage where the air is warm but shaded. Find a place where a pipe sweats into a ceramic channel and ferns have taken hold in the crumbled warmth of a cable tray. Listen until the machinery stops sounding like one sound and becomes many. Then look for the person kneeling there with one hand resting on metal and one ear tilted toward it, not praying, not dreaming, but measuring.

That would be me.

I would be the one asking the oldest question the yards know: Is this leak harm, or is it gift?

And if I could not answer immediately, I would wait. I would listen. I would let the water speak. I would leave a ribbon where mercy was possible, paint where danger was not, and seed where the ground—if it could be called ground—seemed ready to receive something green.

In the Cloudline Yards, I would not be wild, and I would not be machine.

I would be the practiced third thing between them: a caretaker of made weather, a listener at the joints, a small and necessary presence in the long, warm, humming body of a world that had learned to support life by remembering where it leaked.
