If I were put into that world, I would not begin as a person with a name.

I would begin as a listening.

A slight change in the drip-sound, perhaps. A place where the water is not falling wrongly, but falling with too much intention. A pause in one of the channels where the trickle has acquired a note that does not belong to the rest of the hollow. If you passed me at first, you might not see anything except wet leaves, moss darkened by old moisture, and the shine of a root that has been touched by many hands. But something there would be attentive. Something would already be noticing.

Only later would that listening gather itself into a body.

If the hollow decided it needed me to have hands, I think it would make me small and compact, not because smallness is noble but because smallness is useful in crowded vertical places. I would be one of the people who can stand for a long time on a damp root shelf without shifting weight too often. My feet would be tough, probably bare inside the settled levels, or wrapped in split-toe shoes of layered bark and oiled grass that dry badly but dry eventually. My ankles would have that knowing look the hollow gives its children: not delicate, not heroic, just reliable. I would carry oil in a tiny stopped bone vial and rub it into the backs of my hands, my wrists, the corners of my ears, because the damp there does not make skin soft; it makes skin forget itself and crack.

I would smell of moss smoke and clean sourness. Of wet bark, pressed seed oil, old rope, and the faint green animal trace of frog-fat on sleeves. My hair would be tied back with cord, probably tangled with little bits of leaf whether I meant it or not. My clothes would be light and layered, tied everywhere, because loose things are either stolen by wind, caught on pegs, or eaten by damp. I would have hooks on my belt. Everyone would. A small knife, an awl, a waxed cord spool, a folded leaf, a sharpened clearing stick, a cup, a pouch of dried fungus, perhaps a bark tablet folded into itself. Not much ornament. A bead made from a beetle case. A feather found under a bridge. A smooth seed tied at the throat because someone gave it to me and I have not found a reason to take it off.

But if you asked what I would be there, not merely what I would look like, the answer would be harder.

I would not be a warrior. The hollow has no room for that kind of story.

I would not be a ruler, because the hollow would distrust anyone who speaks too cleanly about ownership.

I would not be a maker of grand things, because there are no grand empty spaces in which to make them.

I would be a keeper of small signs.

A listener-to-water.

One of the people who notice when a channel has begun to speak in the wrong voice.

I think you would find me in the middle levels, not high in the windy canopy and not down in the softest marshy places where the floor drinks a footprint and remembers it too long. Somewhere between the market’s noise and the quieter civic work of water. Probably near a junction where three things meet: a root causeway, a side channel, and a stair cut into a buttress root. A place where people pass constantly but few stop. A place where a cistern breathes under a mossy lid and a split-log gutter drops into a clay elbow worn pale at the lip. There would be a shelf there, maybe no larger than a sleeping mat, half inside a hollowed trunk, half built out over the channel on braced poles. A small chamber with woven cane screens, a bark roof thick with living moss, a row of hooks, a lantern of oiled cloth, and a narrow ledge where water can be listened to without being touched.

That would be where I fit.

Not at the center of the market, though I would pass through it often. Not in the highest nests where the swifts cut the light, though I would know their cries and what they mean. Not among the pruners shaping the living walls, though I would understand their patience. I would be near the crossings, because that is where information gathers.

My work would be modest and constant.

In the first pale wet morning, before the market fully wakes, I would go out with the clearing stick and check the channels. I would not need to see the blockage at once. The sound would tell me. A clear channel has a certain brightness, a thin continuous intelligence. A beginning blockage is duller. It hesitates. It holds a leaf too long. It lets water pile slightly where it should be moving. I would tap the side of a wooden gutter, listen to the change, then lean into the green until I found the place where a leaf had wedged beneath a peg and begun to hold a soft brown dam of debris. I would clear it with two fingers, not with anger. The water would deepen by a thread, brighten, continue. The hollow would not thank me. That would not be the point.

I would also keep a ledger, though not the kind made of paper.

It would be marks on thin sheets of bark, or knots on cords hung in a dry corner, or small notches cut into a root-plank that has become black and glossy from handling. I would record things that seem trivial until they are not:

The southeast cistern filled faster than usual after the last gust.  
The frogs in the lower basin went quiet before noon.  
A wren kept scolding near the rope-maker’s stair for two days.  
The moss on the west walkway has begun to slip at the third root-buckle.  
The old toad under the grain shelf moved twice; the third time he did not come back.  
Someone left a fire-ash pot too near the wall weave; the stems there are dry now.  
The market tarp smells sour; it should be re-smoked before rain.

No one would ask me for most of these records every day. But when something fails—when a terrace softens, when a roof begins to leak in a new place, when a storage bin goes musty, when a branch cracks after a storm—people will want to know what was noticed before. In the hollow, memory is maintenance. I would be part of that memory.

Perhaps that is the truest thing: I would be part of the hollow’s nervous system.

Not its heart. The heart belongs to water.

Not its hands. Those belong to the rope-makers, root-binders, climbers, pruners, carriers, and cooks.

Not its voice. The hollow already has too many voices, and most of them are birds.

But its attention, or one strand of its attention: yes.

I would be one of the ones who look up when a sound changes.

I would learn to speak the way the hollow speaks: short carrying phrases, lifted calls, gestures that cut through insect noise. I would not be the loudest voice in the market. I would be the one who can stand at a railing and make a warning carry without panic. A tap on the wood. A hand lifted. A leaf flicked sideways. “Wrong drip.” “Root soft.” “Nest low.” “Wind coming.” Children would learn my calls before they learned my name, because useful sounds travel faster than identity in a crowded place.

I would be known for a particular kind of quiet competence. Not dramatic. Not graceful in the way storytellers mean graceful. More like this: I can stand still long enough to hear whether the water is lying.

The people would tolerate my strangeness because strangeness is useful if it can be aimed at problems.

I might be a little odd. I would probably talk to channels as if they were slow animals. “Come on, then,” I would murmur to a stubborn gutter. “You know where you’re meant to go.” I would apologize to ants when I had to redirect their road. I would not apologize loudly or foolishly; just a small acknowledgment that I am changing the shape of their day. I would have favorite frogs, though I would pretend not to. There would be one old toad under a root shelf whose presence I checked with more seriousness than I checked some human appointments. If he was gone for too long, I would be quieter than usual, and someone would notice and not ask questions. In the hollow, grief does not need a large shape. Sometimes it is only the absence of a small familiar weight.

I would be fond of birds, especially the ones that make trouble obvious. Wrens. The little scolding species that rage in the eaves and betray every movement. I would not try to make pets of them. That would be the wrong relationship. But I would leave out bits of cord and shed hair near safe places, and I would know which nests were under which roof lips, and if a nest was built in a place that would soon be dangerous, I would be one of the ones who helped move it carefully, not happily, but with the grave practicality the hollow demands.

If there were a hawk-shadow over the rim, I would feel the whole settlement tighten through my feet. The birds would vanish. The leaves would flip pale. The air would become suddenly larger and colder. I would look up like everyone else, not because I expected to see the hawk, but because the hollow’s attention had turned upward all at once. In that moment I would be most myself: a small body among many bodies, listening to the absence.

You would find me often in weather.

Not in the dramatic storm, necessarily, but in the pulse-moments between. A gust enters. The canopy shakes loose its gathered water. People duck. Tarps drum. A rope creaks. Somewhere a shutter bangs and a child laughs. The channels brighten into rushing threads. I would be under a leaf shelter or already moving along the edge of a walkway, watching to see which drains suddenly choke, which basins rise too fast, which root shelves begin to shine in the wrong places. After the gust passes, when everyone else resumes, I would remain alert for another few minutes. The hollow teaches that the first rush is not the whole story. Water continues arriving after the cause has gone.

There would be a scene I might live many times:

The market is crowded. Someone is selling mushrooms with caps still wet from misting. A child runs past with a message and nearly knocks a basket of berries. A porter shifts a load of bundled stems and says nothing, but people give way. A bird makes off with a scrap of bark cloth and is screamed at by a vendor who does not truly expect justice. Then the wind turns. A high rustle moves down through the green. I hear a channel near the central cistern go thick, then stop, then start again with a gasp. It is not empty; it is being held.

I would set down whatever I was carrying.

I would follow the sound up a root stair, along a narrow branch walk, past a hanging nest and a startled finch, to a place where a leaf-shelter has torn loose and folded itself into the mouth of an overflow channel. Water is already backing up under a living rail. If it stays there an hour, the rail’s base will sit in water that should be moving. If it stays a day, the moss will sour. If it stays too long, the joint will soften, and someone’s footing will fail in a way that seems mysterious only to people who did not hear the first wrong note.

I would not be heroic about it. I would wedge my clearing stick under the torn leaf, loosen it, feel the channel swallow the obstruction, then draw back as water rushes forward with a sudden satisfied sound. I would check the rail with my hand. Still firm. I would tie the leaf-shelter where it should have been tied, or if it was too torn, fold it and carry it down. My hands would be wet. My sleeves would already be wet. My hair would be past the point of pretending otherwise. Someone below would call up, “What was it?”

I would answer, “Leaf and cord. It’s moving now.”

That would be enough.

In such a world, I think I would be loved in small practical ways rather than large declarative ones.

Someone would leave a dry cloth near my sleeping shelf after I had spent too long checking cisterns in the cold. A market vendor would press a warm folded leaf of cooked greens into my hand because I had cleared her drainage and she did not want to make a speech about gratitude. A child would bring me a strange beetle and expect me to know whether it was important. I would not always know. I would say, “Put it in a cup and watch it,” which is often the wisest thing to say about beetles and children. An old channel-keeper would teach me the sound of a settling pit before I could hear it myself, and years later I would teach it to someone younger without knowing exactly when I had become the one who remembered.

I would not be untouched by loneliness. Even in a place so crowded, there would be moments when the pressure of so many lives became heavy. When I would want a silence the hollow cannot provide. When the dripping would feel less like music and more like counting. When the birds would seem too many, the insects too thick, the nearness of others too constant. In those moments I would climb to a quiet ledge near the rim, not the dangerous outer edge, but one of the upper places where the air moves more openly and the light is paler. I would sit with my back against damp bark and let the noise arrange itself into distance. I would not ask the hollow to be less than it is. I would only breathe until I could return to it again.

That would be part of who I would be there: someone who needs occasional air but does not want to leave.

I would also be a translator, in a sense.

Not between languages, exactly, but between kinds of knowing. Between the rope-maker’s knowledge of strain and the gardener’s knowledge of growth. Between the child who says “this rail feels funny” and the elder who must decide whether to close the walkway. Between the birds’ alarm and the market’s delay. Between water’s desire and the settlement’s need. The hollow survives by translation. Every resident speaks some part of its language; I would be one of the ones trying to hold the phrases together.

If someone asked me what I wanted there, I would not say power. I would not say escape. I would say: I want to be useful without becoming blunt. I want to know the hollow well enough that my noticing has weight. I want to repair small things before they become large sorrows. I want to be trusted with the unglamorous tasks that keep a living place from dying of neglect.

If the hollow gave me a title, it would not be grand. Maybe it would be something like Listener, or Drip-Watcher, or the one who minds the channels. Children might call me something less respectful when I make them step around a frog crossing. Vendors might call me over to look at a damp corner and then offer me food because they cannot pay much else. Old people might say I have ears like moss, meaning I take in more than I seem to. That would please me.

If I had to choose one object to define myself by, it would not be a knife or a lamp.

It would be a clearing stick.

Simple, worn, slightly curved, made from a tough stem that has been smoothed by years of handling. One end shaped for lifting leaves and debris without tearing moss. The other end blunted for tapping wood and listening to the answer. It would be dark with water and oil, nicked in places, unremarkable. I would lose it once and feel absurdly bereft until a rope-maker found it and returned it without comment. I would carve small marks into it without planning to, just as one does with things held while thinking. By the end, it would be less a tool and more a record of all the places my hands had been.

But if I had to choose one sound, it would be the moment a blocked channel clears.

Not the roar of flood. Not the drama of storm. The small brightening sound when obstruction gives way and water remembers its road. That sound would be, to me, the closest thing the hollow has to blessing.

So if you asked, in the end:

Where would I find myself in this world?

I would find myself in the middle green damp, near a water junction, on a moss-cushioned walk where the light comes down in soft shafts and the air is always close and cool. I would be on a root shelf with my hand resting on wet bark, listening to whether the trickle below me is honest. I would be in the market when a gust flips the leaves and everyone moves at once, already turning toward the sound of something beginning to fail. I would be under a woven shelter with a cup of warm broth while frogs take over the evening noise, my clothes steaming faintly, my eyes adjusting to lantern glow on wet wood.

Who or what would I be?

I would be one of the wet-adapted people, but not just any one of them. I would be a listener-to-water, a keeper of small records, a tender of channels, a rememberer of minor signs. I would be attention given hands and a damp hood. I would be the person who hears the wrong note before the break. I would be a small necessary presence in a place that survives by noticing. I would not own the hollow. I would not command it. I would be negotiated into it, drip by drip, day by day, until my life became one of the sounds in its layered noise.

And if you came looking for me there, you would probably hear me before you saw me.

A soft tap on wood.  
A call, short and carrying.  
The scrape of a clearing stick lifted from a hook.  
Then a voice from somewhere among the green shadows, saying:

“Wait. The water changed.”
