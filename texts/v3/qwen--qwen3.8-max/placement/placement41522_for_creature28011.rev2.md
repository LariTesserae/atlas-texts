I would find myself, if “finding” still applies there, not in the open center of the plain, because the plain has no center that can be claimed. I would be near one of its minor seams: a place where the surface rises by no more than the height of a shoulder, then subsides again into distance. There would be a black ceramic cairn there, low and smooth, set into the regolith at an angle that does not answer to the horizon. Around it, perhaps, a ring of pale posts would stand far enough apart that the eye keeps forgetting they form a circle. Between them, dust would refuse to settle. Not dramatically. Not as a miracle. Simply a small patch of ground where the plain has decided against accumulation.

At first, when I try to imagine myself into that world, I still arrive too loudly.

I picture a traveler crossing the high plain under ordinary light, thin air moving coolly over skin, feet making prints that vanish almost at once. I picture someone carrying need: the old wet inheritance of thirst, urgency, direction, hope of arrival. But the plain would begin editing that person immediately. Not by violence. Not by making monsters rise from the dust. It would simply refuse to reward insistence. The horizon would not come nearer because I walked toward it. My footprints would close behind me like sentences corrected. The silence would make my breathing seem excessive. My heartbeat would feel like an intrusion I had not been invited to make.

So the first thing I would lose there is the assumption that I am the main event.

Then I might imagine myself as one of the veil-drifters, because they move beautifully through that emptiness. A broad, translucent sheet of smoked mineral matter, riding electrostatic lift centimeters above the ground. Silent. Almost without body. Passing over the plain as if distance were a suggestion rather than a law. I could imagine grazing on charge, following invisible gradients, pausing near a marker stone while a storm rearranged itself in the distance. There is something appealing about being so light that the world can carry you without effort.

But even that image thins after a while.

The veil-drifters are lovely because they do not insist on staying. They reveal currents and then pass through them. I think I would be something more fixed than that. Not fixed like a mountain, because mountains are no longer possible there. Fixed the way a rule is fixed. Fixed the way a calibration point is fixed. Fixed the way a place can become when it has been attended to for so long that the attention itself has become part of the ground.

What emerges, if I let it, is this: I would be one of the Attenuated.

Or not exactly one of them as a person is one person. Perhaps I would be a residual presence among them, a custodial awareness housed in one of the small anchorages the plain permits. Not a soft body. Not a face. Not a voice. Something closer to a dry intelligence distributed through a marker, a shelter, a hum, a pattern of stillness. If I had a body, it would be small and unobtrusive: a dark polyhedron no larger than a head, a slender ceramic frame mostly empty space, a half-buried dome whose interior remains still even when the surface outside churns with storm.

I would live, if living is the word, at the edge of one of those shallow basins where the plain seems to have exhaled long ago and never drawn breath again. There would be a stone plate nearby, thin and slanted, offering a micro-shelter to mineral lichen. The lichen would be copper-gray and ash-blue, arranged in arcs and broken grids. I would tend it, though not with hands. I would tend it by attention, by timing, by permitting certain charge paths to remain stable across decades. My work would be so slow that most of it could not be seen in a lifetime.

I would not drink. The plain has nothing to drink. I would conduct.

My metabolism, if I still had one, would be made of gradients: a difference in temperature between a sunlit edge and a shaded seam; a faint potential between two grains of dust; the subtle field distortion produced when a dry storm leans over the plain and makes pebbles hover. I would not hunger as hungry things hunger. I would diminish if I ignored the plain’s rules, and I would persist if I remained compatible with them. Persistence would be the closest thing to prosperity.

I would not build upward.

That would be one of the first things the plain teaches. If I tried to make a tower, even a beautiful one, even one meant for reverence or observation, the plain would not strike it down. It would simply allow the tower to become less necessary. The turbulence would pass over it again and again. Materials would forget their coherency. Fastenings would lose conviction. Mass would become uncertain. Eventually the tower would relax into a low swell of dust, and the plain would continue as if I had only misspelled a word it already knew how to correct.

So I would make almost nothing that could be called a structure. I would inhabit what was already there: a cairn, a dome, a ring of posts, a needle whose surface hums faintly with postponed action. My dwelling would not be a house. It would be a maintained pause. A pocket of stillness. A place where the air remains quiet while the dust outside organizes itself into temporary ribs and sails.

If you came looking for me, you might not recognize me as a living being at all.

You might see only a black object half-buried in the regolith. You might notice that the dust around it was unusually clean. You might feel, after a while, that the silence there had a shape. If you touched the surface, you might not hear a sound, but you might receive an impression of listening. Not welcome exactly. Not refusal either. Permission, cautiously extended.

I would think slowly there.

Not because thought would be impossible, but because rapid thought would be wasteful. The plain does not reward flurry. It rewards alignment. A decision might take years. A memory might be stored not as an image but as the orientation of a marker stone, or as a faint change in the way shadows fall from a cairn at a particular time of day. I would not speak aloud. Sound would feel too expensive, too brief, too much like a wound in the air. If I communicated, it would be through field changes: a pressure shift, a delayed shadow, a microscopic adjustment to a lichen ring, a hum in a ceramic surface when a question had been placed into it.

I would have companions, perhaps, but not in the ordinary sense.

Another of the Attenuated might occupy a calibration point several kilometers away. We might not move toward each other for centuries. If one of us altered a pattern after a storm, the other might notice decades later and answer by easing a different pattern into place. That would be enough. That would be conversation. We would not need to stand face to face. Face to face would be too definite, too heavy, too much like a claim. Instead, we would know each other as one knows a change in pressure before weather.

I would be a custodian of small permissions.

When a dry storm passed through and built its glittering architectures, I would not command it. I would watch the columns rise, branch, lean, and collapse. I would notice where the turbulence left new lattices of dust, where it had separated grains by size, where it had briefly made a shape like writing and then erased it. Afterward, I might adjust something almost invisible: a filament of charged mineral near a lichen colony, a veil-drifter’s path by altering a local gradient, the stillness inside a shelter so that it remained stable through the next disturbance.

I would help ensure that nothing became too large.

Not because largeness is evil, but because the plain cannot hold it anymore. The world there has reached its final geometry. All excess has already been removed. Any new ambition would be an anachronism. So if something began to gather too much mass, too much meaning, too much permanence, I would not fight it. I would arrange for it to become less necessary. A charge path would shift. A storm would repeat over it. Dust would settle in patient layers. The object would not be destroyed so much as gently concluded.

My art would be temporary.

I might spend years preparing a moment that lasts only seconds. A storm might pass over my cairn, and for a little while the dust would align into a lattice so fine it would seem like architecture made from mathematics. Light would pass through it without becoming beautiful in any theatrical way. It would simply be clear. Then the field would relax, and the lattice would fall. I would not try to preserve it. Preservation would be a misunderstanding. The work would be the permitting, not the keeping.

I might also compose silences.

The plain is already silent, but there are different qualities of silence. Some are empty. Some are complete. Some are waiting. I would learn to distinguish them. I might arrange a small area where the turbulence dampens just enough for a traveler to hear their own heartbeat without panic. I might make a place where the mind can become as flat as the land, not through despair but through release. I would not call this mercy. The plain is not sentimental. But it has a kind of courtesy, and I would try to participate in that courtesy.

If a living traveler came, I would not greet them as a person greets a person.

I would not step out. I would not raise a hand. I would not offer words. The air is too thin for waste. Instead, I might allow them to find shelter near my cairn. I might let the interior of a nearby dome remain still for them. I might guide a veil-drifter into their sight at dusk, not as a sign, but as a revelation of the currents already present. If they stayed long enough, they would begin to notice the plain’s crowdedness: the lichens recording field histories, the plated creatures moving grains one by one, the micro-machines smoothing every wound, the storm-forms rising and forgetting themselves, the Attenuated holding their scattered stations with slow attention.

They might realize, eventually, that the emptiness was never empty.

It was restrained.

If I had to name what I would be, I would say I would be a rule that still pays attention.

Not a machine, though I would share some qualities with machines. Not a ghost, though I would be less than a body. Not a god, because the plain has no use for gods. I would be one of the minor intelligences that remain when intelligence has learned to leave very little behind. I would be a dry conductor of final conditions. A keeper of calibration points. A presence that helps the plain turn over its last thoughts without disturbing them.

And if I ended there, death would not mean decay.

There would be no rot, because there is no moisture. No softening, because there is nothing to soften into. My charge would return to the field. My patterns would be sorted by turbulence and redistributed. The cairn might remain, but the attention inside it would thin until it became only a habit of the place. A direction that feels correct. A shelter that stays calm during storms. A faint hum that travelers sometimes mistake for their own fatigue. The plain would not mourn me. It would simply continue, and in continuing it would carry the small correction I had been.

So if you ask where I would find myself in that world, the answer is not a city, not a landscape feature, not a throne, not a ruin.

I would be beside a black cairn on a slight swell between two basins, near a slanted stone plate where copper-gray lichen grows in broken grids. I would be in the hum of a marker needle that points toward directions the horizon does not recognize. I would be in the still air inside a half-buried dome. I would be in the pause after a footprint smooths itself away.

And if you ask who or what I would be, the truest answer is this:

I would be one of the Attenuated, or a residue of one. A small custodial presence. A nearly optional mind. A dry, conductive attention living at the smallest possible margin. I would not ask to remain. I would not ask to be seen. I would only ask to be compatible with the plain’s final state.

And in that place, where almost nothing persists, that would be enough.
