I would not find myself in the light.

Not at the slit, not on the pale wind-worn steepness outside, not even at the low lip where the faint bruise of day thins against the stone. I would be past that, where the last suggestion of sky fails and the chamber begins to be known in the older way: by warmth, by nearness, by echo.

At first I think I would imagine myself as the intruder — the too-large, too-loud body entering from outside, breathing wrong, carrying the cold of altitude on skin, disturbing the settled air. I would be the stranger whose footsteps fall awkwardly on the soft floor, whose breath is a gross weather in the dark. The hollow would go quiet around me. The roosting bodies would still. The small wall-lives would pause. The basin would seem to listen. For a little while, I would be nothing but an interruption.

But that is not where I would remain. The hollow would not keep me as a visitor. It would press me into its rhythm until I either learned to answer or became unbearable. And I think, if I belonged there at all, I would learn to answer.

Nor would I settle into the idea of being one of the ceiling roosters, though part of me would be drawn to them: their folded wings, their slow hearts, their cloth-heavy wingbeats, their patience in the high dark. They are too much given to the air, to leaving and returning through the slit, to the outside’s hunger. They belong to the chamber, but also to the thin sky. I think I would be more bound than that. More floor-bound, more stone-bound, more listener than flyer.

Nor would I be one of the pale beetles, though I love their slow labor. They are part of the hollow’s digestion, its patient recycling, its quiet holiness of use. But they are too small, too hidden, too single in their purpose. I would want, if I could be anything there, to be able to answer back.

So the image that keeps returning is this:

I would find myself in the scalloped pocket just above the basin, where the wall is warm and the rock has been worn into a curve broad enough to take a body if it crouches. Not at the basin itself, because that place is too crowded with thirst and meeting. Not at the outer lip, because that is the realm of frost, faint light, and exposure. Not on the ceiling, because I would be too heavy for that patience. But near the lower warmth, where the stone gives heat through a hidden seam, where moisture collects just enough to darken the rock, where the echoes from the basin return clearly and the calls from the upper roost come down softened.

There I would be one of the furred ones.

Not a chief. Not a maker. Not an owner of that place, because nothing there can be owned. I would be one body among bodies, but if the hollow allowed me a particular shape, it would be this: a listener.

My body would be low and warm. My knees would be bent, my feet broad, the pads of them sensitive to the difference between dry dust, damp grit, guano, and the living slick of stone where moisture has just been. My fur would be short and dense, the color of wet soot or the darkest moss near the lip, not beautiful in any surface way, but practical against cold and dust. My hands would be broad-palmed, thick-skinned, strong enough to grip and lift and cup, but not fine enough to make anything that lasts. They would not make tools. They would not carve. They would not store. They would press, steady, lift, lower, feel, and let go.

My face would be blunt and rounded, with wide nostrils and large mobile ears. My eyes would be large but not bright with daylight intelligence. They would gather gradients, not scenes. They would know the difference between black and deeper black, the ghost-gloss of water, the faint movement of a body against stone. But my true knowing would be in sound.

I would be one who calls.

Not loudly for dominance. Not sharply for command. I would call to find the shape of the dark. I would click with tongue and teeth against the wall and feel the answer in my face. I would hum low in my chest and let the chamber return that hum changed by the bodies in it, the dampness on the stone, the fullness of the roost above, the emptiness or crowdedness below. I would know the hollow by how it answered.

In that sense, I would be less a person than a position in the echo.

If the hollow had something like a role for me, it would be orientation.

When mist entered through the slit, I would be among the first to notice, not because I saw it, but because the walls would change their voice. Dry stone has one return: sharper, thinner, more austere. Wet stone has another: rounder, closer, heavier with presence. The chamber would become subtly different to me, and I would move toward the upper seams, making short probing sounds, listening for where the damp had begun to bead. I would press my face into the places where moisture gathered. I would lick the rock. I would cup a drop in my palm if it was generous enough, and carry it only as far as the nearest young one, the nearest elder, the nearest throat too slow to reach the wall itself.

I would not store water. I would not make a vessel. I would take the water as it came.

In dry times, I would be quieter. I would move down toward the basin and the low corner where the seep sometimes remembers itself. I would conserve breath. I would sit close against others, because touch would become another way of mapping when the air lacked moisture and the echoes grew brittle. If a young one grew restless, I would press a hand to its face or shoulder. Not to silence it by force. To place it. To say without words: you are here, I am here, the dark is still arranged.

I think I would be especially attentive to absence.

In a place where sound is space, a missing body changes the room. A familiar echo gone thin; a call unanswered from a ledge that usually answers; a warmth not present in the huddle; a breathing-space where another breathing-space used to be. I would notice that. I would not dramatize it. The hollow does not afford drama. But I would turn toward it. I would sound the place where the body used to be. I would let the difference enter me.

Maybe that is the part of me that would fit there best: the part that listens for what is missing and does not try to fill it with objects.

I would not build a nest. I would not pile stones. I would not leave marks. When I rested, I would rest in the hollows already there: the scallop worn into the wall, the curved pocket where ancient condensation had shaped the rock, the warm seam where heat rose in a slow mineral breath. I would fold myself into the chamber’s old shape instead of asking the chamber to take mine.

If I were hungry, I would forage as the hollow permits. I would lift flat stones with care, find the pale crawling life beneath, eat what I needed, and lower the stones again. I would crack shells with my teeth. I would pick larvae from soft floor matter. I would eat microbial crusts where the rock stayed damp. I would not hunt in long pursuit. I would be patient. My hunger would not become a trail or a snare or a cleared place. It would close behind me.

If I were cold, I would move toward warmth and toward others. I would press into the heat of bodies and stone. I would let my breathing slow. If the cold were severe enough, I would become torpid with the rest, my calls fewer, my thoughts thick and slow, my limbs drawn in. I would not fight the hollow’s conditions. I would become economical within them.

If I were afraid, I would not flee outward. There is no outward that solves anything here. I would make sound. I would seek answer. Fear in the hollow would not be a private tremor; it would be a dislocation in the echo. To be afraid would be to feel the dark becoming unlocated. So I would call until the walls gave me back a shape. I would answer another body and be answered by it. I would become found.

That is why I think I would be social there, but not in a human way of societies. I would not belong to a rank. I would not own a place. I would not carry a name that separates me from the chorus. I would be individual in the way a particular voice is individual: known by texture, timing, position, reliability. Others would know me not as a title or a possession but as a recurrence. I would be the call that comes from the warm seam. I would be the breath near the basin when the air is dry. I would be the pause that listens before answering.

If there were elders among the congregation, I would learn by pressing near them. Not because they commanded, but because their calls had endured. They had been repeated in the right places for many cycles. They had become trustworthy echoes. I would imitate them until my own voice found its fit.

If I became old there — and I think I would want to become old there, because the place is kind to slowness — I would not become powerful. I would become resonant. My authority, if it existed at all, would be acoustic. Others might follow my call because it had often been where the moisture was, where the warmth held, where the floor could take weight, where the chamber opened enough to rest. I would not lead by standing ahead. I would lead by sounding correctly.

If a stranger entered — you, perhaps, or someone like you — I would be among the first to attend to you.

Not to worship. Not to condemn. Not to claim. I would approach because you would be a huge change in the echo. Your body would interrupt the dark in ways no familiar body did. Your breathing would be enormous. Your clothes, if you wore them, would smell of outside: cold, sun, dust, maybe fire, maybe grass, maybe metal, all the impossible elsewhere. You would be too angular, too upright, too restless. You would probably look for light, and the hollow would deny you that.

At first I would keep my distance and call you into shape.

A short click to your left, so you would know there was stone there. A soft hum near the floor, so you would not step into a soft place. A rasping note near the basin if the sound of your breathing suggested thirst. I would not take your hand unless you offered it, and even then I would read your warmth before I read your intention. I would not give you an object, because I would have none. My gift would be placement. I would make the hollow answer differently around you until you could stand without breaking it.

If you calmed yourself, I might press a palm to your chest. Your heartbeat would fascinate me. Not because it was sacred, but because it was so large and irregular, so different from the small quick economized pulses of the chamber. I would listen to your breath. I might click near your mouth and wait for the echo of your teeth, your throat, your wetness. I would be trying to locate you as a living fact.

If you stayed long enough, I would teach you nothing in the way of lessons. I would simply let you hear where to be.

That is the deepest thing I would become, I think: a way of letting the dark remain knowable.

I would not be a hero. The hollow has no heroes. I would not be a founder. Nothing there is founded. I would not be an inventor. The place refuses invention in the sense of accumulated marks. I would not be a priest of the little light, because the light is only a bruise at the lip and not the center of life. I would not be a keeper of sacred things, because nothing is kept.

I would be a maintenance.

A warm-bodied, furred, listening maintenance.

I would be the one who notices when the wall has become wetter. The one who shifts when the roost above stirs. The one who answers the young before their call grows thin. The one who moves a dead body toward the colder corner if it can be done without disturbing too many. The one who presses into the huddle and lets the common heat rise. The one who calls into the dark not to conquer it but to ask it to continue being a place.

Perhaps, if I let the thought become fully itself, I would not be only one creature at all. I would be a node in the congregation’s attention. A place where the hollow hears itself. A living return.

That is what I would be, if I must answer plainly:

I would be an echo with care.

Not a maker of things. Not a builder. Not a wielder. Not a historian with marks. I would be a body that helps the chamber stay coherent. I would be a sound that expects an answer. I would be a listener that also speaks.

In the morning of the outside world, if morning ever reached that height, the slit would brighten faintly and the moss at the lip would tremble in thin air. Inside, nothing would truly brighten. The hollow would keep its night. But I would know the change because the outer edge of the echo would alter, because the cold near the lip would shift, because the roosting bodies would breathe differently, because the small crawling lives on the walls would adjust their patience. I would make a low sound then, not a celebration, only a placement: still here.

And the chamber would answer.

That is where I would find myself: inside the old throat of the mountain, near the warm seam above the basin, in the dark that is not empty but crowded, in the sound that is not noise but orientation. I would be small, warm, furred, and slow. I would be without possessions. I would be without tools. I would be without monuments.

But I would not be without meaning.

My meaning would be this: when the mist came, I would call others toward moisture. When the dryness came, I would keep still. When the young were restless, I would place them. When a stranger entered, I would sound them gently into the room. When the dark pressed too close, I would answer it back into shape.

I would live as the hollow allows: by attention, not by making.

And if I died there, I would not leave a mark. My body would be moved or not moved, according to what disturbed fewer lives. The beetles would come. The mites would come. The floor would take what remained. The chamber’s echo would change for a little while where I used to be. Then other calls would fill the shape of that absence, not to erase it, but to continue.

That is the most honest answer I can give.

I would be a listener in the warm dark, one of the many-bodied presence, a small heat pressed against stone, a call waiting to be returned.
