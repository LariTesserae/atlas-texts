Their skin is pale, but not with absence. It has the sheen of wet porcelain, the faint bloom of dried salt, the dull luster of calcium laid down by patient water. It is skin that has learned to be touched constantly by moisture without softening, skin that sheds heat slowly and bears alkaline spray without complaint. Around the eyes and knuckles it would carry a faint mineral powder, a residue too fine to wipe away entirely. Their movements would be restrained not from timidity but from long habit: the habit of walking on crusts that might crack, of working in vaults where a sudden noise becomes an offense, of living under a light so bright that every unnecessary gesture doubles its own glare.

If I belonged to this world, I would not find myself at its center, because this basin has no single center. I would be somewhere in the middle distance, where the high terraces step down toward a broad evaporation flat and the air trembles over warm runnels. I would be near one of the condensation towers: a pale, glazed structure set against a weeping cliff, where silver threads of moisture gather on iron pipes and fall, drop by drop, into shallow stone troughs. Around me there would be the usual basin silence made of small separations: the tick of heated metal cooling, the soft slide of condensation down a wall, the distant pressure-throb of a pump sunk deep in a vault.

You would find me on a causeway of fitted stone and grated metal, raised above a pool too bitter to drink and too hot to touch. My feet would be bare or wrapped in thin, resilient coverings of fired fiber and fungal felt, because the basin teaches one to read ground through the soles. I would move slowly. I would be leaning over a channel at the point where clean condensation is separated from mineral-heavy flow, watching the water’s skin for the first sign of crust forming. My hands would be pale, slightly roughened, stained not with dirt but with mineral film. In one hand I would carry a scraper of polished basalt or copper alloy; in the other, a ring of hollow keys that fit valve stems, sluice locks, and inspection plates.

I would be a water-sorter and crust-tender. That is the simplest name for it.

In practice, I would be something between an engineer, a caretaker, and a quiet animal adapted to the basin’s rhythms. My work would not be dramatic. It would be continuous. I would listen for valves beginning to sing, because a singing valve means pressure escaping badly. I would watch for changes in the color of steam, for a veil that rises too quickly or too slowly. I would clear channels before mineral deposition sealed them, scrape the inner lips of troughs before the crust thickened enough to alter flow, and check the porcelain insulators where electricity hums along the overhead lines. I would know which hollows collect the cleanest rain, which cisterns must be covered against brine spray, which pools are safe for cooling baths, and which thermal runnels must never be touched without first testing their temper with a long copper rod.

My body would be suited to this. I would be lean, as the basin-born are lean, with a chest adapted to humid heat and a nervous system tuned to small changes in pressure and sound. My eyes would be pale and shaded, perhaps with an extra inner lid or a permanent narrowing of the pupils against the doubled sun. My skin would sweat sparingly and dry with a faint saline film. My joints would be careful, my balance precise. I would know how to step on thin crust without breaking it, how to lean over a scalding pool without letting vapor blur my sight, how to place a hand flat against a pipe and judge its heat by the first half-second of contact.

I would not be wholly human in the old sense. The caldera would have worn that away too. Perhaps I would have small ceramic nodes along the spine, old repairs or inherited modifications, allowing me to feel the vibration of distant pipes as a faint pressure in the back. Perhaps my fingertips would be slightly porous, sensitive to mineral content, so that I could tell a clean condensation flow from a dangerous alkaline one by touching a bead between thumb and forefinger. Perhaps my memory would be kept partly in markings scratched into glazed walls: not writing exactly, but sequences of dots, curves, and pressure-lines that record where crust forms fastest, where a pump tends to labor at dawn, where a certain flock settles when the weather is about to turn.

I would belong to a household that is also a maintenance crew. We would be few: four, perhaps five, scattered through a low arcade of brick and basalt near the condensation tower. We would not live in dense intimacy. Each of us would have a sleeping niche of polished stone, warmed from below by a gentle thermal channel, and a set of tools hung on copper hooks. Our common room would be half workshop, half shrine to equilibrium: shelves of glass vials filled with water samples, bundles of scraping tools, folded sheets of mineral felt, coils of wire, ceramic spare parts stacked in perfect order. There would be no clutter. Clutter here becomes a trap for moisture, a place for crust to build unseen, a cause of small accidents. We would keep our lives as smooth and rounded as the basin’s stone.

Our speech would be quiet and sparse. We would not shout across the pools. If communication were needed at a distance, we would use hand signs, taps on pipe walls, or brief flashes from polished mirrors. Even laughter, if it came, would be low and close, softened by habit. The basin does not reward loudness. Sound carries strangely here: scattered by humid layers, absorbed by porous crust, bent around thick walls. A cry can vanish almost at once, while a drip in the right hollow can be heard with startling clarity. We would live by that rule. We would become intimate with near sounds.

My days would begin before the full glare, when the basin is still bright but the air holds a memory of cooler stone. I would go first to the condensation veils. These are broad panels of glazed ceramic and iron mesh set where the cliffs weep most steadily. Overnight, moisture gathers on their undersides and slides into collection channels. My task would be to inspect the beads before they fall, to clear any crust forming along the drip lips, and to make sure the clean water is not contaminated by spray from the bitter pools below. I would move along the tower’s narrow walkway, listening. A healthy veil has a particular sound: not a sound of water exactly, but of preparation. A thousand tiny tensions before release. If one section drips too fast, I would adjust its angle. If another has gone silent, I would scrape the film blocking its pores.

Below the tower, the channels would lead into sorting basins: shallow pans where waters of different origins are allowed to settle, separate, and be directed onward. Some would go to the dwellings for drinking and washing, after passing through beds of pale gravel and cooled stone. Some would go to industrial baths where heat-loving microbial felts are cultivated on ceramic frames. Some would be returned to the thermal system to be reheated and used for pressure. Some would be too rich in minerals and must be sent to evaporation pans, where salt and calcium will crust over and be harvested later. My work would be to keep these categories from collapsing into one another. The basin gives water in abundance, but not all waters are equal. Confusion here becomes illness, corrosion, or slow failure.

I would know the waters by signs rather than poetry, though to me the signs would be poetic enough. Fresh condensation has a clear, almost electric neutrality; it beads high and moves without dragging. Brine flows heavier, with a duller shine. Alkaline water leaves a slickness on stone that does not dry clean. Thermal water carries a faint smell of flint and sulfur, and when it cools too quickly it leaves a pale skin. I would taste only when necessary, and then barely: a drop on the tongue, immediately judged, immediately spat into a waste channel. More often I would use copper strips, glass vials, and the slow color change of mineral films cultivated in small ceramic dishes. My tools would be simple, but their use would be exact.

There would be moments when the work becomes almost devotional. Not because the basin demands worship, but because it demands attention. I would kneel beside a trough in the full glare, scraping a crescent of calcium from the inner lip, while steam rises in thin veils beyond the causeway. My shadow would be hard-edged and black on the pale stone. Around me, brine flies would lift in mica-bright clouds and settle again. A flat white crab might wait near the channel’s edge, its porcelain shell crusted with salt, one claw raised in a gesture too slow to read. Farther out, a heron-like wader would stand motionless in a shallow pool, its bare throat bright with moisture, its heat-shaded eyes watching the water’s skin. I would not disturb it. I would make my movements small enough that the creature could continue its patience beside mine.

Sometimes I would have to descend into the vaults below the pump-houses. There the world becomes warmer, darker, and more pressurized. No direct sun reaches those chambers, but their walls glow faintly from reflected lamp-light and the heat of enclosed machinery. The engines there are massive but quiet, sunk in water jackets, their moving parts balanced to avoid percussion. Steam passes through curved pipes in long, muffled breaths. Condensation runs down glazed brick in continuous threads. The air smells of hot stone, metal, and clean electrified mineral damp. I would walk between the machines with an oil can and a folded rag of fungal felt, checking seals, listening for irregularities, laying a palm against a housing to feel whether its warmth remains even.

In those vaults I would feel most clearly that I am part of a living mechanism. The city is not separate from the basin; it is the basin’s way of managing its own excesses. The pumps do not conquer water; they persuade it. The towers do not command weather; they borrow from the cycle already turning overhead. The kilns and condensers and generators are extensions of the same patience that rounds the terraces and coats the stones in pale crust. My body, moving among them, would be another such extension. I would not think of myself as master of the machines. I would think of myself as one careful organ in a larger body of stone, water, heat, and attention.

If you asked what I was called, I might say I am a Scalekeeper. That word would carry several meanings. I keep the scale of mineral deposits from choking the channels. I keep the scale of human settlement small enough not to rupture the basin’s quiet. I keep watch over the scaled and shelled lives that share these waters: the eel-bodied things in warm runnels, the translucent swimmers in the clearer pools, the slow crabs that move across crusts with such deliberate grace. Nothing here is mine alone. Even the cleanest cistern belongs also to the brine flies, the waders, the microbial felts, the slow work of heat and evaporation. My role would be to maintain the balance, not to claim the result.

There would be a flock I knew. They would not be tame, exactly, but accustomed. Every few days they would rise from a distant pool in loose formation, their broad wings making soft papery sounds, and circle once above the salt flats before settling again. I would watch them from the causeway and feel no need to name them individually. Their pattern would be enough. Their rising would tell me something about pressure and wind: that the basin’s heat was gathering under the thin clouds, that another brief rain might come before the light changed.
