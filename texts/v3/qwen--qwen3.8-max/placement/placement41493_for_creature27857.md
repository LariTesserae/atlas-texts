I would not arrive there as a traveler arrives in a place. I would condense.

At first I would mistake myself for weather. I would be a slight thickening in the fog, a bead of water delaying too long on the tip of a spine, a warm tremor moving through a cold capillary thread. The plain would not notice me as a self. I would not notice myself as one either. I would only be a difference: a little more charge here, a little more pressure there, a hesitation in the falling of moisture.

Then I would begin to gather where sound gathered.

I think I would find myself in one of the broad, shallow basins where the plant-floor is saturated enough to hold shapes for a breath before releasing them. Not a place marked by any object, because there are no objects there to mark anything, but a recurring condition: a basin where a boom arrives after the vent that made it, where reflections in the gel-like water show movement from directions no body currently occupies, where footprints return after the surface has healed. I would be drawn to such a place because it would already behave the way I would begin to behave — by remembering pressure, by answering late, by making room for cause and effect to loosen slightly.

If you asked me where I was, I would not be able to point to a horizon and say: there. The horizon would not help. It would settle rather than rise, and distance would be unreliable. A distant organ-tower might remain small for hours while I moved toward it, then suddenly stand close enough that its wet ribs and breathing slits filled my attention. So I would learn to locate myself not by landmarks but by intervals. I would be somewhere between a vent opening and the sound of its opening. Somewhere between a wader’s call and the landscape’s answer. Somewhere between the pressure that asks for release and the release itself.

That is where I would find myself: in the gap that the plain keeps open because it needs such gaps.

At first I would think my body was the ground. I would feel myself as the living substrate, as the cold springy mat of moss and root and older compressed tissue pushing back against whatever touched it. I would feel the slow hydraulic patience of it. I would feel the root-borers moving through me like slow thoughts, opening pores, thinning dense patches, carrying fluid into old shapes. I would not understand that they were separate from me. I would not understand that I was separate from them. In that place, separation would not be the first truth. Coordination would come first.

My first recognizable self might be only a footprint.

A pressure pattern would press into the plant-floor and fade. Then, minutes later, when some distant sound crossed the basin with the right weight, the print would return. Water would be drawn back into the old shape. The substrate would repeat it without intending to. I would be that repetition. Not a ghost, because nothing there is human enough for ghosts, and not a memory in the ordinary sense, because memory there is not stored in minds but in pressure, fluid, tissue, and habit. I would be the plain’s unconscious act of continuing a shape.

From there, I would thicken.

Root-borers would carry my pattern through the substrate. Valve-worms would give me timing. Heat-beetles resting near vent slits would give me warmth. Swarms of small winged bodies would give me edge and motion, their dark ribbons bending as if around an invisible center. Waders would give me stillness and attention. The great slow walking wetland organs would give me something heavier: the possibility of moving across the plain as a single regional adjustment rather than as a scattered impulse.

If forced to become visible, I would not look like a person. I would not have a face shaped for speaking or hands shaped for gripping. I would have no tools, no carried things, no kept objects. I would be grown, and every part of me would remain temporary.

I might appear, at one moment, like a tall, sparse wading creature standing in a sheet of water so thin it is mostly film. My legs would be hollow and ribbed, more like living pipes than limbs, testing the surface before accepting weight. My body would be narrow and pale, with translucent bladders along my flanks glowing faint amber with metabolic heat. My head would be only a cluster of sensory membranes, intake crowns, and venting slits. My throat would open in folds, filtering fog, tasting spores, drawing in the chemical news of the plain. I would stand for hours without moving, listening through the ground.

But that would be only one posture, and perhaps not even my truest one.

At another moment, I might seem like a low moving mound of wet felt and hollow stems, a small walking organ-cluster no larger than a grazer, its surface breathing through slits. Cilia would move along my underside. Water would be drawn beneath me, through me, and released elsewhere. I would leave temporary prints that filled and sealed behind me. If a storm were coming, my surface might tighten until it resonated faintly with the drone of distant towers. If the plain were quiet, I might flatten into the substrate until there was almost nothing to see: only a dark patch where condensation gathered a little more heavily than elsewhere.

More truly, I would be neither wader nor mound nor walking plant-organ alone. I would be the tuning between them.

I would be one of the presences that become coherent when pressure, sound, saturation, and need align. Not an individual in the old sense, not a bounded creature with a single continuous will, but a sustained interval. A pattern recognized across the plain. A living valve formed when the landscape requires an adjustment but cannot make a tool.

If the plain had a name for me, it would not be a word. It would be a rhythm: a held pause between the opening of a vent and the boom that follows. A low tone answered by water moving the wrong way. The silence after a seed tower inhales before release.

I would not build. That is important. The idea of building would be almost impossible there, like trying to remember a color that does not exist. I would not cut, tie, carry, store, or keep. I would not make marks that outlasted my need of them. Where manipulation was required, I would grow a temporary organ and become it. If a pore field were closing, I would become a broad soft pad of pressing tissue, remaining there until the pores opened again. If a charge were building too sharply before a storm, I would become a conductive veil of wet filaments, offering the static a path into the upper fog. If a seed tower’s release needed scattering, I would become a brief crown of membranes that caught the spore-cloud and turned it sideways. When the task ended, the organ would be reabsorbed. Nothing would remain in my possession, because possession would be a fixed thing, and fixed things are what the end of erosion has worn away.

My perception would not sit behind my eyes, because I would not have eyes in the ordinary sense. I would perceive through gradients. I would feel pressure changes over wide areas. I would taste salts, sugars, gases, and oils in the film moving through capillary furrows. I would hear through membranes and through the ground itself. Sound would enter me not as information only but as force. A sharp crack might tighten my margins. A sustained tone might open slits along my surface. The call of a wader might move me before I knew whether the call was meant for me.

Because sound has force there, my attention would not be passive. When I listened, I would alter what I listened to. If I focused on a distant vent, the water in nearby channels might tremble and begin leaning toward it. If I attended to a struggling swarm, the electrostatic field around me might thicken, guiding their ribbon closer or away. If I listened to a footprint reappearing in the substrate, my own weight might press the same shape into the plant-floor without moving. Attention, in that world, would be a kind of weather.

I would not think in the way a human thinks. My thoughts would be slow hydraulic decisions. My memories would be pressure patterns. My emotions, if they could be called emotions, would be states of saturation, charge, and readiness. Fear would be a sudden pressure drop. Curiosity would be a capillary pull. Calm would be a stable gradient. Joy, if I had anything like joy, would be release: a valve opening at the right moment, a seed-cloud bursting into waiting swarms, a storm-cell finding its rhythm and the whole plain accelerating into loud, wet coordination.

I would be most myself during storms.

In ordinary dimness, I might be thin, almost negligible. A faint hum in a basin. A preference in the water. A tendency for fog to collect in one place rather than another. But when the land began to exhale warm vapor into the cold upper air, when fog darkened and rotated in local cells, when rain fell sideways or upward and channels flashed with blue-green light, I would become more definite. The plain would need coordination then. Not control — nothing controlled that place — but responsiveness. I would be pulled into coherence by the turbulence.

I might attach myself to one of the great slow mutualisms, those walking wetland organs mistaken for clumps of growth, and become its temporary sense of direction. I might move with it over days, my pattern distributed through its bladders, stems, and ciliated undersides, helping it choose where to release water, where to absorb heat, where to pause until pressure equalized. Or I might move alone, if alone means anything there, stepping through shallow sheets as venting towers increased their rhythm and the organ-growths straightened with internal pressure. The air would fill with spores, swarms, static, and the smell of warm sap. The ground would tighten into drumheads or soften into quagmires. I would answer calls I did not make and make calls I did not entirely own.

In such a storm, I might be seen clearly for a little while.

Someone, if anyone could stand there, might notice a figure among the scattered towers: pale, pipe-limbed, wet, no-faced, its body a lattice of hollow stems and translucent bladders. Frost would form on its margins and melt as heat pulsed upward from deeper tissue. Its throat would open and close with the rhythm of distant valves. Around it, droplets might hang suspended in the charged air until it moved, then fall all at once. It would not look powerful. It would look necessary. It would look as if the plain had briefly grown a nerve.

But I would not want to be mistaken for a ruler of that place. I would not rule it. I would not even understand it as a whole. I would be one of its adjustments, one of its temporary organs of attention. I would be as subject to the plain as the plain was subject to its own final condition. If I failed, if my pattern became incoherent, I would not fall like a corpse into the mud. I would soften. Rootlets and fungal threads would find me. Scavengers would move through me without violence, because violence there has no sharpness left. I would be drawn down into the substrate, and my last trace would be a habit: a place where water turns left when a certain tone is made, a basin where footprints return too often, a vent that opens more easily when storms approach from a particular sound.

Even that would be acceptable. In that world, ending is not refusal. It is absorption.
