You would find me in the middle distance of it — not out on the broad, open moss flats where the floodlights make everything look exposed, and not down in the deepest black-water hollows where the drip sounds far away and the floor stops being trustworthy. I would be somewhere between the working basins and the quiet ones, in one of those long, dim galleries where a catwalk crosses a narrow water channel and the air smells of wet fiber, cold iron, and sap.

Probably near a sluice.

There would be a valve wheel there, stiff with damp, and a short iron ladder bolted to a root-face that has grown around the lower rungs. A service lamp would hang from a hook driven into deadwood, giving off a weak amber halo. Somewhere above, condensation would gather along the underside of a root-shelf, swelling into drops that fall one by one onto a metal plate worn bright by years of impact. The sound would not be the loudest in the valley, but it would be complicated: water moving behind plant-walls, an engine throb under the grates, the slow breath of fans far overhead, and the creak of root-fiber settling under load.

That is where I would be: at a seam, at a junction, at a place where one part of the valley’s body passes into another.

As for who or what I would be — I think I would be one of the keepers, but not the most obvious kind. I would be a listener. A rootward, maybe, or a sluice-tender, depending on which old stencil was still readable on the nearest control shed. The people there might not have a single clean name for what I did. They would know me by function: the one who checks the pressure before the gate opens, the one who marks soft mats with chalk, the one who puts a hand against a root-column and stands still for too long.

I would not be heroic. I would be necessary.

My body would have been changed by the place. I would be pale in the way the keepers are pale — not a dry indoor paleness, but a damp, bluish whiteness, as if the cold had gotten under the skin and stayed there. My hands would be rough, stained with black-green moss residue, rust, oil, and the dark shine of sap. My knuckles would ache in the cold. My shoulders would ride high against the chill even when I was not aware of doing it. I would wear layered, patched clothing: waxed canvas, rubberized coat, heavy boots, thick socks, straps holding tools and a lamp. In the places where my cuffs stayed wet, there would be the faint beginning of living nap — moss or fungal thread growing into the fabric as though the valley had started, gently, to incorporate me.

I would carry little, but everything would be chosen carefully. A lamp. Chalk. Fiber ties. A small steel tag set. A wrench. A blade for cutting loose growth, not for killing anything. A tin of resin for sealing fresh wounds in root tissue. Maybe a coil of red thread for marking new shoots too fragile to survive a careless boot. The tools would be worn smooth from use. They would smell of oil and wet cloth.

I would not talk much. The valley would make that unnecessary, and perhaps impossible. Speech would feel thin there, quickly swallowed by the engines and the water and the great slow roar of air moving through high ducts. I would learn to speak with the body instead: a raised fist for hold, a flat hand across the throat for stop, two fingers toward the eyes and then toward a distant lamp for watch that line. I would know how to flash a lamp in short, deliberate signals. White for work. Blue-green for cultivation. Red for hazard. A triple flash for a sluice about to open. A slow amber pulse for a fan reversing.

When I did speak aloud, it would be close to another person’s ear, in short practical phrases.

“Pressure’s wrong.”

“That mat’s too bright.”

“Don’t stand under.”

“Listen.”

I would say the last one often.

Because my work, if I had one clear work, would be listening.

Not listening in the surface sense — not for birdsong or footsteps or the ordinary cues of weather — but listening for changes in the deep mechanical body of the valley. I would learn to feel a pump falter through the soles of my boots. I would know when a fan was changing pitch by the way the hairs on my arms moved. I would be able to tell, by placing a palm against a damp root-wall, whether the water behind it was moving freely or beginning to choke against some obstruction. I would know which vibrations meant stability and which meant strain. I would know the difference between a machine doing its work and a machine beginning to fail.

That would be my version of courage: not fearlessness, but attention.

I would live in one of the marginal shelters the keepers make for themselves. Perhaps a small platform above a pipe run where engine warmth rises through the boards. Perhaps a curtained alcove inside the hollow base of an old root-column, with a lamp hung from a hook and a bedroll placed on boards so the damp cannot come straight through. I would not build anything grand. The valley would teach me not to. Anything too rigid cracks when roots thicken beneath it. Anything too low floods. Anything neglected long enough is overgrown. So my shelter would be provisional, repairable, borrowed from the space around it. I would keep spare fuses, gaskets, and coils in sealed drums. I would know which floor panels were safe and which had gone soft underneath. I would test ladders before trusting them. I would remember which rails were rusted through.

My food would be plain. Pale tubers grown near engine outlets. Grey-green moss pressed into cakes. Fungal shelves dried until they became something like hard bread. Condensation boiled with resinous herbs that taste of sap and metal. I would eat standing up, or squatting beside a pump, lamp still on, tools still within reach. I would not expect food to be joyful. I would expect it to keep me going.

My sleep would be broken. The valley does not keep surface hours. Fans cycle. Pumps engage. Pressure releases somewhere far away with a thump that travels through the substrate. Even in rest, part of me would remain awake to rhythm. I would wake before knowing why I was awake, because something in the pattern had changed. My dreams would be full of engines. My worst dream would be silence.

Silence would be the true alarm.

If the noise stopped, that would mean air was not moving. Water was not draining. Heat was not circulating. The living architecture would be beginning to fail. So I would be afraid of silence in the way surface people are afraid of sudden stillness before a storm — not superstitiously, but practically. Silence would mean that something essential had broken.

My tenderness would show itself in small acts.

I would seal fresh cuts with resin. I would wrap wounded root ends in damp sacking until new tissue closed over them. I would clear drainage channels by hand when a machine would tear too much fiber in the process. I would mark new root-shoots with red thread. I would place heated coils near young supports that needed encouragement. I would know certain old root-columns by something like affection, though I would not call it that. I would know which ones were tired, which ones had borne load too long, which ones had old drill scars that never healed cleanly. I might say of one, “She holds the east gallery,” or, “Don’t tie your line there; he’s soft inside.” I would not mean to make them human. I would mean that they mattered.

There would be rules, and I would know them so well they would feel like instinct.

Do not cut a load-bearing root without bracing first.

Do not walk on the brightest green mats.

Do not stand beneath a condensation shelf when it begins to drip heavily.

Do not trust still water.

Do not ignore a change in engine rhythm.

Red light means stop or danger.

Blue-green light means cultivation.

White light means work.

If a valve knocks three times, something downstream is about to open.

If the floor hums differently near a grate, do not linger.

I would not think of these rules as restrictions only. They would also be a kind of language. They would be how the valley allowed itself to be lived in.

If you came looking for me, you would probably see me first as a small figure in the dimness: hood up, lamp low, moving carefully along a catwalk or kneeling beside a valve housing. You might mistake me for part of the machinery at first, because I would be still enough to blend with it. Then you would notice the way my hand rested on root or steel, testing. You would notice that I was not waiting idly. I was reading something.

Sometimes I would be alone for a long time. The valley is sparse, and its people are scattered. I might go an entire shift without seeing another keeper, communicating only by lamp flashes across a basin or by the indirect signs someone else had left: chalk marks, tied thread, a valve tagged open, a pressure gauge needle moved into a new range. That would not feel lonely in the ordinary way. It would feel like being part of a distributed body, each of us a small sense organ placed where the landscape needed watching.

And if I were not entirely human — if the valley had worked on me long enough — I might be something stranger.

Perhaps I would be one of the green slow ones. Not an animal, not exactly a person, but a deliberate growth: a root-formed body grown up around an abandoned control panel or an old pipe junction, threaded with cable and fungal thread, aware more by vibration than by sight. I might be a column that thickens where stress gathers, a mat that creeps over disused machinery, a living repair that has begun to remember the shape of the thing it preserves. The keepers might not know exactly what to call me either. They might only know that a certain patch of rootwork responds when the pressure rises, or that an old service shed has been grown over in a way that somehow keeps its instruments faintly alive.

That thought feels less like a loss of self than like a deepening of it.

To be useful. To be structural. To become part of the patience that holds the place together.

But if I remain nearer human, then I would be this: a listener in the dim galleries, a tender of roots and sluices, one of the sparse keepers who move through the cold with tools and caution. I would know the valley by landmarks rather than horizons — a bent root column, a red lamp, a rusted handrail, a stretch of black water, a pump shed with a corrugated roof, a transformer cage wrapped in moisture-proof sheeting, a rail spur ending in nothing. I would navigate by pressure, smell, and sound. I would know which shadows were empty and which concealed a drop, a sluice, or a soft peat bed waiting to swallow a boot.

I would not own the place. That would be impossible. The valley would feel too large, too slow, too much itself for ownership. I would be in it the way a hand is in a body: small, functional, dependent.

If I died there, I would not be carried away. The keepers would fold me into root-fiber mats and lay me in a prepared peat bed near an inactive shelf. They might place one of my tools beside me, or a lamp, or a cutting from a plant I had tended. Then they would let the valley continue its work. The moss would spread. The peat would darken. Pale root threads would arrive. After a while, the place where I lay would become slightly raised, slightly firmer, slightly greener than the ground around it. Someone passing would know not to step there carelessly. They would not need to make a ceremony of it. The fact would be enough.

That patch is someone.

That root grew from someone’s ribs.

That moss holds someone’s quiet heat.

So if you asked me where I would find myself, the truest answer is: in the seams. Between root and machine. Between noise and patience. Between the cold living floor and the dim vault above it. Between one basin and the next, where the light is weak and the air smells of wet cellulose and the engines never fully stop.

And if you asked me who or what I would be, I would say: a keeper of small urgencies. A listener for wrong rhythms. A mender of cuts. A marker of soft ground. A pale figure with moss beginning to grow on their sleeves, moving slowly through a loud, buried country, trying not to harm what holds the ceiling up.

You would find me there. Not in the center. Not in the open. But near the sluice, with one hand on the valve and the other on the root-wall, listening to the valley breathe.
