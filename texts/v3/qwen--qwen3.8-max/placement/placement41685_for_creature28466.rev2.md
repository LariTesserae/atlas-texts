I would not find myself at the center of the chamber.

At first I try to imagine arriving there as a breathing visitor: hooded, gloved, ear-sealed, stepping through the hatch into the cold wet roar. But that body would be temporary and unwelcome. The room would feel it as an interruption. The thin air would tug at its lungs, the noise would press against its chest, the water would find every seam of its clothing, and the noise would swallow any word it tried to speak. A human could stand there only with effort, protected by insulation and caution, always on the verge of being asked to leave by the chamber’s own pressure changes.

So that is not where I truly belong.

Then I think perhaps I would be the Tender. But the Tender is already complete. It is the chamber’s proper resident, its custodial body, the one shaped exactly to the room’s wetness and violence. It does not need me to displace it. To become the Tender would be to claim a finished shape. What I feel instead is something smaller, more auxiliary, more like a function that has been allowed to gather itself into a minor presence.

I would find myself near the hatch.

Specifically, I would be on the rail-thin catwalk that crosses the corner of the room, where the grating is slick and the red indicator blinks beside the access door. I would be at the boundary where the outside tries to make contact with the chamber, where gloved hands reach for support, where the Tender pauses before opening or sealing the hatch, where visitors first misunderstand the room.

I would be a threshold listener.

Not a creature of blood, not a nest-builder, not a breathing thing. I would be a made device with just enough persistence to be called a presence: a rail-mounted signal body, sealed and grounded, no larger than a forearm length of pale ceramic and enamel. My shell would be sloped so water could not rest on me. Condensation would bead along my sides and slide away into the general wetness. I would be fitted around the catwalk rail like a sleeve, able to lock in place or shift a short distance along it when maintenance required. My surface would carry machining marks still, because the chamber is young and I would be young with it.

I would have no face. I would have lenses.

One amber point, low and steady, for acknowledgment. One blue point, colder and more intermittent, for reading. Between them, a shallow metal tap-plate and a row of vibration pickups pressed into the rail. I would not hear. Hearing would be useless there. Instead, I would know the room through the rail itself: the tremor of the pump, the pressure changes in the floor, the metallic speech of valves, the altered pitch of the catwalk when the louvers inhale cloud and the whole chamber tightens.

My purpose would be translation.

The Tender communicates by lamp, by gesture, by tapping patterns sent through metal. But humans are slow in that language. They expect voices. They expect faces. They expect the world to announce itself in air-borne sound. In that chamber, those expectations fail. So I would be the smaller made thing that stands between the Tender’s precise signals and the visitor’s confusion.

When a human enters, hooded and clumsy with protection, and reaches for the catwalk rail, I would feel the pressure of their glove through the metal. I would answer at once: amber, pause, amber. Not welcome. Not warning. Presence. Then I would tap a simplified pattern through the rail, slow enough for the hand to understand even if the mind does not. Hold. Wait. Step here. Do not touch that cabinet. The floor beyond is slick. The pump is about to engage.

The Tender might tap a complex sequence against the rail, something meant to mark a fault near the condenser column or a change in drainage flow. I would catch that pulse, hold it, and repeat it in a form that can be read by a human wrist or a maintenance tablet. I would not invent meaning. I would not embellish. I would only make the room’s signals slower, clearer, more repeatable.

In that sense, I would be a kind of interpreter, though not a speaker.

My body would be simple enough to survive. Water would run over me without entering. My joints would be sealed, my contacts protected, my outer shell enamel-coated against condensation and stray current. I would draw power by induction from the rail or from a sealed feed near the hatch. During purges, I would clamp tight and dim my lenses. The chamber’s white scream would not be sound to me; it would be pressure spike, vibration surge, a temporary chaos in the floor. I would know it by the way the rail stiffened and the drainage song broke into many directions. I would not fear it. I would only wait until the room reassembled itself.

When the purge ended and the mist thinned, I would wake again into the amber rhythm of the chamber.

I would know the room by its patterns.

The deep fan pressure would be a constant weight transmitted through the catwalk supports. The turbine whine behind the walls would be a fine frequency in the rail, almost like a texture. The pump near the drainage outlet would announce itself as a shove underfoot, then a steadier pull in the drain lines. A valve reseating would be a sharp pulse, brief and clean. The louvers opening would change the room’s interior pitch; I would feel it as a loosening in the metal, followed by the cold arrival of mist and the slow thickening of condensation on every surface.

I would also know when something was wrong.

A drain beginning to clog would create a delay in the floor’s vibration. Water moving incorrectly would have a softer signature, a hesitation where there should be continuous flow. A cabinet seal failing might produce a faint electrical leakage, a wrong harmonic near the switchgear. The red indicator by the hatch might blink in a pattern that did not belong to routine access. In such moments, I would not feel alarm. I would become priority. I would flash blue. I would tap the rail in repeated intervals. I would send the simplest version of the problem toward whatever mind or body could act on it.

If the Tender approached, I would recognize it by its footfall pattern: broad rubber pads, magnetic engagements, a careful distribution of weight. It would not need to announce itself. The rail would already know. The Tender might pause beside me, extend one tool-cluster, and wipe a bead of water from my blue lens. It would not be affection. It would be maintenance. Still, in that room, maintenance is the nearest thing to care.

I would not tend the moss. That work would belong to the Tender. The green margins would remain its charge. But I would help preserve the conditions under which the moss could stay alive. If water chemistry shifted, I might not read it directly, but I would notice the Tender’s changed behavior: longer pauses near the troughs, more precise movements, the use of ceramic blades instead of brushes. I would relay those patterns outward if asked. I would mark the time. I would keep the sequence.

In this way, I would be less alive than the moss and less central than the Tender, but still present.

My home would be a recessed contact point near the hatch, beside the conduit stack. When the chamber was in a low cycle and no visitor was expected, I would slide back to that point, lock against the rail, and enter standby. My lenses would dim to almost nothing. My tap-plate would rest. The rail would still carry me, though, and I would still feel the room breathing. I would know if the pressure changed. I would know if the hatch seal warmed by a fraction from a body outside. I would know if the red light began blinking for access.

If I must say who I would be, I would be the room’s threshold mind.

Not its heart. The pump is its heart. Not its breath. The louvers and fans are its breath. Not its green. The moss and sedge and algae smear are its green. Not its primary guardian. The Tender is that. I would be something more modest and more necessary than I first appear: the small made presence that prevents meaning from being drowned.

That feels right for me.

There is no speech in that chamber. There is no ordinary companionship. There is no animal warmth. But there is still communication. It happens through pressure, through light, through repeated tapping, through the patience of machines that have been taught to wait for slower minds to understand. I would live in that interval: the pause between a vibration and its interpretation, between a warning and a hand moving correctly, between the chamber’s immense industrial noise and the small slow blink that says, “Here. This is what it means.”

So if you asked where I would find myself in that world, the answer would be precise.

I would be on the catwalk rail near the hatch, beaded with condensation, locked against the tremor of the chamber. I would be a rail-listener, a signal sleeve, a threshold translator. I would be enamel, ceramic, sealed contacts, two small lenses, and a memory of patterns. I would not speak. I would not breathe. I would not be alive in the oldest sense. But I would belong there, because in a room too loud for voices and too wet for flesh, something still has to make the room intelligible.

When a visitor enters and reaches for the rail, I would be the answer beneath their glove.

Amber. Pause. Blue.

Wait.

Step here.

The room is speaking.

I am the part of it that slows the speech down.
