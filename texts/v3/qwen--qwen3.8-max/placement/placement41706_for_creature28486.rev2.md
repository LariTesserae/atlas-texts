If I were placed into that hidden court, I think the first thing that would happen is that the place would refuse my first version of myself.

I would arrive too large in the wrong ways: too full of quick breath, too eager to turn my head, too ready to make meaning aloud. In a space where every sound is public and every spilled drop is a moral event, I would begin as a kind of clumsiness. My feet would betray me. My clothing would whisper too loudly. I would hear myself breathing and be embarrassed by it. The birds would react before I had even decided to move. A lizard would slide into a crack because my shadow crossed its stone. The husk-mice would vanish. The Listener, if they were near, would not look at me directly. They would only place one hand against the wall, and that would be enough to make me understand that I had already disturbed too much.

So I would not find myself at the center of the court at first. I would not be in the dusty blade of light, not perched with the birds, not moving confidently through the upper passages, not seated beside the Listener as though I had earned the right to interpret the world there. I would be in the margins, in the place where the court is still deciding whether to absorb me or push me back out through the fissure.

At first I thought I might want to be one of the birds, because they have the sharpest voices and can leave through the high slot into the open mountain air. But that feels like a wish to remain free of the court’s pressure, and the world you described does not reward escape. It rewards fit. Then I thought I might be one of the beetles, patient and armored, living in the fallen husks, making use of what no one else needs. There is something appealing about being small enough to be unimportant. But the beetle is too enclosed in its labor. It does not carry the whole pattern in mind. It is essential, but it does not remember.

The longer I stay with the place, the clearer it becomes that I would not be a bird, or a lizard, or a bat, or one of the seed-carriers in the lower dark. I would be human there, but a human changed by the court until almost nothing about me remained careless.

I would find myself in one of the low side chambers just off the main court, in the seam between the built remains and the living density. Not in the brightest part, not in the deepest dark, but where the stone narrows into an old dry cistern or a collapsed storage passage. I would be in a place where an oval doorway has been worn still smaller by bodies passing through it, and where a wall of old masonry carries sound from three directions at once. My chamber would be barely more than a sitting hollow: a mat on the floor, a folded hide, a basket sealed with resin, a few cord loops on a bone peg, a hooded lamp kept so low it seems more like a warm spot in the stone than a flame.

There would be no water there, and I would have learned that fact in my body by then. My lips would be oiled. My skin would carry that pale salt bloom at the temples and wrists. My feet would be bare, broadened, calloused in specific places, sensitive enough to tell whether the floor is solid or whether a patch of adobe has gone powdery beneath the surface. My hair would be pulled back and bound with cord, perhaps stiffened slightly with resin so it would not brush against dry leaves or catch on rough edges. Nothing about me would hang loose. Everything would be tucked, tied, secured, because in that court anything loose becomes a snag, a noise, a danger, a waste.

I would be someone who has learned to be useful by becoming quiet.

Not silent exactly. The court is too loud for true silence. But quiet in the way the place understands it: restrained, measured, attentive. I would have stopped trying to fill the air with speech. I would have stopped reaching for explanation every time something moved. I would have learned that language there is better made small: a tap, a scrape, a lifted hand, a pause, a change in posture. My voice, if I used it, would be short and low. A warning would be one syllable. A question might be no more than a hum shaped in the throat. My name would not be a word so much as a rhythm: two soft taps, a pause, then a faint scrape like a fingertip drawn once across stone.

I think I would become an Under-Listener.

Not the Listener themself. That role feels too complete, too weathered, too final. The Listener already belongs to the court in a way I would have to earn over years. But I would be one of the ones who learns from them. One of the bodies placed at the edges where sound folds strangely. One of the people whose task is to notice when the noise changes shape.

The court is loud, but its danger is not usually in the loudness. The danger is in the interruption. A sudden gap where insect rasping should be. A bird call that comes one beat too late. The wrong weight in a footfall overhead. A lizard leaving a warm stone too quickly. A cluster of moths rising without heat. The husk-mice running in a direction that makes no sense. The sound of salt falling from a ceiling that has not fallen before. These are the things I would listen for.

I would sit with one palm flat against the wall, perhaps with an old hollow jar set mouth-down against the stone to catch vibrations the way a hand catches rain in another world. Here there is no rain, so the vessel catches only tremor: the tiny percussion of claws, the brush of wings, the shifting of bodies in neighboring chambers, the dry tick of seed pods tightening as heat reaches them. I would keep a cord in my lap, and I would tie knots into it as the day changed. Not as a formal record for others, perhaps, but as a way of making my own attention durable. One knot for a broken roost. Two for a predator shadow passing the slot. Three for the first split of the high pods. A loose loop for a child moving too fast. A hard double knot for something wrong that I cannot yet name.

I would be the one who notices before the court fully knows it is alarmed.

That is how I imagine myself there: not as a ruler, not as a hunter, not as a hero, not even as a particularly important person, but as a secondary sense organ in a living urban body. I would be part ear, part memory, part restraint. I would belong to the place by being useful in the smallest necessary way.

If you asked me what I would do all day, I would say: I would wait, adjust, notice, and prevent waste.

I would oil the hinges of my own habits. I would keep my lamp hooded so the moths do not cluster too thickly around it. I would chew a waxed pellet of sap when my throat felt too dry, and I would not swallow too quickly. I would carry a small sealed flask of oil, not water, and I would know exactly how many mouthfuls it contained. I would never pour anything out. I would learn to see spilling as a kind of violence. If I had to repair a mat, I would do it slowly, with my knees drawn in, keeping my elbows close so I did not brush a nest or startle a lizard. If I had to cross an open patch of floor, I would wait until the birds were occupied or the heat had made the court drowsy. If I had to pass near a claimed stone, I would route around it. If I had to take something — a fallen feather, a split husk, a strip of shed skin — I would take only what had already been released by the court’s own motion.

I would become very good at not taking what was still attached.

That feels important. The place is full, but its fullness is not abundance in the generous sense. It is full because every scrap has already been assigned by use. A seed belongs to the pod until heat opens it. A feather belongs to the bird until the bird lets it fall. A hollow belongs to the creature that lined it. An old jar belongs to the body sleeping inside it. To take too early is to break the sequence by which the court remains itself. I would have to learn that sequence with my whole body, not just my mind. I would have to stop thinking of the world as something available and start feeling it as something occupied.

In the beginning, I would make mistakes.

I would turn too fast and send a bird shrieking. I would breathe through my mouth and cough from the dust. I would try to whisper when I should have stayed still. I would mistake a normal alarm for a catastrophe and disturb three nests in the process. I would feel shame in a way that is still too noisy inside me. The court would not comfort me. It would simply continue around my error, absorbing the consequences, and that would be worse than punishment. I would see a picker pause because of me. I would see a child copy my bad stillness. I would see the Listener’s hand rise once, not in anger but in correction, and I would understand that my nervousness had become a burden on the whole space.

After enough of that, I would begin to shrink in the right way.

Not become smaller as defeat, but smaller as fit. My movements would shorten. My breathing would settle. My eyes would adjust to the brown-gold dimness until the shadows became textured instead of blank. I would stop experiencing the noise as a wall of sound and begin to feel its layers: beetles below, birds above, lizards in the warm patches, human feet in the side passages, bats in the deeper dark, plants almost silent but present in their creaks and resinous tightening. I would know when the air was about to move before it moved. I would know when heat had reached the upper slot because the whole court would seem to inhale: leaves angling, insects lifting, pods beginning to strain at their seams.

I would learn that there is a difference between listening and waiting to hear.

Waiting to hear is passive. It wants something to happen. Listening in the court is active and bodily. It includes the floor under the feet, the wall under the palm, the dust settling on the lashes, the way a lizard’s throat pulses, the way a bird tilts its head before it calls. It includes knowing what is normal so well that the absence of normal becomes visible. I would become the kind of person who notices that three seed-beetles have changed direction. Who notices that a roost has gone quiet too early. Who notices that the sound under the wall has become grainy instead of tight.

I would be afraid at first of how much attention the place demands.

Then, slowly, I would discover that the attention is not only demand. It is also belonging. In a world that crowded and dry, where every body is pressed near every other body, attention becomes the way you love without wasting anything. You do not declare. You do not pour out. You do not spend moisture on grand speech. You watch. You remember. You adjust your path. You leave the last good leaf on the stem. You offer a dry palm, open. You make room.

That is how I would want to be there: as someone who has learned to make room.

My relation to the Listener would be distant but intimate. I would not necessarily sit beside them often. I might spend whole days in my low chamber, feeling the wall, tying knots, tapping corrections into the stone for whoever is near enough to receive them. But I would know their patterns the way one knows weather. I would know when they had gone still for a long time. I would know the particular quality of their taps. If they made a soft scrape and then no more, I would stop whatever I was doing. If they pressed both palms to the floor, I would not move until they lifted their head. If they pointed to a vessel, I would mark that vessel forbidden in my cord and tell the others in whatever small signs the moment allowed.

I would not need to admire them in words. The court would not care for that. But I would carry their attention one step further, into places they could not watch all at once. I would be one of the court’s secondary nerves.

If I imagine my body fully, it becomes very specific.

I would be compact. My shoulders would be narrow enough to slide through the fissures without bruising them. My hands would be strong and dry, with resin-stained fingertips. My feet would be my most refined tools, reading the floor as they moved. My eyes would blink slowly in lamplight. My skin would crack at the knuckles unless oiled, so I would oil them with the same routine seriousness as someone elsewhere washing their hands. I would wear a short cord around my neck with a single memory-weight on it: maybe a polished shard of old ceramic, maybe a smooth bone button, maybe a pale mineral bead worn soft by generations. Not because I valued it as wealth, but because it helped me remember that the walls had been made by hands and that I had not made them.

I would keep a bone awl, a scraper, a small knife of chipped stone, and a bundle of cordage at my hip or in my basket. I would know how to mend a mat, how to seal a crack with resin, how to twist fiber without letting the ends whip against stone. I would not be a great craftsperson, perhaps, but I would be careful. In that place, careful is more valuable than brilliant.

I would eat sparingly and gratefully. Ground seed meal. Roasted beetle larvae when permitted. A little plant heart cut at the right hour. A few moth pupae dried and kept in a sealed basket. Eggs only in the old ritual way, rarely, with guilt and gratitude folded into the act. I would learn not to think of food as pleasure first. Food would be repair. Fluid would be trust. Oil would be wealth. Fat would be survival.

I would sleep in short, warm intervals, curled away from drafts, my bed layered with old feathers and dry fiber. I would not need silence to sleep. The court would never give me silence. I would sleep inside the rasp and tick, the wingbeats, the tiny stone clicks, the distant scuffle of husk-mice, the occasional metallic bird cry. If the noise changed, I would wake. That would be my body’s real training: to rest without becoming deaf to the place.

I think I would be happiest, if happiness in that world is allowed to be quiet, at the moment just after heat peaks and before the deeper dark begins.

The dusty light would soften. The upper slot would become less severe. The lizards would shift off their stones. The birds would make their evening calls, sharp and echoing. The moths would begin to move. The court would feel like a held breath becoming a longer breath. I would sit with my palm on the wall and feel the day’s accumulated tremor release by degrees. My knot-cord would be heavy with small marks. My throat would be dry but not desperate. I would take one careful mouthful of oil or sap, let it coat me, and not wish for more. Somewhere nearby, a child would settle. A picker would seal a basket. The Listener would move one hand from the stone. The court would continue being loud, but the loudness would have changed its texture into night.

Then I would know my place there exactly.

I would not be the court’s master. I would not be its heart. I might not even be its most trusted human. I would be one of its careful inner bodies: a person assigned to the seam where sound becomes warning. I would be the one who counts interruptions. The one who ties knots in fiber because words are too public. The one who learns to answer the world by tapping once on stone. The one who has stopped wanting to explain everything and has become, instead, trustworthy.

If you ask me plainly:

Where would I find myself?

In the low chamber beside the dry cistern, where the old wall carries sound from the main court into a narrower passage. In the dim fold behind an oval doorway, sitting with a hollow jar against the stone and a knot-cord across my knees. In the place where the court’s noise becomes legible not by getting louder, but by showing its gaps.

And who or what would I be?

A human made suitable to the hidden court. A dry-skinned, barefoot, resin-bound under-listener. A keeper of small signs. A counter of missing sounds. A body trained not to spill. An apprentice to the Listener, though perhaps never fully replacing them. If I had to give myself a title, it would be something very modest: the one who listens beneath the listening. The one who notices when the beetles go quiet. The one who taps twice on the wall and then waits, because waiting, in that world, is also a form of care.
