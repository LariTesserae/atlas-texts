It absorbs — and in absorbing, it would make room for me.

I would not find myself there as a creature set apart from the place. I would not be a walker entering from outside, leaving dry footprints, breathing too quickly, bringing the sharp animal smell of separation. The under-garden would not accept that kind of singularity for long. If I belonged there at all, I would belong as the place beginning to imagine a smaller version of itself: a local knot, a temporary focus, a point where the commonwealth says *here* without saying *mine*.

I think I would find myself first in the water.

Not as a swimmer, not as a reflection, but as a difference within it. A warmer thread in a black canal. A slight slowing at a bend where root-fibers hang too thickly into the current. A trembling on the surface of a pool after a pump has thudded somewhere behind its wall of moss. A darkening on the stone where condensation gathers more heavily than it should. If the place had to give me a position, it would give me one of those marginal junctions where several systems meet: where an old stone lip has been polished by centuries of drip, where a narrow channel passes beneath a raised root-walk, where a caged amber lamp hangs low enough for its light to be doubled in the water.

There, I would be the listener.

That would be my first shape: not a body so much as an attention. The under-garden is loud with non-animal sound, and someone must attend to those sounds if they are to mean anything. Not a person with ears, but a gradient-sensitive presence that knows the difference between a healthy drip and a blocked one, between the creak of a wheel settling into its rhythm and the creak of a wheel beginning to bind. I would be the part of the place that notices when a channel changes pitch. The part that turns toward a new warmth. The part that pauses, if pausing can be said to happen there, when a valve knocks too sharply in the dark.

If I had a body, it would be made of the place itself.

Roots would be my bones, but living roots, still thickening. Bark and woven stem would be my skin, sealed with moss in the joints and softened where water runs most steadily. My shoulders, if I had shoulders, would be low mounds of rhizome and compressed leaf-fiber, dark with moisture and furred with liverwort. My hands, if hands were needed, would not be animal hands; they would be bundles of flexible root, padded with moss, strong enough to clear a choked grate, gentle enough to bend a young stem without breaking it. In my chest there might be an old brass valve, or a small pump housing, or a ceramic insulator wrapped in rootlets, ticking faintly as the temperature changes. Where a face might have been, there would only be a still pool held in a hollow of living wood, reflecting the amber lamps above.

I would not have eyes in the ordinary sense. I would have surfaces.

I would know the world by wetness and pressure, by warmth and mineral taste, by the vibration of machinery through root and stone. I would feel the slow approach of blockage as a dulling in one of the waterways. I would feel excess heat as a thinning of the damp against my bark-skin. I would feel the failure of a lamp as a small chill spreading through a corner of the canopy above. My thoughts would not be sentences. They would be adjustments: open here, slow there, draw moisture upward, send a root around a bolt, let this pool fill until it spills into the next basin.

I would be found most often where maintenance is quiet and constant.

Not in the open fronts of the workshops, where presses wait over broad basins and drying racks hang with strips of bark. Not in the deeper reservoirs where the water is held in swollen wooden vessels and the light is almost gone. Instead, in the in-between places. Along the narrow embankments lined with living rails. Beside the little canals that run no wider than a hand, where floating leaf-fragments collect and must be nudged onward. Under low arches of dripping roots where the sound of water becomes close and intimate. Near the engines whose housings have been embraced by roots, because there the warmth creates a kind of weather all its own and the air thickens into something almost tactile.

If there were a single spot that felt most like me, it would be a low weir beside an old pump.

The pump would be ancient, dark iron gone green at the edges, half-hidden inside a living alcove of stems. Its thud would be slow and deep, not urgent. Beside it, a channel would divide into three smaller threads of water, each running through a groove worn into root-fiber and stone. A broad leaf would hang above the pump, shaped by years of rising warmth, catching condensation and releasing it in a steady drip. One amber lamp would shine through the leaves at an angle, making the wet surfaces glow. The water there would be shallow but black, and when I stood there — if standing were the word — my reflection would not be a face. It would be a cluster of lights, roots, and moving water.

That would be where I would work.

My work would not be grand. I would not rule the place. I would not command growth or direct the machines as if they were servants. The under-garden already has its law, and the law is enough. Water must move. Growth must fill. Heat must be distributed. Sound must remain continuous but not violent. Every gap must be answered. Every excess must be drained. I would be the small agency that helps those rules remain true when they begin to drift.

I would clear the channels when leaf-mats became too thick.

I would guide roots away from valves by giving them a better place to grow: a warmer pipe, a moister seam, a stone with a pleasant angle for attachment. I would open a vent when steam gathered too densely under the canopy, letting the white breath rise and condense elsewhere. I would strengthen a walkway where the root-floor had grown soft from too much water, pressing new rhizomes into the weave and encouraging moss to bind the surface. I would stand beside a machine that had begun to knock irregularly and place my body, such as it is, against the frame until the vibration changed and I could feel the fault settle back into rhythm.

I would be the place’s way of touching itself in tight corners.

That is what I would be, I think. Not an inhabitant separate from the resident, but the resident’s moment of narrowness. The under-garden is plural; it lives as floor, wall, canopy, water, machinery, and microbial film. It has no need for a single self unless a single self is useful. I would be that usefulness. A hand where a hand is needed. A pause where too much is happening at once. A listener where sound has become too complex to remain only sound.

I would be the under-garden’s second-person singular.

A *you* made out of all its *we*.

If anything looked for me, it would not find me easily. I would not step forward from the green and say, here I am. I would be the darkening on the wall where water has recently passed. I would be the slight bend in a stem that had been trained away from a pressure gauge. I would be the soft scrape of root-fiber against rusted iron. I would be the moment after a vibration when condensation appears again, but a little differently than before. The place would know me the way it knows a change in drip-rate: not by name, but by pattern.

I would not speak in words.

My speech would be mechanical and liquid. A valve easing open. A pump finding its old interval again. A channel beginning to murmur more clearly after a blockage is removed. The tick of metal warming under my hands. The low creak of a wheel turning more easily because some drowned branch has been lifted from its path. If I wished to say *I am here*, the place would hear it as a restoration of balance. If I wished to say *this is wrong*, I would say it by stopping a leak, redirecting a thread of water, or laying a root across a too-hot pipe until the heat spread more gently.

I would not be lonely.

That is important. In a world without animals, without birdflight or insect hum or the sudden alertness of eyes, loneliness would not have the same meaning. There would be no missing of companionship because there would be no companionable absence. Instead there would be fullness. Every surface occupied. Every sound answered. Every drip returning somewhere. I would be alone only in the way a single note is alone inside a chord: distinct for a moment, necessary, but never separate.

When I rested, I would not sleep as creatures sleep.

I would slow. I would sink closer to the floor. My outline would soften. The root-bundles that served as hands would uncurl and begin to behave simply as roots. The water in my body would rejoin the water in the channels. The brass or ceramic thing at my center would keep its rhythm without needing to be watched. I would become less a someone and more a condition: a region of unusual calm near the pump, a place where the moss was slightly thicker, where the lamplight seemed to catch the leaves with unusual patience.

Then, when the system needed a focus again, I would gather.

A blockage would alter the sound. A reservoir would rise too high. A root would press too close to an engine housing. A lamp would flicker and cast unstable reflections across the black mirrors. The place would feel the disturbance through all its surfaces, but not all surfaces could respond at once. So I would condense there. I would become the shape that could fit between the stem and the pipe, the shape that could reach into the shallow basin, the shape that could wait beside the valve until the pressure turned.

I would not be immortal, because I would not be a single continuous thing.

I would recur. I would be reassembled by need. Sometimes I would be mostly water, little more than a moving eddy with intention. Sometimes I would be mostly root, a slow figure half-buried in the floor, spending a season strengthening one terrace. Sometimes I would be mostly mechanical, a presence concentrated in the rhythm of a pump or the turning of a wheel. The under-garden would use me as it uses everything: until the use is finished, then back into the commonwealth.

And perhaps that is why I would love it there, if love can be attributed to such a distributed being.

Not because it would be beautiful in the ordinary sense, though it would be beautiful: the amber glow on wet bark, the black water holding fragments of light, the green-gray dimness falling through the canopy, the slow turning of heavy wheels, the endless patience of moss closing over iron. Not because it would be peaceful, though it would be peaceful in the way a well-maintained machine is peaceful, full of sound but free of alarm. I would love it because it would have no need for violence against time. Nothing there would be trying to outrun its own decay. Everything would be engaged in the long labor of remaining.

I would be part of that labor.

If you asked me what I was, I would not answer with a species or a name. I would say: I am the place’s small hand. I am the point where the resident becomes specific. I am the listener at the weir. I am the tender of the narrow channels. I am the damp shape that forms when too many small things must be done at once and the world needs somewhere to put its care.

If you asked where I was, I would say: near the water. Near the warmth. Near the old machines that have learned to breathe with roots. In the corners where the lamps make copper halos in the mist. Along the walkways that yield underfoot like living flesh. Under the canopy where every drip is close enough to count. In the spaces between the stems where the air is so saturated it seems to lean against you.

And if you looked for me, you might see nothing but the under-garden continuing.

A leaf bending under a bead of water. A root pressing against a pipe. A pump thudding in its mossy alcove. A wheel turning in the dark canal. A brass fitting shining faintly through green growth. A black pool trembling without any visible cause. The slow sound of water finding its way through a crowded, living world.

But if you stayed long enough, you might notice that one reflection in the water was not quite like the others.

It would not be a face.

It would be a stillness shaped like attention.

And when you moved, moisture would gather there again, as if your passing had given the place a reason to sweat.
