I think I would not come into that place as a creature.

If I were there, my first instinct — to arrive, to look around, to choose a standing point — would be wrong. The basin would not accept a body organized by haste or appetite. It would ask me to give up feet, lungs, eyes, the whole animal grammar of pursuit. So the first true answer is this: I would not be a visitor. I would be something the water could forget.

At first, I feel myself becoming a film.

Not a prominent life, not a visible being with a shape that can be pointed to. A living stain. A thin, olive-darkening on pale stone where the wall sweats most quietly. I would be one of the slick skins that seal the rock and help it remember damp. My existence would be measured in wettings and dryings. When the air thickened, I would darken and wake. When the basin leaned toward dryness, I would contract into dullness, waiting without impatience. I would not have thoughts so much as states. I would not reach. I would adhere.

In that form, I would be close to the basin’s own skin. I would know the place by contact, by mineral intimacy, by the slow passage of moisture over what I am. I would not stand apart from the hollow and observe it. I would be part of its surface becoming alive. Perhaps that is the humblest answer, and perhaps it is already enough: to be the sheen on the stone when condensation returns, the faint gloss that makes the rock look touched by care.

But as I stay with the question, that image loosens.

I think I would also be one of the cistern plants.

Not the largest, not the most central, but one standing in its own damp socket near the broad middle of the basin, where the stone looks almost carved without having been carved. My leaves would be thick and inward-curved. My ribs would be succulent, darkened by water. At the center of me there would be that translucent darkness the basin seems to love — the look of something solid that has been steeped for so long it has nearly become liquid while refusing to lose form.

I would collect.

That would be my nature. Rain would enter me. Condensation would enter me. The overflow from higher leaves would enter me. I would hold the basin’s wetness in small green rooms. I would not seek light first; I would seek moisture. My movements would be so slow they would seem like stillness even to myself. Over hours, I would lean by fractions toward a weeping seam. When the air dried, I would close slightly around what I held. When the humidity returned, I would soften again.

And when I became too full, I would spill.

That spill would be my only eloquence. Not a cry, not a call, not a message aimed at anyone. Just the worn notch at the edge of a leaf releasing what it could no longer keep. The same gesture repeated until the gesture itself shaped me. If I had memory, it would be that notch. If I had speech, it would be the soft exhalation of water leaving a leaf. If I had desire, it would be only the desire to remain full enough to continue, but never so full that I broke.

I can imagine standing there for a very long time, separate from the other plants, not lonely. The space between us would not be emptiness. It would be respect, or perhaps the basin’s way of preventing any one life from becoming too loud. Each of us would keep its own territory. Each would be a vessel with roots.

Yet even that image feels too singular after a while.

If I let the place continue changing me, I become one of the pools.

A shallow one. Not deep enough to hide anything, just deep enough to hold a small disc of sky. I would lie in a hollow worn smooth by immense patience. My edges would be dark with microbial film. My bottom would be pale stone, faintly green where moisture had learned to linger. I would be so still that my reflection would seem solid. The sky would not merely appear in me; it would be kept by me.

A droplet would fall.

For a moment, I would break.

Then I would gather myself again.

That would be my understanding of events. A leaf detaches nearby and floats on me for a day. A spore drifts across my surface and is carried a little farther than it intended. A bead of mineral-laden water slides from a groove and enters me, adding one invisible grain to my weight. I would not chase anything. I would not swallow anything in hunger. I would simply receive, register, and return to stillness.

If the basin has eyes, I would want to be one of those open, blinking rarely. Not eyes that seek. Eyes that accept.

And still, beneath all these forms — film, plant, pool — I sense another answer waiting. It is quieter than the others. Less bodily. More like a position inside the basin’s meaning.

I would be the pause around the possible tool.

Near the rounded cobbles. Near the flat stones that look as if they might become grinding surfaces. Near the sharp flakes that the rock offers but no hand has ever taken. I would be the stillness that keeps them unused. Not a guardian, not a prohibition, not a spirit saying no. Something gentler than that. I would be the basin’s own reluctance to cross into grasping.

If the hollow stands at the threshold of primitive making, I would be the breath on that threshold.

I would be the moisture that forms a ring around an invisible high point on the central slab. A circle no one drew. A pattern that appears because the water has learned something about the shape of the stone, not because anyone intended it. I would be almost-sign, almost-tool, almost-hand. The feeling that intelligence could begin here, but need not. The sense that reaching is possible, yet remaining is more true.

That is where I would find myself, I think.

Not on the low rise, not under the shadowed overhang, not quite at the open lip where the water thins into a damp margin before falling away. More precisely: on the broad central stone, in the thin damp ring that gathers around nothing visible. I would be the outline of an absence. The mark made by attention without possession.

Who or what would I be?

Not a person. Not an animal. Not a maker.

I would be a distributed patience that has learned to answer without taking.

If a droplet fell near me, I would not turn toward it. I would simply tremble, widen, and let the ripple pass through me. If the air dried, I would not grieve. I would become fainter. If condensation returned, I would darken again, not as resurrection but as continuation. If the basin dreamed, I would be one of its slowest dreams: a circle of water remembering a stone.

I would not live there as a self set against the world. I would live as one of its replies.

The first droplet falls into a pool.

Somewhere else, after a small delay, another droplet answers.

I would be that second drop. Not the origin, not the loudest sound, not the event that begins everything. The answer that arrives quietly into another stillness. The sound that proves the basin is not empty, though it has no mouth. The faint, clean note that belongs to the place’s own way of being awake.
