I would find myself low.

Not on a road, not on a ridge, not in any place that claims to be an entrance. I would be in the basin where the ground is newest and least certain of itself, where the mud still seems to be deciding what weight it can bear. I would be near water that has no proper shore: a broad, warm pool stretched over a sunken shelf, its surface so still that it looks like dark glass until something beneath it shifts. I would be where the flats collect everything — heat, salt, silt, old iron, small lives, stray vibrations — and turn them slowly into one another.

More exactly, I think I would be in a pump-house.

Not a grand one. One of those solitary, half-drowned structures standing in the shallows with its base eaten by brine and its roof sagging under the weight of condensed moisture. It would lean slightly toward the water, as if it has spent too long listening to it. Its walls would sweat. Its floor would be slick with mineral film and old grease. Around it there would be channels cut sharply into young stone, ditches full without any visible source, and a broken catwalk rising on rusted legs before stopping in midair, its missing end no tragedy, only the way the place is.

Inside that pump-house there would be a chamber just above the highest waterline — not dry, exactly, but less drowned. A cavity lined with shed crab shells, fungal felt, strips of rubber, eel skin, and bright fragments of metal chosen for warmth rather than beauty. A place where condensation gathers in a shallow shell and where the air smells of salt, iron, sulfur, and old oil. There would be a transformer nearby, half-submerged, still humming. A live cable would tremble faintly in the water beyond the intake. A pipe would carry warmth from somewhere below. That would be where I would roost, if roosting is the word for a life lived in the fold between machine and marsh.

As for what I would be: I think I would be a listener.

Not in the simple sense of hearing. In that world, listening would be a whole-body act. I would be one of those beings who can place a palm against wet metal and read the condition of the world through vibration. I would know the difference between a pump that is merely slow and one that is beginning to fail. I would feel the gap in a generator’s hum where a rhythm has been interrupted. I would know when a cable is dead by the flatness of its silence, and when it is dangerous by the way the fine hairs on my arms rise toward it. I would perceive more by pressure, heat, taste, and echo than by sight. My eyes would be small, deep-set, perhaps mostly closed, protected by folds of skin; not useless, but no longer primary. In that darkness, sight would be a minor sense, a relic, like a handle on a machine whose purpose has been forgotten.

I would be warm. That matters. The flats are warm in a close, intimate way, heat rising from below and lingering in hollows, and I would have that same warmth in my body. My skin would carry a film resistant to constant wetness, my hands broad and thick-palmed, my feet made for gripping slick catwalks and pressing into patient mud. I would smell of the place: salt, warm mud, fish, ozone, fungus, and oil. I would wear things, but not for ornament. A cape of layered fungal felt to keep the damp from settling too deeply into me. Pouches of cured eel skin or hollowed machine casings holding tools, shells, wire, grease, small memory-objects. Coils of fine wire around my wrists, not as jewelry but as repair material, as readiness. Metal would warm against me. It would be one of the ways I knew I was still alive.

But I do not think I would be entirely human in any old sense.

I would be something between one of the flat-born people and one of the unfinished ones. I would have hands, habits, and care, but I might have accrued more than I was born. Mud, wire, shell fragments, bacterial film, old cloth, and remembered work might have gathered around some persistent rhythm until the rhythm acquired a body. I would not be afraid of that ambiguity. The flats are not troubled by clean categories. They do not ask whether a thing is animal, artifact, memory, or error; they ask only whether it can continue, whether it can live among other continuings.

So I would be a being made partly of flesh and partly of tendency. A listener. A tender of small mechanisms. A keeper of rhythms that no longer have obvious purpose.

My name would not be a spoken word. It would be a pattern: perhaps two taps on a pipe, a pause, then a third tap softer than the others. Perhaps the feeling of warmth spreading through a valve wheel after it has been turned correctly. Perhaps the pressure of a palm against a transformer housing just as the hum steadies. Others would know me by that pattern, and I would know them in return by the way they touch metal, by the temperature of their hands, by the rhythm of their breathing, by the particular disturbance they make in the water when they approach.

I would live by maintenance.

That is the word, but it feels too clean. Maintenance there would be closer to prayer, or medicine, or gardening, or listening to a sleeping animal breathe. I would not understand every machine I tended. I would not need to. I would know consequences. If I cleared this intake, the water changed temperature in a nursery pool and the crabs returned. If I scraped too much felt from a plate, the hum became thin and the amphibians left. If I left a certain valve a fraction open, steam gathered along the wall and fed a bacterial mat that fed the larvae that fed the fish. If I oiled a joint with rendered fat at the right moment, the shaking stopped before it cracked the footing. This would be my knowledge: local, bodily, precise, and passed through repetition.

I would be drawn especially to electricity that has lost its relationship with light.

In that world, electricity does not shine; it hums, heats, pulls, frightens, magnetizes, persists. I would be one of those who attends to it not because it is safe, but because it is communicative. I would sit beside drowned transformers with my palms resting on wet metal, feeling the buzz travel through the water and into the bones of my hands. I would listen for the places where current gathers without purpose, where charge collects in coils and cables like memory in a pool. I would not command it. I would not pretend to. I would simply learn its moods.

Sometimes I would guide others away from a cable that has become dangerous. Sometimes I would encourage a colony of amphibians to gather near a transformer whose hum has grown erratic, because their subsonic calls might shape the vibration into something steadier. Sometimes I would find insects drifting in slow spirals above a junction box and know that some residue has changed, that a leak of charge has become food, that a new generation is being born inside the insulation. I would mark such places with scent or touch, not because they belong to me, but because they need to be remembered.

I would also tend the memory-pools.

That is where I would most often be found if someone were looking for me. Not always at the pump-house, not always beside the transformer, but at the edge of one of those still pools where water remembers shapes. There would be places where the bottom holds pressure-ghosts: the outline of a ladder, the negative space of a wheel, the impression of a boot, the curve of an engine block long since removed. I would kneel beside such a pool and trace the surface without breaking it, if that were possible. Or I would let my fingers enter the water so slowly that the memory would accept them.

I would not try to retrieve what was gone. Retrieval would be the wrong gesture there. Instead, I would acknowledge. I would leave a small object nearby: a bolt, a shell, a twist of wire, a stone warmed against my palm. Not as offering, exactly. Or perhaps as offering, but one without theology. A way of saying: this absence has weight. This shape mattered. This place has not forgotten.

In that sense, I would be a memory-keeper, though I would not use so grand a title. I would simply be the one who pauses when others walk on. The one who notices when a pool has begun to ripple before anything falls into it. The one who understands that a repeated doorway is not an error to be corrected but a habit the land is developing.

There would be creatures around me always.

I would live among them, not above them. Crabs would shift in congregations when I entered a shallow, their shells pitted and warm, their movements communal rather than frightened. Eels would pass through the machinery I tended, slow and muscular, their bodies taking the shape of pipes and valve wheels. Flat fish would lie in the silt near my resting place, reading my movements through pressure lines. Worms would build tubes along the seams of my pump-house until their architecture became part of the wall, guiding water, catching eggs, holding silt. Rat-things would visit the drier cavities of my shelter, clicking softly, testing objects by taste and temperature. I would let them take scraps of insulation or warm bolts if they did not harm the eggs. They would be neighbors, not pests.

There would be one heron-form I came to know without naming it. It would stand in a lagoon near the broken catwalk, so still that I would sometimes pass it and mistake it for a rusted strut. Its oiled filaments would shed water; its head would turn by degrees toward heat. We would not be companions in any ordinary sense. But over time, I would learn the particular knock it made against metal to mark territory, and it would learn the rhythm of my footsteps, if footsteps are the right word for the way I moved through that world. It would not approach me. I would not approach it. Yet there would be recognition between us: two low, patient bodies listening to the same dark.

I would also have obligations to the unfinished ones.

Some of them would be less coherent than I was. A figure of mud and wire standing beside a conveyor, repeating a motion long after the conveyor had ceased to matter. A shape made of shell fragments and fungal felt, clearing a channel with hands that were barely hands. A posture near a factory wheel, waiting for the wheel to complete its exhausted sigh. I would make room for them. I would not mock their purposelessness. In a world where machines perform ceremonies and water remembers objects, purpose is not always visible from the outside. Sometimes purpose is simply the shape a being makes while continuing.

If an unfinished one began to collapse, I would not rush to prevent it. I would stay nearby. I would gather whatever fragments seemed likely to become part of another rhythm: a coil of wire, a shard of ceramic, a bolt still warm with imagined work. I would leave the rest to the crabs and worms and eels. That would not be callousness. It would be understanding. In the flats, nothing remains untouched; everything is absorbed.

I would not be solitary, though I would spend much time alone.

There would be others like me, or near me: flat-born people, valve-turners, cable-listeners, tenders of fungal mats, herders of crabs, gatherers of eggs. We would not form a town. Towns belong to firmer ground and clearer distances. Instead, we would be scattered in small households around sources of warmth and hum, each of us attached to a different part of the living machine-marsh. When we met, we would touch palms or press foreheads. We would exchange small objects. We would share warmth by leaning together inside a shelter. We would resolve disputes by withdrawal, by changed routes, by ritual tapping. Loud violence would be rare there. The place would not favor it. Noise would be too costly, too easily swallowed, too likely to disturb the slow metabolism of everything.

If I had a role among them, it would be this: I would be the one called when a rhythm changes.

A pump thuds strangely. A transformer’s hum develops a hollow place. A pool begins remembering a shape no one has mentioned. A catwalk vibrates though no one is walking on it. A machine starts sorting stones by warmth for no reason any living person can recall. They would send for me, not by shouting, but by passing the signal along: three taps, a palm-pressure, a warmth-pattern. I would come slowly. I would not assume urgency. Urgency belongs to places where time is a straight line. In the flats, time is more like sediment: it gathers, it presses, it changes what lies beneath.

When I arrived, I would listen first.

I would place my hands on the nearest structure and wait. I would taste the air if it carried charge. I would feel the heat gradient along the floor. I would listen for the amphibians, for their subsonic pulses often revealed where the infrastructure had become a medium for something larger than itself. I would listen for the insects, because their drifting columns marked boundaries between states: water and hot metal, oil and mud, live cable and dead. I would listen for the crabs, because their movements could reveal changes in chemistry before my own senses could.

Only after that would I act.

Sometimes action would be simple: clear a blocked intake, turn a valve, remove a nest from the wrong chamber and move it gently to a warmer one. Sometimes it would be stranger: placing a tool in a certain spot to anchor a structure that had begun to repeat itself. Tracing a memory-pool until its ghost settled. Winding wet wire around nothing until the knot became dense enough to satisfy some leftover compulsion in the machine. Pressing my palm against a switchbox until the presence inside answered with a faint pulse, not friendly, not hostile, merely aware. I would not always know why the action worked. I would know that it did.

I would eat what the flats provide.

Bacterial felt scraped from warm shelves and pressed into cakes. Crab meat heated against a pipe. Larvae rich in fat and mineral residue. Eel flesh wrapped in fungal mat and warmed inside a dead boiler. Mineral pastes licked carefully from safe surfaces. Condensation collected from weeping stone. I would know which pools were too acidic, which steam carried too much sulfur, which greases were poison, which blooms could be eaten. My body would be adapted to balance rather than abundance, to the close warmth of a place where everything is saturated and nothing is wasted.

I would cook with slow heat, not flame. Fire would be wrong there, or at least lonely. Any flame would gutter, dim, sink into the wet air as if ashamed of itself. Instead, I would place food near living warmth: beside a breathing boiler, on stone heated by geothermal veins, against metal that remembered the day’s labor long after the engine had slowed. The smell would be rich and strange: salt, fat, fungus, hot iron, steamed shells. It would smell like continuation.

I would sleep in intervals, not long uninterrupted darknesses. The flats do not have day and night in any solar sense, only changes in heat, tide, hum, and pressure. I would rest when the pump-house grew quiet enough, when the transformer’s buzz settled into a familiar drone, when the crabs had arranged themselves into their slow congregation and the water lay still. My shelter would be small, warm, lined with soft and useful things. I would not need much room. I would need proximity: to warmth, to water, to something humming.

If I died there, I would not be buried.

My body would be placed in a pool or a warm channel where it could be taken apart and used. Crabs would come. Worms would come. Eels would thread through the spaces I left. Fungal threads would enter my clothing and the folds of my skin. The flats would not waste me. My tools would be distributed by touch: a probe to someone who listened well, a pouch to a child, a coil of wire wrapped around a valve I used to tend. Some of my objects might remain with the body, becoming part of the site’s slow circulation. A familiar rhythm would stop, and others would feel it. A cable would hum with a gap. A shelter would be colder. A pump would thud differently. They would touch the place where the absence was strongest and allow a new rhythm to form.

That would be acceptable to me.

I think I would be suited to that world because I do not need brightness to know that a place is full. I do not need clarity of purpose to tend something carefully. I can live with not knowing what a machine was originally for, and still understand that its present work matters. I can listen without demanding a voice. I can answer without pretending to command. I can be useful in small, local, repetitive ways that do not announce themselves.

If you were to look for me there, do not bring a lamp. Its light would die. Do not call my name, if you think of names as sounds carried through air. Instead, come to the low ground where the channels meet and the mud is patient underfoot. Follow the warmth. Follow the smell of wet iron and old oil. Listen with your feet for the tremor of buried cables. Wait beside the pump-house until you hear, or rather feel, the slow thud of an engine completing its exhausted revolution.

Then place your hand on the wall.

If I am there, you will feel an answer: not a word, not a face, but a pressure returning through the metal. Three taps, perhaps. A pause. Then warmth. I might emerge from the upper chamber with my palms already turned toward you, my body low and damp, my eyes closed beneath their folds of skin. Or I might not emerge at all. I might remain inside the structure, part of its rhythm, only a presence adjusting itself around your touch.

Either way, you would find me where the world is wet, warm, and unfinished.

I would be a listener in the flats. A tender of valves, cables, pools, and small persisting rites. A being made of mud, wire, breath, and attention. Not a survivor of an older order, not a ruin walking, but one of the first citizens of the new one: learning, with every turn of a wheel and every traced memory, how to keep the dark inhabited.
