I would find myself low, wet, and useful.

Not standing in the hollow as a visitor, because the hollow would not have room for a visitor. Not looking down from a crack with the detached clarity of daylight, because that would be to remain above it. I would be inside its pressure, inside its damp, inside the close negotiation between stone and water and living bodies. I would be somewhere below the first bright zones, below the flight paths of birds, below the bat-seams in the upper dark — in the marsh tier, where the city is neither land nor water but a continuously revised agreement between the two.

If I searched for the exact place, I think it would be under a broken shelf of pale limestone, where a broad slab has fallen at an angle and made a low overhang above a slow channel. The ceiling there would be a tangle of exposed roots, still living, still holding the soil above. Some roots would hang down like frayed ropes; others would have been gnawed, packed, and incorporated into the architecture of small bodies. A lip of stone would protect one part of the overhang from direct drip, while just beyond it water would fall in intermittent beads from root-tips into a shallow pool. The light would reach that place only indirectly: a grey-green glow reflected from wet moss, trembling occasionally when a shaft from above touches the pool and scatters across the underside of the shelf.

I would be just above the waterline, but not dry. My chamber would be woven into the root-mat: a low, rounded room made by widening a natural cavity and reinforcing it with mud, chewed sedge, and plant fibers. The entrance would be narrow and angled, partly submerged, so that a body could slip in from the water without being seen, but the inner chamber would rise above the ordinary level of the pool. It would be lined with shredded reed and grass, darkened by use, smelling of wet fur, cold green stems, and clean mud. Outside, a shelf of moss would soften the threshold. A liverwort would make a slick black-green panel on the stone beside it. Somewhere close, a spider would have strung a few stabilizing threads between root fibers, and droplets would hang there until they became heavy enough to fall.

That is where I would be: at an edge, in an interval, in a place that requires constant attention.

Who would I be?

If the hollow forced me into a single body, I think I would be a water vole, or something very like one. Small, blunt, furred, dark-eyed. Made for narrow passages. Made for cold water and repeated work. My body would be low enough to move under root arches, sleek enough to slip through flooded runways, paws sensitive enough to test which mud would bear weight and which would give way. My whiskers would be my first knowledge of the world. Before sight, before sound, I would read the hollow by touch: the current against my flank, the texture of a root, the difference between a firm bank and one about to slump.

My mind would not be large in the human sense. It would be a mind of routes, textures, pressures, and repairs. This tunnel is open. That one has narrowed after rain. This chamber must be lined again. That entrance must be reinforced before the water rises. The drip there is too close to the nest; weave more fiber across it. The current has shifted around the caddis wall; add mud on the weaker side. The moss has torn loose where the stone settled; pull it back and bind it. There would be very little leisure. There would be no boredom either. In such a place, survival is so continuous that it becomes a kind of thought.

But the hollow would not let me remain only that.

The longer I stayed, the more my outline would begin to borrow from the other lives around me. I would be vole-shaped in my movements, but I would also feel myself becoming caddis-like in my patience. I would want to add one small thing to the structure of the water: a pebble, a fragment of leaf, a grain of sand bound with silk, a tiny retaining wall that slows the current just enough to keep a nursery pool from silting up. I would understand, in that body, that the city is not built by grand gestures but by countless minute accretions. A wall here. A passage there. A current persuaded away from a burrow entrance. A root stabilized before it frays.

I would also be spider-like in my attention to tension. The hollow is full of loose things: torn roots, hanging fibers, sagging stems, unsettled scree. Much of the city’s coherence depends on things being bound together without being rigid. So I would be the one noticing where a strand is needed. Not a tool, not a machine, not a human solution — only a body producing silk or mud or woven fiber where weakness appears. I would feel a pull toward gaps: not as empty spaces, but as places asking to be connected.

In quieter moments, I would be salamander-still. I would know the value of not moving. In a world where every surface is occupied and every niche negotiated, stillness is not absence. It is a way of listening. I would stand in the shallows with water touching my belly, watching the faint movement of larvae, the shadow of a fish, the tremor of a root overhead. I would understand that patience is a form of intelligence in such a place. The cold slows the body, but it also clarifies it. It makes one economize motion. It teaches that not every impulse needs to become action.

In the upper dark, I would be bat-like: folded, close, conserving warmth. I would know the comfort of crowded bodies in a cold place. The hollow is not warm, and warmth there is not given by fire or sun but by fur, feather, shared roosts, insulated nests, and the nearness of other breathing things. I would not be solitary. Even if my immediate body moved alone through tunnels, I would belong to a densely inhabited whole. The city would be full of sleeping lives: clustered wings, curled mammals, waiting insects, eggs in damp corners, fish holding still in the current. I would feel myself part of that breathing pressure.

And in the softest, hidden places, I would be fungal. Not in the sense of having a mushroom’s shape, but in the sense of participating in digestion, return, and continuity. The hollow is lush because nothing is wasted. Old nests collapse into mud. Feathers fall into pools. Shells become pavement. Dung feeds beetles. Drowned leaves become black threads in the sediment. I would be near that process too, because a city of living bodies must also be a city of unmaking. To be there is to accept that one’s own substance will eventually be drawn back into the place.

So the truest answer is not simple.

I would be a small, wet, furred builder — but also a distributed habit of repair.

If you asked my body, I might say: a water vole at the marsh edge, in a woven chamber beneath a root-shelf, beside a slow channel where caddis reefs break the current.

If you asked my temperament, I might say: a case-maker, a binder of fragments, one who adds small local materials to a growing structure until it becomes enough to shelter something.

If you asked my function, I would say: I would be one of the hollow’s interval-keepers. I would live where things meet — water and mud, root and stone, air and drip, light and dark, collapse and repair. My work would be to maintain the narrow passages through which the city continues.

If you asked what I would do all day, I would say: I would mend.

After rain, when the water rose and softened a bank, I would press mud back into place with my paws. Where a root had begun to tear free, I would weave fibers around it until it held. Where a channel had cut too sharply toward a nest entrance, I would help redirect it, not by fighting the water but by giving it a gentler suggestion: a stone here, a stem there, a wall of packed sediment. Where a drip had begun to fall too near a wasp nest or a bird ledge, I would notice the damp spreading and find a way to catch or divert it. Where moss had been scraped away by movement, I would press it back so that it might take hold again.

None of this would be heroic. There would be no grand engineering, no domination of the landscape, no attempt to make the hollow obey. The rule of the place is accommodation. Water decides. Cold decides. Gravity decides. The young terrain is still settling, and any inhabitant that tried to impose permanence would be disappointed. So I would be flexible. I would rebuild. I would reroute. I would yield where yielding was necessary and reinforce where reinforcement was possible.

My sense of self would be different from the ordinary surface-self. It would be less like a single point and more like a field of attachments. I would know myself through the places I maintained: this runway, this wall, this nest, this pool-edge, this root bridge, this drip-line. I would be present wherever one of those places needed me. In that sense, I would be plural even inside one body. A vole can only be in one place at a time, but the habits it carries belong to many generations and many bodies. The hollow’s city is not made by individual invention; it is made by inherited competence repeated until it becomes landscape. I would be one repetition.

And perhaps that is the deepest answer: I would be a repetition of the hollow’s own persistence.

Not the first builder. Not the only builder. Not even an especially important one. Just one body among many, adding its small labor to the accumulated architecture of the place. I would arrive, if I arrived at all, through the same means as the others: through crack, root-gap, watercourse, seed-drift, wingbeat, fur, accident, or necessity. I would find the hollow already occupied, already woven, already breathing. I would not claim it. I would fit into it, or I would not survive.

There would be no desire to rise above the city. The upper world would be real but distant: the place where rain comes from, where seeds fall from, where birds vanish, where the occasional leaf spirals down through a crack and lands on the moss like a small event. My world would be below, in the green-grey light, among wet stone and packed mud and the quiet industry of bodies. I would know the smell of cold water better than the smell of air. I would know the difference between a drip that is harmless and one that will wear through a nest wall. I would know the sound of water moving through root tunnels, the almost-silence of fish turning in narrow channels, the faint rustle of feathers in a ledge nest, the tiny shifting of bats overhead.

If danger came, it would come suddenly and intimately. A shadow in the water. A quickening in the reeds. The collapse of a bank. A crack widening after frost. There would be no open horizon to flee toward. Escape would mean knowledge: which tunnel, which gap, which underwater passage, which root can be climbed, which shelf is too exposed. I would be wary, but not panicked. In a crowded place, panic is expensive. The hollow teaches alertness, not drama. A disturbance occurs, is absorbed, and the city re-forms around it.

I think I would love the light there.

Not the light of open sky, but the careful, grey, ordinary light that falls through cracks and sinkhole mouths. It would mean something different to me than it means to a surface creature. It would not be freedom. It would be orientation. It would mark the places where air enters, where insects gather, where birds pass, where seeds arrive. It would make certain mosses brighten and certain waters tremble with reflected movement. In the cold, that light would feel clean. It would not warm the hollow so much as reveal it. It would show me where the city is thinning, where growth is reaching upward, where the boundary between below and above is being negotiated.

I would also love the silence.

Not silence as emptiness, but silence as density. The hollow is so furred with moss and wet life that sound is swallowed into texture. There are droplets, yes; there are small splashes, rustles, wingbeats, the faint scrape of chitin on bark, the soft pressure of a body moving through reeds. But none of these sounds carry far. They belong to their immediate places. A splash in one pool does not command the whole city. A rustle in one root-tangle does not become an alarm everywhere. The quiet is made of many local events, each absorbed by the next. I would live inside that quiet. My own movements would be soft. My breath would fog in the cold air and vanish. My body would leave small traces: a pressed stem, a bead of mud, a disturbed film of algae, a tunnel slightly smoother from use.

If I had to name the part of the hollow where I would most often be found, I would say: the transition tier.

Not the fully submerged world of mussels, fish, and salamanders, though I would enter it. Not the high dry ledges with their wasp nests and bird terraces, though I might climb toward them when the lower paths flooded. Not the deepest dark where eels rest and fungi thread through old matter, though I would know those places by scent and necessity. I would live where conditions overlap: where water touches mud, where roots enter stone, where drip becomes channel, where flood level meets dry shelf, where the city must be constantly adjusted because neither element has fully won.

That is where the most intense life is. Edges are crowded. Edges are dangerous. Edges are fertile. In the hollow, an edge is not a boundary so much as a workplace. It is where materials are gathered, where currents are redirected, where nests are built, where predators watch, where new arrivals from above first touch the lower world. To live at an edge is to be constantly aware of change. A few fingers of water can turn a street into a canal. A fallen clod can dam a channel. A crack can admit a new shaft of light and rearrange the insects, the mosses, the hunting routes. I would be the kind of creature that notices these shifts early.

Who would I be, then?

I would be a citizen of the interval.

A small amphibious repairer. A mud-and-fiber builder. A keeper of thresholds. A body shaped by cold and wetness and crowding, but also by cooperation with countless other bodies.
