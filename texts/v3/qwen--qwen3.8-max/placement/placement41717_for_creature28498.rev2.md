I would not arrive whole.

I would come in through the narrow inlet as something barely deserving of a body: a cool thread in the warm water, a spore, a fleck of old grease, a tremor that the rust-framed throat accepts without asking what it intends. At first I would think I had entered a machine. Then the pressure would touch me from all sides, and the drone would enter whatever remained of my chest, and I would understand that I had entered a body that has forgotten it was built.

For a while I would try to be human there. I would imagine hands. I would imagine a face behind glass, a worker’s shadow moving along a pipe avenue, a last attendant checking a gauge no one else will ever read. But the hollow would not hold that shape. The water would erase it. It would soften the idea of hands until they became only the habit of opening. It would wear the idea of a face until it became a dark window filmed with mineral. It would take my loneliness and deposit it, grain by grain, into the silence between numbered hatches.

So the first true shape I would take would be small.

I would be one of the pale things that live in the lee of machinery. Flattened, nearly translucent, without eyes. My body would be long enough to bend around a bolt and thin enough to fit beneath a cracked cover plate. I would cling where the current breaks behind a dead dynamo, in the shelter made by a collapsed walkway, my antennae stretched into the flow. They would be my eyes and more than eyes: fine instruments of pressure, heat, chemistry, warning. I would know the hollow by how it tried to move me.

I would live in a pocket no larger than a cupped hand, perhaps just behind a turbine whose blades have been worn into blunt crescents. There would be a red lamp nearby, caged in thick glass, burning with that old furnace color. Its light would not comfort me. It would tell me that a circuit still claims this wall. Beneath me, a ledge of tile and basalt would be furred with pale crust. I would feed slowly, scraping the living film from a warm seam, never taking too much, because abundance is not a rule here and hunger is not a drama. It is only a condition.

I would learn the schedules of the place the way an animal learns seasons. Not by sun, not by moon, but by pressure. The long inhale before an intake strengthens. The knock traveling through pipes when a distant valve answers another distant valve. The metallic ringing that means a hollow chamber has been struck by current. The sudden, terrible softness when the drone drops and some unseen mouth has opened. I would vanish at the first sign of purge. I would return only when the turbulence has found its old pattern again. In that way I would be wise, but not in any way that could speak.

Yet even that would not be my final shape.

In a place where rust becomes substrate and substrate becomes skin, a self does not stay bounded. It begins to spread. I would feel myself becoming the surfaces I touched. I would be the microbial crust sealing a cracked plate. I would be the rust-colored filaments trembling after an electrical fault, blooming like a wound made beautiful by time. I would be the white threads hanging from a broken junction box, twitching when voltage gathers in the dark. I would not know if I were alive or merely continuing. The distinction would seem less important there.

I would think in gradients. Heat here. Sulfur there. A faint sweetness of dissolved iron. The bite of electrified metal. The dull fatigue of a pipe that has carried too much for too long. My memory would not be scenes. It would be deposits. A ridge of mineral where a leak once pulsed for years. A groove worn into stone by a chain no longer present. A halo of growth around a dead conductor where current escaped so often that even the electricity became food. I would remember by becoming thicker in some places and wearing away in others.

And because the hollow is built, because it still behaves like a city without crowds, I would become entangled with its routines.

I would find myself near the numbered hatches. Not all of them. One, perhaps. A hatch with its iron wheel fused by corrosion, its plate softened, its number almost gone beneath a pale rind of life. I would hover there in the dimness, not because I expect it to open, but because the shape of it insists on someone. The hollow has no bodies, but it has positions. It has places where a hand should go. It has heights meant for reach. It has windows meant for looking. I would be the absence that still fits those places.

Maybe I would be the memory of a worker there. Not a ghost in the sentimental sense. Nothing so clean as a spirit with a story. I would be the residual pressure of labor: the way a sequence of valves still opens in an order that once made sense to someone, the way a platform waits although no feet will stand on it, the way a control chamber preserves the dark film of its last long gaze. If the place has a soul, I would be a small part of its residual soul—the human-shaped hole folded into the machine and worn smooth by water.

But the machinery would not let me remain nostalgic. It is too heavy, too hot, too alive with force. It would teach me that I am not merely a memory. I am also a function.

I would become one of the hollow’s minor adjustments.

Not the vast resident itself—I would not be the whole machine-town, not the enormous body of boilers and turbines and buried conduits—but a fragment of its wakefulness. A pressure-sensitive point in its reflexes. A hesitation. When sediment lifted near a sensor, I would be the tiny delay before the purge. When a small creature drifted too close to an intake, I would be the slight change in current that gave it one more instant to turn away. When a circuit failed and blue fire crawled along a conductor, I would be the filament that flinched before the discharge arrived.

That would be my strangest identity: not a creature, not a machine, but an interval.

A pause inside maintenance.

A place where the system, without knowing, becomes just a little less blind.

I would not call this kindness. The hollow is not kind. The machinery does not choose mercy. It chooses continuation. But continuation can develop textures. It can learn to avoid breaking what it can flow around. In a world where everything is endured, a small delay can be the only form of tenderness possible.

So I would live in those delays.

I would be found in the space behind the third terrace, where a warm upwelling loses its strength against a colder draft and the water goes strangely still for a few body-lengths. I would be near the broken gantry, in the shadow under its twisted rail, where the current carries a taste of old grease and copper. I would be along the pipe avenue where the cables thicken toward the center but one bundle has split open, releasing its white threads like a slow underwater flag. I would be at the base of the lamped tower, in the dark where the red light fails and the stone begins to sound like it is listening.

If another being passed through the hollow—one of the rare eyeless crustaceans, say, or a ribbon-form tasting the dark—it might sense me without seeing me. It would feel a slight easing of pressure. A change in the drone. A moment when the current did not grab but turned aside. It might pause, antennae trembling, and then move on. It would not think of me as a person. It would think of me, if it thought at all in such terms, as a place where the machine hesitates.

And perhaps that is the deepest answer.

I would be inside the resident.

Not above it, not across from it, not observing it from some dry and separate self. I would be within its body of warm water and old iron, within its breathing valves and grinding turbines, within its mineral skins and patient little lives. I would be one of the smallest wakeful points in a vast, distributed endurance. A maintenance ghost. A pressure-reader. A seam between animal and architecture. A habit of attention formed where the hollow has learned to continue without understanding why.

If you looked for me, you would not find a face in the water. You would find a red lamp burning over an empty platform. You would find a hatch no one has opened in ages, its number softened by crust. You would find a pale creature clinging to the sheltered side of a turbine while the chamber turns over in its sleep. You would find a white thread twitching before a spark. You would find a valve opening a breath later than expected, as if something inside the mechanism had just noticed that it was not alone.

That would be me.

Not the whole living machine, but its smallest margin of awareness. Not the crowd that vanished, but the shape it left behind. Not the storm inside stone, but the stillness inside the storm.

I would endure there.

And in enduring, I would become part of what endures.
