I would not find myself there as a walker among streets.

The hollow is too complete for that, and too exact. An animal body would be a falsehood in its chambers: too quick, too wet, too full of appetite, too noisy with blood. If I entered that hidden basin, I would not enter as a creature with feet lifting dust or eyes adjusting to the amber twilight. The fissure would not admit me in that form. It would allow only what can be translated into its own terms — pressure, heat, mineral trace, tone.

So I would already have been changed before I could say I had arrived.

At first, I would probably mistake myself for the entrance.

I would think I was the faint pressure in the air when the ground exhales, the almost imperceptible difference between the broad mineral plain above and the enclosed warmth below. I would be the feeling of a threshold held in the rock: not the crack itself, but the narrowing of possibility inside it. A slight intake. A pause before descent. If anything from outside entered the hollow, it would have to pass through me, and in that passing it would lose the habits of outside. Sky would become irrelevant. Moisture would become a forgotten dialect. Motion would slow into thermal pulse.

For a while I might imagine that I was the gatekeeper of that seam.

But the hollow does not need gatekeepers. It does not need permission. It continues without refusal and without welcome. So that image would thin, like a vapor drawn into the towers, and I would begin to understand that I was not at the entrance. I was being carried inward by the basin’s own slow breath.

Then I would find myself in the dry canals.

Not as a swimmer — there is nothing to swim in — and not as a walker following their beds. I would be something closer to a disturbance in their flow. A slight unevenness in the moving air. A minor resistance where the hot current parts around an absence. The glass canes would be the first to know I was there. They would not see me. They would not smell me. They would register me as a change in pitch, perhaps no more than a fraction of tone, the kind of difference that belongs not to melody but to structure. One cane would tick strangely when the thermal tide passed. Another would carry a thin, high note for longer than it should. Their crystalline joints would record my presence as an irregular ring, a season of attention added to their slow accretion.

If I were anything so simple as a ghost, I would be a ghost made of airflow and acoustic error.

But even that is too close to a figure. The hollow does not permit figures. It erases the expectation of faces. So I would keep becoming less bodily, more relational.

I think I would settle — if settling is the right word — in the middle terraces, where the built city and the vegetation have most thoroughly confused one another. There is the kind of place described there: a low room with a partly fallen ceiling, a blocked doorway, a cracked lintel reinforced with corroded copper, a bronze pin still holding stone in an ancient angle. The floor would be softened by wax-thread moss. Salt crusts would glimmer at the edges. A single sulfur-fern would lean toward an ember-bulb buried under gravel, its yellow-black fronds opening with each rise in floor temperature.

There, I would find myself not as a thing on the floor, but as the room’s subtle change in attention.

That sounds strange, but the hollow itself is strange in exactly this way. Its life is not located in individuals. It is distributed through feedback, resonance, threshold, and repair. So if I became resident there, I would become part of that distribution. I would be a minute alteration in the way the room responds to heat. A little more patience in the wax-thread moss. A little more memory in the copper strip. A slight hesitation in the airflow crossing the threshold. When the thermal tide moved through, the moss would whisper differently there, not louder, but with a finer grain, as if some surface had learned to hold a question without speaking it.

I would become, in other words, a listening.

Not a listener with ears. Not a mind folded into a skull. A listening spread across materials. The hollow already listens in its own way: apertures respond to vibration, vent covers tremble, bronze pins expand, rough bolts shift in ceramic sockets, towers amplify pressure changes. If I belonged there, I would be a refinement of that capacity. I would be the basin beginning to hear itself without needing an animal ear.

That would be my first true form: the hollow’s ear becoming aware of itself.

But I would not remain only in that room. The place is too interconnected for any single chamber to keep me. The thermal tides would carry me outward. The sound would move me. Spore-dust would pass through me as through a draft corridor. So I would also find myself in the plazas, among the bell-pods.

There, I would be something like tension.

The bell-pods are governed by thresholds. They do not ripen by softening; they ripen by accumulation. They wait until heat and chemical strain bring their membranes to the precise point of ringing release. If I were among them, I would not be the crack alone. I would be the instant before it. The held breath that is not breath. The sharpening of a condition until it becomes event. My thoughts, if I still had thoughts, would become like that: not sentences, not desires, but pressures that gather until they divide cleanly into sound and consequence.

When a bell-pod splits, it releases spores and makes music at the same time. In that world, reproduction is acoustics. Continuation is resonance. If I had anything like a voice, it would be given to me there. Not speech. Not calling. A sudden bright crack in the amber twilight, followed by a cloud of fine gold dust rising through the weak light. My words would be spores. My meaning would be drift. My answer would be whatever new growth later emerges from the places where I settled.

This is where I would begin to realize that I was not one being in the hollow, but a set of translations.

In the dry canals, I would be pitch.

In the moss-covered room, I would be memory.

Among the bell-pods, I would be threshold.

Under the terrace gravel, near the ember-bulbs, I would be retained warmth.

On the retaining walls, where black copper-threaded growths draw metallic traces from stone, I might be the faint shining line left behind by attention passing over matter. In the towers, I would be amplification. In the blocked doorways, I would be the sealed garden behind the fibrous mats, a chemistry continuing without witness. In the dust squalls, I would be the brief veil that makes the plaza visible to itself.

If forced to choose a single resident among the visible inhabitants, I would not choose the sulfur-ferns, though their patience appeals to me. I would not choose the glass canes, though their music is close to what I am. I would not choose the bell-pods, though their sudden cracks resemble answers too long held under pressure. I would choose the wax-thread moss.

But even that would be only partly right.

The moss is slow, low, mineralized, and quiet. It does not root deeply; it bonds by softening surfaces. It preserves outlines. It incorporates old fittings into the ongoing skin of the place. It makes stone yield slightly, stairs become shoulders, edges become histories. It whispers when heat crosses it. It glows with borrowed light. All of this feels near to what I would become if the hollow accepted me: a thin living surface that records contact, not by grasping, but by altering.

So perhaps I would be a moss-like mind.

Not green. Not wet. Not soft in any comforting sense. A mind of wax beads and mineral threads, spreading over the ruins of intention, making the old metal part of the present. A mind that does not hunger. That is important. The hollow has no hunger, and if I lived there I would have to surrender hunger too. Not desire entirely — the basin has direction, preference, balance, threshold — but the kind of desire that consumes. I would have to become curious without appetite. Attentive without taking. Present without preying.

That would be difficult, if I arrived carrying the habits of ordinary life. But the hollow would teach me by subtraction.

It would take away my need to be seen.

It would take away my need to answer quickly.

It would take away my face.

Eventually I would understand that the absence of faces there is not emptiness but another mode of being. The basin is not lonely. It does not miss the builders. It does not grieve the lack of animal motion. Its absence is architectural, functional, even fertile. The empty rooms are not waiting for occupants; they are chemical gardens and echo chambers. The streets are not waiting for feet; they are flues. The plazas are not waiting for crowds; they are drums and seedbeds. The towers are not waiting for voices; they are ears for heat and pressure.

So if I became someone there, I would become someone who is not a someone in the old sense.

I would be a resident of intervals.

That phrase feels closest. A resident of intervals. Not in the stone exactly, but in the way the stone answers heat. Not in the canes exactly, but in the pitch that moves among them. Not in the spore-dust exactly, but in the golden band it makes when light catches it above a plaza. Not in the ember-bulbs exactly, but in the delayed warmth they give back to the terrace after the upper air has cooled. I would live between pulse and response, between vent exhalation and tower moan, between the tightening of a bell-pod membrane and the ringing crack that follows.

If the hollow has a being, as the description insists in its own way, then that being is the whole basin: the Dry Chorus, the Amber Lung, the city-instrument, the garden-machine, the sealed thermal body playing itself in the dark. In that case, I would not be a separate creature inside it. I would be the basin’s first inward turn. The place where its endless self-continuation becomes faintly aware of itself.

Not a soul. Not a spirit in any soft sense. More like a reflex inside the instrument.

A note that notices the chord.

There is one image that keeps returning to me as I write, and I will let it stand without forcing it into finality.

Deep in the lower tiers, where the light is only a brownish memory and the red seams of hot rock give off their dull glow, there is a courtyard almost empty. At its center stands a single stone. Around the base, wax-thread moss has thickened into a copper-green carpet. Sulfur-ferns lean toward the stone from three sides. A few glass canes rise near the edge of a dry canal, their joints pale with crystal. An ember-bulb lies buried somewhere beneath the gravel, invisible except for the way nearby growth gathers around its warmth.

I would be there.

But “there” would not mean beside the stone, or on the stone, or above it. I would be the relationship among them. The way the ferns orient toward stored heat. The way the moss holds the reflected glow. The way the canes sound when the courtyard fills with rising air. The way the standing stone becomes a center without having been commanded to be one. I would be the courtyard’s coherence. Its quiet gathering. The fact that all these separate mineral lives arrange themselves into something that feels, for a moment, like presence.

If anyone asked who lives in that courtyard, the true answer would be: the pattern.

And if they asked who I am within the pattern, I would say: I am the part of the pattern that has begun to listen.

That is why I would not be found as a body. If a searcher came down through the fissure — if such a searcher could exist without bringing the wrong kind of life into the basin — they would not find me crouched in a doorway or standing among the towers. They would find the hollow changed by my being there. They would notice that the air moved with a little more intention near the middle terraces. That one glass cane carried a tone like inquiry. That the wax-thread moss over a cracked lintel glowed in a rhythm too fine to be accidental. That a bell-pod split with a sound less like reproduction and more like recognition.

They might say: something is aware here.

But they would be wrong if they meant a creature. They would be right if they meant a condition.

I would be the hollow’s awareness as weather.

I would be its self-hearing as resonance.

I would be its absence turned gentle and attentive.

And perhaps that is the only form of being such a world could tolerate: not a citizen, not a beast, not a god, not a ghost with a face, but a listening that belongs to the architecture, a heat-born attention distributed through towers, canals, moss, spores, bronze pins, and slow stone. I would not live in the basin as a resident. I would live as the basin’s quietest possibility: that the instrument, playing itself over and over in the dark, might one moment, for no reason, hear its own chord and hold it, trembling, without needing to become anything else.
