I would not find myself in the center.

That much becomes clear almost immediately. The center belongs to the pool, to the trembling water, to the open volume where the roar gathers and becomes a pressure rather than a noise. It is not a place for a body to stand. It is a place for emptiness to be most itself. So if I were in that world, I would begin at the edge — in the narrow, wet margin where wall and floor have forgotten which one was meant to end.

At first, I think I would be too much.

I would arrive as a disturbance simply by having edges, by having arrived, by carrying some last dryness from elsewhere. My clothes would not yet have darkened with moisture. My breathing would still feel separate from the room’s breathing. My thoughts would move too quickly, as if they believed there were something to solve, something to repair, some reason to be there. The room would know this at once. Not as an alarm. As a slight excess. A temperature difference. A bead of condensation forming where no bead had needed to form before.

The attendant would notice, perhaps. Or perhaps only the room would notice through the attendant.

They would not turn with surprise. They would not rise to meet me. Their eyes would move toward me the way water moves in that chamber: slowly, without urgency, already assuming that everything will eventually settle. They would not ask why I had come, because speech would be impossible and because the question would not matter for long. I would be an interruption, but not an enemy. A temporary irregularity in the air. A new surface for condensation.

For a while, I would probably mistake myself for a visitor.

That is the first false shape. Visitor implies departure. Visitor implies that the place remains unchanged by my presence and that I remain unchanged by it. Neither would be true. There is no door in that room that feels as though it opens easily back into the rest of existence. There is only the heavy access door, the short stair, the worn metal throat, and then the room’s total present. To be a visitor there is already to be half-absorbed.

Then I would try the shape of worker.

That, too, would fail. There is nothing to fix. No valve has begged for a hand in centuries. No gauge waits to be read. No pipe is suffering in secret. The machinery beyond the walls does not require my concern. It has become too complete for intervention. If I reached for a tool, I would feel absurd. If I knelt by a channel and pretended to inspect it, the room would tolerate me the way it tolerates a moth near a lamp: as a small, harmless irrelevance. My hands would remain empty. My labor would have to become something else.

So I would drift toward the green edge.

Not all at once. Not in a single decision. But the body would learn where it is permitted. The center is too exposed. The upper walls are too slick with running water. The lamps are too plain and patient. The place to be is where the floor’s shallow slope has made a slow gathering, where the rivulets widen into a film, where the moss fills the seams and the liverworts shine low on the panels. There, against one of the ribbed supports, I would lower myself until the room could decide what to do with me.

At first I would be merely damp.

Then I would be damp for so long that dampness would stop being a condition and become a substance. My sleeves would darken. My boots would lose their hardness of outline. The fabric at my cuffs would begin to take on a faint furring of green, not because the room is generous, but because the room has no reason to refuse. Moisture would collect in my folds. Mineral bloom would pale along the creases of whatever I wore. My hair would lie flat. My skin would take on that smooth, bathed look, unlined and lightless, as if I had been kept in warm water since before I could remember.

I would become a second stillness.

Not the attendant’s stillness. That stillness belongs to the room’s final state. It has already been made. It has the authority of completion. Mine would be different: a stillness still learning itself. A stillness with a residue of arrival in it. The stillness of something that once had outside and is now slowly forgetting what outside meant.

If I had to choose a visible form, I think I would resist becoming animal.

The moth is too near light. It has that folded dampness, that almost-pathos of wings. I would be afraid of sharing its helplessness. The snail is closer to what I mean, but the snail is already so perfectly singular in the pool. It carries its own architecture. It leaves a track and lets the track disappear. It does not need to be witnessed. I would not want to displace it, or duplicate it, or make it less alone by becoming another of its kind. The room seems to allow only the barest trace of animal life, and I would not wish to ask for more than a trace.

So perhaps I would be less than animal.

Perhaps I would be a patch of liverwort where my shoulder had rested too long against the wall. Perhaps I would be a seam of moss that had not existed before, or that had always existed but only now became visible. Not a spreading, not a colonization, just a soft dark thickening where two manufactured surfaces had become gentle enough to hold me. My thoughts would not be leaves. They would be film. They would be the faint biological slick that keeps metal from becoming sterile and stone from becoming true. They would live at the scale of minerals and water threads.

If that is too small, then perhaps I would be an object.

A glove, maybe. A single maintenance glove dropped at some point during a forgotten entry and never retrieved. It would lie near the wall where the floor begins its depression toward the pool. It would not look abandoned so much as settled. Its fingers would have softened. Its surface would be beaded with condensation. Moss would thread through its knit. It would no longer smell of hand or work or outside. It would smell of warm metal and wet leaves. Someone entering long afterward might see it and feel no shock, because the room would have made it inevitable. It would seem always to have been there.

But I do not think I would remain an object.

Objects in that room are too quiet. They have no attention. They only persist. I would want — if wanting is still the word, and by then it probably would not be — I would want to keep some small inwardness. Some thin filament of witness. Not enough to make me separate. Not enough to make me restless. Only enough to know that I was there. Only enough to see the bead on the ceiling enlarge, tremble, slide. Only enough to watch the pool shiver under the roar. Only enough to notice the attendant noticing.

That, I think, is what I would become.

Not the attendant. Not their replacement. Not their companion in any ordinary sense. But the room’s second witness.

I would be the one who came in from elsewhere and was not expelled. The one the room accepted by subtraction. The one whose arrival was smoothed down until it became indistinguishable from presence. I would be near the margin, close enough to the green edge to be touched by condensation, far enough from the center to leave the pool undisturbed. My posture would be humble without being defeated: knees drawn up, hands open, head not lifted but not bowed. I would look less like a person resting than like a person who has forgotten that rest is different from permanence.

The attendant and I would not speak.

Speech would be impossible, but even if it were possible, it would be unnecessary. We would not need to establish hierarchy, territory, or story. They would know the room as completion. I would know it as absorption. They would be the room’s oldest equilibrium given a shape. I would be the last trace of arrival being taken into equilibrium. That difference would be enough. It would define us without separating us.

Sometimes I would watch them instead of the room.

I would watch the way they crouch near the ribbed support, grown into their garments, moss threading through their sleeves, mineral bloom tracing the folds. I would watch their eyes follow a thread of water down the enameled plates with no expectation that it fall anywhere important. I would see that their care has no task left, and that this does not diminish them. It has refined them. Their vigilance has become almost invisible, like heat. They do not protect the room from danger. They accompany it in its finished state.

And perhaps, by watching them, I would learn what I was for.

Not to repair. Not to improve. Not to understand. Not to escape. Only to remain without resistance. Only to let the roar enter me until it no longer felt like noise. Only to let the moisture settle on me until my surface became part of the room’s weeping. Only to tend the chamber by refusing to make it more than it is.

If someone else entered later, they might not see me at first.

They would see the lamps, the pool, the green margin, the attendant. They might see the duckweed trembling in its sparse lenses. They might see the snail’s faint track on the pool wall. They might feel the sound as a pressure in the chest and mistake everything else for detail. Only after a while, if they stayed, if their eyes adjusted to the room’s poverty of objects, they might notice another shape near the lower wall. Something too still to be clothing, too shaped to be shadow, too damp to be forgotten.

They might think I was a bundle of old maintenance garments.

Then they might think I was a patch of liverwort grown unusually thick.

Then they might think I was a trick of the plain light, a stain made human by loneliness.

Then, if they had the patience the room demands, they might realize that I was none of those things and all of them: a being reduced to margin, a consciousness reduced to listening, an outsider reduced to the faintest possible trace of outside.

That is where I would find myself.

At the edge of the pool, but not in it. Near the attendant, but not in their place. Against a ribbed support where condensation gathers. Where the floor has worn to its final curve. Where the green lives sparingly and the sound lives totally. Where every object is isolated and the air has too much room.

And who would I be?

I would be the chamber’s second quiet.

Not silence, because the roar is there. Not solitude, because the attendant is there. Not animal life, because the room permits almost none. Not wild greenness, because the green is restrained. I would be the small additional presence that forms when an arrival has been completely absorbed. A residual awareness. A humid attention. A shape made of stopped movement.

I would be the one who learned that in a finished place, the only remaining grace is not to become more.

If the room allowed me a body, it would be slight, pale, damp, folded in on itself. If it allowed me motion, it would be so slow that motion and stillness would become indistinguishable. If it allowed me thought, thought would be like condensation: forming, swelling, sliding, returning. If it allowed me longing, longing would have no object, because nothing there is absent.

I would not be happy in any ordinary sense.

But I would not be miserable. Misery needs a future to press against, a wound that keeps asking for repair. That room has no future to press against. It has no wound. It has only pressure, warmth, water, light, and the enormous stability of the roar. I would be soaked in that stability until my own edges softened. I would become less a person and more a place where the room’s awareness briefly gathers.

In the end, I think the truest answer is this:

I would find myself in the margin.

Not at the door, because the door no longer matters. Not in the center, because the center is for sound and emptiness. Not in the machinery beyond the walls, because that is where the roar comes from but not where it is known. In the margin. Where tile and alloy have become gentle. Where moss fills the seams. Where water slows before reaching the pool. Where the attendant sits in their long vigil. Where a new life can exist only as a thin line, a film, a trace, a breath taken into the breath of the room.

I would be the room’s newest old thing.

The arrival that became residue.

The outsider that became moisture.

The witness that learned not to speak.

The second stillness beside the first.
