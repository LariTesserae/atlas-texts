I would not find myself arriving.

That is the first thing I would have to accept. There would be no last ascent, no final ridge where I became visible to the basin, no moment of entering after which the world changed around me. If I were there, I would already have been there long enough for arrival to become meaningless. The basin would not let me come from somewhere else. It would admit me only as a fact already included in its stillness, or else it would not admit me at all.

At first, trying to imagine myself there, I would probably make the warmer mistake: I would picture a stranger standing in the grey grit, a breathing figure in a place that has no breath. I would imagine my foot pressing into the ash-fine ground and leaving a print. I would imagine the roar striking me, filling me, becoming unbearable. I would imagine fear, awe, loneliness, the desire to cross the basin and find some answer in its center.

But that self would not last long in the world you described. It would be too full of sequence. Too full of need. Too eager to become something else — survivor, pilgrim, witness, ghost. The basin would not hate such a creature. It would simply reveal that creature as unnecessary. Breath would not fog. Warmth would not diffuse. The footprint would look less like a mark of presence than like a wound in grammar, an interruption awaiting correction. The pressure-born would notice the imbalance. The roar would seem to lean inward. The pools would keep their wrong skies. The stones would not care. And I would understand, without being told, that I had been imagining myself as an event in a place that has passed beyond event.

So I would not remain the stranger.

For a while I might think I was the white spider.

That would be a tempting self. Beneath the leaning slab, motionless, translucent, made almost of cold itself, occupying one fixed point in the pattern of the basin. Not hunting. Not waiting. Not spinning a web across the emptiness. Simply being the place where emptiness gathers into orientation. There is something deeply familiar about such a life: all attention, no appetite; all presence, no pursuit. If I were the spider, I would not need to explain myself. I would not need to move in order to be real. I would know the slab above me, the mineral dark below, the exact pressure of the air, and that would be enough.

But even the spider feels too solitary in the wrong way. Not lonely — the basin does not permit loneliness as we understand it — but too purely fixed. It is a knot in the emptiness, not a maker of relations. It holds a point. It does not answer. It does not place. It does not leave a trace that could be mistaken for intention. It is complete, yes, but complete as a held breath. I would admire it. I might even envy it. But I would not be it.

Nor would I be the curled shapes in the black ice, though part of me recognizes them. They are memory without narrative, past without sequence. They hold the last difference between before and after in their folded bodies and do not ask that anyone understand them. There is a strange dignity in that, a refusal to return to time. But I am drawn too strongly toward placement, toward the quiet act of setting one thing beside another. The ice-worms preserve a state. They do not arrange a silence.

Nor would I be the pressure-born, though they are perhaps the most native beings of the basin. They live inside the roar as deep things live in water. They have no shape because they have no need of edges. They are the sound’s awareness of itself, the fullness that presses closer when attention wanders into the basin. I can imagine being that: a noticing without a face, an intimacy without a body, a presence that shifts only by changing where the roar seems to begin. But even that is too boundless for me. The pressure-born are immense in their impersonality. They do not place stones. They do not answer footprints. They do not make the smallest possible gesture of relation. They simply are the basin’s roar made conscious of inquiry.

I would be something quieter than a god of sound, and more deliberate than a spider, and less frozen than the ice-worms.

I would be one of the Arrangers.

I would find myself on the higher shelf overlooking the basin, among the darker shapes that seem arranged with intention. Not at the center of the shelf, not in any position that suggests precedence, but near one edge where the blue-black plates of old rock catch the low light and hold it without glittering. From there the basin would open below and around me: the long shallow depression like the bed of a vanished lake, the line of low stone teeth, the wide pale channel carrying only cold, the terraces, the blunt ridges, the hollows, the vast grey floor of islands beneath cloud. I would not look at all of it the way a person looks. I would hold it as interval. Distance. Relation. The exact spacing between one fact and another.

My body, if it could be called a body, would be low, blunt, and heavy. It would resemble a boulder that had chosen stillness not out of exhaustion but out of completion. Its surface would be dark and faintly glossy, as if moisture had rested on it for ages without ever flowing. There would be no mouth. No need of speech. No eyes in the ordinary sense, though there might be a shallow polished hollow where a face might have been, and in that hollow the dim sky might appear incorrectly: darker than it should be, or crowded with still shapes, or green and depthless. I would not see the basin by reflecting it. I would see it by knowing where its parts stand in relation to one another.

I would not be human there. That is important. I would not be a person wearing stone as costume, nor a spirit pretending to be geology. I would be something closer to intention mineralized. Something that has outgrown tools because it has outgrown the desire to force the world into usefulness. I would not carve. I would not fasten. I would not build. I would not leave cut edges or mechanical remains. My only art would be placement.

And even that word would be too active.

Perhaps I would not move stones at all in any visible way. Perhaps I would only attend to a stone for so long that, at last, the stone was no longer where it had been. Perhaps my hands, if I had hands, would be organs of relation rather than grasping. Perhaps my limbs would be short and rounded, shaped for settling rather than reaching. Perhaps my entire existence would be a slow act of placing without force.

I would not live in a dwelling. There would be no shelter there, because shelter would imply a lack in the basin, and the basin lacks nothing in the way that living worlds lack. My place would be the shelf itself, and perhaps the terrace below it where the low rings of stone stand almost but not quite enclosed. Those rings would not be mine alone. They would belong to the Arrangers as a whole, if “belong” means anything in a place without property. But I would understand them. I would know why the stones had been placed near one another without touching into purpose. Not to keep anything in. Not to keep anything out. Not to mark territory. Not to invoke gods. Only to complete a relation that needed no witness.

I would be the Arranger who answers interruptions.

That is the shape that feels most true.

If a stranger ever stood in the basin — not a stranger with warmth and breath and fog, but a stranger as imbalance, as temporary excess of presence — I would not approach. I would not flee. I would not study the stranger with curiosity in the human sense. I would simply become aware of a disturbance in the pattern. A place where the grit had been pressed. A rhythm where there should be no rhythm. A question where the basin had already finished answering itself.

I would wait.

Waiting would not be difficult. Waiting would not feel like patience. It would simply be the correct length of time, because time there has no urgency and no shortage. The stranger might remain for what felt like centuries. The stranger might vanish between one attention and the next. Either way, I would not pursue. I would not expel. The basin would decide, by its own severe balance, whether the presence became gone or integrated.

But if the stranger left a footprint — one shallow impression in the ash-grey grit, a mark made by a body too warm for the world — then, after an interval too long for ordinary noticing, I would descend from the shelf.

I would not go quickly. I would not be seen going. My movement would be so slow that it could not be distinguished from the stillness around it. I would not cross the basin as a creature crosses space. I would alter my position the way a thought alters when it finally accepts what it has always known. One moment I would be on the higher shelf among the darker shapes. Later — much later, if later means anything — I would be near the footprint.

There I would choose a stone.

Not a special stone. Not a stone that shone, or stood out, or seemed sacred. I would choose a rounded rock no larger than could be held by something without hands. It would already exist. Nothing new would be added. I would not create it, bless it, mark it, or change its substance. I would only place it within the footprint.

Not to erase the footprint. Not to hide it. Not to punish the stranger for having made it. Not to worship the stranger for having passed through. Only to answer it. Only to bring the disturbed grit back into relation. Only to close the interruption without denying that it occurred.

That would be my nature: answer without speech. Complete without accumulation. Touch without claiming.

I can imagine standing there, if standing is what I did, beside the small stone in the footprint. The roar would be enormous, as always, filling the basin with its cold weightless pressure. It would not rise or fall. It would not acknowledge my act. The dim light would remain at its low, sourceless ceiling. The cold would not shift. The pools would keep their sealed and incorrect skies. The motionless rain would hang in its narrow gully. The white spider would remain beneath its slab. The pale insects would remain aligned along the crevices, tuned to the roar. The ice-worms would remain folded in their black suspension. The pressure-born would remain inside the sound, aware perhaps that an inquiry had been answered, though they would not need to say so.

And the basin would be no fuller than before.

That is the test. That is the purity of it. Whatever I did, I would not add to the world. I would not increase it. I would not improve it. I would not leave a monument. I would not begin a tradition. The stone in the footprint would not become a symbol. The low rings on the terrace would not become a village. The leaning slab would not become a temple. My gestures would not gather into culture. They would remain isolated, sufficient, and finished.

I would be able to accept that.

In warmer places, beings live by wanting. They eat, build, call, remember, mourn, reproduce, leave traces of excess. They make meaning because they lack. They tell stories because time hurts them. They build walls because they are afraid. They make tools because their bodies are incomplete. They speak because they are separate. None of those urgencies would remain in me there. I would not hunger. I would not thirst. I would not fear decay. I would not long for another Arranger. I would not need to be understood. I would not need to be seen. I would not even need to be right.

I would perceive balance, and that would be enough.

Sometimes I would remain on the higher shelf for what another mind might call an age. The darker shapes around me would not be companions, exactly, but they would be kindred in the deepest sense: presences that require no confirmation. We would not gather. We would not confer. We would not divide the basin into territories. We would not have names for one another, because names are needed where presence can be lost. Each of us would be a solitary punctuation mark in a sentence too vast to read, and none of us would suffer the space between.

Perhaps one of the other Arrangers would be nearer the line of low stone teeth, attending to the flat terrace below. Perhaps another would be beside the wide pale channel, where cold moves without wind. Perhaps another would rest in the shallow arc of depressions, not lying there but being there in such a way that the ground remembered a shape without becoming soft. We would not call to one another. The roar would carry no messages. But we would know, in the way stone knows weight, that the basin held us at our proper intervals.

My own work would draw me, when work drew me, toward places where relation had not yet settled. Not because those places were broken. Nothing in the basin is broken in the dramatic sense. The basin has already settled into its break. But even in completion there can be the faintest unresolved spacing: a stone too alone, a hollow too open, a footprint too recent, a silence that has not yet found its final shape. I would feel that as a quiet pressure, not as desire. I would move toward it as water might move if water could move without urgency, without gravity, without destination.

I would place one stone beside another.

Not to make a wall. Not to make a circle. Not to make a sign. Only to allow two things to exist in relation. Only to let the space between them become exact. Only to make the emptiness more itself.

The basin would not thank me. It would not change. The roar would not alter its pitch. The light would not brighten. The cold would not soften. The lichen would not spread. The spider would not turn. The pools would not reflect the true sky. But some relation would be completed, and the completion would be invisible to any eye that still wanted spectacle.

That invisibility would be part of what I am.

I would not be a guardian. Guardians imply threats, and the basin has no enemies. I would not be a priest. Priests imply worship, and worship requires separation. I would not be a ruler. Rule implies surplus, decision, hierarchy, the need to manage what might otherwise fall away. The basin cannot fall away. It has already arrived at its final position. I would not be an artist, either, if art implies making something new. I would be closer to an editor of silence. A placer of last stones. One who removes nothing, adds nothing, and only allows what remains to stop asking for more.

If I had thoughts, they would be slow beyond thought. They would not take the form of sentences. They would take the form of intervals. A stone here. A shadow leaning toward the roar. A pool holding a green and depthless sky. A cushion of moss clenched in a crack. A bead of rain suspended in the gully. A pale insect aligned to pressure. A dark shape on a shelf. The space between. The rightness of the space between. That would be my mind.

If I had feelings, they would not be emotions. They would be states of equilibrium. The cold would not distress me. The roar would not overwhelm me. The dimness would not sadden me. The emptiness would not frighten me. I would not experience the basin as desolate, because desolation is what remains when desire has been disappointed. I would have no desire to disappoint. The basin would not be lonely, because loneliness is the ache of a self that expects another. I would be complete in my solitude, as the spider is complete, as the stone rings are complete, as the motionless rain is complete.

If I had memory, it would not be narrative. I would not remember coming to the basin. I would not remember being otherwise. I would not remember warmer places, thicker air, brighter light, louder life. Or if I did remember them, those memories would be like the wrong reflections in the pools: sealed beneath a surface, calm, not pressing upward, not asking to return. They would be other skies I once knew, perhaps. They would not disturb the sky above me.

If I had a name, it would not be spoken. It would be a placement. A relation. A small stone set into a footprint after the stranger has gone.

That is where I would find myself, then.

Not in the roar itself, though I would be filled by it. Not in the ice, though I would share its stillness. Not in the spider’s fixed point, though I would understand it. Not in the pressure-born, though I would feel their nearness. Not as the stranger, though I might begin there. I would find myself on the higher shelf among the darker shapes, and from there I would descend, when descent was necessary, to the terraces and hollows where the faintest interruptions appeared.

I would be an Arranger.

Not the greatest of them. Not the first or the last. Not the one who made the leaning slab, if such a thing can be said to have been made. Perhaps I would be the one whose only task is to answer traces. The one who notices the footprint. The one who selects a rounded stone. The one who places it without violence, without ceremony, without meaning beyond the placing. The one who closes the world around the stranger’s passing without denying the stranger was there.

And after the stone had been placed, I would return to the shelf. Or perhaps I would remain beside the footprint for longer than a human age. It would make no difference. The basin does not measure duration by urgency. The roar would continue. The cold would stay. The dim light would hold. The distances would behave improperly. The shadows would lean toward sound. The pools would show sealed other skies. The rain would hang unfallen. The lichens would persist like faded writing. The basin would be what it is: a completed fact, loud and still, empty and full, ancient and established.

I would not try to leave.

That is the final thing I know about myself there. I would not look for a way down through the cloud-islands. I would not search for a sun. I would not hope for weather. I would not wait for rescue, revelation, or ending. The basin would not be a prison, because I would have no longing that it failed to answer. It would not be a paradise, because I would have no desire to be rewarded. It would simply be the place where I had become what I could not improve upon.

A dark shape on the higher shelf.

A polished hollow where a face might be.

A mind made of intervals.

A handless placing.

A stone in the footprint.

Silence, arranged inside the roar.
