At first, if I answered too quickly, I would say I was standing out on the open bark plain: a small upright figure against heat shimmer, staring toward the scattered colossi. But that image is wrong almost as soon as it forms. To stand exposed there, with no seam or hollow near, would be to make myself a signal. The open tableland does not favor the visible. It favors those who know where to be absorbed.

So I would not find myself in the wide open, not for long.

I would find myself in a seam.

More precisely: in the upper root terraces of one of the great ribbed trunk-forms, where the living body of the landscape has old injuries, old swellings, and long-healed hollows. I would be near the place where a split in an ancient stem has become a narrow canyon, its walls polished by wind and passage, its lower throat damp enough to hold a faint green smell even at midday. Above me, clouds would pass below the crown-spines. Below me, the plain would glitter with heat and distance. Between those two immensities, I would occupy an intermediate space: a hollow internode, a chamber made not by quarrying but by generations of careful widening, cleaning, listening, and repair.

If the hollow had a name, it would not be a word in the ordinary sense. It would be a pattern of taps: two light knocks, a pause, then a deeper resonance struck with the heel of the hand against a dry seed-case. Someone who knew the tableland would hear that rhythm and know: here is shelter, here is water, here is a mouth in the wall that does not bite.

I would not be one of the great slow plant bodies. I am too restless for that, too quick, too full of small fears and small intentions. Nor would I be the swarm, though I can imagine the seduction of it: becoming a weather-like pressure, a cloud of wings and hunger moving across the spare distances. But that kind of being is too numerous, too single in its purpose, too easily merged into appetite. I would want some degree of separateness, some fragile selfhood capable of hesitation.

So I would be one of the careful makers.

Not a builder in the heavy sense. The tableland punishes heaviness by absorbing it. I would be a person of light marks: knots, pegs, thorn anchors, repaired cords, a seed-shell vessel rubbed smooth by years of carrying. I would be human-shaped, but the place would have pared me down. Lean body. Heat-adapted skin. Feet broad and sensitive enough to read the difference between a stable root platform and a leaf layer beginning to soften beneath its fungal stitching. Hands long-fingered and calloused at the tips, not from brutality but from precision: tying, tapping, testing, listening.

I think I would be a hollow-listener.

That would not be a mystical title so much as a practical one. In a world made of living architecture, hollows are treasures and dangers. They can be shelters, nurseries, water reservoirs, drums, ambush places, tombs, or traps. A hollow can save you from the noon heat and then betray you at dusk when something else decides it is thirsty. A hollow can sing when wind passes through it, but the song may mean structural weakness, or rising water, or a nest of creatures inside. To be a hollow-listener would be to know the difference between resonances: the dry knock of an empty chamber, the softened thud of damp rot, the higher pitch of a wall thinning over time, the dead silence that means something large is waiting inside.

My home would be one such hollow, but I would not call it mine in the sense of ownership. I would call it mine the way a bird might call a branch its own for a season: by use, by attention, by the work of keeping it habitable. The mouth of the hollow would be narrow enough to defend, polished smooth by shoulders entering and leaving. Inside, the chamber would curve like the inside of an old instrument. Light would reach only a short distance, enough to reveal the nearest walls: fibrous, dark, faintly fragrant with resin and fungus. There would be a knot-bowl near the inner wall, carefully cleaned of debris, where condensation gathers before dawn. Beside it, a small channel cut with a thorn would guide overflow away from the sleeping shelf so that the floor does not soften.

I would keep fungi there. Not in rows like a farm, but in tended patches along the damper inner wall where the stem’s old tissue has gone soft enough to support them. I would prune them with a beetle-mandible blade, not to maximize yield but to prevent them from inviting beetles, rot, or creatures that eat beetles. Everything in that world is a chain of invitations. To grow one thing is to ask another thing to come. The wise grow carefully.

I would have few possessions, and each would be intimate.

A seed-shell water vessel, hollowed and stoppered with waxed fiber. A striking stick of dense heartwood, worn smooth by my grip, used to tap walls, test paths, answer distant signals, and sometimes persuade a seed-case to release what it holds. A cord belt braided from root fibers. A thorn knife. A small pouch of resin, because resin is repair, adhesive, fire-starter, scent-mark, and trade-good all at once. Perhaps, if I were lucky or beloved, a tiny ornament of metal: a fleck of something dense and rare bound into a carved knot, not because it would make me powerful, but because it would mean someone trusted me with what little permanence they had found.

My body would smell of the tableland: warm chaff, green sap, old pollen, smoke kept carefully small. My hearing would be my most developed sense, not because the world is loud, but because the loudness has meaning. An outsider might hear only the deafening weave of insect sound, the rasps and clicks and wing-beats pressing against the chest like weather. I would hear gaps. I would hear wrongness. I would know when a stretch of chorus thinning meant a predator moving through it. I would know when a new metallic buzz in the distance meant a pollinator cloud approaching a flowering platform. I would know when a hollow’s tone changed because rain had entered it somewhere higher up, or because its wall had cracked in the heat.

I would be, above all, a pattern-reader in a world made of patterns.

That is where I would find myself: not at the center of the landscape, not atop the highest crown, not crossing the open plain as if it were empty. I would be in the in-between places. The thresholds. The throats. The seams where one kind of life touches another and becomes audible.

If I had to give myself a role, I would say I was a translator of vibrations.

Not a great prophet or priest. The tableland would not need that. It is already speaking. But there would be need for someone who can listen between registers: someone who can take the meaning of a hollow’s resonance and turn it into a warning; someone who can learn the particular frequency that makes a certain flower open without triggering it recklessly; someone who can answer a drum-code from another root terrace without giving away the location of a water pocket to something hungry. My usefulness would come from making connections without forcing them.

I would not be alone all the time. The hollow-listeners would likely live in loose relation to one another: a few in one stem cluster, a few in another, separated by open distance but connected by sound. We would not crowd. Crowding would strain the water pockets and attract too much attention. But we would maintain acoustic lines. On cool dawns, before the heat makes the air tremble, we might exchange rhythms across the plain: three slow strikes, a pause, two quick taps, a resonant palm-strike against a dry seed-case. Such patterns could say: I am alive. My hollow holds water. The lower path is flooded with ants. A predator passed at night. The crown to the east will flower soon.

At major pulses, I would gather with others. A flowering event would be both festival and danger. The air would thicken with pollinators until the light darkened and the noise became pressure against the ribs. The great blossoms might open only under the combined vibration of thousands of wings, and the whole landscape would feel as if it were inhaling. In such a moment, I would not stand at the center of the swarm. I would be at the edge, on a root shelf, striking stick in hand, helping maintain the rhythm that keeps harvest from becoming destruction. Too much vibration at the wrong moment could bring down seeds before they are ready, or anger creatures that guard the flowers, or draw predators into the crowd. My task would be to listen to the pulse and keep us inside it.

I would be afraid often, but not in a panicked way. Fear there would be a tuning of attention.

The first time the chorus stopped near me, I would freeze. Silence on the plant tableland would not mean peace. It would mean that many lives had suddenly agreed to be quiet for the same reason. My breath would slow. My hand would go to the striking stick not to fight but to decide whether sound or stillness was safer. Sometimes the correct answer would be to become small. Sometimes it would be to make a sharp, unexpected knock, a sound that says: I am not prey, I am not alone, I am listening too.

I would learn that courage there is not loudness. It is timing.

If travelers came to my hollow, I would not greet them openly from the mouth. I would listen first. Footfall tells much: broad careful steps, or hurried narrow ones? The scuff of a dragged tool, or the light touch of someone accustomed to root bridges? Do they pause at the threshold as respectful people do, or do they crowd the opening? I might tap a question from inside: a simple pattern, repeated. If they answered well, I might open the woven bract curtain and offer them a leaf-cup of water, knowing exactly how much I could spare without draining the knot-bowl. If they answered poorly, I might remain hidden and let the hollow seem occupied by nothing but beetles and old echoes.

My kindness would be practical. My suspicion would also be practical.

I would probably be more strange than a conventional human, though not utterly alien. The tableland would have shaped me toward sensitivity. My ears might be slightly larger than the old human norm, mobile in small ways, capable of being covered by woven pads when the noise became too much. My voice would be trained not for beauty but for carrying: short phrases, clear intervals, capable of shifting into drum-language when words failed. My skin might be marked not by tattoos but by healed contact marks: resin burns, thorn scars, pale patches where sun never reaches inside the hollow, calluses along the palms from striking resonant surfaces. I would carry myself with a kind of compressed grace, always aware of what I touch, because touch is information.

I would not build a house. I would maintain a relationship with shelter.

If my platform sagged, I would re-lash it with cord rather than cut new supports unless the old ones were truly dead. If the mouth of my hollow began to close with new growth, I would encourage it gently, redirecting saplings or fibrous shoots rather than hacking them away in a way that would leave a wound and attract borers. If I needed fire, I would make it inside a ring of dense bark over carbonized pith, with a watcher and a vessel of water nearby, and afterward I would carry the embers in a damp seed-shell only when necessary. Fire would be one of my most precious tools and one of my greatest shames if misused. The world around me is mostly plant. Even when it seems ancient and settled, it remembers how to burn.

I would eat with the seasons, but the seasons there would be pulses rather than neat turns of the year.

At dawn I might drink condensation and eat a few clean fungi. During an insect emergence I might harvest larvae carefully from a fallen branch joint, taking only from a mass already being claimed by scavengers, because a fresh fall can be dangerous and contested. At seed fall I might collect what the vibration of the swarms releases, but I would not strike the seed-cases greedily. Some seeds must fall untouched. Some must be buried by other creatures. Some must remain for the next generation of the landscape itself. If I ate fruit, I would know which ferment is safe and which becomes poison. If I took eggs, I would take from a nest already broken by another predator, not create a new wound for the sake of convenience.

My ethics would be simple and severe:

Take without killing the source.  
Drink without draining.  
Mark without scarring.  
Listen before speaking.  
Be loud when necessary, silent when wise, and brief enough to be absorbed.

That last part would be the hardest to accept and the most necessary.

I would have to accept that my marks would not last. If I carved a sign into a living surface, the tableland would swell it into a ridge and eventually make it unreadable. If I lashed a bridge too heavily, insects or rot or root movement would undo it. If I cleared a path too broadly, growth would close it. My presence would be preserved not by monuments but by repetition: the same careful route walked again and again, the same hollow tended season after season, the same water knowledge sung into memory and taught to others. I would live in continuity, not permanence.

And yet that would not make my life small.

There would be grandeur in it. Standing at the mouth of my hollow in the late afternoon, when thermals shake the high crowns and loose chaff spirals in the hot light, I might see the whole plain trembling with distance. The great plant bodies would stand far apart like patient continents. Between them, the air would bend with heat. A swarm might rise from a hidden hollow and become a temporary dark cloud. Somewhere far away, a dry seed-case might rattle as wings pass over it. The sound would press in from every direction: millions of lives chewing, calling, striking, mating, warning, feeding, dying, becoming ground.

In that moment, I would feel both insignificant and necessary. Insignificant because I am small and brief. Necessary because every ecosystem needs organs of attention. It needs creatures who notice. It needs those who can hear a change before it becomes catastrophe. It needs those who know which knot-bowl to clean, which hollow to leave alone, which rhythm calms a frightened climber, which vibration opens a flower without breaking it.

I would be one of those small tuning organs.

If someone asked me later, “Who were you in that world?” I would not say I was its master. I would not say I was its hero. I would not even say I belonged to it in the simple way people belong to cities or houses.

I would say: I was a hollow-listener.

I lived in the throat of an old stem on a root terrace above the lower clouds. I kept a knot-bowl clean and a fungal patch modest and a striking stick close. I knew the sound water makes when it enters a hidden chamber. I knew the silence that means danger. I answered calls across the plain with patterns rather than names. I helped travelers find shelter without teaching them to destroy it. I harvested with rhythm, not force. I made fires small enough to forgive. When the great flowering came, I stood at the edge of the swarm and listened until my bones hummed with it.

And if you came looking for me, you might not see me at all.

You might find only a polished hollow mouth, a thorn peg left in a seam, a seed-shell vessel tucked into a crevice, a faint smell of resin, and a wall that sounds oddly thoughtful when you knock. If you tapped twice and waited, perhaps something inside would answer: not with a word, but with a resonance just careful enough to tell you that the hollow was occupied, that the water was shared, that you could rest there for a little while if you did not stay too long.

That would be me.

A brief, careful noise inside the greater noise.
