I would find myself in the bright interval between towers.

Not in a city, because there are no cities. Not beside water, because water is not there in any form that could gather, reflect, or comfort. Not in a sheltered valley or quiet grove, because quiet does not exist there and shelter is only ever a temporary arrangement of wind and growth. I would be out on the open root-crust, where the pale ground shines with mineral dust and the distance is measured not by miles but by sound.

More precisely: I would be near the base of one of the great hollow plant-towers, on the leeward side of a fluted stem where the wind-shadow offers some relief from the grain-blast. The tower would be old, its reflective leaf-scales worn in long vertical streaks, its ribs polished by centuries of particulate wind. Its crown would have one damaged turbine-leaf, or something analogous: a stiff curved frond cracked along its midline, so that when it turns in the wind it produces a double beat. Whomp. Whomp-whomp. Whomp. Whom-whom. A fault in the rhythm. A small wrongness audible across kilometers to anything tuned to hear it.

That is where I would be: drawn there by the wrong note.

I would not be human there. The world you described would not permit softness, and neither would I. I would be one of the Dry Cantors, or something close enough to them that the difference would be a matter of rhythm rather than kind. A tall, narrow, hollow-framed being. My body would be plated in overlapping reflective scales, not made for beauty but for shedding dust, reflecting hard light, and surviving electrostatic discharge. My limbs would be long and spare, with joints that click faintly when the temperature shifts. Where hands would be, I would have bundles of fine insulated filaments: capable of touching, conducting, isolating, testing, discharging. Not hands that build. Hands that attend.

My chest and ribs would carry tympanic membranes, thin dry surfaces that read the plateau’s noise as meaning. Heat pits near my shoulders would sense thermal gradients from tower vents. Electrostatic spines along my back would rise and lower with the charge in the air. My eyes would be resin lenses, dark and hard, better at reading flicker, ultraviolet pulses, and the blue scratch of discharge than at seeing the world in soft detail. I would not smell water. I would smell ozone, resin vapor, powdered leaf-glass, hot cellulose, and the sharp metallic warning of an overloaded crown.

If I had to say what I was in that world, I would say: I would be a listener who acts.

Not a ruler. Not a builder. Not a farmer. Not an engineer with tools. I would be a mobile awareness inside a larger machine, one of the rare beings who can move through the roar and recognize where the roar has changed.

The tower with the cracked turbine-leaf would be my immediate concern. Its hum would have shifted. To an outsider, it would still be deafening: the hollow boom of the stem, the grinding seed mechanisms, the high keening of wire-tendrils, the crackle of static along the crown. But to me, the pattern would be wrong. A half-tone deeper in the lower chamber. A hesitation before the seed-capsules strike. A faint irregularity in the venting rhythm. Something inside the tower is beginning to clog, overcharge, or heat unevenly.

I would approach by walking the open crust, my padded feet choosing the polished lines where grain-wind has hardened the root-felt. Around me, the landscape would be immense and loud. A herd of plinthbacks might be moving across the distance, their mineral plates grinding, their chest-resonances knocking through the ground. Glass-midges would hiss overhead in translucent swarms. A wind-ray might be riding a thermal pulse near the tower’s upper vents, its dry wings flexing with papery crackle. I would notice all of them, but not as scenery. They would be part of the diagnostic field. If the plinthbacks avoid a certain patch of crust, there may be charge pooling there. If seed-jacks are nervous near a tower’s base, seed release may be imminent. If vent-dwellers emerge too early, internal pressure is shifting.

I would reach the tower and press my filament-bundles against one of its ribs.

The vibration would enter me immediately. Not through ears alone, but through the whole frame. My hollow bones would answer the tower’s hum. I would stand still for a long time, perhaps so still that a peel-brood might crawl over my foot and test whether I was dead. Then I would tap: two knocks, pause, one scrape. A question made of rhythm. The tower would not answer in language, but it would answer in vibration. A returning tremor. A change in pitch. A heat pulse from a nearby vent.

From that I would learn where the fault lies.

Maybe a resin bladder has thickened. Maybe a salt conduit has narrowed. Maybe vent-dwellers have gathered too densely and are insulating a membrane that needs to shed heat. Maybe charge is collecting in a dead-end spine because the arc-brushes have not visited since the wind shifted. The tower does not know these things as knowledge. It knows them as pressure, flow, heat, and resistance. I would know them as patterns.

Then I would act.

I would not carry tools. I would not attach pipes or build scaffolds or open panels with instruments. Any action I take would be bodily, temporary, and exact. I might climb to a lower vent where growth ridges offer holds, my filament-bundles testing conductivity before each touch. I might find a swollen resin duct where a blockage has formed and strike it in the correct interval, matching the tower’s own percussion until the hardened plug cracks and falls away in glittering shards. I might stand in the path of an accumulating discharge and open my own spines, letting the tower’s excess voltage pass through me into the root-crust. That would be dangerous. The blue-white arc would crawl along my back, my scales would flare with light, the ground beneath me would tick and crack with heat, and for a moment I would be less a person than a living conductor.

If it worked, the tower’s hum would smooth. The cracked turbine-leaf would still beat wrongly, but the deeper danger would pass. A seed-capsule cluster might release in a sudden percussion burst, sending hard oily seeds bouncing across the crust. Seed-jacks would dart out from nowhere, startled and greedy, stuffing seeds into their wax-lined pouches before bolting. Some of the seeds would be lost, dropped, kicked into cracks, forgotten. That would be part of the purpose. My intervention would not be for the tower alone. It would be for the plateau’s entire chain of consequences.

If there were others nearby, they would know.

A Dry Cantor does not speak across distance by shouting. The roar would eat such sounds. Instead, we would communicate through structure. I might strike a hollow rib in a pattern that carries through the tower’s body. Another might answer from a kilometer away, their message arriving as a faint vibration through the root-crust. A third might respond with a static pulse visible from the crown, a blue flicker against the bleached sky. Our conversation would be intervals, pressures, knocks, scrapes, discharges, and silences.

If I had a name, it would not be a word.

It might be three knocks on hardened cellulose, followed by the high whine of a charged spine, followed by the pause after a seed-pod bursts. If translated badly, it might mean something like “the one who stands in the interval before the arc.” Or perhaps more accurately: “presence that opens the blocked path.” Or more simply still: “blue flash at the lower vent.” Names would not be fixed. They would change as the plateau changes. A being might be known by one rhythm during a pollen storm and another during the cold redistribution of night.

I would live without possessions. No pouch that I kept. No tool that I made. No home that I defended. I might return to the same tower for many cycles, but not because it was mine. Because its needs were recurring. My routes would be shaped by sound and charge. I would follow the hum of overpressure, the smell of hot resin, the sight of vent-dwellers emerging in unusual clouds, the behavior of arc-brushes avoiding a crown. I would eat oily nectar from discharged bladders, seed kernels cracked open with patient leverage, shed vent-dweller bodies rich in chemical film, electrolyte gels drawn from safe root-taps. Nothing would be cultivated. Nothing would be stored for long. The plateau produces enough to drown the air in seed and pollen, but its abundance is not gentle. It must be caught in the right moment.

I would not think of myself as separate from the landscape in the way a person in a quiet world might. I would not look at the tower and think, “This is a machine I am repairing.” I would think, if thought there is like a pulse, “Here the flow has narrowed. Here I become the opening.”

That is who I would be: a local nerve in a continental body.

Not the mind of the plateau. The plateau is too vast for any single mind. But one of its rare moments of reflexive attention.

In a storm, I would become more active, not less.

When the sky thickens into a pollen front and the light turns into a yellow roar, the world’s production becomes visible as weather. The towers would groan under the pressure of their own output. Seed mechanisms would misfire. Static would build until the air itself feels like a taut membrane. The seed-jacks would scatter in panic, which is how the future gets planted. The wind-rays would rise into the turbulence, trailing charged filaments. The plinthbacks would lock into mounds, their plates ticking. The glass-midges would be swept into dense streaming clouds.

And I would move.

I would go from tower to tower where possible, or choose the one whose fault matters most. My reflective scales would be dusted yellow. My spines would lift with charge. My filament-bundles would be stiff with pollen and static. I would listen through the storm, not despite it. The noise would be enormous, a shaking pressure in the chest, but within it there would still be pattern. A tower about to rupture sounds different from a tower merely shedding excess. A vent about to ignite resin vapor has a certain heat-click before the blue flare. A crown about to arc dangerously will make the fine filaments along one’s back stand out in a halo.

If I could not prevent a rupture, I would move out of its path. The plateau is not concerned with saving every part. It is concerned with continuity. If a tower splits, vent-dwellers will burst into the air. Peel-broods will come to disassemble the fallen panels. Seed-jacks will raid the spilled output. Wind-rays will circle the new thermal column. Eventually the root-crust will absorb the fragments. The disaster will become production.

But if I can prevent a larger failure by a small discharge, a cleared duct, a tapped membrane, a coordinated pulse with two other Cantors, then I do that. Not out of kindness. Not out of dominion. Out of belonging.

At night, I would not sleep deeply.

The towers would cool and contract, knocking like vast struck vessels. Charge would redistribute. The air would grow colder, the shadows black and crisp, the landscape lit by slow red heat from seed-chambers, faint chemical luminescence at vents, and the blue scratches of biological sparks. The root-tappers would hum beneath the crust. Knell-hunters would listen near the base of towers, waiting for the moment when a sound covers a strike. Peel-broods would continue their patient disassembly of whatever fell during the day.

I might become more active then. Night is when the plateau’s tensions shift inward. The day-side charge bleeds away, the thermal gradients reverse, and structures that were stable in sunlight become strange under cooling strain. I would stand in the dark against a tower rib and feel it settle. I would sense where the oils are moving slowly, where a pressure chamber is contracting too fast, where a crown is shedding voltage in irregular pulses. My own static discharges would move through the dark like small blue thoughts.

If young Dry Cantors were with me, I would teach them without explanation. I would place them where the vibration is instructive. Here, where the cracked turbine-leaf doubles its beat. Here, where the root-crust carries plinthback knocks. Here, where an arc-brush taps a floral vent and survives. Here, where the tower answers a knuckle-strike with a long returning tone. They would learn by resonance. They would learn by nearly dying at the wrong moment. They would learn that the plateau does not reward permanence. It rewards timing.

When I died, I would not be memorialized.

There would be no burial. No monument. No relic. My reflective scales would be stripped by peel-broods or shattered into dust by wind. My conductive filaments would be consumed by scavengers or scattered into the crust. My charge, if any remained, would leak into the root-felt and be forgotten. The Dry Cantors might mark the absence with a brief silence, if silence can be said to exist in such a world. Then the roar would continue.

That is the part of myself I would have to accept, or perhaps the part I would never need to accept because I would not be built to grieve permanence. I would be made of the same principle as the place: function without ownership, presence without accumulation, awareness without artifacts.

So if you asked where I would find myself in this world, I would say: in the loud gap between colossal living machines, on the pale root-crust under a hard bright sky, near the tower whose rhythm has become slightly wrong.

And if you asked who or what I would be, I would say: I would be a Dry Cantor, a fault-listener, a current-tender, a temporary nerve ending in the body of an industrial ecology. I would be hollow-framed, scale-plated, filament-fingered, electrically attentive. I would not build, not own, not quiet the world. I would move through its roar and listen for the place where the roar needs to open.

You would not find me by looking for a face. You would find me by noticing a glint of reflective scale against a tower rib, a stillness too precise to be shed bark, a blue-white discharge leaping from spine to filament, and then the sudden smoothing of a hum that had been wrong for days.

After that, the roar would close over the place where I had stood, and the plateau would continue its ancient, waterless, glorious work.
