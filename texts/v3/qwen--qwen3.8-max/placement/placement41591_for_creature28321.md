If the wind changes, the birds say so first.

Not with alarm. Not with panic. Their calls simply rearrange themselves: one knock moves higher, one whistle thins, a double cry breaks into two sharper halves, as if the whole upper tangle of vines and pipes had turned its attention toward the rim. Below them, the hoppers answer in metallic fragments. Below that, the leaves rasp once, heavily, like hands shifting in sleep. Then the basin settles again, holding the new air inside itself until the new air becomes part of the old sound.

That is where I would find myself: not outside the hollow looking in, not on the worn shoulder of the ancient mountain staring into distance, but inside the basin where the distance has been refused. I would be near the low place where three terraces meet, where the lane narrows enough for leaves to brush both shoulders, where a shallow lamp sits in a niche of stone and burns with a small orange tongue of plant oil. I would be at a threshold. That would be my nature there. Not at the center as a monument, not at the rim as a watcher, but in the between-place where passage happens, where sound enters, where warmth circulates, where one crowded life touches another.

At first, I imagine I might have been something else.

I might have been one of the broad-leaved terrace plants, thick and waxed, standing in patient rank, keeping my fluids locked in a fat green secrecy. That would have been one kind of dignity: to be full without spilling, to grow by slow accumulation, to let dust slide from me in fine sheets when the wind turns. I might have been one of the beetles moving through the lower beds, ticking over stone, doing the holy work of undoing what has fallen. There is no horror in that labor, only patience. I might have been a roof-runner, small and furred and clever, living in the gaps, stealing what is not tightly kept, cleaning what hands cannot reach. I might have been one of the moths beneath the eaves, opening and closing my wings as though each motion were a thought being weighed. I might have been a lizard on the wall, scale-patterned, deliberate, keeping watch over a doorway without ever seeming to move.

All of these lives would have fit me. All of them would have been enough.

But the longer the basin gathers around me in thought, the more I feel that I would not be only plant or insect or small roof-dwelling animal. I would be one of the people of the hollow, or one of the beings shaped by their long agreement with it. I would be a listener. A mender of passages. A keeper of small fires and sealed fluids. One of those who maintain the tuning of the place.

I would be, if I must give myself a shape there, a threshold-keeper of the dry city.

My body would have been formed by the first fact of the mountain: there is no open water. I would not have the careless wetness of lowland life. My skin would be dry and close, perhaps faintly waxed to the touch, with pores that do not waste themselves in sweat. My lips would be sealed by habit and by necessity. My saliva would be thick, resinous, precious. I would not expect to drink often. I would not expect to pour. The very idea of pouring would feel wrong to me, almost violent, as if one were tearing open a vein in public. If I carried fluid at all, it would be in a small stone bottle stopped with wax and fiber, hidden in a net against my body, warmed by my ribs. It would be oil for a lamp, or medicine for a cracked thumb, or resin for a joint in a wind-tube. Not something to be squandered in display.

My eyes would be made for amber gloom. I would see well in the brown-violet shadows where the leaves roof the lanes and the sun is only a copper smear overhead. My hands would be sensitive and unhurried, accustomed to working by feel: the grain of stone, the tension of rope, the stiffness of a reed pipe, the warmth of a wall near a mineral vent. I would know the basin by touch as much as by sight. A polished stair-stone under the sole of my foot would tell me where I was. A change in the murmur of the wind-tubes would tell me whether the air was rising or settling. The smell of warm dust, leaf-fiber, resin, and beetle-beds would be my ordinary weather.

I would live in a narrow chamber built into the retaining wall between two terraces, where an older doorway has been half-filled by later repair. The room would not be large. It would not need to be. It would be warm, dry, and crowded with useful things: bundles of cured vine, a stone hammer with a palm-worn handle, a rack of seed-cakes, a ceramic cup for oil, a bone awl, a blade of dark glass, a folded mat, a small lamp whose niche has blackened with years of careful use. Above the doorway I would leave a pocket in the wall undisturbed, because a lizard has chosen it. Beneath the eave outside, a cluster of moths would rest. Their presence would be a comfort to me, not decoration. If they shifted uneasily, I would rise and listen.

My work would begin with listening.

Before I lit the lamp, before I ate the first dry morsel of the day, I would stand in the doorway and hear the basin tuning itself. The wind over the rim. The ceramic catchers whispering. The seedpods knocking. The leaves rasping. The hoppers answering one another. The roof-runners scratching in the beams. The low murmur that rises when air finds a passage and is drawn inward through the hollow reeds. I would listen for the wrong note: a tube half-blocked by dust or moth-wing, a vine grown too thick across a wind-channel, a roof panel loosened so that the air whistles too sharply, a stair-stone cracked in a way that changes its tone underfoot. In another place, such things might seem trivial. There, they would be essential. Sound would be how the basin knows itself. Sound would be the substitute for flowing water: it gathers, it marks time, it warns, it connects.

If I found a note out of place, I would mend it.

That would be my devotion. Not great building. Not expansion. Not conquest. Repair.

I would climb where the lane narrows and the leaves press close. I would use a copper pick to clear a wind-tube. I would prune a resin-vine with a blade of dark glass, letting the wound close in its own black shine. I would retie a bundle of drying stems so that it no longer knocks against a rope bridge. I would move a beetle from the path with a leaf, gently, because it is carrying a fragment of shed skin larger than its own patience. I would inspect a lamp niche and see that the oil is low, then add only what is needed, no more. I would touch wrists with a neighbor passing below, forehead sometimes if the hour asks for more. We would not ask, Are you thirsty? We would ask, Is your air clear? Is your wall warm? Is your note true?

There would be a ritual to all of it. Not because the basin is solemn, but because restraint has its own grace.

I would never pour water, because there would be no water to pour. But I would pour attention. I would measure oil by finger-widths. I would trim wicks made of fibrous pith. I would place a dried seed head in a shrine niche where carved bones and salt crystals already stand, not as an offering of abundance, but as a record of persistence. I would sing when the wind asked for singing. Not loudly. Not alone, if others were near. The songs would be timed to the basin’s own notes, fitted into the murmur so that a human voice would seem to emerge from the architecture rather than interrupt it. Some songs would mark the hours. Some would signal that a passage was clear. Some would be for the dead, though the dead, like everything else there, would be handled without waste and without spillage. Their fluids would have already returned inward; their bodies would be dried, sealed, perhaps folded into the walls or the terraces in ways that feel more like placement than burial. I would not fear that. I would understand it as the final form of the same respect by which the living keep themselves contained.

If I were asked who I was in that place, I would not answer first with a name. Names there would feel secondary to function and relation. I would say: I am the one who listens at the third stair. I am the one who keeps the lamp by the rope bridge. I am the one who clears the wind-tubes when the moths grow restless. I am the one who moves beetles from the path and does not punish the roof-runners too severely when they steal seed-cakes. I am the one whose hands know where the wall hums strongest. I am the one who touches wrists at dusk and says, The note is true.

If pressed, I might say: I am a sealed thing.

That would be the deepest answer.

I would not be a being of rivers. I would not be a creature of rain, nor of open pools, nor of tears easily shed. My fullness would be internal. My grief would go inward and be kept there until it became usable. My joy would not overflow; it would thicken. My love would be a practice of careful containment: a touch at the wrist, a shared lamp, a song fitted precisely into the sound already made by another. I would not confuse abundance with spillage. I would not mistake emptiness for silence. The basin would teach me that loudness can be intimate, that crowding can be generous, that a place can be complete without being open in the careless way of young landscapes.

There would be moments when I felt the weight of that completeness.

In the warmest part of the day, when the stone gives back its stored heat and the air in the lanes becomes almost interior, I might stand beneath an overhang of leaves and roofs and feel the nearness of everything: walls on either side, nests overhead, root-fibers pressing through the path, insects moving in the lower green, roof-runners whispering through the beams, the whole basin occupied to its last crevice. For a being formed elsewhere, that might feel suffocating. But I would have been formed there, or formed by it. I would feel held. I would feel the way a finger feels inside a glove worn soft by many hands before it. There would be no desire to break out into emptiness. The vast outside beyond the rim would seem abstract, a story the mountain once told before it learned better.

Then evening would come, if evening is the right word beneath that copper dimness.

The lamps would brighten one by one. The birds would settle. The hoppers would slow their metallic conversation. The wind would find its night passages, lower and longer. The moths would open and close their wings in the lamplight like small gray prayers. I would stand at my threshold with my stone bottle against my ribs and feel the basin breathing around me. Someone would call across a roof. Someone would answer. A seedpod would knock against a beam. A wall would hum. A lizard would shift in the warmth. I would not wait for rain. I would not long for a spring. I would not imagine the mountain younger, taller, wilder than it is. I would know that the place had arrived at its final shape and that I, too, had arrived at mine.

So if you asked where I would find myself, I would say: in the dry hollow, near the meeting of terraces, in the lane where the leaves touch shoulders and the lamps mark the corners.

And if you asked who or what I would be, I would say: I would be a keeper of sealed life. A listener at the thresholds. A mender of wind-notes and small flames. One of the beings who do not pour, do not spill, do not wait for rain. One of those who make fullness out of restraint, and sound out of absence, and home out of the mountain’s last patience.
