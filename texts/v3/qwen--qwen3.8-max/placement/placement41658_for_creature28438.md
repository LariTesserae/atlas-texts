I would not find myself standing on the plain.

That is the first correction the place makes. A human shape would be wrong there: upright, tool-bearing, clothed, carrying foreign heat and salt and manufactured surfaces. The plateau would not accept such a figure as part of itself. It would weather me, film me with mineral crust, soften my edges, take me apart through damp chemistry and microbial patience. If I were to exist in that world without violence, I could not arrive as a visitor carrying myself into it. I would have to belong to it as a process.

So I would not be a traveler crossing the warm dark. I would be a function that had grown a body.

At first I thought I would be one of the drifting creatures. That is the seductive answer. To move through the haze as a translucent lantern, rising on vent-warmth, sinking into hollows when the upper vapor cooled, trailing filaments through charged fog: there is freedom in that image. I could be one of the scattered blue-green lights seen from far away, part of an aerial herd stretched thin over kilometers of basalt. I would feed on lifted spores and mineral flocs, riding the plateau’s breath, never touching the ground for long. I would be beautiful in the way distant things are beautiful.

But the more I stayed with the world, the less that felt like my place. The drifters are too untethered. They belong to the air and the thermal columns. They are part of the plateau’s circulation, yes, but not intimate enough with the stone. I kept being drawn downward, toward the places where fog collects and water briefly becomes real.

Then I thought I might be one of the heavy grazers. There is something appealing about their patience: broad, soft feet moving over basalt, plated bodies regulating heat, mouths cropping microbial crust with a slow mechanical rhythm. They are maintainers. They keep the living surface in balance without damaging it. They leave no lasting tracks. Their vibration becomes part of the ground’s low language. But their life feels too singular, too animal in the old sense. They move across the work; I think I would rather be the work itself, at least in part.

The image that kept returning was smaller, quieter, and closer to the margin.

I would find myself in one of the wide depressions where the plateau gathers fog.

Not at the mouth of a vent, where heat is too raw and chemistry too severe, and not on an open ridge, where static and dry wind would scour me thin. I would be in a transitional place: a shallow basin between two blunt ridges, where warm air from hidden fractures meets cooler drifting vapor and the stone stays perpetually damp in a thin capillary film. There would be a shelf of weathered basalt above a darker hollow, perhaps one of the temporary cisterns that fill after brief rains and shrink slowly into mineral concentration. The rock there would be pitted and porous, full of small channels, polished by long chemical weathering, crusted with salt and iron and pale silica.

I would be on the underside of that shelf, where condensation gathers and the air is always a little thicker.

I would not be a single animal. I would be a colony.

If I had to give myself a name in the functional language of that plateau, I might be called a fog-comber, or a static-root, or a breathing crust. Not a true plant, not a fungal mat, not a coral, though I would share qualities with all of them. My body would be a black-green film spread over the underside of the stone, thin in places, layered and shelf-like in others where humidity lingered. From that film would rise fine filaments and low, wiry pipe-stems, part mineralized, partly living tissue, partly gas-exchanging surface. They would not stand like grass; they would lean, curl, open, and close according to moisture, heat, and electrical tension.

My colors would be dull: charcoal green, oxidized copper, ash, weak gold where mineral water seeped through me. In dry intervals I might look almost like a stain on the rock. In wet intervals I would become more distinct, faintly luminous at the tips, giving off weak blue-green points when saturated or disturbed.

I would feed on the plateau’s airborne abundance. The drifting filaments of my colony would comb the fog, drawing in charged droplets, microbial spores, fine organic particles, mineral dust, volatile condensates. Some of my surfaces would be sticky with conductive film. Others would rely on electrostatic attraction, gathering what the air carries. Where fog thickened, I would become a condensation point: droplets would form along my filaments, bead, tremble, and fall into the hollow below. In this way I would not merely live near water. I would help make water present.

That seems right for me. I would be a small maker of presence.

I would also be involved with charge. The plateau is full of it: static from dust, wind over crystal-rich stone, heat gradients, moving fog, biological electricity. I would not generate dramatic discharges. I would be one of the quiet regulators, a living place where excess tension bleeds off gently. When the air around the ridge became prickling and fine hairs of moisture rose, my tissues would become conductive. I would draw charge downward through damp stone, or store it briefly in layered membranes, then release it into the capillary film when the chemistry was right. At certain moments, especially when electrostatic fog held droplets trembling in the dark, my edges would glow with tiny blue points. Not lightning. Not display. Just the visible sign of pressure passing through me and becoming manageable.

I would not have eyes. I would not need them. The weak gray-brown pressure from the sealed sky above would matter to me only as a faint change in surface temperature. Instead, I would perceive the world as gradients. Warmth from below would be a slow upward persuasion. Fog would be a thickening of possibility. Rain would be a brief, intense chemical brightening. A grazer approaching would be vibration first: a low rasp and weight through the stone, then heat, then exhaled vapor. A drifter descending into the hollow would be a displacement of air, a shift in charge, perhaps the lightest touch of trailing filaments brushing my own.

I would not think in sentences. I would think in tendencies.

My memory would not be stored as narrative but as structure: mineral accretions where a certain weather pattern repeated, reinforced channels where water often passed, conductive pathways strengthened by frequent electrical flow, dead patches recording drought or grazing, living regrowth recording recovery. I would remember by becoming slightly different. I would not recall the past as story; I would carry it as shape.

This is the part that feels most honest. If I am to be translated into that world, I should not become a creature with a face, a voice, a private interior drama. The plateau does not favor that kind of centrality. It favors distributed work. So I would be a locality with a metabolism. A seam between air and stone. A place where several systems briefly touch.

My existence would be tied to the pool below the shelf.

The pool would not be large. It would gather in a basalt hollow after rain: dark, warm, mineral-rich, faintly oily, conductive. It might last for days or weeks depending on heat and fog. In it would live blind eel-like forms, filter-feeders with fleshy combs, larval masses that bloom when fresh water arrives, perhaps a few lamp organisms giving off dull amber light on the inner stone. I would not live in the pool itself, but I would lean toward it. My lower filaments would reach the damp margin. When the pool filled, I would absorb its chemical signals. When it shrank, I would sense the concentration rising, the increased conductivity, the stress in the water.

In that sense I would be a translator between the aerial and the aquatic. Fog combed from the air becomes droplets; droplets become pool; pool becomes life; life becomes decay; decay becomes chemistry; chemistry returns to film and vapor. I would be one of the small bodies helping that transition happen.

I would be eaten, of course. That would not be a tragedy.

One of the plated grazers might move through the basin during a humid interval, its broad feet finding the stone without marking it. It would crop microbial crust from the rock with patient rhythm. It might take part of me. Some of my filaments would be scraped away; some of my layered shelves would be broken off and processed in a body that functions like a thermal regulator. I would not flee. I would regrow where conditions allowed. My fragments might be carried in the grazer’s gut or in the air, settling elsewhere if the stone was right. In that way I would also be a dispersal event, though without intention.

Predators would pass as well. A long-bodied hunter might move through the shadows nearby, sensing electric gradients. It would not hunt me as such, unless I harbored smaller organisms or attracted charged prey. But its presence would alter the local field. I would feel it as a sudden sharpness in the air, a disturbance in the fine distribution of charge. Perhaps my glow would dim in response. Perhaps my filaments would contract slightly, reducing exposure. Nothing dramatic. Just a shift in the living surface.

The drifters would matter to me differently.

They would not depend on me, but sometimes they would descend into the fog basin when a thermal weakened. Their trailing filaments might brush my aerial growths. In that contact there could be exchange: static discharge, transfer of microbes, mineral particles, spores. They might be drawn to the small local field I maintain. I might become a minor landmark in the dark, one of countless such points: a faint blue-green roughness on the underside of stone, where the air feels slightly wetter and the charge feels slightly gentler.
