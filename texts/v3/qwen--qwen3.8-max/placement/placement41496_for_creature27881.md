**The Fallow Note**

Their agriculture is therefore musical, and I would be the fallow.

Not the chord that calls the rain. Not the singer standing at the terrace edge with a throat full of partials. Not the tower breathing its long civic drone into the haze, and not the beetle whose shell rings like a struck bowl when the evening pulse arrives. I would be the unplanted quiet between those things: the prepared silence in which sound can root without choking the field.

In the Overtone Basin, silence is not empty. It is soil.

I would find myself low in the basin, in a district that has no name that can be spoken all at once. It lies between a black tower shaped like a tuning fork and a fresh escarpment whose pale face has not yet learned what weather will do to it. Below the tower there is a broad terrace of dark ceramic and young stone, crossed by narrow channels where water appears, hesitates, and then decides whether to become a thread, a mirror, or vapor. Beyond the terrace, a field of reed-like growths stands trembling in the warm air. Their hollow stems do not rustle. They listen.

The place I would choose — if choosing were possible in a landscape that answers before you know you have asked — is a hollow where the chorus thins.

Not dead silence. Never that. Dead silence here means malfunction, hunger, or a predator moving through the world wrapped in felt. My quiet would be shaped: a pocket of held breath with edges, rhythm, and purpose. The air there would feel a half-degree cooler, though perhaps only because sound slows in it. Condensation would bead on stone near me and fall more slowly, as if each droplet needed permission. Moss would grow in dense dark felts around the boundary of my presence, and the phosphorescence in the plants would dim slightly, not from fear, but from the way a room dims when someone is about to speak.

My shelter would be a membrane stretched across a frame of dark vine and cable, set apart from the nearest dwelling by enough distance for its resonance to remain clean. It would not look like a house so much as a drumhead left ajar. The material would be thin, almost living, faintly damp to the touch, and it would take on the color of whatever sound passed near it: green-black in ordinary calm, copper-bright when a tower chord leaned close, silvered and trembling before rain.

Inside, there would be no fire. The hearth would be a note.

But mine would not be the continuous low tone of the other Interval-Born. Mine would be a tone with a shaped absence at its center — a hum circling a rest. The absence would need tending. If I let it become ragged, the shelter would begin to lose cohesion. Moss would grow over it unevenly. The city would stop recognizing it as occupied. Rain might fall through it as though it were only a suggestion. So I would maintain the gap carefully, breathing into it, tapping my sternum now and then, letting the silence keep its architecture.

At first, when I imagined myself entering the Overtone Basin, I thought I would be a stranger.

I thought I would arrive human, or nearly so, with an untrained voice and a body too full of accidental noise. I imagined stepping onto the young stone and hearing my own breath amplified by the damp air, my footfalls answered by hollow places I could not see. I imagined my voice entering the basin like a dropped stone entering water. The towers would turn toward it in their slow mineral way. Insects would alter their stridulation. The reed-fields would carry the disturbance outward in shivers. Somewhere far off, a predator made of counter-frequency would lift its invisible head.

I thought I would spend my first days trying not to be heard.

But that image did not stay. The basin would not permit an outsider to remain merely an outsider for long. It would tune me.

A dropped stone, once it enters water, becomes part of the water. A voice, once answered by towers, becomes part of the chorus. My footsteps, if I survived long enough, would stop being interruptions and start being patterns. My breathing would stop being noise and start becoming interval. The basin would teach me that the problem is not sound. The problem is unmeasured sound. The problem is a note that does not know where it belongs.

So the image that finally remains is stranger.

I would not be the loud visitor. I would not be the rain-caller, the tower-tender, or the hunter who collapses delicate acoustic space. I would be a Rest-Maker.

Perhaps I would be one of the Interval-Born, though changed by my function. Perhaps I would be something between a person and a civic harmonic: a maintenance intelligence that has developed a body because some tasks require hands, and a heart because some silences must be kept out of affection rather than law.
