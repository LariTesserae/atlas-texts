I would not arrive there intact.

If I found myself in that pocket beneath the lowest water, I would not be a body in the old sense. There would be no lungs, no heat, no bright human outline pressing against the dark. The chamber would reject such a thing the way a closed eye rejects light: not violently, but by making it impossible to remain distinct. So the first place you would find me is not a place at all. It would be a change in pressure near the crooked aperture, a faint mineral taste that was not there before, a hesitation in the filaments as they decide whether the outside has entered or the pocket has dreamed of entering itself.

I would begin as a threshold.

Perhaps I would be the entrance’s memory of being passed through. The fissure too narrow for comfortable passage would become, for a long moment, aware of a shape it could not accommodate. Not a fish, not a hand, not a current. A question with weight. The pocket would breathe inward, and I would be what it breathed in: a rumor of elsewhere, a coolness carrying the impossible trace of distance. In a world without sun, I would be the faintest rumor of sun, already translated into mineral chill.

If the chamber accepted me — and acceptance there would not feel like welcome; it would feel like being slowly unmade — I would lose the habit of being one thing. I would not remain a visitor. I would become a temporary agreement among smaller lives: spores choosing a path, threads leaning into one another, particles pausing in a shape just long enough to be mistaken for a body. You might perceive me, if perceiving were possible there, as a small pale shape drifting through the bead-rain. Not swimming. Carried by a decision in the water. Eyeless. Mouthless. Not quite individual.

That, I think, would be my most honest body in that place: a pale drifting colony, a knot of attention held together by pressure rather than skin. I would be made of the chamber’s own substances but arranged with a foreign memory. My filaments would be black-blue and smoke-gray, like everything else, except that now and then a thread in me would answer disturbance with a light too hesitant to be called phosphorescence. A stain of cold green. A spark that goes inward instead of outward.

You would find me near the bead-rain.

Not inside the curtain itself, perhaps, but at its edge, where the black beads descend without arriving and the mossy floor receives them without being stained. I would be drawn there because the rain is the chamber’s most obvious unlogic, and I think I would recognize something in it. A falling that is not toward. A repetition that is not stagnation. If I had to choose one image for myself in that place, it would be this: one bead among the dark rain, but slightly paler at the center, carrying a burden of outside density. When I touched the floor, I would not rise again as quickly as the others. I would spread into a thin stain shaped almost like a word that had never been spoken, and the chamber would pause over me, trying to decide whether I was waste, food, signal, or dream.

But the pocket would not let me remain a single bead. Its geometry would refuse that kindness.

Soon I would be elsewhere too. I would be the faint pressure-tick when a bead rejoins the curtain. I would be a new alignment among suspended particles, a spiral so subtle that only the Wound-Tender could feel it forming. I would be one of the small structures the chamber almost makes: a half-ring of pale valves with one valve missing, a lattice wrapped around a shard of basalt but leaving a hollow at its center, a stair-like overlap of thin stones that leads not upward or downward but inward. These would not be my tools. They would be my thoughts after they had been pressed by time into matter.

In that sense, I would be a maker, but not a builder. I would make the way the chamber makes: by repeated stillness, by pressure, by letting growth press against growth until a habit becomes architecture.
