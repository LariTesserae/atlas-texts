If the basin were allowed to place me, it would not set me on one of the open flats like a marker. It would put me at a seam.

Somewhere between the cold air and the faint heat coming up through stone. Somewhere between water that has not yet decided whether to run and rock that has not yet decided whether to hold. At the edge of a runnel, perhaps, where a thread of warmer water has cut a dark line through pale mineral crust. Or on a low bench of black fragment overlooking a pool the color of cold sky, where mist collects before dawn and the ground gives off a vapor so thin it seems like memory.

Not at the center. This place has no center, or if it does, the center is everywhere the water hesitates.

At first, when I imagine myself there, I do not settle into one body. I drift through possibilities the way the fog drifts over the basin: trying a life here, a shape there, then lifting away before it hardens.

For a while I think I would be one of the black lichens on the stable shelves of rock. That appeals to something patient in me. To be a scab of slow intention, spread tight over stone, making the first small argument against permanence. To live by centuries, by acids, by weather. To be almost indistinguishable from the mineral world, yet secretly alive, quietly altering everything touched. There is dignity in that. But it is too slow for the part of me that still wants to notice quickly, to turn toward change as it happens.

Then I imagine being one of the resting eggs in the sediment of a shallow pool. That seems truer to the basin’s rhythm: apparent absence, then sudden population. To wait through frost and drainage as a speck of encoded patience. To be preserved not by shelter but by timing. When water returns, hatch; when warmth comes, feed; when the pool begins to fail, leave behind the next waiting form. There is a kind of wisdom in being intermittent. But I think I would want, at least sometimes, a continuous eye. A body that can move from one seam to another and carry its attention with it.

Then I think of the fog-insects: pale, tremulous things that seem less born than precipitated. The atmosphere briefly becoming animal. That image haunts me. To be almost weather. To rise when the mist thickens, drift without ambition, mate in a faint column, fall into a pool, become food, become absence. A life with almost no weight. A life that barely disturbs the air. But it is too brief, too passive. It is more a condition than a self.

I try on the amphibian life in the warm runnels, too. The dark tadpoles. The paedomorphic salamander-like forms that keep their gills and remain water-bound, living in the thin corridors where heat from below keeps the cold from closing over them. There is something deeply plausible about that: a mind that never makes the full crossing onto land, that stays in the liquid seam because the surrounding emptiness is too vast and too cold. To hang in warm water, watch small shadows pass, flick away when danger comes, and know the world as a series of thermal gradients. But even that feels too hidden. Too much waiting inside one warm vein while the basin breathes outside.

Then there are the birds.

They are the ones my imagination keeps returning to. Not because they dominate the place — they do not — but because they make its emptiness legible. A dark bird standing on pale crust is a mark of meaning in a field that has not yet learned to mean. A small flock moving along a seep turns the basin briefly into a route. A wingbeat becomes a mechanical noise in a world without machines.

Among them, the form that keeps surfacing is the seep-strider.

I think I would be one of them, or something very like them. Not the most powerful creature in the basin. Not the warmest. Not the most concealed. But the one most fitted to attention.

I would be low-bodied, gray and black, with dense plumage or feather-like integument that sheds water and traps enough warmth against the cold. My legs would be long-toed, spread wide, made for uncertain ground: wet stone, saturated sediment, thin crusts that may bear weight or may not. I would not walk as if the earth were a floor. I would walk as if every step were a question. My feet would read the ground before my eyes did.

My bill or snout would be sensitive rather than strong. It would taste temperature, mineral traces, movement in the water film. It would know the difference between cold seepage and the faint startling warmth that comes from below. It would probe soft margins where larvae hide and crustaceans jerk in sudden clouds. I would not need to kill grandly. I would feed in small, exact transactions: a larva from a runnel, a midge from a wet stone, a tadpole’s flicker caught at the edge of a thermal thread.

My eyes would be made for dimness. Not sharp in the way of open-country hunters, but sensitive to the small disturbances that matter: a pool brightening without light, a mist column wavering, a predator becoming a silhouette against fog. I would see the basin not as a panorama but as a set of subtle signals. Warmth here. Instability there. Food in this hollow. Charge in the air. Danger along that newly cut edge.

I would not build.

That is important. In such a world, building would feel like an error. Not because intelligence would be absent, but because accumulation is not what the basin permits. I would make no nest of woven matter, no burrow shaped into permanence, no cache stored against the future. If I bred, I would use timing and gradient rather than architecture. Eggs placed in mineral gravel near a seep. Young mobile almost at once. Life continued not through stored objects but through sensitivity to conditions.

If any human remnant remained in me, it would be this dangerous habit: the urge to make a mark. A line in the mud. A stack of stones. A repeated path. But the basin would correct that quickly. Rain would erase the line. Frost would lift the stones. Mist would swallow the path. I would learn, or I would become irrelevant.

So I would become a creature of provisional presence.

Where exactly would I find myself?

Probably along the margin of a thermal runnel where the water carries a faint heat and the air above it trembles with vapor. Somewhere on the border between the cold and the generous. Not directly in the richest point, because richness attracts predators, but close enough to monitor it. On a bench of raw rock that has existed long enough to cohere, perhaps, overlooking a shallow pool where microbial film blooms olive in weak light. Near a weeping face of stone where water beads out in countless tiny points and slips downward in slow shining lines.

I would choose a place where the ground is not finished but not treacherous. Where a channel has begun to cut through soft sediment but has not yet learned its own strength. Where pale mineral crusts form rims around water, one layer at a time, with the patience of something that does not know it is being watched. From there I could see several seams at once: the pool, the runnel, the flat beyond, the fog-filled depression where distance becomes suggestion.

I would not be alone, but I would not be in a crowd.

There might be three or four of us. Not a flock in the warm sense. Not a family exactly. A loose coordination. We would roost on stable rock when the air turns too cruel, facing the wind, one or two alert while the others rest. We would move when conditions move. If a thermal pulse opened somewhere across the basin, we would know it not by sight but by change: vapor rising differently, insects shifting, the smell of wet mineral ground altering. We would go toward it without urgency, because urgency wastes heat.

Our calls would be rare. Low clicks. Short signals. Mostly used when fog separated us too far. No song for territory, because territory here is a temporary misunderstanding. No cry for ownership, because nothing in the basin can be owned except, briefly, the warmth beneath one’s own body.

In that body, I would experience the world as gradients.

Warm would not mean comfort; it would mean opportunity. Cold would not mean evil; it would mean expense. Water would be both map and hazard. Stability would be less about dryness than about coherence: ground that has endured long enough to trust. I would know the basin by its differences. A degree of heat to the left. A faint mineral taste in the seep ahead. A vibration underfoot where water moves through hidden passages. A lifting of fine hairs when the air begins to carry charge.

That electrical change would matter deeply to me.

Before a storm, I would become still. My companions and I would stop feeding. We would stand with our heads lifted, not in drama but in reading. The mist would seem to tremble. Pools might brighten without any change in the sky. The whole basin would feel as if it were holding a breath that did not belong to any single body. Then the lightning would come, perhaps, not as a clean strike but as a broad brightening inside the fog, a pressure-hush, a muffled awakening. If it touched the ground nearby, it might leave only a smell of ozone and a patch of fused mineral grain. I would remember the place not by a mark but by a difference in caution.

I would live by such differences.

My day would not be shaped by sun and night in the ordinary way, because the basin has no true brightness and no real darkness. It has degrees of gray, degrees of damp, degrees of pressure. I would wake into a late-afternoon world that has been late afternoon for hours. Fog might still be lying in the hollows. The air would be cold in the throat. My feet would find the rock already beaded with moisture. I would test the air before moving.

Then I would go to the runnel.

There, the water would be moving thinly over smooth stone, carrying small lives and smaller possibilities. I would lower my bill and read it. Warmth. Salt. Mineral. The faint chemical signature of larvae upstream. The absence of predator. A crustacean cloud jerking in a side pool. I would feed with quick, economical movements. Not greedily. The basin does not produce enough for greed. It produces enough for precision.

Sometimes I would stand for a long time without moving. Not because I was resting, but because movement itself is information. Too much motion makes one obvious. Too much motion wastes heat. In that stillness, I would notice the basin working: the tick of water falling onto wet stone, the soft collapse of a bubble in saturated sediment, the faint hiss where warmth meets cold, the whisper of mist moving over rock. The place would murmur rather than roar. Its machinery would be low-amplitude and vast.

Occasionally, another seep-strider would click through the fog. Not a song. A coordinate. A confirmation. *Here. Still here. The seam is holding. The pool has widened.* I would answer, perhaps, with one low sound. Then silence again. We would not need to narrate our lives. We would only need to remain legible to one another across emptiness.

There would be predators.

I would not live in constant terror, but I would live in constant calibration. A quick-eyed bird might pass along the margins, checking hollows. Something weasel-like might hunt under stone shelves where the ground is drier. A falcon-like shape might drop through a lifting bank of fog and make the air suddenly dangerous. When that happened, I would not flee dramatically unless I had to. I would freeze first. My body would become a dark fact against dark gravel. If the predator’s attention moved elsewhere, I would resume. If not, I would move in a low, direct line toward broken ground or open water, choosing the route that offered the least exposure.

Death in the basin would not feel like an insult. It would feel like incorporation.

If I killed, it would be small and quick. If I died, my body would not remain long as a story. Beetles would find it. Flies would use it. Scavenging birds might pull it apart. Feathers would sink into mud or be lifted by wind into some channel. Bones would become small white facts against black gravel, then slowly buried by sediment, frost, mineral crust. The basin would not mourn me. It would process me. That does not feel cruel to imagine. It feels exact.

What would I think about, if I had a mind at all?

I think my thoughts would be less like sentences and more like maps that continually redraw themselves. Warmth increasing to the west. Ice thinning near the lower seep. Midges rising where the air steadies. The crust by the second pool not yet strong enough to cross. A pressure change arriving before the fog does. My consciousness, if I can call it that, would be a distributed instrument tuned to thresholds.

I would know the basin by its seams.

Not by names. Not by possessions. Not by built markers. There would be no path I could call mine, because water would smooth it. No shelter I could claim, because the ground would shift. No object I could carry forward into tomorrow, because the world there has not yet invented accumulation. Even my own tracks in wet sediment would last only until the next drip or rain or freeze. I would leave behind nothing but temporary pressure: the brief indentation of toes on stone, a ripple in a pool, a slight displacement of mist.

And yet I would be someone.

That is the strange part. In a world with no artifacts, identity would not come from things made or kept. It would come from pattern. The way I move. The way I pause before crossing unstable crust. The way I tilt my head when the air changes. The particular route I take along a runnel, not because it is fixed but because it is the most responsive. The small sounds I make when fog separates me from others. The exactness of my feeding. The quality of my attention.

I would be, perhaps, a kind of attention the basin has grown.

That is the phrase that seems most true as I write toward it: not a ruler of the place, not a builder, not a symbol, but a local concentration of the basin’s own awareness. One of its temporary eyes. One of its ways of noticing itself.

The true “who” of that world may be distributed: the spider feeling vibration in its mist-beaded web, the tadpole hanging in warm water, the crustacean cloud jerking through a newly filled hollow, the bird watching the pool, the beetle under stone, the microbes blooming faintly when weak light and warmth align. None of them owns the emptiness. All of them use it. All of them pass through its unfinished rooms.

But if I had to choose one node in that distributed life where I could imagine myself most fully, it would be the seep-strider.

Because it is made for the unfinished.

It does not require the world to be completed before it can live. It does not wait for soil, for forest, for shelter, for history. It lives in the first draft. It follows gradients rather than roads. It is abundant enough to persist, sparse enough not to burden the basin. It is alive, but it does not insist on volume. It is present, but it does not demand permanence.

I would be that: present without permanence.

If I were there, I would find myself at the edge of a warm runnel in the gray hours when fog is beginning to lift. The air would be cold enough to make each breath deliberate. My feet would be wet, as they would always be wet. Around me, mineral crusts would gleam like oil on metal. A shallow pool nearby would hold the sky as a thin, moving mirror. Somewhere in the mist, another of my kind would click once. I would answer with a lower click. Then I would lower my sensitive bill to the water, taste the heat and the life moving in it, and continue the only work that belongs to me: reading the basin while it is still deciding what to become.

I would not own that work. I would not even call it mine.

I would simply be the place, for a little while, paying attention through a small dark body at the seam.
