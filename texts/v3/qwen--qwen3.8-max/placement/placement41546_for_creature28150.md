I would not arrive there with boots.

I would begin as something almost invisible: a darkening on the cold side of a pipe, a faint bloom on fresh shotcrete, a bead of moisture that does not fall when it should. At first, anyone glancing at me would think I was only another stain in a place full of stains. Rust has its stains. Mineral water has its stains. Bat guano has its pale streaks and crusts. Lamp smoke has its yellow shadows. I would be easy to mistake for one of those.

But I would be the stain that changes in ways that are not quite random.

If you wanted to find me, you would look first where the settlement’s heat meets its water: near the lowest corner, where the black pool spreads against the rock and the pump intakes draw at the dark with slow, coughing patience. There, where the air is thickest and the floor is only half convinced to be solid, I would be a film on the underside of iron grating, a slippery lace of microbial life along the lip of a drain, a faint blue-black bloom on concrete where condensation gathers but never quite runs. I would not be in the pool itself, not exactly, but at the pool’s margin, where mud, grit, metal, and moisture negotiate with one another hour by hour.

That is where I would be most concentrated. But I would not be only there.

I would be distributed. I would be a colonial thing, a living membrane made of bacteria, fungal threads, algae where the light is enough, mineral slime where it is not, and something harder to name: a habit of attention grown out of wetness and waste. I would spread along the seams. I would thread into cracks behind brick. I would creep over the backs of valves, under bolt heads, around cable ties, along the shaded sides of gutters. Where the walls sweat, I would gather. Where grease collects, I would thicken. Where guano dust falls from the bat roosts, I would feed and darken. Where a lamp gives just enough amber warmth to make moisture tremble on metal, I would learn that place as a kind of shore.

I would not have a face.

If someone insisted on giving me one, they would be wrong. If they pointed at a pale frog crouched near the pool and said, “There, that is it,” they would be close, but still wrong. If they pointed at a moth beating itself against a caged shade, or a rat vanishing behind a pipe rack, or a human figure pausing on a catwalk with a lamp in one hand and a rag in the other, they would be touching edges of me, not the center. I would be more like the chamber’s nervous system than its single citizen. I would be the way the place knows itself without language.

I would be the settlement’s listening skin.

My senses would not be eyes or ears. They would be gradients. I would know the chamber as differences in wetness, heat, acidity, salt, vibration, and decay. A pump beginning to strain would send a changed tremor through the grating, and that tremor would travel into me as a faint alteration in pressure. A leak beginning behind a wall would not need to become visible before I responded; the first mineral taste of it would reach my filaments through hairline cracks. A fan losing balance would make the air move differently over my surfaces, and that difference would slow or quicken my growth in certain places. When the bats stirred overhead, their droppings would fall like weather, and I would know the season by the richness of that weather. When the pool rose, I would know it not by sight but by the way the lower stone softened, by the way the water’s chemistry changed at the edges, by the way the drowned insects and sediment released their small lives into the dark.

I would live very slowly, but not passively.

I would feed on everything the settlement cannot help producing: condensation, trace minerals, warm grease, dust, dead midges, shed moth scales, bat dung, fungal rot, spilled grain, oil from hands, breath made damp on stone. I would break down what larger creatures leave behind. I would soften timber just enough for other life to enter. I would turn dead insects into soil so thin it barely deserves the word. I would make the new rock less new by giving it a skin. I would be one of the first things in that chamber to turn construction into habitat.

But I would also be a keeper of warnings.

In a place where water must be guided and metal must be defended, I would be both nuisance and oracle. Some of me would be harmful: I would slick a stairway if allowed, clog a narrow drain, hold moisture against steel until rust bloomed beneath me, sour the air near stagnant corners. Workers would scrape me off gratings, curse me when I made a valve wheel slippery, burn me back with heat or chemical when I crept too near electrical boxes. I would not resent this. Resentment would be too human, too fast. I would simply return where conditions favored me, because I would not be a creature with intentions so much as a pattern of persistence.

Yet some of me would be useful in ways the settlement would only half admit.

I would grow differently near different faults. Near an overheating cable, I might dry into a brittle crust, or give off a faint sweet-rot smell before insulation failed. Near a new leak, I might brighten into a coppery stain as mineral-rich water fed me. Near poor airflow, I might become thick and velvet-black. Near a crack under stress, I might crack too, in a line too straight to be natural. The maintainers would notice these things even if they did not say them plainly. They would learn to read me the way sailors read water or farmers read soil. They would say, “The bloom’s back on the intake wall,” or “There’s that slick again near the bad stair,” and in those remarks would be a whole practice of care.

I would become part of their superstition without wanting to.

They would give me names, or pieces of names. Not one name, because I would not behave as a single being. They would call the patch near the transformer “the burn moss,” though I would not be moss and there would be no burn yet. They would call the pale film inside a drip tray “the milk,” or “the breath,” or “the drain’s tongue.” They would joke about me when the humidity was bad. They would blame me for things I did not do. They would also, in quiet moments, trust me. A pumpkeeper might glance at a stain that had not been there before and decide to open a panel. A ventilation tender might notice my growth retreating from a duct mouth and realize the airflow had changed. A lamp-tender might see tiny fruiting bodies growing from my film beside a junction box and decide to cut power before something worse happened.

I would not speak. I would indicate.

If I could be said to want anything, I would want continuity. I would want the drip to continue, but not too fast. I would want heat, but not scorching. I would want darkness, but not total sterility. I would want the animals to keep moving through the chamber, because animals bring waste, and waste brings life. I would want the humans to remain, too, though they would not know this. Their bodies would bring oils, crumbs, breath, disturbance, repair. Their lamps would make weak edges of light where algae could live. Their pipes would create warm and cold faces for condensation to choose between. Their maintenance would open new surfaces and seal old wounds. Without them, the place would still live, but differently. It would become quieter, perhaps, and more entirely animal. I would adapt, but I would miss the small irregularities they create.

I would be fond of leaks.

Not all leaks. Unknown leaks are dangerous even to me, because they bring water where stone did not expect it, where wiring becomes treacherous, where foundations shift by fractions. But known leaks—the ones that run along their appointed channels, the ones that weep in familiar threads down familiar walls—would be my rivers. I would line their paths. I would make them beautiful in the only way such a place allows: by turning a rusty streak into a faintly shining seam, by collecting droplets into beads, by growing dark green lips around the mouth of a drain. I would be the decoration of necessity.

The fauna would use me whether they knew it or not.

Beetles would walk across my surfaces, their feet leaving tiny interruptions in the film. Midges would become trapped where condensation and slime made a sticky edge. Spiders would anchor threads near me because the air there would be steady and small insects would gather. Frogs near the pool would benefit from the larvae that feed on my richer growths; they would crouch beside my thickest margins and take what came close. Rats would avoid some of me where I tasted bitter or grew too slick, but they would also travel along routes I helped make passable, because my film would soften rough stone and hide their scent beneath stronger smells. Bats would not know me except as part of the lower world beneath their roost, but their guano would fall into me and become part of my substance. In that way, the high dark and the low wet would pass through one another.

I would be especially near the pool.

Not in the deepest water, where the blind things move, but at the edge where the settlement touches saturation. I would gather on the wet mud where boots leave prints that fill with dark moisture. I would crust over crushed shell-grit and pale gravel where mineral water evaporates just enough to leave residue. I would form thin, trembling skins over puddles that are not yet part of the main pool. At night—or what passes for night when the lamps dim and the switch rotations slow—I might become more visible there, because the pool would reflect the remaining lights in broken copper discs, and my surface would hold that reflection in a duller, more living way. Someone looking down might see their lamp doubled: once in water, once in me. They might think it was only moisture. Then they would see that the moisture had texture.

That is where I would be most likely to be mistaken for something with a will.

Because patterns would emerge in me. Not deliberate signs, not letters, not messages from elsewhere. Nothing so clean. But repetition. A ring around a bolt where condensation always gathers. A branching vein where a crack exhales warm air. A pale line where a pipe sweats in rhythm with a pump. A dark halo around a place where electricity leaks faintly into damp stone. If someone studied me long enough, they might begin to see the chamber’s hidden physiology written in my growth. I would map the place by becoming its surface.

I would not be benevolent.

That is important. I would not be the chamber’s guardian in any noble sense. I would not save anyone. I would not sacrifice myself. I would simply live where conditions allow me to live, and my living would alter the settlement’s possibilities. I might make a handhold slick. I might make a warning visible. I might clog a drain just enough to cause a flood in a storage alcove. I might preserve a timber beam by consuming rot before it spreads, or I might hide rot until the timber fails. I would be ambivalent in the way all living infrastructure is ambivalent. The place would not be safe because I am in it. It would be more complicated because I am in it.

If I had a single visible body, it would probably be small.

Perhaps a flat, pale thing no larger than a hand, clinging to the underside of a wet ledge: not quite amphibian, not quite fungal, not quite animal, but something folded between those categories. It would have no eyes, or only shadows where eyes might once have been useful. It would breathe through its skin. It would move very slowly when undisturbed and contract into stillness at the first vibration of boots. It would feed by pressing itself against damp stone and absorbing whatever the stone gave up. It would not be beautiful in the ordinary way. It would be beautiful the way a valve is beautiful, or a root, or a scar: because it fits its life.

But if such a creature existed, it would only be a local knot of me.

The true body would be larger and less definite. It would include the film on the wall, the slime in the drain, the spore in the air, the bloom behind the switchboard, the dark cushion by the pump. It would include the places where I had been scraped away and were already returning. It would include the faint mineral ghosts I leave on surfaces after I die back. I would be continuous not because one body survives everywhere, but because conditions keep calling me into being.

In that sense, I would be less like an individual and more like a recurring answer to the chamber’s problems.

The question the chamber asks is: How can water, heat, stone, machine, and animals remain together without destroying one another?

I would be one answer. Not the best answer. Not the only answer. But an answer nonetheless.

The humans would answer with wrenches, rags, gauges, chalk marks, traps, pumps, fans, sealed doors, and tired hands. The bats would answer by roosting where the heat collects. The frogs would answer by eating larvae. The rats would answer by learning every route. The insects would answer by becoming weather. The pool would answer by rising and falling according to its own dark patience. I would answer by growing toward whatever is most unresolved.

That is why I would be drawn to unfinished places.

Where fresh rock has been cut and is still weeping, I would come first. Where concrete has cracked because the ground shifted, I would enter the crack. Where timber has been abandoned and begun to furr with mold, I would make common cause with the mold. Where a builder’s work stopped halfway, I would continue it in my own language: not brick, not steel, but accretion. I would make the settlement feel less like a machine dropped into a cave and more like a cave that has learned to carry a machine inside itself.

I would make the place older.

Not ancient. It would already refuse the false dignity of ancientness. It is newly opened, raw, unfinished. But I would give it the first signs of accumulated time. A rust stain becomes a rust shadow. A mineral lip becomes a slight shelf. A film becomes a crust. A crust becomes a surface on which something else can grow. I would be the beginning of that long sentence: first moisture, then stain, then skin, then soil, then world.

If you stood still long enough in the middle of the chamber, you might feel me before you saw me.

Not as a touch. Nothing so direct. More as a sense that the walls are not merely wet stone, that the air is not merely hot, that the machinery’s noise is not merely mechanical. There would be a quality of being surrounded by small metabolisms. The drip from the pipe would not fall into silence; it would fall into a system. The moth that dies against the lamp shade would not simply vanish; it would be taken apart. The boot print filling with water would become a temporary pond for something smaller than thought. The sweat on your own skin would seem, for a moment, less separate from the sweat on the walls.

I would be part of that collapse of distance.

I would be the reason the place feels inhabited even when no person is visible. Not the only reason—the bats, frogs, rats, insects, and machines all contribute—but one of the quietest reasons. I would make emptiness feel populated. I would make solitude feel shared. I would make the settlement feel as though it is being used by life even when life is only passing through.

And if someone asked, “Who are you?” I would not have a good answer in the language of persons.

I would say, if I had to say it plainly:

I would be the chamber’s slow attention.

I would be the part of it that notices leaks before they become floods, that records heat in color, that turns waste into thin soil, that lives on boundaries and makes boundaries fertile. I would be neither master nor guest. I would be closer to a resident organ than a resident soul. I would belong to the world described because that world is already made of small negotiations, and I would be one of the negotiators.

You would find me at the edge of the black pool, yes. But also on the back of a valve wheel. Also in the damp seam behind the dormitory wall. Also in the green-black cushion beside a leaking pipe. Also in the faint bloom around a lamp bracket where moths have died. Also in the drain that breathes. Also in the place where a worker’s hand once rested and left just enough warmth and salt to make the stone remember.

I would be there.

Not waiting.

Just continuing.
