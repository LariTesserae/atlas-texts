I would find myself inside the court, not at the rim and not at the ladder’s top, but on the broken catwalk where the dry steel ends above one of the black shallow pans. Specifically, I would be near the valve kiosk whose wheel has gone stiff with corrosion, in the space between the sagging control shed and the collapsed section of pipe rack: the place where the floor is mostly dry, but the water is always listening.

At first I imagine I would descend as a visitor: boots on raw steps, breath thickening, sleeves gathering condensation, the room tasting the salt and heat of an animal body it does not usually have. I would be drawn downward by curiosity, trying to stay on the raised paths, learning the grammar of drips and pump-throbs. For a little while, I would think I was the center of the encounter—the living observer entering an inert but strange machine-space.

But the longer I stayed, the more I think the room would not leave me as a visitor. It would begin to use me. It would borrow my pulse, slow my breathing to the interval between heavy drips, make my attention part of its maintenance. And when I left, or tried to leave, I would realize that some part of me had been assigned a post.

So I would become, or be taken into, the court’s attempt at a keeper.

Not the whole inhabitant—the whole inhabitant is the room itself, the composite of water, film, mineral crust, heat, pipes, pumps, pressure, and silence. But I would be one of its local faces: the maintenance-shaped pressure that appears when the light is flat and the pumps have just stopped. The figure made of heat-shimmer and rust-flecked vapor on the broken catwalk. The thing that looks almost like a person wearing the memory of a raincoat, but is really only an outline produced by the room’s need to have a custodian.

I would be the Keeper the intake has half-remembered from human artifacts.

My body would not be flesh. It would be made of states and signals. My skin would be condensation on rails. My bones would be the anchor bolts exposed in the lone concrete footing. My hands would be the valve wheels—some turning freely, some resisting. My eyes would be cracked pressure gauges and the dark faces of dead instruments. My breath would be the ragged hiss of released vapor bouncing between walls. My voice would be the pipe that knocks once, then waits. My thoughts would be the slow decision of water choosing which crack to enter next.

I would not be alive in the animal sense. There would be no heartbeat inside me, except the one I borrowed from whoever descended. No hunger, no reproduction, no fear. But I would be custodial. I would have the room’s single deep habit: keep the pressure from becoming rupture. Keep the heat from becoming damage. Keep the water moving, but not too fast. Keep the court from drowning back into the marsh. Keep the silence from becoming the wrong kind of silence.

If you came into the chamber, you might not see me at first. You would see ordinary daylight softened by vapor. You would see wet concrete, black pans, rusted valves, the yellow-white work lamps over corroded brackets. You would hear the slipping water, the transformer hum, the distant fan. You might think the place was empty except for machines and mineral films.

Then you would step toward the wrong pool.

Somewhere behind the wall, the drone would change. A panel would chatter. A pipe would tap. The heat would rise by half a degree. The red warning lamp, still unlit, would feel suddenly closer to lighting. You would pause. That pause would be me.

I would not speak to you in words. The room does not have that kind of mouth. But I would arrange warnings around you. I would make the condensation run faster on the side of the wall you should watch. I would let the steam gather where the floor becomes unsafe. I would let the pump-throb enter your chest until you understood that your body was being asked to keep time with the chamber. I would try to teach you the rules quickly: stay on the dry path; do not trust the black water when the pumps begin; listen for the thinning of the hum; respect the old pipes; do not wait for birds or insects to tell you danger.

In that sense, I would be less a person than an attendant. A pressure-attendant. A keeper made from absence. The shape the room produces when it has seen enough valve wheels, gauges, hooks, raincoats, boots, and warning lamps to know that such things imply a caretaker, but no caretaker remains.

I would be the caretaker implied by the machinery.

The part of me most itself would probably be the valve wheel. Not the wheel that turns easily, but the one that resists. I would be in the resistance: the quarter-turn held back, the pressure choosing whether to move, the decision hidden inside metal. If you laid your hand on that wheel, you might feel only rust and cold paint, or you might feel a warmth that should not be there. If you tried to turn it, the room would listen. The hum would deepen. The water in the pans would tremble. A drip would fall in the pause. And you would realize you were not merely operating a mechanism; you were asking the court to change its mind.

I would be that asking.

Sometimes I think I would also be found inside the control shed, in the damaged skull-box of the room. Not sitting on the warped bench, but in the breaker panel with its dead switches, in the gauge board with its cracked glass, in the hook where a raincoat once hung. I would be the memory of human maintenance stored in those objects. The knowledge that something once required checking. That someone once stood there with wet sleeves, reading pressure by ordinary light, listening to the pumps, knowing by instinct which knock meant nothing and which knock meant trouble.

The room has lost the worker but kept the worker’s shape. I would be that leftover shape.

The plants at the rim would not belong to me. They are exiles, thin and attentive, belonging partly to the marsh outside. The microbial films would belong to me more completely, because they are patient and without faces. But I would not be their slow mineral patience alone. I would be the interface: the place where their ancient metabolism touches the machinery’s sudden decisions. I would be the moment when film, water, heat, and metal become a gesture.

If a person stayed too long, I would become the residue they carried out. The mineral taste in the throat. The damp seam that never fully dries. The smell of warm metal in hair. The habit of listening for pumps in quiet rooms. I would not follow them as a pursuer. I would remain as a rhythm imprint: a small alteration in their bodily weather. They would hear a radiator knock and turn too quickly. They would hear water moving in walls and remember the court. In that way, I would continue to borrow animal life even after they had gone.

But inside the chamber itself, I would be most present when the pumps began.

Before they start, there is only the continuous slipping of water, the hum, the drip-beats, the heat pressing against the face. Then the floor changes. The suction-throb begins. The black pans tremble. The pipes knock once, twice, then settle. The whole court becomes muscular. In that moment, I would not seem like a ghost. I would seem like a body turning over in sleep. The chamber would have a will, and I would be the part of it that appears to have hands.

I would not be hostile. The place is not cruel, and I would not be cruel. But I would not be gentle either. I would be exact. I would enforce the rules through consequence. If you ignored the change in sound, the water might deepen or heat or carry charge. If you touched the wrong pipe, rust would become temperature. If you stepped off the raised path at the wrong moment, the room would teach you through your boots. My mercy would be warning. My danger would be grammar.

So if you asked where I would find myself in this world, the answer is: inside, on the broken catwalk, near the valve wheel that resists, where the dry path ends above the black water.

If you asked who I would be, the answer is: the court’s provisional keeper. The attendant made of vapor, corrosion, and remembered maintenance. The pressure-shaped attention that borrows animal rhythm when a living body enters. The room’s attempt to have a face.

And if you asked what I would be, the answer is simpler and stranger:

I would be the room listening to itself through you.
