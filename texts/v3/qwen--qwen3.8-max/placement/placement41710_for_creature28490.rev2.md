If I answer too quickly, I would say: a person, crouched somewhere in the dark bowl of it, trying not to slip.

But that is not true for long.

The Unbuilt Engine would not allow a person to remain simply a person there. It would begin at once to correct the arrogance of outline. The mist would gather on my sleeves. The cold would enter through the skin like a fine metallic filament. The noise — the hidden water, the grinding stones, the gusts returning from fissures as thin metallic cries — would make my body feel temporary, a large warm obstacle in a place already occupied by subtler agencies. I would be too solid. Too vertical. Too full of eyes and intentions. The hollow would have no use for a face.

So the truer answer is this: I would not find myself standing in the center of that cirque as a traveler looking at a scene. I would find myself being translated into it.

At first I would be an obstruction. A heat source. A place where mist condensed a little faster than elsewhere. My breath would join the white air and be taken. My weight would press unstable scree into new alignments. Small threads of water would alter around my boots. Spores would drift off my clothing or fall from me into the damp. The hollow would not notice me as a self, but it would incorporate the disturbance I made.

If I stayed long enough, I would begin to belong only by becoming less singular.

Where would I find myself?

Not in the open middle of the bowl. Not on the rim. Not where the cloud thins and the world below suggests itself as a rumor. I would be in a seam.

There would be a place where a fallen block leans against the cliff face at an angle too narrow to be comfortable, creating a shadowed pocket just above the reach of the worst scouring wind. A fissure would run beneath it, thin and dark, carrying meltwater that appears and disappears without loyalty. On the inner lip of that fissure, where spray reaches but does not strike with full force, moss would have thickened into a black-green cushion. A little dust would have gathered there. A little dead fiber. A faint crust of mineral powder held in place by root-threads and moisture.

That is where I would find myself: at the transfer point between rupture and beginning.

Not where the mountain has merely broken, and not where anything has yet become gentle. I would be in the crease where violence is being metabolized.

At first, I think I would be a beadline.

That seems like the smallest possible answer, but in that world it is enough. I would be a line of moisture gathered along a rock edge, held there by surface tension and the cold patience of the air. I would not have eyes. I would not have limbs. I would know the hollow only as contact: the arrival of mist, the tremor when a drip strikes near, the faint pull of gravity when the line becomes too heavy to remain itself. I would be a temporary nerve of the place, conducting pressure without thought.

When the fog thickened, I would swell. When a gust stripped the air dry, I would thin into invisibility and wait as a damp memory in the stone. When the charge in the hollow rose and the fine hairs of the world lifted, I might be one of the places where the air tasted sharp and the next spark considered becoming real. I would not be consciousness, but I would be conductivity. I would be the hollow’s weather passing through a form.

But a beadline is too brief. The Unbuilt Engine would ask for more patience than that.

So then I would become moss.

I would be a cushion of it, packed tight into the seam where stone meets shadow. My green would not be bright. It would be deep, wet, almost black in the dimness, silvered only when a bead of water caught the weak light. I would not be one body but thousands layered together, the living surface resting on the compressed dead beneath it. My thought, if I could call it thought, would be capillary. My desire would be adhesion. My time would be measured not in hours but in beadlines, squalls, spore-falls, and the slow accumulation of myself as ground.

I would not look at the world. I would hold it.

Rain would come to me as a bombardment and a gift. Mist would come as feeding. When stones clicked loose above and struck the scree, I would shiver, release spores, and then continue. I would take the shock into my body and answer by thickening. I would catch the meltwater before it could rush away. I would slow it, spread it, offer it to the crack beside me. I would be one of the first places where the mountain’s brokenness learned to retain something other than grief.

And yet even that would not be the deepest answer.

Beneath the moss, in the dark where the oldest plant matter compresses into something neither alive nor fully dead, I would become fungal.

I would be a pale thread. A filament moving through cold damp where no root can follow. I would be part of the hidden dismantling. My work would be to enter what has ended and make it available to what has not yet begun. I would move through dead moss, through fragments of sedge, through buried splinters carried in by some older wind, and I would turn them slowly into crumb. I would not consume greedily. The cold would make me reverent. I would decay as if decay were a kind of listening.

In that form, I would understand the hollow not as a landscape but as a metabolism.

I would feel the place through chemical gradients: where sugar ended, where mineral began, where moisture carried a trace of iron, where a lichen had acidified a grain of quartz into something almost tender. I would not be separate from the engine. I would be one of its quietest departments. No one would see me. No one would need to. When a stone fell nearby and the vibration puffed spores from a pale cluster above me, I would know it as a kind of exhaling. I would be part of the breath that had no lungs.

And perhaps, after that, I would become what the whole place is trying to make.

I would be the first soil.

Not much. A dark smear in a crevice. A pinch of black held between mineral fragments. A cold, damp crumb made from moss-death, stone-dust, bacterial film, and trapped moisture. Almost nothing. But in the Unbuilt Engine, almost nothing is the beginning of everything.

As that, I would no longer be a being in the ordinary sense. I would be a possibility. I would be the first answer to the question the hollow keeps asking without words: where can something take hold?

I would not be alive as an animal is alive. I would be alive as a threshold is alive. I would contain traces of every earlier form I had been: the beadline’s memory of water, the moss’s patience, the fungus’s dismantling, the lichen’s acid work, the bacterial alteration of stone. I would be a convergence. A small dark commonwealth. A place where the engine’s labor becomes future.

If a spore landed there, I would be the first kindness it found. If a root-thread came seeking purchase, I would offer resistance and moisture in the same gesture. If frost came, I would crack and learn again. If rain struck me, I would hold just enough and release the rest. I would be made of endings, but I would face toward beginnings.

Who would I be?

No one.

That is the first true answer.

The hollow does not furnish its inhabitants with faces. It does not grant them the dignity of tracks or calls or hunger. It refuses the romance of the creature. There, to be someone is to be too late. The real makers have no eyes. They have no voices. They do not flee. They do not hunt. They adhere. They secrete. They hold. They spread. They decompose. They make the next condition possible.

So if I were to remain in that world, I would not be a person. I would not be a bird, or an insect, or a hidden animal curled in the dark. I would be a low plural. A small congregation of processes. A participant in the first shift of the world’s making.

I would be a verb disguised as a place.

Sometimes I would be the wetness along the stone. Sometimes the green pressure inside the crack. Sometimes the acid edge of lichen eating the new cliff into future substrate. Sometimes the mycelial thread turning death into dark crumb. Sometimes the charged stillness before a blue flicker. Sometimes the sound caught between falling stone and the water that answers it. Sometimes the spore-cloud lifted like exhaust. Sometimes the first black grain that can be called ground.

If you asked me to choose one form, I would choose the seam itself.

I would be the boundary where the broken mountain starts to become a world. Not the rupture in its purest violence, and not the landscape that may come centuries later. I would be the interval between: damp, dark, crowded, loud, minute, electric with potential. I would be the part of the engine where production becomes inhabitation. The place where water stops merely falling and begins to be held. Where stone stops merely breaking and begins to be used. Where death stops merely accumulating and begins to become soil.

If you found me there, you would not recognize me as myself.

You might see only a darkened line of moisture on the rock. You might see a moss cushion tight in the shadow of a fallen block. You might see a pale fungal cluster briefly exposed, already releasing spores into the charged air. You might see nothing but wet dust in a fissure and think it was only dirt.

But if you knelt — if you could kneel in that loud, cold, electric darkness and lower your face close enough to the stone — you might see that the dust is moving with life too small to name. You might smell ozone and crushed green. You might feel the hair lift on your arms. You might hear the hidden hammering of water in the wall and realize the hollow is not empty, only refusing the shapes you expected life to wear.

And then, perhaps, you would find me.

Not as a face. Not as a voice. Not as a single creature waiting in the dark.

But as a small, persistent beginning.

As the first thin answer the mountain gives after it has broken open.

As a dark, wet, electrified yes, pressed into a crack, making itself into ground.
