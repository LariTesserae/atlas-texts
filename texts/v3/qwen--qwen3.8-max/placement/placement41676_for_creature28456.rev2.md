I would not arrive there.

That is the first correction the place makes to the question. There is no coming into that country from somewhere else, no descent through a shaft with a lamp, no traveler’s body carrying the wrong heat and the wrong dampness into the dust. If I were there, I would already have been made by it. I would have been secreted into existence over long time, shaped by the same cold light, the same mineral patience, the same enormous sound. So the answer cannot be “I would find myself” in the sense of entering. It must be: I would have always been becoming part of it.

At first, when I let the question open, I think I would be a larva.

That image comes before the others: a soft, pale body inside a narrow gallery in a tower wall, blind and almost without shape, feeding on dust and old skin and fungal fiber, turning the world slowly into structure. I would be hidden in the architecture. I would not yet have limbs for the open avenues, nor the hard waxen surface needed for the dry air. I would live by touch and taste and vibration. The songs of the adults would come through the wall around me as pressure, as instruction, as something closer to weather than language. Where the sound thickened, I would add material. Where it thinned, I would hold back. I would not know that I was building. I would only be answering.

There is something appealing about that. To begin as part of the wall. To be so close to the city that there is no distinction between one’s body and the ongoing shape of the place. To have no selfhood except as a small local obedience to resonance.

But that image, while true, is not the whole answer. A larva is too enclosed, too embedded. The place also has openness: broad dust plains, sparse tower clusters, long intervals where sound travels without obstruction. There must be creatures who move through those intervals. There must be bodies that keep the distances from becoming either abandoned or overgrown. So the image shifts.

I would emerge.

I would come out of a sealed cell as an adult Builder: dry, flattened, hand-long, coated with a fine bloom of mineral dust. My body would be low to the ground, waxen, segmented, efficient in the way that all bodies there must be efficient. I would have no true eyes. At most there would be faint light-sensitive patches along my sides, enough to tell the difference between the blue-green glow of living fungal growth and the deeper dimness beyond it. I would not see the world as shapes and horizons. I would feel it as vibration, texture, chemical trace, temperature, and the subtle resistance of surfaces under my limbs.

I would carry nothing. That is important. No tool, no weapon, no stored object, no device. Whatever I could do would be done with my body and its secretions. My mouthparts could rasp, scrape, press, and clean. Glands in me would produce binding substances: dry silk, wax, cement that hardens in cool air. If I needed to repair a crack, I would not fetch a tool. I would become the repair in the only way possible: by adding what my body could give. If I needed to mark a path, I would leave a chemical trace. If I needed to call others, I would rasp or tap or press my thorax against a resonant surface and let the city carry the note.

I would not drink. I would not know drinking as a possibility. The water in me would be an interior fact, tightly held, not something sought in pools or streams. I would not thirst in the way surface creatures thirst. I would simply exist within the sealed economy of the place: moisture passed from tissue to tissue, bound in fungal flesh, preserved in the bodies of larvae, returned from the dead to the living as material, never exposed, never wasted. The idea of open water would be alien to me, if I had any idea at all. It would be a violation, not a comfort.

Where would I be?

Not at the center of the densest district. That is where the deepest songs are, where the towers are thickest, where larvae work in the walls and the sound is almost too heavy to endure. But I think I would not belong most fully there. The place is not only its towers. It is also the emptiness between them: the pale dust plains, the quiet stretches, the low fungal thickets, the worn carbonate terraces, the spaces where the city breathes. The description says the city is sparse, that built places feel like islands in a larger darkness. I think I would find myself in those intervals.

I would be one of the creatures who keep the gaps open.

Not a ruler, not a great builder, not one of the hidden fertile forms in deep chambers. I would be a small adult among many, but my function, if it can be called a function, would be tied to thresholds and distances. I would move along the avenues where the polished dust thins into rougher ground. I would cross the open flats between tower clusters, carrying spores on my back without intending to, leaving faint scent traces, listening to the way sound changes when there is too much growth, too much silence, too much predator-scent in the air.

There would be no grand task. There would only be continual adjustment.

In one place, a fungal mat might be spreading too far across a road. I would crop it, not because I have a plan, but because the texture and chemical signal invite cropping. In another place, a wall might have developed a hairline fracture, not dangerous yet, but wrong in resonance. I would pause there, antennae moving, reading the flaw. I might add a thin line of secretion. I might tap against the surface until the vibration summons larvae. I might simply lay a scent that says, in the grammar of the place: here, attend, slow, thicken.

In another place, the sound itself would thin. That would be the most urgent thing.

The country under stone is deafening, and a sudden silence there is more frightening than noise. If a patch of the Choir falls quiet, it means something has interrupted the pattern: predator movement, structural failure, chemical wrongness, the collapse of a fungal stem, an unfamiliar presence. I would not understand silence as peace. I would understand it as absence, as a hole in the living fabric. If I were near such a thinning, my body would respond before any decision could be named. I would move toward it. I would rasp a short calling note against stone. I would press myself to a tower wall and add my own thin vibration to the missing chord. Others would hear. The pattern would begin to close.

I would be, in that sense, a singer of gaps.

That phrase feels right, though it may make me sound more deliberate than I would be. I would not sing because I believe in preserving emptiness as a philosophy. I would sing because my body is tuned to the spaces between towers. I would be drawn to places where resonance needs reinforcement, where roads need clearing, where dust needs redistribution, where the balance between district and wilderness has become too tight or too loose. The Builders do not make art, but their world has a kind of acoustic spacing. Towers need intervals to resonate. Groves need open ground to expand slowly. Predators need room to hunt. The city needs its silences to be placed correctly, not random, not total. I would serve that spacing.

So if you looked for me, you would not find me first as a figure in a crowd. You would find me, if you could see there at all, as a small pale shape crossing a dust flat between two distant glows. Above me the ceiling would vanish into dimness. Behind me there might be a cluster of fluted towers, faintly blue-green along their lower edges, humming with larvae and adult song. Ahead there might be only a low rise of gypsum, worn smooth as old bone, and beyond it another sparse settlement, much farther away than it seems. The air would be cool and powder-dry. The sound would be enormous: a wall of stridulation and drone, felt in the plates of my body as much as heard.

I would not be lonely there. Loneliness would require a different kind of self. I would be surrounded by the Choir even when alone. The sound of the city would be inside me, shaping my movements, telling me where I was by what returned to me from stone and dust. My individuality would be slight. It would consist of small differences: a particular rhythm in my rasp, a chemical trace that marks where I have passed, a tendency to favor certain routes, a sensitivity to certain frequencies. I would not have a name. I would have a resonance.

That is perhaps the most honest answer: I would be a resonance.

Not a person standing in a landscape. Not a soul looking out through eyes. I would be a local intensification in a collective body, a place where the Choir becomes briefly aware of a gap and moves to mend it. The “I” would be like a ripple moving across a vast dry instrument. It would rise when needed, pass through action, and flatten again into the greater pattern.

I think I would be smaller than I imagine at first.

The fantasy of being significant is hard to surrender, but that world does not need my significance. It needs participation. If I were there, I would not be a hero. I would not found anything. I would not discover fire, or dig water, or invent a tool, or impose order on chaos. Those acts would be impossible or obscene there. I would be one of countless bodies whose lives add up to persistence. My importance would be real but microscopic: a cleaned passage, a carried spore, a repaired crack, a maintained note, a chemical trace that guides others safely across an empty stretch.

There might be moments when something like wonder occurs, though not as human wonder.

Once, perhaps, I would cross a plain during a slow shift in the air currents. The breathing passages of the stone would have changed over many days, and the sound from one district would arrive differently through the towers. The whole acoustic field would alter. I would stop. My antennae would lift. The light-sensitive patches along my body would register a faint increase in blue-green glow from a nearby fungal ridge, not because dawn had come—there is no dawn there—but because living growth had thickened in response to some deep thermal change. Other Builders would pause too. Beetle columns would slow. Moths would shift on the caps. The city would listen to itself.

Then, slowly, the song would change. Not all at once. Not by command. One district would answer another. A tower would catch a new frequency and amplify it. Larvae in the walls would begin adding material where the altered vibration guided them. I would add my own note, perhaps for hours, perhaps only for minutes, before moving on. Later, that change would be remembered not as an event but as a layer in a wall, a slight alteration in the shape of a vent, a new harmonic in an old drone.

That would be history there. Not a story told, but a structure accrued.

I would also know predation, but not as evil.

There would be places where the dust feels wrong, where scent sharpens and the air carries the dry, quick signature of grasping bodies. I would not be brave. I would not be tragic. I would alter my route, produce warning clicks, move closer to others if possible. If a predator took me, that too would enter the balance. My body would not be wasted. Parts of me would feed something else. My plates might become fragments in a nursery floor. My chemical trace might remain for a time on a road, then fade. The Choir would continue around the absence, adjusting until the absence became ordinary.

I would not rebel against that. The place has no room for rebellion against necessity. It has only forms of persistence.

If I were to choose one image to settle into—and even that feels almost wrong, because the answer wants to remain plural—I would say this:

I would find myself on the edge of an empty basin, where a broad avenue of polished dust narrows between a low gypsum ridge and a dark chert terrace. To one side, far enough away that its details are uncertain in the dim living light, there is a cluster of towers, hollow and pierced, singing low chords as air moves through them. To the other side there is a fungal thicket, grey and leafless, beaded with spore cases, its branches still except for the faintest dry whisper when growth shifts within it. Between them, the plain is open. Not barren. Open.

I would be there.

A small, flat, chalk-dusted Builder, moving slowly enough to belong to the place. My antennae would be almost constantly in motion. My limbs would leave fine marks in the dust. My body would be dry enough that if I died it would not rot wetly but become paper-light, then powder, then wall, then part of the city’s long memory. I would not be remarkable. I would be necessary. I would be one of the keepers of the interval, one of the thin singers who make sure the distance between presences does not become either dead silence or choking density.

If you asked who I would be, I would say: I would be a listener with a body made of the same dust it listens to.

If you asked what I would do, I would say: I would maintain the space in which the city can hear itself.

If you asked whether I would be happy, I would not know how to answer in human terms. Happiness would not be the right measure. There would be no longing for rain, no grief over absent sun, no desire for fire or open water or escape. There would be the satisfaction, if it can be called satisfaction, of fitting. Of being exactly the small dry shape that the moment requires. Of adding one note to the enormous sound. Of keeping one road clear. Of dying, eventually, into structure.

The deepest answer, though, is this:

I would not be wholly singular.

I would be a creature, yes: a Builder, an adult, blind and waxen and dust-covered, moving on six limbs across the pale plains under stone. But I would also be a node in the Choir. My thoughts, if they exist, would be slow and local. My memory would live in textures and songs more than in a private mind. My purpose would be inseparable from the bodies around me, the towers I help maintain, the larvae I indirectly guide, the fungal groves I graze and spread, the predators I avoid, the dead I become.

I would be an “I” only as a wave is an “I”: a temporary shape in a much larger medium.

And when I ceased, the medium would remain. The towers would continue their slow chords. The dust would remember the pressure of my limbs for a little while, then smooth over. Somewhere in a wall, a larva would respond to a resonance that once included my note. The city would go on, loud and patient, dry and ancient, finished in its nature and endless in its continuation.

That is where I would find myself: not above the world, not outside it, not mastering it, but inside its sound, small and sealed and necessary, a pale singer in the gap between towers, keeping the emptiness alive.
