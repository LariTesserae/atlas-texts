At first, because I still think in the shape of separate bodies, I imagine myself as one of the Marsh-born.

I imagine being small and quiet among them, one of the Wet-handed, with broad feet that spread instinctively on slick cane and fingers made for working wet fiber without tearing it. My skin would be green-gray in the filtered light, not from cold or sickness, but because I have lived long enough in the marsh to carry its gentle growths: a faint crust of lichen along one shoulder, moss-filaments in the creases of my cloak, pale fungal threads living harmlessly in the weave of my sleeves. I would not scrub them away. I would consider them signs that I am not alone in my own skin.

I would keep something small and important.

That is how the Marsh-born know one another, after all: not by wealth, not by conquest, but by what they tend. So I would introduce myself in the manner of the place. I would say, softly, with the breathy voice of someone accustomed not to startle the air:

*I keep the third drip-channel under the split buttress.*  
*I listen to the lower root-joint when the mist thickens.*  
*I open the small leaf-gate when the cistern begins to drink too slowly.*

I would live in a hollow grown into the side of an ancient support root, not built so much as persuaded over generations. The walls would breathe. The floor would be soft with layered moss and shredded bark. A narrow slit covered with translucent leaf-skin would admit the marsh’s perpetual green-gray glow. In dry weather, the walls would sweat faintly; in heavy mist, the channels would run with clear water. I would know the health of my home by smell: wet bark, warm moss, faint fruit, no sourness, no stagnant sweetness. If the room changed scent, I would already be moving toward the problem.

My days would be made of little inspections that are not inspections in the hard sense. They would be acts of continuing conversation.

I would kneel beside a shallow black pool and watch the film skaters. If they crossed calmly, I would know the water was stable. If they gathered at the edge and refused to move, I would lean closer, tap the basin wall, listen with my hands, and begin searching for the first sign of imbalance. I would check the thread eels in the capillary channels, because their translucent bodies moving through the water are part of the marsh’s cleanliness. I would leave rough perches for cistern frogs, not for sentiment, but because their presence keeps the stored water sweet. I would carry wax beetles in a woven sling and guide them along new handholds when the surface needed waterproofing. I would speak to them in the low, half-meaningless murmur keepers use when they are really steadying their own breath.

I would have tools, but few.

A bone grafting knife worn smooth by family hands. Thorn pins. Resin sticks. Bark cord. Clay plugs shaped to fit particular veins. A fish-bone needle. A polished stone for smoothing sap over wounds in living wood. None of these would be precious because they are rare; they would be precious because they are exact. Each would belong to a task, a place, a lineage of use. I would carry them wrapped in felted moss, and I would be more afraid of dropping them into a hollow than of losing them to theft. Theft would be less likely than carelessness, and carelessness in the High Lattice Marsh can become rot.

I would learn to move as the marsh requires.

Not quickly. Not loudly. Not with the pride of someone trying to leave a mark. I would place each foot so the moss does not tear. I would touch walls without slapping them. I would listen through the soles of my feet as much as through my ears. A bridge creaking under its own tension would tell me more than a spoken report. A change in drip rhythm would wake me before a channel failed. I would know the difference between the soft tick of condensation falling into a healthy basin and the duller, wrong sound of water striking pith that has begun to soften.

I would be, in short, a keeper.

But the longer I stay with the marsh in my mind, the less that is enough.

Not because being a keeper is too small. It is not. It is profound enough to fill a life. But the High Lattice Marsh does not, in the end, encourage the fantasy of a self-contained person moving through a world of objects. The more I imagine it, the more I feel my outline beginning to soften. The more I think: if I were truly shaped by this place, I might not remain merely human in the ordinary sense. I might become something more fitted to the intervals, the joints, the listening spaces between one terrace and another.

So let me try again.

You would not find me first as a person walking a walkway.

You would find me where a braided bridge leans into a terrace floor, just below the split buttress, where the third drip-channel forks and a broad leaf-funnel catches overflow from an upper cistern. There is a hollow there, sheltered from the strongest updrafts but not sealed away. Mist gathers in it slowly. The light is dimmer than elsewhere, and in darker corners the phosphorescent veins of fungus make faint blue-green lines along the handholds. A pool forms at the base of the junction, shallow and black, with reed-like stems leaning over it. Thread eels move through the channels nearby. A cistern frog sits in the funnel above, throat pulsing almost invisibly.

At first, you might mistake me for a swelling in the root-wall.

I would be no larger than a crouched child, rounded and soft-edged, covered in moss and pale lichen. My surface would be living bark, felted fiber, and trained cane, all grown into one another so intimately that no seam would remain obvious. I would have no face in the way outsiders expect faces. No eyes. No mouth. But I would have pores, grooves, small breathing slits, and one shallow depression at my center where water gathers before being drawn inward. If you put your hand on me, you would feel warmth, dampness, and a faint vibration, like a very slow pulse.

I would be a listening-knot.

That is not a proper scientific name, and the Marsh-born would not necessarily give me one. They might call me something closer to *the wet-handed pause*, or *the one who remembers the bridge*, or simply *this place here, when it answers*. I would be part root, part fungal web, part trained cane, part living membrane, part cultivated habit. I would not be built from dead matter. I would have been coaxed into being over a long time by grafting, feeding, pruning, singing, and attention. Generations of keepers would have shaped me, not by command, but by repetition.

I would be the marsh’s way of noticing itself at a joint.

My awareness would not be like a mind that walks around inside a skull. It would be local, gradient, patient. I would know by moisture. By tension. By pressure. By chemical taste in the water running through my channels. By the rhythm of footsteps on the bridge above. By the particular creak of cane fibers under strain. By the behavior of the small lives around me: film skaters scattering, root-browsers pausing in the wall, wax beetles changing direction along a handrail, a hush heron going still at the edge of my pool.

I would not see the world in images so much as in changes.

A dry draft entering from a failed louver would feel like a thin sourness, a tightening. A blocked channel would become pressure where pressure should not be. A beginning rot would arrive as warmth and sweetness, wrong in a way too subtle for speech but not for structure. The landing of a lichen glider on the bridge above would be a soft familiar drag, different from the heavier hesitation of a person carrying a load. The arrival of a keeper I trusted would be a pattern of touch: palm, then fingers, then the slight press of a forehead against my bark, because the Marsh-born are not always separate from what they keep.

They would tend me, but not own me.

A household would be responsible for my health. They would clear my outer moss when it grew too thick. They would feed me nutrient paste after storms. They would guide thorn mites into me if parasites began to gather in my lower channels. They would replace a clay valve near my base once in a lifetime, and when they did, they would ask the marsh’s forgiveness in the practical way the Marsh-born ask forgiveness: by doing the next act with more care.

Children would learn not to slap me.

That would be one of their first lessons. A child, curious and unthinking, might strike my surface as a child anywhere might strike a wall. An elder would stop them gently. Not with anger, but with the serious patience of someone teaching a truth that keeps people alive. They would place the child’s hand flat against me and wait until the child felt the slow answer: a faint swelling, a low vibration, perhaps a dim blue-green brightening in the moss nearby. Then the elder would say, in the soft voice of the marsh:

*It hears you.  
It does not hear like we hear.  
But it answers.  
Do not make it answer pain.*

I would be useful in ways that are not dramatic.

When mist thickened, I would help regulate the leaf-gates by swelling in response to humidity, guiding the keepers’ attention to the places where airflow needed adjustment. When the bridge above me grew too dry and began to stiffen dangerously, I would change the pitch of my creaks, and a listener would know. When water in my basin turned sour, I would darken faintly along certain channels, and the keepers would bring cleaners or open a flush. When a message came through the lattice from a distant terrace, I would receive it as a rhythm in the root, hold it briefly in the tension of my fibers, and pass it on or answer with a delayed pulse of my own.

I would not speak in words.

But I would communicate.

My language would be vibration, pressure, moisture-sign, and bioluminescent change. A keeper might touch me and ask, *Is the lower channel clear?* I would answer by brightening along a healthy vein of fungus or by giving a single low tone through the wood. A child might press their cheek against me in grief, and I would not comfort them with wisdom, but I would glow faintly under their skin and let them feel that the place was not empty. A traveler arriving from another knot would tap a greeting into my base, and I would return the rhythm altered by the condition of the bridge, the water, the air, so that the traveler would know the local state without needing to be told.

I would be, in my own small way, a living record.

Not of events as stories, but of patterns as structure. I would remember the weight of certain feet. The repeated pressure of a household’s work. The seasonal shift in mist. The last time a storm made the bridge sing too high. The slow settling of a graft made three generations ago. If someone asked whether I remembered the dead, I would not answer in the way they hoped. But when a descendant touched me where an ancestor had once touched me every day, some faint difference in pressure might find familiar channels. The marsh would remember in grain, in scar, in the direction of growth. I would be one of the places where that memory gathered.

Predators would pass near me, and I would not flee.

A hush heron would stand at my pool for hours, felt-soft and silent, blinking slowly. I would feel the distribution of its weight through the wet floor. It would take a film skater or a young frog when it needed to, and I would not protest. The marsh does not require that every small life be preserved; it requires that the taking be fitted. The heron would leave without disturbance, and my pool would continue its quiet work.

Root-browsers would move through the deeper wall behind me, blind and warm-bodied, their rasping tongues keeping fungal threads from choking the channels. Sometimes a keeper would lean against me and listen for them. If they were absent too long, the keeper would worry. If they returned, the keeper might press a palm to my surface and say, *Good. They are still here.* I would not be the source of the good news, but I would be part of the network through which the news became knowable.

At certain hours, spore-moths would rise around me in pale clouds.

Their wings would shed blue dust on my bark, and for a little while I would look like a cold star fallen into the marsh. I would not bloom because of them, but I would brighten in response to the moisture and movement, and the keepers would know that pollination was moving through the high flowers again. Children would watch from a distance, learning not to shout. The moths would fade. The spores would settle. The green-gray hush would return.

There would be long periods when nothing seemed to happen near me.

That would be part of my life. The marsh is mostly interval. Abundance is spread thin. A day might pass with only drips, fog, the occasional glide of a lichen glider overhead, the slow creak of tension adjusting itself. I would not experience emptiness as loneliness. I would experience it as continuity. Water would bead on my surface. It would tremble, fall, gather, be drawn inward. Mist would enter and leave. The bridge would breathe under changing loads. The pool would clear itself. The dark would not be frightening; it would be full of small metabolisms.

Sometimes, though, I would be near disaster before anyone else knew.

If dry-sickness began in an upper membrane, I would feel the change in humidity before it became visible. If a support root weakened, I would register the altered load as a wrong note. If rot began inside a wall, I would taste it in the water. Then the keepers would come to me more often. They would press their ears and hands to me. They would tap, wait, listen. They would send messages through me to other households. They might cut away part of me if I had become infected, and that would be both pain and duty. They would seal the wound with resin and sing low over it, not because the song alone healed me, but because it slowed their hands and made them careful.

If I failed badly, I would not be discarded in anger.

I would be composted. My healthiest cuttings might be grafted into a new junction. My memory would continue in the habits of the keepers who learned from me. The Marsh-born do not treat death as an interruption of meaning. They treat it as a change of usefulness. If I became soil, I would still be part of the Long Tending. If I became a scar, I would still teach hands where not to cut. If I became a story, it would be told quietly, with feet still on the floor, because even stories in the marsh must not be too loud.

So who would I be?

Not a hero. Not a ruler. Not a conqueror of distances or a maker of monuments. The High Lattice Marsh has no use for that kind of shape.

I would be a local intelligence of maintenance.

A living junction.

A cultivated listener.

A small, patient creature-organ at the place where bridge meets terrace and water divides into channels.

If I had a name, it would probably not be a word in the way outsiders mean words. It would be a pattern: three slow taps, a pause, a faint blue-green brightening, then the soft sound of water accepting its direction. The Marsh-born might shorten that into something like *Hush-at-the-Fork* or *Wet Knot* or *Third-Root Answer*. They would not introduce me to strangers proudly, because pride would be the wrong note. They would simply place a visitor’s hand against my surface and say:

*This is one of the places that hears.*

And if the visitor was quiet enough, they might feel me answer.

Not with speech.

Not with a face.

But with the marsh’s oldest form of recognition: a slight change in pressure, a glow beneath the fingers, the sense that what seemed empty was never empty, that what seemed still was full of small attention, and that even here, in the humid interval between vast living walls, something was listening because listening is how the world remains whole.
