If I belonged to that high, dark sea, I would not begin as a solitary swimmer. At first I imagine myself as one of the long, eyeless drifters, trailing streamer-fins through the open black, but that is still too much a visitor’s body: a body passing through. Then I imagine one of the broad grazers, polishing the living walls with endless patience, but even that feels too separate from the architecture. What keeps emerging is something attached, something plural, something less like an animal crossing the world and more like the world developing a voice.

So I would be in the dark arch.

Not the widest part of the basin, not the dome of fused trunks, not the plain of compressed leaf-fiber, but the worn throat where the old waterway has been shaped into its final curve. I would be one of the pulse-colonies clinging to the inner lip of the arch: a lattice of pale, translucent cups, each no larger than a fingertip, joined by fine living threads to the polished root-wall. My color would be cold ivory with faint violet shadows, my surface glassy where the current had worn it, my edges soft enough to tremble with every pressure change. I would have no eyes. I would have no hands. I would have no single center that could be pointed to and called “the creature.”

I would be an “I” made of many small responders.

My life would be sound and pressure. The arch would carry the basin through me: the low boom of water moving through grown sluices, the creak of plant-fiber under tension, the distant resonant calls of the whale-like filterers, the quick clicks of shoals adjusting themselves around one another. I would know a large body passing not by shadow but by the way the water thickened against my membranes. I would know a current changing because my own pulse would slow. I would know the basin was still intact because the echoes would return in their ancient order.

My purpose, if purpose is even the right word, would be maintenance of the arch’s voice. The colony would emit synchronized pulses: sometimes quick and needle-fine, sometimes so slow that the silence between them mattered more than the sound. When the great filterers approached, we would fall into a deeper rhythm, letting their calls pass through the channel without breaking. When eyeless swimmers entered the arch, our clicks would give them a fine acoustic edge to follow, not guiding them by intention, but becoming part of the water’s legibility. When a surface nearby grew too thick with microbial film, our tone would change because our bodies would be altered by the flow around it; that change would carry outward until grazers came, drawn by chemical and acoustic difference, and restored the smoothness.

I would not build anything. I would not cut, tie, command, or contrive. My effect on the world would come from remaining there. Generations of me would live and thin and be reabsorbed into the basin. Small parts would shed as pale tissue into the dark water and become food for drifting filaments. New buds would form along the old threads. The arch would wear me smooth, and I would wear the arch smooth, though “wear” is almost wrong; it would be more like long agreement. Inhabitation would become form.

I would not miss light. In that place, light would be an interruption. The faint blue-green glow that sometimes traveled along my membranes when disturbed would not be for seeing. It would be proof that something had touched me, a brief local star in a darkness that did not need stars. I would not long for the outside air, the frost-stiffened canopies, the cloud-banks below the rim. I would know height only as a tension in the root-work, a pressure in the grown bowl, the sense that the whole sea was balanced at the top of itself and had learned to remain.
