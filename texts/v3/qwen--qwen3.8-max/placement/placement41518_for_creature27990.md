If High Mere were to find a place for me, it would first have to take away my hurry.

The first image that comes is too bright: I am at the surface, something birdlike, breaking the water with quick wings, taking fish, leaving rings, making the pool notice me too suddenly. But High Mere would not love that. It would find me too abrupt. It would ask me to stop announcing myself.

The second image is too deep: I am a fish, silver and silent, moving through the channels with no need of air. But fish already belong there in a way I would not. They are citizens of the current, not keepers of meaning. They do not need to ask what the city is; they swim through it and are enough.

The third image is too human in the wrong sense: I am a visitor, a stranger from dry places, fascinated and clumsy, full of questions, reaching for everything. High Mere would not keep such a person long. The water would slow me until I learned that reaching is not the same as understanding.

So the image that remains is quieter.

I would find myself in the middle terraces.

Not at the bright surface, where birds strike and rain drums and the sky is almost too loud. Not in the oldest lower floors, where the root masses thicken into darkness and the water holds its breath for long centuries. Not quite there. I would be in the middle green, where light still moves but has already lost its surface sharpness; where the city is inhabited but not crowded; where channels open into space and space opens into rooms; where a person can rest between air and depth, between one household and another, between sound and silence.

There is a place I can almost name when I think of it.

A chamber grown into the curve of a root bank, not large, not grand. Its outer wall is an old seed-case or trunk-hollow, softened by years underwater, its edges rounded until it seems less made than remembered into shape. The door is elliptical, smooth, worn by slow bodies passing through. Above the doorway, living stems have been trained into a low arch. They are still growing, but slowly, as all things grow in High Mere. Their roots descend in thin braids, brushing the water like patient fingers.

Inside, the chamber holds air.

Not all of it, and not always. Enough. A dome of woven root and compressed leaf traps a lens of breath beneath its ceiling. The air is cool, wet, green, sweet with oxygen from cultivated plants. A translucent leaf panel, thinned by careful tending, lets in a wash of gold when the sun is right. When clouds pass over the basin, the light turns to jade. When rain comes, the whole ceiling trembles faintly with the sound, and the chamber becomes a drum held over water.

The floor is springy: peat, leaf-mat, fine rootlets, a woven mat over the most worn places. There is a low perch of lashed reeds. A rack of cords. A row of seed-pod vessels. A breathing tube of hollow stem, polished by use. A small obsidian blade wrapped in bark cloth. A bronze needle, if I am fortunate enough to inherit one, kept in a split reed case and treated with reverence. No fire. Fire would be an impossibility here, and even as an idea it feels dangerous. If warmth is needed, it comes from movement, from dried wraps, from the shared heat of bodies resting in stillness.

This chamber might be called something like Second Breath.

Not because it is a place of rescue, exactly, though it can be that. But because it is a place where one returns to oneself after long submersion. A place where lungs open. Where a diver floats upward through green water, presses through a curtain of fine roots, and enters the hush of held air. Where the body slows until it can listen.

That is where I would be.

And what would I be?

At first I would say: one of the Understory. A person of the pool. Human-shaped but soaked into the world. Long-limbed, cool-skinned, broad-chested, with fingers slightly webbed and feet made for gripping wet roots. My skin would carry the basin’s stain: tannin-brown, greenish at the shoulders, mottled like light through leaves. My hair would be oiled and braided, weighted with small seed-beads so it would not drift into my face underwater. My eyes would have learned to read movement in the green dimness. My pulse would be slow. My breath would be deliberate. I would not have gills. I would have training, patience, and the knowledge of where air remains.

But that is still too simple.

High Mere would not make me only a body. It would make me a role.

I would be a Listener of the Hollow Stems.

Perhaps the Understory would have another name for such a person, something wet and resonant in their tongue, something that means “the one who keeps the water from becoming noise.” But in plain terms, I would be a keeper of echoes, signals, intervals, and necessary silence. My work would be sound, distance, and attention.

The city is loud, as you say. It is loud not with chaos but with life. Rain on the surface. Water through hollow trunks. Roots creaking. Fish drumming in the deep chambers. Woven gates tapping against posts. Crustaceans clicking in old leaf beds. Birds calling from the bright skin of the pool. Voices carrying strangely through air bells. Every sound has use, but use can become disorder if no one listens.

So I would listen.

My chamber would be placed where sound gathers without becoming confused. One wall would be anchored to a thick root column that carries knocks from other households. Another would touch a hollow stem that brings low tones from the upper channels. A third would open toward a stretch of central water where messages travel far but must be kept from crossing too sharply. From Second Breath, I could hear the basin without being at its center.

That is important. I would not be at the center.

High Mere would not put me in a place of command. There are no thrones in a city made of living plants, no high seats that do not eventually become roots, fish shelter, or spawn. If there is authority there, it is distributed like buoyancy: held by those who know how not to sink the whole. I would be useful, but modest. A nerve, not a crown. A listening place. A careful interval.

My days would begin with air.

Before diving, before messages, before the first cross-basin knock, I would tend the breath of the chamber. I would check the oxygen-giving plants along the roof and inner walls. I would thin any decay before it fouled the air. I would taste the chamber as the Air-Keepers do: not in a mystical way, but with practiced attention. Sweet, green, light? Good. Flat, heavy, sour? Something must be opened, thinned, cleansed. I might spend an hour coaxing a new leaf panel to admit more light, or clearing a small channel so oxygen-rich water moves nearer the air bell.

Then I would listen.

A slow tap on the root column: three notes, spaced wide. The morning question. Is the basin whole?

From across the water, perhaps a Fish-Tender answers: two knocks and a pause, meaning the pens are calm. From an upper platform, a Weaver taps a seed-pod: a quick, dry sound meaning the reeds are drying, no damage from the night. From below, maybe nothing. Maybe the deep channels answer only with the low croak of fish moving in the green dark. But even that is information.

If a gate is tapping the wrong rhythm against its post, I would know something is loose. If the weir’s hush has changed, I would know silt or leaf-fall is altering the flow. If the surface sounds thin and metallic, a storm may be coming. If the fish drums are too urgent in a spawning chamber, something has disturbed them: an otter, perhaps, or a collapsed fringe of root, or a child who dove too fast and frightened the water.

Then I would answer.
