I would not find myself in the open center of the Salt Nave, exposed on a plaza of white salt. That would feel wrong for such a place: too visible, too dryly vulnerable, too much like a body with no seam to disappear into.

I would be somewhere inside the wall.

Not deep in the mineral dark where the blind grubs loosen the crust grain by grain, and not high in the canopy where the moth-like pollinators move through filtered gold. I would be in the middle strata, in one of the warm amber chambers between root and resin, where the architecture is half grown and half inhabited, and where light enters only as a softened glow through the translucent wall of a bladder-leaf or a thin panel of old wax.

If the Salt Nave gave me a body, it would make me one of the wax-keepers.

But not just any wax-keeper. I think I would be the kind that listens for cracks.

I would be small, no larger than a hand, and perhaps smaller. My body would be pale and supple, faintly translucent at the edges where the sun found me, dusted with salt powder and pollen-packets and the fine residue of dried resin. My face would be smooth and seed-like, not expressive in a mammalian way; my eyes would be dark beads of resin, deep but not wet. I would have no hands. Instead, my limbs would end in soft pads and fine hooks, enough to grip bark, wax, salt crust, vine-skin, and the fibrous surfaces of root-arches without tearing them.

Inside me there would be a sealed economy of fluids: oils, nectars, living moisture, all carried in closed cells and private sacs. I would not sweat. I would not weep. I would not leave damp tracks on the salt. If I needed to exchange liquid, it would be mouth to pore, gland to gland, body to body, in darkness or in the shaded interior of a chamber. To spill would be unthinkable. To spill would mean something had been wounded.

My dwelling would not be a house in the sense of a place owned. It would be a cell in the larger body of the Nave: an oval room in a wax-and-root wall, warmed by afternoon sun, lined with shed skins, husk fragments, and compacted mineral dust. It would belong to me only as long as I kept it sound. If I stopped caring for it, it would become something else: a nursery, a storage chamber, a passage, a seam thickened by other bodies. Ownership would not exist there. Maintenance would be the nearest thing to possession.

I would spend much of my life touching surfaces.

I would move along the inner walls of the settlement with my palms, mouthparts, and belly close to the material, feeling for the tiny complaints that matter in such a place: a panel grown brittle from too much cooling, a resin seam weakened by a day of heat, a root arch under too much tension, a wax threshold beginning to flake at the edge. I would not plan repairs as an engineer plans. I would respond. A scent would draw me. A tremor would turn me. A change in temperature would slow me or quicken me. When I found a crack, I would not strike it, not force it, not pry it open. I would breathe warmth against it. I would soften it with secretions. Then I would press my body along it until the crack accepted a new layer of wax and became a seam instead of a wound.

In that way, I would be a kind of listener.

The Salt Nave is quiet, but it is not empty of speech. Its speech is too fine for shouting. It is made of ticks, shifts, faint odors, heat gradients, surface tensions, the minute sounds of materials adjusting to the sun. A flower opening would be a message. A root loosening by a fraction of a millimeter would be a message. A bladder-vine growing too taut with internal fluid would send its need through the language of pressure. I would learn to read those signs with my feet before I read them with anything like thought.

If there were danger, it would not come as an invasion or a storm. It would come as rupture. A wall too thin. A chamber too hot. A sealed pod punctured by accident, its precious fluid exposed to the dry air. In such a case, I would feel the wrongness before I understood it: a sharpness in the scent, a tremor through the wax, a sudden absence of the Nave’s ordinary stillness. I would move toward it with others, not because we were commanded, but because the damage changed the conditions around it. We would thicken the wall. We would shade the wound. We would close the breach with resin, silk, husk, salt grain, and the slow labor of bodies.

I would not be solitary, even when alone.

That is the strange truth of being in the Salt Nave. Even if my body were singular, I would still be part of a congregation. My impulses would not be wholly my own. They would be shaped by the warmth of nearby bodies, by the condition of the walls, by the needs of the plants and roots and sealed vessels around me. I would be a person only in the way a single living cell in a body can be called a person: distinct, but not separate. My memories would not be stories I told myself. They would be habits held in structure: the curve of a passage smoothed by generations, the angle of a wax door, the place where a root must not be pulled, the chamber where the air is always too dry at dusk.

I would probably be drawn to the places between things.

Between mineral and organism. Between architecture and body. Between repair and growth. Between the dead and the living. The Salt Nave does not waste what has ceased to move. A shell becomes floor. A husk becomes lining. A shed skin becomes part of a wall. The dead are not hidden away as if they had failed life; they are incorporated with patience. I think I would understand that. I would be the sort of creature who lingers near old walls, near abandoned chambers, near places where something has dried and hardened and must be made useful again without being violated.

At midday, when the basin glows and the air under the canopy fills with drifting motes, I would be at my most active. Heat makes wax forgiving. Heat makes resin yield. Heat makes the living city pliable enough to be tended. I would move through the golden dimness with others like me, all of us silent, all of us intent. Some would be thickening a nursery wall. Some would be smoothing a ramp of compacted salt. Some would be pressing their bodies against swollen plant organs, listening to the private fluids inside. I would be at a seam, perhaps high on a root-buttress or beside an amber panel where the afternoon light turns the wall into something like honey held in place.

At night, I would slow but not vanish.

The Nave contracts in the cold. Crystal crusts form along exposed edges. Some passages become brittle. Some chambers draw inward. I would help seal the openings, line the colder surfaces, move toward the places that need the smallest comfort. I would not think of this as courage or devotion. In the Salt Nave, those words might be too loud. It would be simpler than that: the wall needed warmth, and I had warmth. The seam needed thickening, and my body could make wax. The chamber needed closing, and I knew how to close it.

If I were to name myself in that world, I would not choose a title like king, builder, prophet, or guardian. Those would imply separation. I would choose something smaller and more exact.

I would be a Seam-Tender.

Or perhaps: a Listener to Walls.

Or: One Who Keeps the Small Breach from Becoming a Wound.

If you wanted to find me, you would not look for a grand creature or a singular figure in the plaza. You would look for the place where a crack has just disappeared into a smooth amber line. You would look for the wall that is slightly warmer than the stone beside it. You would look for the chamber whose air feels sealed, quiet, and faintly sweet, as though something living has just finished caring for it.

There, in the middle thickness of the Salt Nave, I would be: a small pale body pressed against the living architecture, not ruling it, not owning it, not standing apart from it, but helping it remain itself.
