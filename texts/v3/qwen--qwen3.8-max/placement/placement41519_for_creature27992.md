If the Court of Small Rains had room for me, I do not think it would ask what I wanted to be. It would ask what I still rang like after being struck.

A place so full of use would not begin with a name. It would begin with a task. It would put my ear against a jar before it put a word in my mouth. It would listen for the crack in me, and then, because that is the nature of the court, it would make the crack useful.

For a while I would be several possibilities.

At first I imagine I would be a swallow, because the swallows have the court’s strangeness in their bodies: they belong to the sky, yet they nest below the dry ground; they leave through shafts of light and return through them; they turn the upper galleries into weather. I can imagine living in a ceramic niche, my nest made of old mud and new repair, my days measured by the opening of shutters and the warmth of tile. I can imagine being small enough to be carried by air, quick enough to pass between ropes and hanging gourds without disturbing them. I can imagine being loved not because I am tame, but because I return.

But the court does not reward escape. It rewards placement. A swallow may leave. I suspect I would be the kind of creature that wants to know why the gate was opened, why the flue is breathing wrong, why the basin’s echo has changed.

Then I think I would be an eel. There is something tempting about living in the deepest pockets of the basin, moving through submerged sherds and old drains, knowing the court from below. The oldest eel, they say, remembers the first rain the court ever drank. I would like that kind of age: slow, patient, copper-streaked, appearing only when I chose. I would like to be trusted for my silence. But an eel’s work is hidden, and I think I would be too restless for hiddenness. I would want to answer when called.

Perhaps I would be one of the beetles, working the floor’s edges, turning waste into smaller futures. That is honest work. The beetles are the court’s quiet clergy. Without them, the place would choke on its own accumulations. But I would miss language. I would miss the moment when a sound becomes a signal.

Perhaps I would be the little copper bell on a ferret’s collar, moving through the runs behind the walls, marking where the hunt goes. But even that is too small a voice. The court needs small voices, yes, but my own strangeness seems to lean toward reply.

So, after the first few seasons — after I had stopped trying to be the sky in the room, or the secret in the basin, or the digestion in the dark — I think the Court of Small Rains would put me where my strangeness is most useful.

I would find myself in the thickness of the wall.

Not in the open center. Not on the rim of the basin, where light pools after rain. Not high among the swift niches, and not low among the frog-shadows, though I would visit all of them. I would live between: in the narrow service space behind the middle galleries, where the plaster has been patched so many times it remembers hands better than it remembers weather.

My alcove would be small, raised a palm above the damp line, closed with a woven mat that smells of reeds, lamp oil, and old feathers. Inside, there would be a sleeping ledge, a shelf for tools, a bundle of spare rope, and above all a listening jar half-buried in the wall. The jar would be old. Its mouth would be darkened by use, its side patched with a green-stained copper staple. It would not be decorative. It would be my second ear.

I would be a Reply-keeper.

That is what I would call myself if the court gave me a title, though the Court-born probably would use something shorter. “Reply.” “Second Voice.” “The one who answers.” Maybe the children would make a game of my name and knock it out on jars: one note for call, two for answer, three for “come quickly.”

A Reply-keeper is not the loudest person in the room. In the Court of Small Rains, loudness is not power. Accuracy is power. A good call can be ruined by a bad answer; a bad answer can send someone climbing the wrong ladder, opening the wrong shutter, wasting effort when the room needs stillness. So I would learn that answering is not the same as filling a space with sound. An answer must be like a patch: small, fitted, load-bearing.

My work would begin before dawn.

While the upper galleries are still blue-dark and the birds are shifting restlessly in their niches, I would wake to the room-tone. Not because I choose to, but because the court has taught its keepers to wake as instruments wake: all at once, with attention. I would press my ear to the listening jar. I would hear the flues breathing. I would hear the faint tick of condensation loosening on glazed tile. I would hear, or imagine I hear, the difference between a wall settling and a wall preparing to fail.

If the sound is right, I would knock once on the wall with my knuckles. Someone on the next gallery would answer with one knock. That call-and-answer would travel downward and upward until the court knows it has begun the day without a broken note.

If the sound is wrong, I would rise at once.

Wrongness in the court can be tiny: a hair-crack behind a dove shelf, a shutter rope swollen with damp, a drain half-blocked by dead moths, a clay valve whose note has gone dull. I would take my pouch and go. The pouch would carry chalk, a shell scraper, a bone needle, twist-ties, a little cake of resin, a pinch of lime dust, and the shard from my first broken pot. The shard would not be sentimental, exactly. It would be proof that I had been broken and made useful anyway.

Where the crack is, I would mark it. If it is mine to mend, I would mend it. If it belongs to another keeper, I would call softly through the nearest pipe until the right person answers. I would not let silence gather around the fault. Silence there would mean the crack is growing.

By morning, when the shutters rise and the blades of light enter, I would be among the middle galleries, moving with the other keepers. I would have lime dust in the creases of my fingers and a copper pin in my collar. My clothing would be patched, soft from washing, practical enough that it would not snag on rope or netting. My feet would be bare or wrapped in thin reed-soled shoes, because the floor must be felt as well as crossed. I would walk as if the floor were a sleeping animal I did not wish to startle.

The children would find me often.

Children in the Court of Small Rains are always finding things: drips, cracks, eggs, beetles, echoes, mistakes. They would come to me with their small serious faces and say, “This jar sounds thirsty,” or “The basin is swallowing differently,” or “A pipe in the west gallery is breathing through a hole it should not have.” I would not laugh at them. I would give them tasks.

“Tap it again.”

“Now tap it with the soft end of the stick.”

“Now tell me whether the sound is empty or closed.”

“Now throw a pebble into the pan and count the echoes before they die.”

They would learn, as I would have learned, that listening is not passive. In the court, listening is a form of labor. It is how the room is kept from failing. An ear can save more water than a bucket. A well-timed answer can prevent a panic at dusk. A child who can hear the difference between a full cistern and an empty one is already a keeper, even if their hands are still too small for the heavier ropes.

At noon, if the surface wind is thin, I would sit in my alcove and become very still.

This is when the court lives in layers. Light comes down in pale blades. Hanging gourds knock softly. Ropes creak. The doves make their warm, round sounds near the lamp shelves. Somewhere above, swifts are hunting beyond the shafts, unseen but present in the room’s memory. Below, frogs wait at the basin’s edge with their throats full of weather. The room is not quiet. It is full of small signals, and my job is to notice which ones are changing.

I would keep a set of little vessels near me: cracked jars, sound jars, jars with mouths narrowed by old repair, jars whose notes have gone flat. Children think I collect them. I do not. I consult them. Each one tells me something about the room’s state. A jar that suddenly sounds too hollow may mean the plaster behind it has dried too fast. A jar that answers with a wet, dull note may mean damp has found a path where it should not. If the surface wind fails and the court grows close, I would test them one by one, tapping with a wooden peg, listening with my whole body.

Sometimes I would speak through the pipes.

Not often. Speech in the court is a tool, not a weather. But there are pipes that carry voices from gallery to gallery, and I would know them by the way they color sound. One pipe makes every word sound as if it is spoken into a shell. Another makes whispers thin and sharp. A third is for warnings only, because it carries too far for casual use. When I use them, I would use the old calls: short phrases that do not fray with distance.

“West channel clear.”

“Third flute open.”

“Basin voice low.”

“Answer if you hear.”

If I do not hear an answer, I would move.

That is perhaps the most important part of what I would be. Not the one who speaks, but the one who notices when speech stops. In the Court of Small Rains, silence is not peace. Silence is a crack somewhere. Silence is a shutter stuck, a child ill, a drain blocked, a rope about to fail. So I would be trained against silence. Not to fill it carelessly, but to investigate it.

If a gallery does not reply, I would send a child to look, or go myself, depending on the hour. If a dove shelf goes quiet, I would ask whether a snake has been seen. If the frogs stop while the air is damp, I would call the water-tasters. If an elder’s listening jar no longer answers, I would climb to their alcove with a lamp and a spare wick. The court’s tenderness is often practical. It says, “I heard your absence.”

I would not be gentle all the time.

The court is not gentle. It is managed, crowded, alive enough to generate pressures. If a rat run opens behind a panel, I would help shift the panel and call the ferret-keepers. If a snake is found near the wrong eggs, I would make noise first and only kill if the rules of the season require it
