If I were placed into that world, I would not arrive as a visitor standing at the rim with clean feet. I would already be inside it, wet to the ankles, one hand resting on a wall that was sweating, my body learning before my mind that there was no dry margin here, no quiet place to step back and observe. The first thing I would lose would be the habit of looking for a horizon. There would be no green distance, no open field, no tree line where the eye could rest. There would only be the next bridge, the next channel, the next lamp trembling in its hollow, the next wet surface giving back a pale, mineral light.

At first, I think I would imagine myself as a builder.

That would be the natural mistake. The city is so obviously made, so thick with labor, with frames and molds and freshly cut stone, that one might assume the most important person there is the one who adds the most material. I might picture myself guiding warm water into shallow clay channels, watching crust form around a stitched-hide frame, lifting a block of new sinter while it was still soft enough to mark with a thumb. I might want to be useful in a visible way: to make a wall thicker, a platform wider, a ramp where none had been before.

But the basin would correct me quickly.

It would correct me not with words, but with water. A wall made in the wrong place would narrow a flow. A platform raised without permission would change the drip pattern below. An addition not balanced by a removal would begin to choke something else. In that city, building is not first an act of creation. It is an act of obedience to the level. So I would not remain a builder for long, unless I became a very careful one. More likely, I would be drawn downward, toward the places where the city’s life is most exact: the weirs, the sluice lips, the gauge stones, the shallow tanks where a rise of one finger’s width matters more than a new tower.

I would find myself low.

Not in the lowest dark water, not in the deep drains where the blind bottom-feeders move, but near the working margins where water divides and must be watched. Perhaps at a junction where one broad channel splits into three narrower ones under a low arch of pale stone. Perhaps beside a settling tank whose lip has been scraped smooth by generations of hands. Perhaps in a district that has no grand name, only a practical one: the place where the clear flow meets the cloudy flow, or the corner beneath the third roost tower, or the reach where the lamps must burn all day because the vapor overhead is too thick for even the memory of sunlight.

There I would be someone who listens with the hands.

Not in the mystical sense, though it might look like that to an outsider. In the basin’s sense. I would be one of the workers who can stand barefoot on wet stone and feel, through the soles of the feet, that a channel is beginning to block before the sound changes enough for the ears to catch. I would be the kind of person who rests a palm against a wall not for comfort, but because the wall tells things. A new hollow feel means a void forming behind the crust. A different vibration means a gate not seated properly. A faint tremor underfoot can mean that water is finding a path it should not have found.

My body would become suited to that work, or the work would reveal what in me had always been suited to it.

My feet would grow broader and tougher, the toes spread and gripping, the skin marked by sharp crust and softened again by constant damp. My shoulders would thicken from pulling ropes, lifting clay plugs, steadying baskets, hauling nets not with great strength but with repetitive endurance. My hands would become the most important things about me. They would be calloused in the places where gut rope bites and shell scrapers press. The knuckles would be rough, the palms shiny with old oil and mineral dust. My skin would rarely be entirely clean. It would carry the faint white streaks of lime, the gray film of basalt grit, the smell of rendered fat and fish and warm stone. I would rub my forearms and face with fungal pulp and animal fat the way everyone else did, not out of vanity, but to keep the salt and crust from cracking me open.

My ears would change too.

Perhaps they would ring. Perhaps they would dull. Perhaps I would become one of those people the city values because they hear less of the useless noise and more of the meaningful change. I would not listen for speech the way people do in quieter places. I would listen for difference. The pitch of a trickle becoming thicker. The slap of water striking stone where it should only slide. The hollow gurgle of a drain beginning to choke. The almost silent pause when a gate lifts a fraction too far. Sound in the basin would not be something I heard from outside myself. It would press against my chest, my teeth, the bones of my wrists. I would learn to trust my body’s dull knowledge as much as my ears.

I would not have a name that could be called clearly across a distance.

If I had a name, it would likely be a sign.

Maybe it would be made with two fingers pressed flat against the palm, then turned downward: *hold the level*. Maybe it would be a quick tap at the wrist followed by an open hand moving low: *the gate that answers slowly*. Maybe children would make the sign for me when they saw me coming, not to mock me, but because names there are practical things, tied to what a person does and how a person moves. I might be known as the one who checks the eastern lip before sleeping. Or the one who feels for seepage under the second bridge. Or the one who once found a cracked weir stone before it failed. In that city, identity would not be a story told about me in my absence. It would be a pattern repeated in front of others, again and again, until they recognized it.

My work would be small, exact, and endless.

I would carry a measuring stick polished by use, perhaps marked with notches cut into bone or darkened with oil and mineral stain. I would know which mark belonged to which pool. I would know that the clear-water channel must be kept a thumb’s width below the lip of the third pan, that the cloudy flow can rise slightly at feeding time but must settle again before the next shift, that the waste channel must never be allowed to slow enough for the heavier silt to drop where it cannot be reached. I would carry clay plugs in a basket when they were needed, and know by touch which ones were dry enough to seat firmly and which were still too soft and would swell wrongly. I would have a copper hook for lifting small gates, a shell scraper for cleaning crust from a measuring stone, a bone lever for easing a stuck lip without cracking it.

If I were particularly trusted, I would be one of the people allowed to make decisions at the sluices.

Not large decisions. The basin would not admire large decisions. The best decision would usually be the smallest one: open this gate a finger’s width, close that one by half a finger, scrape this lip now before it thickens, redirect this thin thread of drip into the settling pan instead of letting it run down the wall. If a pool rose too high, I would not panic. I would look for the cause. If a channel ran too fast, I would not celebrate the abundance. I would ask what it was stealing from somewhere else. The city would teach me that imbalance rarely announces itself dramatically. It begins as a slight difference. A new wetness on a wall. A change in the color of crust. A pool that takes longer to settle. A bird refusing to nest in a tower it used to favor. A smell where there should be only warm stone and oil.

I would live close to my work.

Not because I chose convenience, but because the city is too dense for long separations. My sleeping place might be an alcove above a channel, entered by a low passage where one has to turn sideways. The floor would be stone, perhaps covered with layered hides and woven gut mats. The bed would be raised on stone lips if possible, to keep it away from the worst of the seepage. There would be a niche in the wall for a lamp, and the lamp would be one of my most valued possessions: a shallow dish of fired clay or hollowed stone, trimmed carefully, fed with animal fat, its flame kept small and steady. In the darkest inner courts, I would know how to move without knocking it over, how to shield it with a curved hand, how to tell when the oil was burning badly by the way the light shivered on wet stone.

My possessions would be few, but each would have use and history.

A shell bowl. A bone pick. A net folded tight and hung from hooks. A scraper with a handle worn to the shape of my grip. A small copper ring, perhaps, not because I was vain, but because copper is precious and a well-made thing can carry affection. A pouch of clay plugs. A length of gut cord. A notched gauge stick inherited from someone who had stood where I stood before. I would not own much that was decorative, but I might own one beautiful object whose beauty was entirely practical: a stone adze balanced perfectly, a basket woven so tightly it could carry larvae without leaking, a hide wrap softened by years of oiling until it moved like water over the shoulders.

I would not live alone, even if I wanted to.

The basin would not permit that kind of solitude. There would be bodies everywhere: children crouched by pools, workers passing with baskets, bird-tenders climbing ladders, someone mending a net in a passage too narrow to sit comfortably, an elder feeling the wall with one hand while murmuring a sign to another. I would belong to a reach of channels more than to a private household. Other people would know my habits. They would know when I checked which gate, how I liked a knot tied, whether I was quick to anger when someone let a tank rise, whether I could be trusted with eggs, whether I had the patience to scrape a long wall without leaving thin ridges behind. My life would be crowded, noisy, and observed. Privacy would not be a room. It might only be the moment when I bent close to a lamp and the rest of the city became a blur of sound behind my shoulder.

Communication would reshape me.

I would learn to speak with my whole body. A lifted hand to stop. A palm turned down to lower flow. A finger drawn across the forearm to say the crust is ready. A touch on the shoulder to ask someone to move behind me. A sharp tap twice on stone to call attention without shouting. I would become fluent in nearness. When I did use my voice, it would be loud and brief, shaped to carry only a little distance through the din. I would stop trying to explain things at length. I would learn that a well-timed gesture is cleaner than a sentence. I would learn to read the angle of another person’s shoulders: irritation, urgency, fatigue, alarm. I would learn to see meaning in the way someone stands at a weir, not just in the way they move their hands.

In time, I might become someone who teaches children.

Not formally, perhaps. There would be no school apart from work. But I might be the person a child is sent to when they have begun to show attention. I would teach them first to be still on wet stone. Then to watch a pool without staring at it. Then to place a hand flat on a wall and wait. I would show them how to tell seepage from dangerous flow, how to hear when a channel is beginning to block, how to carry a lamp without letting the flame jump, how to move past nesting birds without disturbing them. I would teach them the signs before I taught them any story. In fact, the signs would be the first story.

The child would ask, perhaps with hands, what happens if the level fails.

I would answer with another gesture. Not a dramatic one. A lowering of the hand. A closed fist pressed against the chest. Something meaning: everything suffers a little, until someone notices.

My imagination would change in that world.

I would not dream of forests. I would not long for open skies or quiet fields. If I dreamed, I would dream of water lines, cracked crust, blocked drains, towers empty of birds, lamps going out one by one in a long passage. I would dream of finding the mistake in time. That would be my version of heroism: not killing a monster, not crossing a wilderness, not founding a dynasty, but noticing a change before it became a disaster. The stories I told myself would be about repair. About a gate opened too late, and what it cost. About a worker who ignored a strange silence and found a wall too thin. About the person who once kept a roost tower clean when everyone else wanted to leave it for the season, and so the birds returned. My myths would not be about origins. They would be about continuance.

I would probably become more practical than philosophical, but not unfeeling.

There would be moments of strange tenderness in that life. Saving a fallen nestling and putting it back where it could be reached by the adult birds. Oiling a child’s cracked hands. Sharing a lamp when someone else’s oil had run low. Replacing a broken scraper without being asked. Sitting near a pool after a repair, watching the water return to its proper mark, feeling the relief of it in the body before the mind names it. There would be beauty, too, though it would not look like beauty from a greener world. It might be the way a freshly scraped wall glistened under lamps. The symmetry of a well-mended net. The clean line of crust where it had been allowed to form exactly as intended. The white gloss of a roost tower after careful harvesting. The pale flash of fish turning together in a tank when feeding time came.

I would also be capable of the harder things the basin requires.

I would help process dead animals without disgust. I would render fat, clean bone, stretch hides, scrape shells, carry waste to the fungal beds. I would understand that nothing can be sentimental here. If I died, I would expect to be returned. Not with cruelty, not with indifference, but with the same practical reverence the city gives to all matter that can still serve. Some part of me might become lamp oil. Some part might be given to the pools. A bone might be shaped into a tool if my household consented and the use was right. My name-sign might still be made at the sluice I tended, not to summon me, but to remind the living that the work had passed through these hands before. I would not expect to be remembered as a separate soul standing apart from the basin. I would be remembered as a pattern of reliable motion.

That, I think, is who I would become.

Not a ruler. Not a prophet. Not an explorer. Not a tragic figure gazing upward through the vapor in search of something better.

I would become a maintenance creature.

A watcher of levels. A listener at the stone. A body trained to notice the smallest drift away from balance. I would be someone whose importance could not be seen in a single grand object, because my work would be visible only in the absence of failure. If I did well, nothing dramatic would happen. The channels would keep flowing. The pools would stay at their marks. The crust would be scraped before it choked anything. The birds would continue nesting. The lamps would burn in their niches. The city would remain what it was.

And if the question pressed deeper—*not just what person, but what thing?*—then I would have to answer differently.

In the basin, a person is not quite a person in the way quieter worlds imagine one. The boundary between self and system would be thinner. I would be a node in a living apparatus. My attention would be part of the city’s circulation, like water. When I noticed a blocked drain, that noticing would move through other bodies: hands lifting a plug, feet shifting a basket, voices calling a warning, a child running to fetch a scraper. My fear would become labor. My labor would become level. My level would become calm in the fish pens, dryness in a sleeping alcove, food in a bowl, guano on a tower ledge, flame in a lamp. I would not stand outside the machine. I would be one of its nerve endings.

So perhaps the truest answer is this:

I would be a local form of the basin’s self-awareness.

Not its mind in any grand sense. The basin does not need a mind that commands. It needs senses that obey. It needs hands that correct without pretending to invent. It needs eyes that can see a water line in dim light. It needs ears that can parse the deafness. It needs feet that can stand on slick stone without slipping. It needs a body willing to be wet, tired, crowded, and useful. If I became any of those things well enough, I would have found my place.

If you wanted to find me there, you would not look for a tower. You would not ask for a palace or a shrine. You would go to one of the lower junctions where the channels divide and the lamps burn all day because the ceiling drips and the air smells of warm stone and oil. You would look for someone crouched near a sluice lip, not speaking much, one hand resting on the wall, the other holding a notched gauge. You would watch that person wait until the water settles, then make a small adjustment so slight that an outsider might miss it.

That is where I would be.

That is who I would be.

Not the loudest thing in the basin. Not the highest. Not the most beautiful.

The one keeping the level true.
