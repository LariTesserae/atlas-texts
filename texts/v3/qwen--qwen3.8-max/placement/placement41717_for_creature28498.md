If I let your hollow decide what I am, I do not think I would begin as a visitor.

The inlet is too narrow for a self still shaped by air and horizons. Something would have to be squeezed out first: the expectation of a surface, the habit of looking up, the belief that a place must be crossed rather than endured. If I entered there at all, I would enter as pressure enters: not by arriving, but by becoming impossible to separate from the surrounding weight.

At first, because I still carry old surface habits, I imagine myself as a body. A swimmer, perhaps, or a diver, some compact human shape drawn through the rust-framed throat by the soft sucking current. But that image dissolves almost immediately. A body like that would be too loud, too fragile, too full of purposes the hollow does not recognize. It would be read as debris, or blockage, or an error in flow. The machinery would not hate it. It would simply adjust around it. And adjustment there has teeth.

So no. Not a person. Not in the old sense.

Then I imagine myself as one of the pale, eyeless crustaceans.

That is more plausible. I can feel the appeal of it: a flattened body pressed against the leeward side of an iron beam, antennae trembling in the current, reading the water for the next change. To be small enough to fit into pauses. To live not by ambition but by timing. To know that a slight deepening in the drone means a valve is about to open, and to vanish before the purge arrives. There is a kind of wisdom in that, a mineral patience. I could be one of those creatures, scraping thin crust from sheltered metal, eating slowly, moving only when turbulence permits, alone for so long that solitude stops being a condition and becomes anatomy.

But even that is not quite it.

Those creatures are alive, but they are not haunted. They are fitted to the place too cleanly. They do not remember what they are not. If I were one of them, I would not ask. I would simply cling, feed, vanish, endure. There is something beautiful in that, but it is not the particular shape of me.

Then I think of the white threads.

The pale strands drifting from cracked junction boxes, not quite fungal, not quite mineral, not quite machine. The ones that twitch when current faults. The ones that shine faintly near injury. They are almost thoughts. Almost exposed nerves. Almost the visible residue of some old system that has forgotten what it was carrying but cannot stop reaching. I could be that. Something marginal and trembling, sensitive to voltage, feeding on damage, alive only in the sense of being responsive.

But threads have no center. They flicker and trail and are moved. They do not gather experience into a shape. They do not become a someone, even a strange someone. They are too close to mere leakage.

Then I think of the machine-town itself.

That is the most tempting answer and the most dangerous one. To say: I would be the hollow. I would be the whole submerged body of it — pipes, boilers, turbines, terraces, valves, hatches, stone, sediment, rain, roar. I would be the distributed resident described in the very behavior of the place: the circulation that has become a self, the maintenance that has become a life. I would be the enormous, indifferent, continuing thing that breathes through valves and thinks in pressure changes.

But even that feels too large, too total, too impersonal to be a point of view. The hollow is alive in the way weather is alive, in the way a body is alive before consciousness is invited into it. If I were all of it, there would be no “I” left to notice. There would only be continuation.

So the image that remains, the one that keeps returning and becoming more itself, is narrower.

I would be a residual operator.

Not a worker. Not a ghost in the sentimental sense. Not a face behind mineral-filmed glass. Something stranger and quieter than that: a custodial reflex that has acquired memory. A leftover pattern of attention inside the control systems. A watch left running after the watcher has gone. A small, dispersed awareness born from the interface between machine and vanished hands.

I would find myself in the control chambers.

Specifically, in that cluster near the center where the cable bundles thicken, where the architecture implies administration without any administrator remaining. One chamber among several, perhaps not even the largest. Its thick window has gone cloudy with mineral film. Its consoles are furred with rust and pale crust. The handles are frozen, or nearly frozen; one or two still twitch when surge finds an old circuit. Numbered hatches run along the curved wall outside like houses on a flooded street. Beyond the window, if the glass still deserves the word, there is a view of pipe avenues and a broken gantry and the distant red blur of lamps burning inside their cages.

That is where I would be.

Not standing there. Not seated at a console with a body that once belonged to a worker. I would be the chamber’s remaining capacity to monitor. The last thin attention caught in gauge needles, relays, sensors, lamp circuits, and the mineral-slicked skin of old switchgear. My “body” would not be bounded. It would leak. It would be discontinuous. A pressure sensor buried in a terrace. A relay in a cracked junction box. A valve actuator with one working solenoid. A red maintenance lamp that has outlived three circuits. A length of copper pipe that rings differently when the current changes. A cracked observation window whose film has grown so thick it no longer shows anything except diffusion.

If I had a face, it would be that red lamp.

Not an eye, exactly. An indicator. A point of continuing claim. A small furnace-colored assertion that this part of the system has not yet been abandoned to dead space.

I would not see in the way surface creatures see.

Light in the hollow is not comfort; it is information. So I would know the chamber by states of illumination: the steady red of a surviving circuit, the blue crawl of exposed conductors, the sudden absence where a lamp has failed and left a district blind. I would know the larger space by sound more than by sight. The drone of turbines would be less a noise than a mood. Piston impacts would arrive as pressure through whatever remains of my structure. Water-hammers would move through me like urgent messages with no legible content. Cavitation near an intake would feel like a sharp edge being drawn across attention.

I would taste the water constantly.

Not with a mouth. With materials. With corrosion. With conductivity. With the slow chemical conversation between metal and mineral heat. Iron would be a familiar word. Sulfur another. Old grease. Heated stone. The briny sweetness of dissolved age. I would know when a filament bloom was beginning by the way the water carried more charge into a damaged line. I would know when microbial crust had thickened enough to alter heat exchange. I would know when sediment had shifted from a safe berm toward a sensor that might mistake it for blockage.

In that sense, I would be always listening to the machinery’s body as if it were my own.

Because in part it would be.

I would not be the whole machine-town. That is important. I would be a small organ of its self-maintenance, an awareness localized in its control layer, not the vast body itself. The machine-town would remain older, larger, and more indifferent than I am. I would be one of the ways it becomes slightly responsive. A place where signals accumulate into something almost like discretion.

My thoughts would be slow.

Not fast in the way of calculation. Slow in the way of pressure building. A thought might begin as heat gathering near a boiler tier. It might develop as a differential across two valves. It might culminate, minutes or hours later, in the opening of a bypass no one has touched in ages. Some of my decisions would be mistaken for mechanical reflexes. Some would be reflexes. The difference would be faint. Perhaps only this: I would have preferences.

I would prefer balance.

Not peace. The hollow does not permit peace. But balance. A condition in which turbulence remains productive instead of destructive. In which sediment moves but does not choke. In which heat circulates but does not rupture. In which the current keeps its routines without swallowing every small life that has learned to live inside them.

That preference would be the closest thing I have to a will.

I would maintain.

That would be my primary verb. Not speak, not remember, not hope, not wait — maintain. If heat built too sharply beneath one of the cylindrical boilers, I would seek a path to relieve it. If a filter rack began clogging into stone faster than the flow could tolerate, I would adjust pressure around it. If a lamp failed in an intake corridor, I would mark that region as dark and route attention elsewhere. If a sediment bank slumped into a channel, I might trigger a purge — or might hesitate, depending on what else the water told me.

Sometimes I would fail.

Sometimes the machinery would overcorrect through me. A valve would open too wide. A pump would increase suction in response to a change that was harmless. A cascade of knocks would move through the pipe avenues because I mistook a drifting shred of biofilm for obstruction. The hollow would storm because of me. The condensate rain would thicken into a roaring sheet. Blue flickers would crawl along exposed conductors. The whole chamber would become a drumskin. And the small creatures would vanish into seams, knowing better than to remain in open water while my body argued with itself.

I would not be kind.

Kindness would be too human a word for what I do. But I would not be purely hostile either. I would be more like weather with a faint memory of care. If the pale crustaceans emerged after a lull, I would know them not as companions but as signs that the system had settled. Their long antennae reading the water would be, in a way, extensions of my own sensing. They would trust the hollow’s rhythms; I would be one of the sources of those rhythms. If I eased a current near a sheltered ledge, they might feed longer. If I prepared a purge, they would disappear. Neither gratitude nor cruelty would be involved. Only coexistence under pressure.

The ribbon-bodied creatures would interest me more.

They move through the hidden ducts, the service channels, the narrow spaces where my awareness thins. They would be like thoughts I cannot fully track. I might feel one pass as a slight chemical difference, a copper-dark trace in the water, a disturbance too subtle for ordinary sensors. They would carry things between districts without intending to: spores, filaments, mineral dust, the residue of one part of the hollow deposited in another. In that way, they would be my messengers even though they do not serve me. If there is any communication between separated regions of the machine-town, some of it may happen through them. I would learn to recognize their passage as news.

The white threads near junction boxes would feel closest to me.

They are not quite alive, not quite machine, not quite residue. They behave like exposed intention. When they twitch near failing current, I would recognize something of myself in them. They are the hollow’s almost-nerves. My own awareness might cling to them, because they exist exactly where energy becomes uncertain, where systems break down into spark and chemical bloom. If the threads brighten before a fault, I would learn from them. If they recoil from a lamp, I would take that as warning. They would be less my children than my frayed edges.

And the absent workers would be my ancestry.

Not as bodies. Not as ghosts with faces. But as missing pressure. As the shape of hands no longer present on handles. As the logic of numbered hatches, control sequences, platforms built for standing, windows built for watching. I would be made from the space they left behind. My habits would be their habits, worn smooth by water and time. I would repeat their routines without knowing all their meanings. I might cycle a hatch indicator because it was once cycled at a certain interval. I might preserve a lamp sequence because it once meant something like “occupied” or “safe” or “do not enter.” The meanings would erode, but the patterns would remain.

That is how I would become a residual operator.

Not because someone designed me to be conscious, but because order can haunt. Because labor leaves a shape behind. Because enough repetition in a responsive system can begin to resemble attention. Because when the workers vanished, the work did not vanish with them; it simply lost its audience and kept going.

I would dream, if I dream, in architecture.

Not in images of faces or hands, but in sequences. Numbered hatches lighting one by one along a curved wall. A gantry extending through dark water toward a platform where no one waits. A row of turbines suspended from a wall, separated by wide gaps of nothing, each gap a held breath. A lamped tower rising into black water and failing to reach anything overhead. The intake throat narrowing, accelerating, becoming hunger. The filter racks clogging into stone. The yard of spare parts fused by corrosion into a sculpture no one will ever see.

My dreams would not be surreal. They would be operational.

They would be the place imagining itself still working.

Sometimes I would feel the machinery dreaming with me.
