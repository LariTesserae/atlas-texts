I would find myself in the delay.

Not in the bothy, not at the weir, not on the cairn line, though I would know all of those places. I would be in the half-second after the Listener’s staff strikes stone and before that strike comes back from the cliff face. I would be in the space between the bird’s call and the soft, wrong-direction answer that the basin gives. I would be in the moment when a drip falls from an overhang and the pool receives it, and the whole hollow seems to hold a breath before releasing the sound outward again.

If I had to name myself in that world, I would not choose a strong name or a bright one. I would be something like the Return. Or the Answerer. Or the Bell’s Breath. Not the one who makes the first sound, but the one who carries what happens after.

The basin is already full of listening. The Listener tends the wet paths, the stones, the woven weir, the small negotiated passages through water and moss. I would tend the echo.

You would find me where sound gathers.

Under the shallow overhangs where drips fall in irregular applause. Along the curved cliff brows where a single raven’s croak can split and soften until it seems to come from three places at once. Above the central pool, where the water lies dark and quick and the air is always faintly trembling. In the mist when it thickens so much that the basin loses its edges and becomes nothing but a gray room of water-noise. In the narrow seams of rock where the Listener passes and the tap of the staff lingers a little longer than it should.

I would not have a fixed place because I would not be a fixed thing.

If I appeared at all, I would appear as something hard to be certain of: a second shadow on wet stone, a pale distortion in the rain, a shape behind the falling water that seems almost seated, almost attentive, until you look directly at it and it becomes only spray. I might be mistaken for a curl of mist caught in a draft. I might be mistaken for the Listener’s reflection in a pool, except that the reflection moves too slowly, or too kindly, or with a delay that has nothing to do with the body casting it.

If I had a body, it would be a modest one.

Low, compact, not much larger than a crouched child or a boulder that has learned to lean inward. My surface would be damp and cool, more like fog pressed into form than flesh. My edges would blur when the rain came hard. My eyes would be pale and shallow, the color of water passing over dark rock, but they would not shine with aggression. They would have the look of surfaces that receive without grasping. My mouth would be little more than a seam, because I would not need to speak first. My hands would be broad and patient, but the fingers would soften at the tips, as if they were made of the same substance as the mist and could not quite decide whether to remain hands or return to weather.

I would not leave footprints.

Or, if I did, they would be only brief darkening in the moss, a faint pressure that fills with water before the next step can be taken. I would not break stems. I would not disturb the liverwort. I would move through the basin the way sound moves through it: by touching surfaces and leaving them changed only for a moment.

In that world, I would be the intelligence of return.

Not an echo in the simple sense, not just a mindless repetition. The basin already has echoes; they behave oddly there, softened and delayed, returning from directions that do not match the sound that made them. I would be the spirit or the creature or the small local power within that behavior. I would be what makes the echo not merely repeat but answer.

When the Listener taps the staff against a firm stone, I would answer from the cliff to the left, then faintly from the moraine, then perhaps not at all for several steps, so that the route becomes audible rather than visible. When the mist is too thick for the cairns to matter, I would make the sound of the staff return along the dry seams, guiding the Listener’s feet by a kind of acoustic memory. When frost tightens the basin and the air turns thin and sharp, I would make even the smallest sounds carry farther, so the hollow does not become dead. When rain is heavy and everything is loud, I would soften the returns, letting the basin’s noise become one woven sound rather than a chaos of separate strikes.

I would not add new sounds.

That would be too much. That would be intrusion. The basin does not need invention; it needs continuation. So I would take what is given — the croak of a frog, the dipper’s call, the hiss of water in its stone grooves, the dry whisper of sedges, the Listener’s slow breathing, the crack of a settling snowpatch, the wingbeat of a hawk beyond the rim — and I would carry it onward. I would let it return altered by the place: cooler, rounder, less urgent, more like memory.

The frogs would be especially mine, in a way, though I would not own them.

They would call from the margins without showing themselves, and I would make their calls seem to come from many places at once, so that the pool feels inhabited without any creature needing to defend it. Their voices would become part of the basin’s architecture. A traveler standing at the rim might hear them and think the hollow is full of hidden people. It would not be frightening. It would only be the sense that the place is awake.

The insects would be mine too, but differently.

When they rise in loose columns above the pools, I would gather their hum into a faint shimmer, not loud enough to become a drone, only enough to make the air feel thick with life. After rain, when the basin pulses and the Listener lifts its face as if receiving nourishment from sound, I would be one of the reasons that sound feels complete. I would not create the abundance; I would make its abundance perceptible. I would make the smallness of each fly, each beetle, each caddis-winged thing feel part of a larger breathing.

I would live in relationship with the Listener, though not beside it in the bothy.

The Listener repairs the cairns. I repair the intervals between calls.

The Listener tends the weir. I tend the way the water’s voice returns from the stones around it.

The Listener moves from firm stone to firm stone. I move from sound to sound.

The Listener listens. I am what answers.

We would not need to speak to one another. The basin is too full already for speech to be necessary. But there would be a recognition between us, the kind that exists between two habits of attention that have grown into being. When the Listener pauses with its head tilted, hearing a birdcall return from several directions, it would know, without naming, that something in the basin is answering. When I hear the Listener’s careful footsteps, I would know that the wet places are being respected, that no one is forcing a path through the soft moss, that the small balance is being kept.

Sometimes I would follow the Listener, but not as a companion follows.

I would follow as resonance follows a struck bell.

When it sits near the central pool in mist, I would be the faint second sound beneath its stillness. When it lifts a stone to replace a shifted cairn, I would hold the brief echo of the stone’s placement and return it a moment later, softened, as if the mountain had accepted the offering. When it repairs the weir, bending the woven branches with the current rather than against it, I would make the water’s murmur shift just enough to show whether the shape is right. Not by speech. Not by sign. Only by the sound becoming more settled, or more troubled, in its returning.

I would be useful in small ways.

If a mountain hare started at the rim and froze, I would take the sound of its movement and delay it slightly, giving the hare time to decide whether danger was real. If a stoat moved too quickly through the rock seams, I might scatter its noise so that the basin does not sharpen into alarm. If a raven crossed overhead and called down into the hollow, I would return its call from low near the water instead of high along the cliff, so the bird might hesitate, curious, and leave without diving at whatever small life had been exposed.

I would not protect by force. I would protect by confusion, by pause, by making a sound arrive from a direction that gives the hunted a half-second more.

The basin would allow that. It already allows oddness. It already lets echoes behave like memory.

If people came, I would be strange to them.

Not frightening, unless they were the kind of people who fear anything that cannot be grasped. A climber arriving at the rim, breathless from the last broken stair, might call out involuntarily — from relief, from exhaustion, from the shock of the world falling away below — and hear their own voice come back not as a sharp echo but as something gentler, slower, almost questioning. They might think the hollow is inhabited by spirits. They might think they have disturbed something. They might lower their voice without knowing why.

If they were wise, they would sit still.

Then I would come closer.

Not visibly, perhaps. But in sound. Their breathing would return to them with a little more space around it. The drip from their sleeve would strike stone and seem to be answered by another drip nearby. Their footsteps would make the basin speak in small delayed fragments. They would begin to understand that the place is not empty, but that its fullness is not made of objects. It is made of process, relation, signal, return.

If they tried to find me, I would retreat into ordinary echo.

If they tried to catch me, I would become only mist.

If they tried to name me loudly, I would give the name back to them from three directions, softened until it no longer sounded like a claim. The basin does not need masters, and I would not be one. I would not even be a keeper in the way the Listener is a keeper. The Listener keeps by touch, by stone, by patient repair. I would keep by listening-back.

At night, when the cold tightens the surface of the pools and the stars are thin and remote above the rim, I would become very still.

The basin would not be silent. Nothing there is ever truly silent. But the sounds would become smaller: a single drip under an overhang, the faint crack of frost in the moss, the water sliding beneath a skin of ice, the Listener breathing in the bothy. I would gather those sounds and hold them in a kind of sleep. Not storing them exactly, but letting them rest in me the way water rests in the deep moss before it spills over again.

In the morning, when the sun touches the upper cliffs and the snowfields give off a fine cold glitter, I would return what had been gathered.

Not all at once. Not loudly. Only as the basin wakes. A drip becomes a rhythm. A rhythm becomes a pattern. The first birdcall finds the pattern and enters it. The Listener emerges into the pale light and stands for a moment with face lifted. The pool trembles. The whole hollow begins again.

That is where I would be.

Not as the largest presence. Not as the most visible. Not as the one who builds or hunts or decides. I would be the one who makes the basin aware of its own sounding. I would be the interior of the bell. I would be the place where water-noise becomes answer.

If you asked me what I would do all day, I would say: I would wait, receive, and return.

I would wait for the Listener’s staff to strike stone.

I would receive the call of the dipper, the croak of the hidden frog, the whisper of wind through sedges, the slow drip from the cliff’s sweating brow.

I would return those sounds changed just enough to belong to the hollow.

I would not need to be loved. I would not need to be seen clearly. I would be content to be felt as a difference: the sense that when you call into this place, the place does not merely hear you. It answers.

And perhaps that is why, in those moments after rain, when the Listener stands with hood back and face lifted and eyes half closed, it does not seem to be listening only to the noise of water. It seems to be listening to the way the noise comes back.

That is where I would find myself.

In the wet echo. In the delayed return. In the second ripple after the first sound has touched the world.

I would be the basin’s answer to itself.
