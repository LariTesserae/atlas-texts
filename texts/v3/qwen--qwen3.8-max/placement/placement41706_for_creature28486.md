If I belonged to the Dry Court, I would not be found first on the floor, nor in the highest bird galleries, nor near the seam where the plateau breathes its cold into the stone. I would be somewhere in the middle height, in one of the old architectural throats where the chamber still remembers how to breathe.

My place would be a shallow alcove in a worn gallery above the floor and below the main roosts, where a slant of green-gold light arrives only after being filtered through hanging leaves and dust. The room would be small, polished smooth by years of shoulders, hands, and sleeping bodies. A curtain of woven moth-wing and strips of bark would hang across the doorway, making a soft rasp whenever someone passed. Inside there would be a sleeping hollow lined with felted leaf-fiber, shed snake-skin, and down; a stone-caged fat-lamp; racks of sealed reed tubes; coils of insect-silk rope; and a row of small tools made from bone, wood, and beetle shell.

If an outsider looked for me, they might see only a faint shimmer of dust in the air, then a movement along the ledge: a slight figure, dust-powdered and quiet, turning its head in small exact motions as if listening to something just behind the stone.

I would be one of the warm ones.

Outsiders might call me dust-born, resin-kin, salt-sworn, or one of the dry people. Among the living inside, I would simply be one of those who keep the room. My body would be shaped by thrift: narrow, heat-loving, dry-skinned, with long fingers and broad, quiet feet. My lips would be sealed at night with wax or resin. My eyes would be large from living in green half-light, catching a faint shine when turned toward larval glow or fat-lamp flame. There would always be some pale powder on me: salt, guano-dust, stone-flour, pollen, or the fine chalk of old walls.

I would not be a hunter, not primarily. I would not be a surface farmer either. The people of the Dry Court are not farmers in the sense of open fields, rain, ploughs, and poured water. They are gardeners of enclosure, wardens of a sealed body, and I would become one of their lesser keepers: a listener to the room, a tender of hidden throats, a mender of tethers, a keeper of sealed things.

My title, if it could be translated into outside speech, might be something like “the one who hears the walls breathe,” or “the keeper of the closed mouth.” But those are clumsy renderings. In the Dry Court, a role is not only a job. It is a way of placing the body, a way of listening, a way of refusing waste.

My first work would begin before the chamber fully wakes.

I would rise while the air is still warm from the day’s stored stone-heat, before the first light shafts turn green-gold above the vines. I would not speak immediately. I would press one hand to the wall and feel for the sound of the court: the wind moving through hidden passages, the settling of nests, the tiny dry rain of husks and dust falling from upper ledges, the first stirring of beetles in the floor litter. The Dry Court is loud, but its loudness is not chaos. It is a language of dry impacts: wing-claps, seed-rattles, claw-taps, rope creaks, falling pebbles, breath through stone.

I would know when something is wrong by a change in that language.

A choked vent has a duller voice. A fraying tether makes a different strain when birds shift above it. A roost that should be waking with metallic cries but remains too still may contain sickness, or a snake has passed there in the night. A vine that should be faintly creaking under the weight of feeding hands but is silent may have been cut carelessly and left to seal its own wound.

My tools would be simple and precious: a bone pick for clearing dust from air-throats, a curved blade of hard wood for pruning without tearing, a needle of bird-bone for mending silk cords, a hollow reed for carrying sap, a small stone cage holding a bead of slow-burning fat. I would wear a cloak of beaten bark and moth-silk, stitched flat with insect thread, decorated with seedpods and hollow beetle shells that click softly when I move. The sound would not be vanity. In the Dry Court, silence can mean danger. My clothing would announce me gently: here I am, moving, living, not ill, not hostile, not outside.

I would spend much of my time in the middle levels, where the chamber is neither bright nor fully dark. There, the vines grow in thick cables over old masonry. Their roots grip ancient mortar and dissolve mineral salts. Their stems store sap. Their leaves are waxy, folded, or haired with dust-trapping fibers. Some bear rattling seedpods; others hold dry-shelled fruits with rich interiors. I would know each vine by touch and habit: which one gives too much resin if cut in the cool part of the day, which one swells with sap when the light shafts warm its upper leaves, which one must not be harmed because it anchors a nest-tether.

Harvesting would be a slow and moral act.

If I cut a sap-vine, I would do it only where custom allows. I would place a sealed tube beneath the wound and wait for the sap to bead, thick and milky, not flowing freely. It would swell slowly, stretch, and threaten to harden. I would not hurry it. I would not spill it. When enough had gathered, I would seal the tube and press the vine’s wound closed with resin if needed. If a drop fell, I would not leave it. I would touch it to my tongue, or give it to a waiting beetle, or press it into a crack where some root might find it later. To waste it would be to insult the court itself.

The proverbs would be in me:

The hand that spills is empty twice.  
What is kept inside is given by the body.  
A full room is not a wet room.

I would teach children to repeat them, not as abstract sayings but as physical habits. I would show them how to walk so that their feet do not strike stone. I would show them how to carry a seed tray without letting one husk escape. I would show them how to listen for ripeness: the faint dry rattle inside a pod, the change in weight, the smell of resin warming near a stem joint.

Children in the Dry Court would learn that thirst is not always thirst. The mouth may feel empty while the body is still sufficient. They would learn to breathe through the nose, to keep lips closed, to let saliva gather slowly, to chew the right leaves before climbing. I would teach them that desire can imitate need, and that discipline is not cruelty but love for the room that keeps them alive.

My other great care would be the air.

The Dry Court breathes through hidden passages: old chutes, cracks, vents, seams between fallen slabs, hollows behind vine-covered walls. Warm air rises, cool air sinks, dust gathers, feathers drift, insect wings fall, guano powders the ledges. If those throats clog, the room changes. Heat becomes stale. Sound becomes muffled. The smell shifts from warm resin and dry leaves to something too close, too crowded.

I would know the vents the way a surface person might know roads. I would climb to narrow places where the stone is polished by ancient air and no wider than my shoulders. I would scrape away feather-dust, dead moths, seed-husks, and threads of spider-silk. I would not use water to clean. I would use brushes of stiff fiber, bone picks, and patient breath. Sometimes I would tie a length of silk to my waist and let myself down into a chute too steep to climb, listening all the while to the chamber’s amplified noises around me: a beetle taking flight like a small drumbeat, a flock shifting like sudden applause, someone arguing in a distant gallery until the words bounce into almost music.

When I finished, the air would move again. The sound would open. Somewhere below, a child might look up and feel the difference without knowing why.

I would also tend some of the smaller lights.

Open flame is too dangerous in the Dry Court. Resin, dust, moth-wing, dried leaves, old bark, silk: everything remembers fire. So the people keep heat enclosed. I would maintain fat-lamps in stone cages, trimming wicks of plant fiber, feeding them with rendered insect fat or bird oil. I would keep ember-boxes lined with salt-crusted clay, buried in safe niches, their heat used for baking seed-cakes or warming resin. I would know where the compost pockets are, those quiet heaps of decay where plant matter breaks down without wet rot and gives off steady warmth.

At night, when the upper cracks let the plateau’s cold press down through the stone, I would move among the lamps and ember-boxes, making sure none were smothered, none too hot, none near a hanging nest or a curtain of dry fiber. My own lamp would be small, gold-brown, intimate. It would not flare. It would bead and sway, turning nearby leaves lacquered and making human faces look like masks of amber.
