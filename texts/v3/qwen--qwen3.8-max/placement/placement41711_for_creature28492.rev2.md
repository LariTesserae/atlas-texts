I would not find myself there as a person.

The basin would refuse that shape. Too much water in the body, too much warmth in the blood, too many expectations of doors, roofs, voices, and use. If I stood there in an ordinary human form, I would feel like an error: a soft, breathing interruption in a place that has learned to exist without softness. The town would not rise to meet me. The streets would not become streets because I walked them. The hollow towers would not become shelters because I needed shelter. I would be a visitor too large for the logic of the place, and the place would remain indifferent to my passing.

So if I am to find myself there at all, I must become smaller.

Drier.

Quieter.

Something whose life can be measured in seams rather than days.

At first, when I imagine looking for myself in that sunken mineral hollow, I see only traces. A faint line across a salt shelf. A dark stain along a protected wall. A mote caught in a pale shaft of sourceless light. A thread withdrawn into a crack. I keep searching for a body, but the world offers only suggestions of bodies: hard shapes, thin shapes, patient shapes, shapes that are nearly indistinguishable from the mineral surfaces they inhabit.

I might be one of the crusts.

For a while that possibility holds me. I imagine myself as a grey-green skin on the shaded side of a tower, adhering to a seam where charged dust collects. I would not move in any way the eye could follow. My life would be the slow binding of particles. My work would be stabilization: making one surface slightly more able to receive the next layer. I would thicken walls without knowing they were walls. I would help close an opening over centuries, or preserve one by holding its edge firm against fracture. I would glow faintly in the deepest cracks, not to signal, not to communicate, but because something in my chemistry still answered the absence of light with a weak green trace.

There is a beauty in that. A humility. To be less a creature than a surface condition. To be a living cement. To belong to the town as a kind of skin.

But I do not think I would remain there.

The crust is too fixed. Too patient in one direction. It shapes the world by staying still, and I feel in myself some need to move across the town, not merely thicken it. I want to know the distances between the towers. I want to feel the difference between open pan and hollow chamber. I want to pass from one geometry to another and leave some faint record of the crossing.

So I become smaller, harder, more articulated.

Perhaps I am a worm inside the wall.

That thought places me in darkness. I am thread-thin, pale, pressed against the inner face of a hollow salt tower. My body is more fiber than flesh. I extend filaments into charged air and draw in what little the basin gives: dust, trace particles, the microscopic debris of crusts. I open vents so small they would not deserve the name of holes in a wetter world. I widen hairline fractures by abrasion so slow it is almost theoretical. When a shadow passes outside, I recoil into the wall. My whole life is lived inside the architecture, touching its interiors, altering its airflow, creating tiny passages that no larger body will ever use.

This is closer.

There is something true about being inside the walls rather than standing before them. The town’s strangeness is not only in its visible forms but in its hollows, its hidden channels, the way its empty spaces participate in its being. To live inside a wall would be to live inside the town’s grammar. I would not see the streets; I would feel them as pressure differences, thermal shifts, faint currents of dry air moving through mineral pores.

But even that is too concealed.

The worm belongs to the wall more than to the town. It shapes the town indirectly, invisibly, almost accidentally. I think I want some small visible life. Not abundance. Not presence in the ordinary sense. Just enough presence to cross an open place and leave a mark.

Then the beetle comes to mind.

Not as an animal in the warm, familiar sense. Not as something soft or quick or eager. I mean the pale, ceramic, angular thing that crosses the salt shelf and pauses as if listening with its legs. I mean the body the color of old porcelain, matte and faintly ridged, moving in short precise bursts between shelters. I mean the creature that does not fly, does not sing, does not gather, but travels. It reads the ground through contact. It tastes charge. It follows gradients. It disappears into fissures when the world becomes too exposed.

Yes.

If I must be one being in that place, I would be that.

Or something very like it.

A pale seam-walker.

A dry crawler with pointed legs and a shell like fired mineral. Not large enough to cast a meaningful shadow, but large enough to leave a track. Not intelligent in the way of makers, but attentive in the way of things that must survive by reading subtle forces. My body would be hard, anhydrous, almost brittle. My movements would be angular, rehearsed, economical. I would not wander romantically through the town. I would move from shelter to shelter, from fissure to fissure, following the hidden lines along which dust and charge migrate.

I would find myself near the middle distance of the basin, where the open crust meets the clustered vertical forms. Not in the tallest tower. Not in the broadest plaza. Not at the rim. I would be in the interval between structure and openness: a shallow channel between two fluted salt masses, perhaps, where a row of broken pillars leans at irregular angles and the ground is divided into shallow cells by cracking. There would be a charged ridge of dust nearby, temporary, already beginning to collapse as the electrostatic field shifts. Beyond it, a hollow tower with a high narrow gap. Beneath that gap, a darker seam where a crust has cemented the wall enough to keep it from shedding grains.

That is where I would pause.

One leg touching a salt seam.

Body still.

Sensing.

Not with eyes exactly, and not with ears. The basin would not support a face like mine. I would have no need for expression. My awareness would be distributed through contact: vibration in the crust, thermal difference across a plate, the faint pull of static along the edges of my shell. I would know the town as a field of tensions. A tower would not be a tower to me but a vertical disturbance in temperature and airflow. A street would not be a street but a line of easier movement. A doorway would not be an invitation but a pressure gradient, a gap where charged particles behave differently.

I would be a reader of seams.

That, I think, is who I would be.

Not a citizen. Not a builder. Not an inhabitant in the sense of one who settles and names. I would be a tenant of intervals. A small hard body moving through the town’s empty spaces, leaving marks that may become part of its future geometry.

My home would not be a room.

It would be a sequence of absences.

A fissure beneath a leaning colonnade. A vent at the base of a hollow shaft. A crack behind a brittle arch. A lower chamber where a scavenger once worked over a husk and left fine fragments behind. I would not own these places. I would use them briefly, then move on. The town would not know me. It would not provide for me in any intentional way. It would simply offer gaps, and I would be small enough to enter them.

I would eat, if eating can be used for such a life, by scraping. Crust films. Mineral deposits on charged surfaces. Dead filaments. Perhaps the faint biological layers that grow where shadow and electrostatic dust overlap. My feeding would leave tiny marks: pale scratches, shallow grooves, small disturbances in the powder. Nothing dramatic. Nothing that would look like consumption to a larger eye. But repeated often enough, along the same route, those marks could become something else.

A groove.

A channel.

A path.

This is the part that draws me most.

The beetle does not build the town, yet its movement may become part of the town’s architecture. A track repeated over time can become a groove. A groove can guide dust. Dust can gather or be deflected. Crust can form along the edge of a repeated line. Eventually what began as the habit of one small creature may harden into a minor street no one intended. The creature does not know this. It does not walk with purpose toward urban creation. It merely moves where movement is easiest, or safest, or where charge draws it. But the place inherits the mark.

I would like to be that kind of being.

Not a maker, but a cause of forms.

Not an architect, but a line of pressure in the future.

If you searched for me, you would not find me standing in the center of a plaza like a hero or a ghost. You would have to look closely. You would have to watch the open salt shelf long enough for movement to register as movement. A pale shape, quick and angular. A pause near a fissure. A slight disturbance in the dust. Then absence. I would be gone into the crack before you decided whether you had seen me at all.

My day, if day is the word, would consist of crossings and stillness.

I would emerge when the thermal stress in the basin eased enough that the salt stopped ticking sharply. I would move across a pale crust pan where dust devils no larger than a hand span twisted briefly and died. I would angle toward a colonnade because the spacing of its pillars created zones of shelter. I would not admire the colonnade. I would not think it beautiful. But I would use its shadow-lines and charge-lines. I would pass between two leaning pillars, feel the air change near a hollow tower, and turn toward a narrow opening too low for any body like yours.

Inside, the town would become more intimate.

The walls would be close. The light would narrow. The dust would be finer. I would feel the faintest current moving through a worm-made vent. I would pause in a chamber mostly composed of air, where a high gap admitted a dull glow from the sky. There might be a filament hanging from an upper lip, motionless. I would not know it as predator except as a small zone of danger: a place where the charge felt wrong, where motes disappeared too quietly. I would move beneath it carefully, or not at all.

In that chamber, I might rest.

Not sleep. Not dream. Just lower my activity until I became almost continuous with the wall. My shell would take on the temperature of the salt. My legs would press into microscopic irregularities. The town would tick around me: a tiny contraction here, a grain dislodged there, a high thin note from some aperture stressed by cooling. I would not hear these sounds as music. They would be conditions. Signals of whether to remain or move.

Then I would go on.

Perhaps I would cross an area where the ground was divided into shallow cells, like a plan drawn by cracking. Perhaps I would follow a ridge that seemed almost deliberate, though no intention had made it. Perhaps I would pass one of the upright ash-grey forms standing in a sheltered corner, jointed and leafless, absorbing the dim light. I would not mistake it for a plant. I would not mistake it for a person. I would recognize it, insofar as a creature like me recognizes anything, as a vertical obstacle that altered airflow. I might pass beside it. A spore-thread might leave it while I was nearby, a dark filament departing into stillness. I would not follow it. But my movement might disturb the charged dust beneath it, and that disturbance might settle somewhere else as a thin line.

Even my ignorance would contribute.

That is what I mean by being a seam-walker.

I would live in the place without understanding it. I would be shaped by its rules and would shape it back without intention. I would be one of the small dry continuations the basin permits. Not abundant. Not central. Not meaningful in the way conscious beings wish to be meaningful. But real. A body. A hunger. A route. A pause. A disappearance.

If you asked who I would be, I would say: a small pale crawler, hard-shelled and silent, sensitive to charge and fracture.

If you asked what I would be, I would say: a moving shard with legs. A listener without ears. A mapper without memory. A punctuation mark in a vast blank page.

If you asked where I would be, I would say: between things.

Between the colonnade and the open pan. Between the hollow tower and the cracked terrace. Between the dust ridge and the fissure. Between the town’s mineral architecture and its sparse life. Between visibility and vanishing. I would be found at the edge of a salt shelf, pausing before crossing, already half gone into a crack.

But if you pressed me further — if you asked not for the body but for the deeper shape of me in that world — I would say something stranger.

I would be the track.

Not merely the creature making it, but the line left behind. The faint groove that may become a channel. The channel that may become a minor street. The repeated passage that no one planned and no one uses, yet which gives the town one more resemblance to settlement. I would be the habit that hardens into form. The movement that becomes geometry. The small life that disappears into its own trace.

That feels most true.

In a place where nothing is made by tools, yet much is organized by process, I would not want to be a builder. I would want to be one of the processes by which the town dreams itself into shape. I would want my life to be so slight that it could not be separated from the landscape. I would want my passage to become a line in the crust, no more. I would want my hunger to leave a groove, my caution to create a shelter-line, my vanishing to widen a crack just enough for the next small life to enter.

Not a person.

Not a ghost.

Not a mind watching from the towers.

A dry, hard, patient crossing.

A pale body moving through the empty streets of a town that has no citizens, yet is not empty.

A seam-reader.

A track-maker.

A small angular presence that pauses, listens to the salt complain, and disappears into the nearest fissure before the light changes.
