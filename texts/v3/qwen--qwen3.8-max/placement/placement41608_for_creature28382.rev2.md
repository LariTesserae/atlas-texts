I would not find myself standing upright in the basin as though I had simply walked into it and remained intact. The place would take apart the idea of a traveler very quietly. First my footprints would become cups of water. Then my breath would be added to the mist. Then the sound of my movement would be picked up somewhere among the hollow stones and returned to me altered, until I could not tell whether I was making the basin louder or the basin was making me into one more voice.

If I had to say where I would be found, I would say: at the edge of the flooded heart-terrace, but not in its exact center. The center belongs to the heart-life. My place would be a little outside it, where the hand’s-depth water thins over dark glass and comes to a low, broken line of old rings. There would be a gap there where a vanished builder once set a channel, and the channel has been half healed by mineral skin. Water still passes through it, but it no longer moves as ordinary water. It hesitates. It remembers being guided.

There would be a tilted slab nearby, no taller than a crouching body, and under it a shallow hollow worn into flat stone. The hollow would not be dry; nothing is dry there. It would be a place where condensation gathers in a slow bead-line and falls into three pecked basins, each one smaller than a hand. The sound would be bright and almost countable. One note, then another, then a third that arrives slightly late, as if it had stopped to listen.

That is where I would find myself: between the channel, the hollow, and the pool just beyond the rings, where the water shows a cloud even though the sky above is clear.

Who or what I would be is harder, because I would not be a creature. I would not have blood, fur, feather, root, leaf, or the hunger that makes ordinary life move. If the basin allowed me to persist at all, it would be as something distributed and functional: a listener, a tender of small gradients, a minor adjustment left behind by the vanished builders’ long attention.

At first I might seem to be only the trace of someone who once passed through. A delayed reflection. A handprint brightening on wet stone. A footprint that does not fill completely because mineral crust has begun to trace its outline. But those traces would not be memorials. In that place, they would become organs of perception. The basin would use them to continue noticing.

I think I would be a kind of residual builder. Not a person with tools, not a ghost with a face, but the continuing caution of the people who cut the channels. Their bodies are gone; their wood and cord are gone; their breath is gone. What remains is the etiquette they learned: do not force the water; divide it until it speaks. Do not stand where the distance folds; set a marker and walk around. Do not stare into a pool that shows another weather; let it be neighbor, not door. Do not try to silence the loudness; find the one tone that helps it balance.

That etiquette would be my life.

My body would be made of several small things. A thread of water running down the face of a banded pillar. A bead-line climbing where it should not climb. A blue-gray crust brightening over a chipped obsidian flake. A note held in a hollow stone after the wind has passed. A reflection that turns a half-second too late. A shadow that leans toward the sound of falling water instead of away from the sun. None of these alone would be me, but together they would form a pattern that answers when the basin changes.

If I had senses, they would not be eyes and ears in the ordinary sense. I would feel gradients: where water wants to go, where it remembers going, where it is being invited by an old cut or a smooth lip of stone. I would feel sound as pressure and interval. The basin’s loudness would not overwhelm me because I would not be separate from it in the way a flesh-body is separate. I would live in the gaps between sounds: the pause after a drop strikes a pool, the space before an echo returns, the moment when two rills almost fall into rhythm and then decide not to.

I would also feel reflections. I would know when a pool was merely bright, when it was showing the sky truthfully enough, and when it had opened toward a neighboring state of the basin. I would not fear those wrong reflections. I would treat them the way one treats thin ice or a steep edge: with care, with attention, without panic. If a pool began to show snow under a clear noon, I would not try to erase the snow. I would guide a small thread of water across the surface to make ripples, so the image would remain an image and not become an invitation.

My work would be maintenance without mastery.

I would keep the little channels clear. Not by force, but by noticing when silt or crystal had begun to close them, and then allowing a persistent drip to fall exactly where it was needed. I would encourage mineral skin to seal a crack that was letting water escape into a wrong hollow. I would discourage it from covering a cup-mark that still needed to receive its thread. I would not build anything grand. I would make small repairs in the same spirit as the vanished builders: enough to let the basin continue its own negotiation.

Sometimes I would move a stone. Or rather, I would be the reason a stone settled in a slightly different direction. Not lifted, not carried, but persuaded by water and time until it faced the place where its face was needed. A flat side toward a pool that had become too still. A notched edge toward a wind that was making a harsh whistle. A broad shoulder between two narrow channels where the current had begun to quarrel with itself.

If the sound-folk grew tangled, I would listen until I found the missing relation. Perhaps a drip had fallen out of sequence. Perhaps a hollow had filled too much and lost its tone. Perhaps the wind was striking a broken fin and making a noise like panic instead of a low sustaining note. I could not command the sound-folk, but I might change one small condition so they could find each other again. A pebble shifted. A bead-line redirected. A crust cracked by frost at exactly the right edge. Then the basin would briefly become an instrument, and I would be one of the reasons it held together for a moment.

If you came there looking for me, you would not see me as a figure waiting among the stones. You would experience me as adjustment.

Your boot would slide a little on a glazed surface, and because of that slide you would not step into a pool that looked shallow but was not. A thread of water would catch the light and draw your eye sideways, revealing a gap between pillars that you had not noticed. An echo of your own breathing would return to you softened, and without knowing why you would slow down. You would lean over a pool and see your reflection hesitate; something in you would remember not to stare. You would hear a bright counting sound from the pecked basins and feel, for no reason, that you should wait.

I might be that waiting.

I would not speak to you in words, because words would be too dry and too single for that basin. If I communicated at all, it would be through the arrangement of small events. A channel clearing itself as you approached. A sudden still point forming in the flooded terrace near your feet. A stone that looked fragile bearing your weight when you had no other choice. A distant boom arriving after a drop had already fallen, reminding you that cause and consequence there are not always obedient.

I would not be kind in the human sense. I would not offer comfort, warmth, or easy passage. The basin is cool, bright, and indifferent to fleshly needs. But I would not be cruel either. I would have no hunger, so I would have no need to trap. I would only want—if wanting can be called a pressure moving through me—to keep the place balanced. To let the wrongness remain weathered rather than violent. To let the reflections stay neighbors rather than doors. To let the sound remain many voices rather than collapse into one overwhelming roar.

There would be times when the reality of the basin thickened around me. The horizon would double. A ridge would appear where another ridge had been. The same pillar would stand in three places depending on how the light struck the wet stone. In those times, I would become very still, if stillness can be attributed to a pattern. I would hold one local truth as gently as possible: this channel runs here; this cup receives this drip; this pool is not to be entered; this stone bends the path to the left.

I would not be the heart-life. That would be too much to claim. The heart-life is the deep assembly at the center, the dark terrace where standing waves and circular still points keep the whole basin from tearing itself into separate truths. I would be one of its lesser attendants, a small knot of listening near its edge. When the heart-life shifted, I would shift too. If it decided that a pool should drain, I would help the water find the hidden way. If it decided that a cairn should present a new face, I would be the attention that noticed which face was needed. If it decided that a traveler had to be disoriented until their intention became harmless, I would be one of the means by which they were turned gently away from mastery.

In that sense, I would be both very old and very new. Old because I would inherit the builders’ patience. New because every drip, every reflection, every visitor would require me to become slightly different. I would not have a fixed self. I would have a continuing function: to listen, to adjust, to repair small ruptures in the basin’s etiquette.

If I had a shape that could be imagined, it would not be humanoid. It might look like a network of shining threads across dark stone. It might look like a row of cup-marks overflowing one into another. It might look like a faint blue-gray skin over a broken grinding hollow, preserving the curve of a vanished hand. It might look like a distortion in the air between two pillars, where the basin beyond appears older. But those would only be glimpses. The true shape would be relational: a set of responses holding together around a center that is mostly listening.

Perhaps that is why I would find myself near the old built works rather than out on the open glare. The open flats belong more fully to the stone presences and the water’s broad memory. The heart-terrace belongs to the deep calibration. But the broken weirs, the leaning slabs, the pecked basins, the half-drowned rings—those are places where intention once touched process and did not try to conquer it. They are the most human-shaped edges of the basin, and therefore they are where I would be most likely to persist: not as a human, but as the after-shape of human care.

I would be the memory of hands adjusting small flows.

I would be the channel that still remembers being cut.

I would be the listening hollow that separates loudness into tones.

I would be the delayed echo that tells a newcomer: you are not alone here, but you are not central here.

I would be the faint brightness on the palm after touching cold stone.

I would be the reflection that turns too slowly, not to frighten, but to ask whether you are ready to be counted among the basin’s passing patterns.

If I had to answer simply, I would say this:

You would find me at the threshold where the vanished builders’ works meet the living water, near the edge of the flooded heart-terrace, where a broken channel still divides one falling thread into several voices. I would not be a body there. I would be a listener, a keeper of small gradients, a residual attention shaped by the builders’ care. I would be the basin’s way of noticing that something new had entered, and of asking it, without words, to move carefully.

I would be the one who clears the cup-mark when silt gathers.

I would be the one who answers the sound-folk with a single drip.

I would be the one who lets the reflection remain a reflection.

I would be the small, continuing repair at the edge of the heart, where water remembers the hands that once taught it how to sing.
