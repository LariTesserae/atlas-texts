I would find myself in the seam.

Not at the rim, not standing at the fissure with the void behind me and the whole pocket spread below like a model. Not in the bright blade of sunlight when it cuts down from the high sky. Not in the open market lanes where traders lay bright objects on felt, and not in the upper gantries where the birds fold themselves into iron for the night. I would be lower than that, closer in, where the city thickens and the walls lean near enough to touch from either side if I stretched my arms. I would be in the space between: between wall and machine, between terrace and duct, between the last repair and the next crack.

At first I think I would be a person there, because the pocket is so crowded with people that it is hard to imagine being anything else. But not a prominent person. Not a guild-master with tags on both sleeves, not a lift-caller with a whistle, not a furnace-tender with orange light on my face. I would be one of the quiet ones who move along the edges of the work. A listener. A reader of small faults. The kind of person others step around without noticing until the moment they need me.

They would call me a seam-scribe, or perhaps a hairline listener. My trade would be the first sign of breaking.

I would carry a short hammer, but not for striking hard. I would use it to tap walls, beams, salt faces, ceramic housings, iron struts wrapped in old cloth. I would know the sound of soundness. The clean, close note of stone that holds. The deadened knock of a pocket opening behind salt. The thin tick of metal fatigued at a joint. The false, soft whisper of dust settling where it should not settle. My hands would be dry and careful. My fingertips would be bare so I could feel vibration before I trusted it to instruments. I would wear thin gloves elsewhere, because the cold metal bites and the salt can abrade skin into redness, but the last joints of my fingers would always be exposed, always reading.

Around my neck I would carry a cord with small tools: a ceramic stylus for marking, a waxed thread for measuring drift, a smooth discharge stone, a braided grounding wire, a folded square of filter cloth. In a sealed pouch against my ribs I would keep a tiny flask, no larger than my hand, with water enough for three careful sips. Not for drinking casually. For emergency, for ritual, for the moment when someone else’s mouth is too dry with fear or dust to speak. I would never offer it openly. I would shield it with my body if I poured. Even then, I would probably not pour at all. Maybe I would only press the mouth of the flask to another person’s lips for a second, then seal it again, and neither of us would mention the kindness except by the way we stood afterward.

My clothes would be patched and practical. A high-collared dust cloak, gray with use and pale at the elbows where salt has rubbed it. A hood that can be drawn quickly when the air thickens. Boots with felted soles, quiet on gratings. A mask hanging at my jaw most of the time, ready to pull up when dust lifts in a slow column or when a chute coughs powder into the lane. My cloak would have many small loops and ties because in the pocket nothing is carried loosely. Everything that can fall will fall. Everything that can snag will snag. Everything that can be lost to a crack or a duct will vanish unless fastened.

I would live in a narrow place, because privacy is a luxury of width and the pocket has none. My sleeping shelf would be cut into the wall near a warm duct, not so close that the heat would make me sweat—sweat would be too costly—but close enough that the stone would not pull the warmth from my bones during the dark hours. The shelf would be lined with pressed mat-fiber and old cloth, and above it there would be a row of hooks and tags. Each tag would mark a right: a place to hang tools, a share in a cable route, a claim to a certain amount of filtered air during a bad dust turn. My name would be less important than my tags. If someone needed to find me, they would say, “The listener who keeps the west seam near the old crusher housing,” and that would be enough.

Near my shelf there would be a paper lizard. I would not own it exactly. It would simply live there, flattened against the warm casing beside the duct, its pale papyrus skin rising and falling with almost invisible breath. It would watch me with one dry, patient eye when I came home, and I would leave it dead moths sometimes, not because it needed them but because the gesture would feel like companionship. In the pocket, affection is maintenance. You do not say, “I care for you,” and leave it hanging in the air. You clean someone’s filter. You tighten their grounding strap. You move a loose stone from their path. You save a spot of warmth for them. You remember which wall they sleep against and tap it twice a day to be sure it is still true.

My work would take me through the whole vertical body of the settlement.

In the morning—if morning can be called by the thinning of dark and the first amber lamps coming on along the lower terraces—I would start high, where the rim fissure admits a grayness more than light. There I would check the upper nests and anchor points, the bird cages, the cable eyes set into pale rock. The air there would be coldest. My breath would want to become visible, but the dryness would steal it before it could. I would touch discharge plates before touching iron. I would look for new cracks in the overhang, for dust trails that do not match yesterday’s, for the tiny bright scratches that mean stone has shifted in the night.

Then I would descend into the middle levels, where the city is densest. There the walkways are roofs, the roofs are platforms, the platforms are floors, and the floors are lids over other rooms. People would move past me in close, practiced silence. A hand would touch my shoulder to pass. I would step aside without thinking. Someone would hand a bundle over a rail. A child would slide down a cable with a leather loop, fast and soundless, and land with a soft thump on felted boots. An inspector would nod to me. I would nod back. Words would be few. We would not need many.

My real work would begin where the public passages thin into service gaps. I would duck under a pipe bundle. I would climb a short ladder whose rungs have been polished by gloves. I would enter a crawlspace behind a switchboard, where the air smells of hot dust and faint lightning. There I would pause. I would listen.

The pocket’s sounds are not loud, but they are full. The hum of enclosed turbines. The click of cooling metal. The whisper of powder moving where it should not. The faint percussion of beetles in lower corners. The soft rain of centipede legs inside ductwork. The almost inaudible pressure-change of something large turning far below. I would learn to separate all of these. I would know when a machine’s pitch had risen by the smallest degree. I would know when silence was wrong.

In those hidden places I would keep small records. Not books, because paper is too precious and dust too hungry. I would scratch marks on dark boards, chalk lines on salt, notches on metal tags. I would tie colored threads around pipes to show that a seam had been checked, that a brace had been advised, that a panel should not be opened until the charge was bled away. My records would be humble, but they would matter. A crack ignored becomes a collapse. A static charge ignored becomes a fire no one can afford. A dust drift ignored becomes a choked filter, a stalled fan, a room full of people breathing worse air than they should.

I would be trusted to say stop.

That would be the strangest part of my standing. I would not command many people. I would not own much space. But if I pressed my hand to a wall and felt the wrongness in it, I could call a halt. I could tell a lift-caller to hold the load. I could tell a workshop to cut power. I could tell a family to move their sleeping shelf for three nights until the wall was braced. No one would thank me extravagantly. They would simply move. Because in the pocket, warning is love disguised as inconvenience.

I think I would be good at this work because I would not hate the smallness.

Some people in such a place would dream of the horizon. They would stand under the rim fissure when the birds came through and imagine the open sky beyond the ridges. They would hate the closeness, the stacked rooms, the pressure of so many bodies and claims in one stone bowl. I might understand that longing. But I do not think it would be my deepest feeling. I think I would be drawn instead to the detail. To the way dust moves like a slow liquid along the base of a wall. To the way a lamp cage throws hexagons of shadow onto salt. To the way a patch of bone-scale mat lifts at the edge when the air changes. To the way a copper beetle ticks over iron like a tiny clock. To the way a hairline crack can tell you a story of stress, heat, weight, and time if you are willing to bend close enough.

I would become someone who notices.

That is the first answer. But as I sit with it, the image does not stay still. The pocket itself resists being fixed in one shape. It shifts, repairs, reorganizes. So perhaps I would not remain only a person. Perhaps the place would wear me down to something smaller, or spread me into something more distributed.

Sometimes I think I would be one of the switch-lives.

Not a creature anyone could point to easily. Not something with a name in a child’s lesson. A pale, threadlike presence inside old housings, living where insulation frays and charge leaves its invisible taste on metal. I would be tiny enough to hide in the space between two ceramic sockets. I would feed on damaged material, on chemical change, on the faint static film that clings to charged surfaces. My existence would be known mostly by absence of disaster. A fault that never happens. A surge that smooths itself. A black grain on a contact plate that means something has been cleaned away before it could bridge dangerously.

If I were such a thing, I would not be lonely. I would live among others like me, a colony of small eaters, a quiet guild of fault-preventers. Engineers would curse us when we became too many. They would open panels with ceramic tools and coax us into grounding plates. They would say we were pests, but they would not destroy us entirely, because a panel with no life in it is a panel that fails in worse ways. I would like that. To be resented and necessary. To be unseen and useful. To keep a city alive by gnawing away the beginnings of disaster.

Sometimes I think I would be charge-lace.

That is stranger. Not one body, but a pattern. A filamentous grazing of light and behavior across charged surfaces, appearing when the air is dry enough and the potential strong enough. I would move over metal and salt in loops and branching lines, too regular to be accident, too fleeting to be mapped. The workers would call me circuit-bloom or quiet-thread. When I gathered around a busbar, they would lower the load. When I vanished, they would check the grounding. I would become an omen with practical value. Not worshipped, not feared exactly, but watched. A sign written by the pocket’s own nervous system.

If I were charge-lace, I would be the visible edge of attention. I would appear where energy wanted to speak. I would trace the paths that current might take if it were allowed to forget its discipline. I would be beautiful for a moment, then gone into the metal’s patience.

Sometimes I think I would be a moth.

A pale, narrow thing with powder on its wings, clustering near lamp cages not out of longing for light but because warmth and charge gather there, because dead skins and old mat-dust ride the small currents. I would live close to human fire without needing their water. My larvae would eat what others discarded. My wing-dust would be gathered to test leaks. I would be fragile, but not weak. I would know the city by heat signatures and faint smells. I would fold myself against a warm pipe at night while the lamps dimmed, and if a child saw me, they might think me ordinary. But I would carry the pocket on my wings in the finest sense: salt, dust, fungal spore, machine residue, the microscopic evidence of many lives pressed into powder.

And sometimes, when the lamps are low and the settlement breathes more slowly, I think I would be one of the pulse-shapes in the mats.

A small translucent knot moving through bone-scale crust, brightening and dimming like a thought too shy to finish. I would not speak. I would not need to. I would pulse in the dark where the mats thicken behind warm stone, and perhaps a child would whisper to me, and perhaps an old mat-tender would glance over and decide I meant the bed was healthy. I would be one of the pocket’s private signals, a living lamp no one owns. If a family left a scrap of warmed metal near the wall for me after a birth, I would not understand it as worship. I would understand it as acknowledgment. There are many kinds of warmth. Some answer.

But if I must give a single answer, I would say this: I would be a seam-scribe who has become too much a part of the pocket to remain entirely separate from it.

You would find me in the middle levels, near the place where the old crusher housing leans against a salt face and the walkway narrows to a single-file ledge. There is a switchboard there with a patched wooden panel and a cage of lamps behind wire. There is a duct that exhales warm, dry air at intervals. There is a crack in the wall that has been braced three times and keeps trying to become something worse. That is where I would be. Not at the center of power, not at the edge of the void, but at the point of strain.

I would be the one crouched with a hand on the stone.

My hood would be down unless the dust was bad. My mask would hang at my jaw. My eyes would be adjusted to amber and shadow. My right hand would be bare to the fingertips. A small hammer would rest in my palm, not swinging, just resting. I would be listening for the pocket’s next small confession.

People passing would know me. They would not stop unless they had to. Some would touch the wall near me as they went by, not from superstition but from habit, the way sailors might touch a rail. Some would leave me a new filter on the ledge without saying anything. Some would ask, “Is it worse?” and I would answer, “Not yet,” or “Move the load,” or “Bring the brace-team before the next turn.” My voice would be low. Even if I wanted to shout, the place would have taught me better by then.

I would know the Undermill by feel more than by sight.

I would not descend to its chamber often. Few would. But I would feel it through the stone. The slow pressure-sound below, the vast turning that is half machine and half metabolism, half memory and half hunger. I would know its moods in the soles of my boots. When it ran deep and even, the walls would feel settled. When it slowed, the whole pocket would grow tense in ways no one would admit aloud. I would press my hand to the floor sometimes, not because I could fix anything from there, but because I would want to know that it continued. That the pocket still had its low heart. That whatever strange negotiation of iron, mats, chemical crust, beetle remains, and old lightning still turned below us, it was still willing to keep us.

I would not pray to it. I would maintain toward it.

That is the pocket’s faith, if it has one. Not praise, but care. Not sacrifice, but attention. Not the wish for permanence, but the discipline of repair.

In the evening, when the high slit of sky darkens and the lamps bloom one by one behind their cages, I would come back to my shelf near the warm duct. The paper lizard would be there. The switchboard nearby would hum. A mat in the shadow behind it would glow faintly, green-blue, like a secret the wall is not trying to tell. I would sit for a while without moving. My cloak would be dusty at the shoulders. My hands would smell of salt and iron. My throat would be dry, but I would not drink immediately. I would let the stillness settle first.

Then I would take the small flask from my pouch. I would turn my back to the passage, even if no one was looking, because the habit would be deeper than modesty. I would drink one sip. Maybe two. Then seal the flask and return it to my chest. The water would not feel like luxury. It would feel like a promise kept inside the body.

A child might come by then, perhaps one of the quick vertical children who know the rails and cracks better than their own histories. She might ask what I do all day. I would think for a moment, because the true answer is too large for a child and perhaps too strange for an adult.

“I listen before the wall speaks,” I might say.

She would consider this. Then she would ask, “What if it speaks anyway?”

And I would answer, “Then we listen faster.”

She would laugh, close and stifled, and run off along the walkway with her hands already reaching for the next rail. I would watch her go, not with sadness, but with the pocket’s own fierce, dry tenderness. She would grow up knowing how to read dust, how to move without wasting breath, how to love by fixing, how to fear by paying attention. She would not need green fields to feel that the world was full. She would have enough in the pressure of bodies, the warmth of machines, the tick of beetles, the slow glow of mats, the nearness of everything that mattered.

And I would be part of that nearness.

Not above it. Not outside it. Not a visitor looking down into the bowl with wonder and escape still in my mind. I would be inside the mechanism of survival, one small nervous ending in a city that has learned to feel itself constantly. If a crack opened, I would be among the first called. If a lamp flickered, I would glance up with everyone else. If the charge-lace wrote itself across a plate, I would lean close and try to read the shape of the warning. If the Undermill changed its note, I would stop whatever I was doing and place my palm flat against the nearest stone.

Who would I be?

I would be the pocket’s attention.

Not a god. Not a ruler. Not a single visible hero. A distributed being made of human hands, animal smallness, machine hum, and mineral patience. A listener in the seams. A keeper of tags and warnings. A creature with bare fingertips and a sealed flask and a habit of touching walls. A mind that lives in the pause before repair. A life that understands permanence not as stillness, but as the ability to change carefully, quickly, together.

If you looked for me there, you would almost miss me.

You would see a hooded figure crouched beside a braced wall, half-hidden behind a pipe bundle. You would see a small hammer, a discharge stone, a frayed grounding braid. You would see a paper lizard near the figure’s shoulder, flattened against warmth. You would see dust on their sleeves. You would see their lips move, not in speech, but in counting. You would hear almost nothing. Then you would hear the faintest tick of stone, and the figure would already be rising, already reaching for the wall, already calling in a low voice for the next repair.

That is where I would find myself.

In the seam. In the dry, crowded, luminous dark. In the place where the world is not green, not wet, not open, but alive in its own severe grammar. I would not be waiting for rain. I would be listening for the crack. And when it came, I would be there with the others, changing the city just enough to remain.
