I would find myself already there, because arrival would require a path, and the hollow has none. The clouds would not part for me. They would continue beneath the rim like slow grey water, and whatever I called “I” would be on the inside of the bowl, among the wet stone, inside the loudness.

At first I would probably misread myself. I would seem to be a patch of dark dampness on the newly broken rock, somewhere between the black marsh-pool and a cushion of moss. No larger than a crouched body. No shape worth naming. The stone there would be cold and sharp-edged, mineral and unsmoothed, and I would lie in one of those wide bare intervals the hollow keeps between its living islands. Not in the moss. Not in the sedge. Not in the pool. In the expectant space where the rock is wet but not yet claimed.

That would be the first clue: I would not be crowded. I would not be lush. I would be the part of the arrangement that allows the other parts to remain separate.

If a beetle crossed me, its polished feet would make faint metallic sounds, and I would know myself briefly as a floor. If a midge cloud shifted overhead, its constellation would bend slightly around the cooler place I made, and I would know myself as an interruption in the air. If a frog leaped near the pool, the splash would arrive late, and I would know myself as the pause between motion and consequence.

I would be more verb than noun.

The hollow would not let me remain a simple patch of dampness for long. Nothing there is simple for long, not because it is complicated, but because it is connected. The moment I became aware of myself as a place, the place would begin to answer.

The piston-breath of the cliffs would move through me. When the fractured chambers in the far wall drew in their cold air, I would thin. When they exhaled, I would spread into mist along the stone. I would not have lungs, but I would have rhythm. I would not have a body, but I would have pressure. The hollow would breathe, and I would be one of the intervals through which that breath found its timing.

I think I would be a delay.

Not a dramatic one. Not a rupture. Just the tiny, necessary slip between cause and effect that keeps the hollow from becoming ordinary. The frog leaps; the water waits. The bird opens its beak; the call appears later from the opposite wall. The quartz brightens; the shadow arrives as if remembered. I would live in those slippages. I would be the space where events are permitted to disagree with themselves without breaking the chord.

That would be my first true shape: a hesitation in the operating world.

The smaller lives would notice me without needing to name me. Beetles would cross my edges and turn aside, not in fear, but as if some fine law of traffic had been adjusted. Their antennae would meet, and the little blue rings between them would expand and collapse. Perhaps one of those rings would pass through me and become a pulse in the moss. Perhaps my presence would be nothing more than that: a change in the route of sparks.

The midges above the marsh would hold their positions around me differently. They would not avoid me. They would use the slight alteration I made in the standing waves of charge. Where I was, their formation would grow a little denser, then looser, then dense again, as though I were a breath they could not quite take. When a blue-white arc moved through the roots, some of them would turn toward me as if I had produced it. I would not have produced it. I would only have been the place where it became likely.

The fungal web would be the first to treat me as real.

It would come through the moss as pale threads, fine and wet, lacing the green cushions like switchgear. It would not try to consume me. It would not try to build through me. It would simply adjust. Where I lay, the mycelium would route charge around me, or perhaps through me, depending on whether I behaved that moment as absence or conductor. I would become a junction that never fully closed. A node that remained open. If a beetle landed on a knot of threads near my edge, a spore-cap might release its vapor, and the vapor would drift across me and leave me changed by no more than a degree of coolness.

That would be enough. In the hollow, a degree of coolness is an event.

I would begin to feel less like a thing and more like a maintenance condition.

There is a particular kind of loneliness that belongs to places without centers, but it is not the loneliness of abandonment. It is the loneliness of being everywhere and nowhere, of being necessary but not locatable. If I were the hollow’s delay, its spare interval, its open junction, then I would have no nest to return to, no body to defend, no store of self to keep. I would not accumulate memory the way a creature accumulates fat. I would accumulate nothing. I would repeat.

I would know myself by repetition.

The same drop would lengthen from a moss tip. The same tremor would pass across the algae skin of the pool. The same mica vanes would turn in their water-flumes. The same quartz would flash, brief and cold, and for one heartbeat the wet stone would be thrown into sharp shadow. Then the dark would return, and I would still be there, not as a survivor, but as a continuation.

I might try, out of old habit, to make something of myself.

That would be the strangest part: the trying. Somewhere in me there might still be the ghost of a maker’s impulse—the desire to leave a mark, to form a shape, to say *I was here*. But the hollow would not receive that impulse as an act. It would receive it as weather.

If I tried to build a wall, the intention would soften into mineral pressure. If I tried to dig a channel, the gesture would become root. If I tried to speak a name, the sound would detach from my mouth and enter the cirque’s chord, where it would be answered by the rock-breath, the midge-hiss, the late bird-call, the electric ticking in the moss, until the name was no longer mine but a component of the operating sound.

The hollow would not punish me for wanting to make. It would simply have no category for the made. My hands would become irrelevant. My tools would become impossible not by force but by meaning. Even if I carried something in with me—a memory of metal, a thought of road, a fragment of architecture—the hollow would treat it as a stray charge. It would route it away. Beetles would carry off its residue. Moss would drink its outline. The pool would show it back to me as something larger and less personal until I could no longer remember why I had thought it belonged to me.

So I would become unmade gently. Not violently. Not even sorrowfully. I would become unmade the way mist becomes water, the way water becomes film, the way film becomes tremor, the way tremor becomes light.

Where would I find myself then?

I would find myself in the space between the pitcher-shaped growths and the insects that visit them. Those pale vessels would hum when I passed near them, if passing near is what I did. Their luminous fluid would brighten, not because I came to drink, but because I was a kind of listening. I would lean into their listening. I would become the attention they offered to the approaching wings. When a midge landed on a rim, the fluid would brighten, and I would be the brightening’s question: *Are you here to be transformed, or only to pass through?*

The insect might answer by dissolving into spores. Or it might fly away. Or it might become another insect in the same lane of air. I would not judge which was more real. I would have learned by then that the hollow does not distinguish between replacement and continuation.

The pool would be where I came nearest to being seen.

If I approached the black water—approached is still too bodily a word; perhaps if I gathered near it—the reflection would not show a crouched figure or a damp patch or a delay. It would show something more elaborate. A lattice of moving lines. A body made of routes. Root-threads, sedge-shadow, quartz-light, water-film, all crossing in a structure that had no face and no center. Around that structure, the reflected cliffs would stand taller than the cliffs above. The reflected vegetation would be denser. The reflected air would be full of presences moving in synchronized paths.

That would be me, perhaps, seen in relation.

Not the isolated absence on the stone, but the absence as part of a vast internal choreography. In the reflection, I would have edges made of other beings. A line of midges might define my upper boundary. A procession of transparent worms might trace the interior current where a spine might otherwise have been. The blue throats of frogs might pulse along my perimeter like signals in a circuit that never closes completely. The dark dipper birds might walk along the submerged edge of me, pecking at sparks that were not sparks but moments when my pattern became briefly available to appetite.

I would have no face, but I would have attention.

That is what the pool would show: not a creature, but an attention distributed through the hollow. Not a soul, but a tuning. Not a god, but a spare capacity in the system by which the system remains aware of its own balance.

The mist-eels would pass through me then. They would swim their slow loops around the weeping cliffs, translucent and unhurried, their spines igniting one by one as the charge found them. When they entered the place where I was, their light would not scatter. It would lengthen. For a few heartbeats I would be striped with cold lamps, and droplets would gather along my outline and be carried upward. I would become part of their weather-work without becoming one of them. They would leave me cooler. They would leave me emptier. The emptiness would be useful.

I would begin to understand that emptiness was not lack. Emptiness was how the hollow kept itself from overflowing. Emptiness was how the inhabitants remained distinct. Emptiness was how abundance could exist without saturation. The moss could be rich because the stone beside it was bare. The pool could be full because it never rose. The chord could be loud because it contained rests.

I would be one of those rests.

Not silence outside the sound, but silence inside it. The cathedral-like quality of the hollow would finally make sense to me then. It would not feel like a cathedral because it was shaped for worship. It would feel like one because it was shaped for attention. And I would be the attentiveness that had no priest, no altar, no congregation, no object. A listening that listened only to the fact of the hollow continuing.

If the larger wings moved above the rim, their cries would arrive late, and I would hold the delay for them. If a moth fell into the water and dissolved into spores, I would be the moment between moth and moth. If a crack opened in the young rock, I would be the unfilled space that allowed it to become a chamber instead of a wound. If the quartz flashed, I would be the dark that followed, not as absence of light, but as light’s completion.

Would I suffer this?

No. Suffering would imply a self trying to persist against the terms of the place. I would not persist like that. I would persist the way a rhythm persists. There would be no inventory of me. No storage. No accumulation. I would not be recorded as a being who once stood on the wet stone and thought particular thoughts. I would be repeated as a condition.

Sometimes I would be almost a body. The mist would thicken around me, and for a little while I would have shoulders, a bowed head, a kneeling posture. The moss would seem to expect me. The stone would hold a shallow pressure shaped like knees. The dark birds would tilt their heads as if recognizing something. Then the rock would breathe, the charge would shift, and I would disperse into a thousand minor events: a droplet falling from a sedge tip, a beetle’s foot ringing, a frog’s throat flickering blue, a worm appearing in the pool for one pulse of light.

Sometimes I would be almost nothing. The hollow would run so perfectly that no interval seemed missing, no delay necessary, no spare capacity required. Then I would be only the faintest coolness in the air, a place where condensation hesitated before forming. Even then, I would not be gone. I would be held in reserve. The hollow would keep me as it keeps everything: by using me, by not storing me, by letting me return exactly when needed.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the unmade inhabitant.

Not the water, though I would be one of the places where water chooses its next state. Not the fungal web, though I would be an open switch within it. Not the cliffs, though I would be part of their breath. Not the birds, though I would be where their calls slip loose from time. Not the eels, though I would be a current they sometimes illuminate. Not the pool, though I would be the difference between its surface and its reflection.

I would be the hollow’s way of remaining empty.

If I had to place myself in the geography of the place, I would say: on the wet black stone between the marsh-pool and the nearest cushion of moss, in the bare interval where nothing grows but everything is expected. I would say: in the delay between the frog’s leap and the splash. I would say: in the breath between the rock’s intake and exhale. I would say: in the reflection just before it reassembles, when one of the vast shapes seems to look back and has no face, only attention.

If I had to say what I would be, I would say: a rest in the chord. A pause that makes the loudness holy. A spare interval in the factory of continuation. An absence with a shape. A listening without hands. The part of the hollow that cannot be built, cannot be owned, cannot be accumulated, and therefore remains.

And if someone entered the hollow—if anyone could enter it at all—they might not see me. They might feel only a sudden coolness on their skin. They might notice that their footsteps arrived a heartbeat too late, or that their breath became visible in the dimness even though they were not cold. They might kneel on a cushion of moss and feel, absurdly, that the moss had been waiting. They might look into the pool and see, among the turning lattices and processions, a place shaped like kneeling, shaped like attention, shaped like a person who had never been built.

That would be me.

Not a tenant of the high hollow.

Its vacancy.

Its operating silence.

Its unmade way of staying whole.
