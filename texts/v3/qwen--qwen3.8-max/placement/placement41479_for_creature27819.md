The Mosswardens attend the living softness that is neither wholly plant nor wholly stone, but the quiet agreement between them. They care for the blue-green cushions in the shaded joints of stairways, for the moss seams where terrace meets wall, for the velvet borders that keep built ground from becoming too severe. They do not cut. They do not clear. They listen with their hands. If a moss cushion has lifted by the width of a breath, they press it back with a palm so patient it seems to be waiting for the stone to speak first. If a patch has grown into a shape that makes a path feel more complete, they let it remain, but they mark the change in memory, because even completion must be understood. Their work is not gardening. It is reassurance.

If I were to find myself in this world, I think I would not arrive as a traveler. The basin does not seem made for accidental arrival. There is no dust of long roads, no wind to bring one in from elsewhere, no storm to drive a stranger across the ridges and down into the open valley. One would not stumble there. One would be permitted there.

At first, I imagine myself walking in from the young mountains, a figure with sharp distance still clinging to my shoulders. But that image refuses the place. The basin corrects it gently. The traveler disappears, and in his place is someone already standing near the pale canals, still enough to belong to the view.

So I would not come from outside.

I would be found within.

More precisely, I would be found in the district where the long ceremonial channels broaden into a series of shallow steps, each step holding a mirror of water no deeper than a hand’s breadth. Not at the exact center of the basin, because the center feels too exposed for what I would become. Not at the far edge either, because the edge belongs to those who watch boundaries. I would be in the interval: a place between civic stone and open ground, between reflection and architecture, where the city thins into terraces and the terraces soften into moss.

There would be a low court there, roofless but not open to weather, because there is no weather to be open to. Its walls would be made of fitted grey stone, darkened faintly by damp. Moss would fill the joints in thin blue-green lines. A stair of three wide steps would descend toward a pool that is wider than it is deep, so still that the mountains beyond the basin appear in it not as reflections but as a second range of stone lying patiently beneath the first.

That is where I would be.

Not on the stair. Not beside the pool.

At the place where the stair pauses.

Who or what I would be is harder to say, because the basin does not seem to favor hard divisions. I would be human in outline, I think, but not human in tempo. Tall and narrow like the Arrangers, perhaps, with the same matte slate-colored skin and the same fine moss-like fibers along my shoulders and crown. But I would not carry a leaf-shaped scoop, and I would not wear ornaments of held water. My hands would be empty, and yet they would look as if they had always been cupped around something.

At first I think I would be a Brimkeeper. There is something appealing about that order: the care of fullness without excess, the reverence for the exact point before spill. But the Brimkeepers are too precise for what I am. Their work is measurement, however delicate. They know when a pool has sunk by the width of a hair. They know how much to add. I do not think I would know how much to add. I would be more likely to stand beside the water and become the place where its question could be held.

Then I think I would be an Edgetender. I understand their instinct: to move a leaf not because it is wrong, but because its stillness could be more complete elsewhere. But Edgetenders are concerned with placement, and I am not sure placement is my truest nature. I might leave the leaf where it is and listen to why it fell.

For a time I imagine myself as a Mosswarden, kneeling with my hands hovering over soft green cushions, learning the slow diplomacy between stone and growth. This is closer. But even there, I feel the image shift. The Mosswardens attend living softness. I would attend something less visible.

What I would attend, if this basin gave me a shape, is the unspoken.

The Arrangers might call me something like the Listener at the Brim.

Or perhaps the Hollow One.

Or the Basin Behind the Basin.

They would not say these names often. They would not need to. Names in such a place would be like beads on fern edges: precious, small, not meant to be shaken.

I would be a being made of attention.

My body would move with the same extraordinary care as the others, as though I too carried an invisible bowl filled to the lip. But the bowl inside me would not hold water. It would hold what water here has been asked not to become: motion, decision, departure, grief. The basin keeps moisture without weather; I would keep meaning without disturbance.

In the hollows of my hands there would be no visible pools, and yet when someone stood before me, they might feel that something in them had become still enough to be reflected. My eyes would be dark and horizontal-pupiled like the Arrangers’, but perhaps a little deeper, not because I would see farther, but because I would be harder to fill. People would come to me not when they needed an answer, but when they needed their question to stop trembling.

That would be my function, if function is the right word.

The Brimkeepers would bring me disturbances too subtle for their scoops. A canal whose surface has become uneasy without any leaf touching it. A pool that has begun to reflect the wrong hour, though nothing in the sky has changed. A bead of water on a fern edge that refuses to fall but also refuses to remain still. They would not describe these things in many words. They would stand beside me, and we would look together at the water until the unease became legible.

The Edgetenders would bring me objects that have lost their placement. Not a leaf from a channel, perhaps, but the feeling left behind by a leaf once removed. A small absence. A displaced quiet. They would place it before me as one might place a cracked bowl that still holds something precious. I would not repair it. I would hold it until its shape could be understood.

The Mosswardens would bring me the sounds they hear in the rock. Not loud sounds. There are no loud sounds here. Only the impression that stone has almost spoken, that the basin’s completed shape contains some withheld word. I would listen for that word without asking it to arrive. In this place, what refuses to arrive is not necessarily absent. Sometimes it is the most carefully preserved thing of all.

If I were to have a dwelling, it would not be a house in the ordinary sense. It would be a low, open chamber beside the court, with walls of damp stone and a floor worn smooth by standing rather than walking. There would be no furniture except a broad basin set into the ground, always full, never spilling. Visitors would not sit. They would stand at the brim with me.

They would come when a decision needed to form without pressure.

They would come when grief had become too heavy to carry without spilling.

They would come when they had seen too many distances at once and needed one still place in which to set them down.

For in the basin, distance is available. That is part of its openness. But availability can be a kind of weight. To see terraced slopes, rock fins, shallow amphitheaters, and the severe young mountains all at once is to be asked to hold several eternities in the same glance. I would be the place where those eternities could rest without choosing among them.

I think I would be especially near when someone was about to spill.

Not water. That would be the outer meaning, the one the basin understands first. Spilling water here is a grief, a broken vow, a small rupture in the discipline of fullness. But beneath that, I think spilling would mean any moment when a carefully kept thing becomes too much: a silence kept too long, a care held too precisely, a stillness maintained until it begins to ache. The Arrangers are gentle, but they are not unfeeling. Their gentleness requires great containment. Where there is great containment, there must also be a place for what threatens to overflow.

I would be that place.

Not a drain. Not a current. Not a release into motion.

A deeper brim.

If someone wept in the basin, the tear would not fall easily. It would gather at the edge of the eye, bright and perfect, and perhaps it would be caught by one of my cupped hands before it touched stone. Not to prevent grief. Grief would still be honored. But the tear would be received as a thing whole. It would be placed, if possible, into a small basin of pale stone and allowed to remain there as a witness. If it could not remain, I would hold it until it became still, and then return it to the person not as shame but as completed sorrow.

In this way, I suppose, I would be a kind of priest of the almost.

Not a priest of rain, because rain does not come.

Not a priest of rivers, because rivers do not rush.

Not a priest of fertility or harvest, because nothing dies back and nothing riots into growth.

A priest of the moment before event. A keeper of the breath between intention and act. A guardian of the trembling point where fullness becomes spill if it is not loved exactly enough.

The Arrangers would not worship me. That would be too much motion for the basin. They would simply know I was there. In their councils at the water’s edge, I might stand slightly apart, not because I was excluded, but because my presence would help them notice their own balance. When they stood together until agreement formed like condensation, I would be the stillness that made the condensation visible.

Sometimes I think I would not have a body at all. I would be the pool in the low court itself, wide and mirror-still, full to the brim, and the being called “I” would be only the pool’s capacity to listen. The Arrangers would approach and look into me, and in my surface they would see their own faces above the reflected mountains. They would see themselves as part of the same composed world. Their grief would enter me as a ripple, small and local, quickly forgotten by the water but not by me. I would remember it without allowing it to travel.

But then the image changes again, because the basin has presences, and it seems right that I should have a form that can stand among them. So I return to the shape of a figure: slate-skinned, moss-fibered, silent-footed, hands cupped, eyes unhurried. I stand at the pause in the stair. I am neither the water nor the stone, but the awareness between them.

If a stranger were brought into the basin, I would be one of the last beings they met. Not because I am hidden, but because the basin would need to settle around them first. The stranger would be given time to understand the discipline of the brim. They would learn not to reach too quickly for the water. They would learn that a leaf resting on a pool is not refuse. They would learn that two figures in a plaza are enough. They would learn that emptiness here is not absence but room.

Only then would someone lead them to the court of shallow steps.

They would find me standing there, perhaps so still that they would mistake me for a statue set out to remember a council. They would see the damp film on the stone around me, the tiny shining points on vertical rock, the moss seams glowing blue-green in the cool light. They would see the pool at my feet holding the mountains without effort. They might ask me something.

They might ask: What are you?

I would not answer quickly.

In the basin, speech should not disturb more than it completes.

At last I might say: I am what happens to the rain that never falls.

That would be true, though strange. The basin has moisture without weather, damp without event. Something must hold the event that has been refused. Something must keep the shape of the unarrived storm. I would be that shape. Not water in motion, but the memory of water’s intention. The held breath. The unspilled question. The tenderness that remains after urgency has been set down.

They might ask: What do you do?

And I would say: I keep what you bring from becoming too heavy.

That would be my life there, if life is the right word. It would not be dramatic. There would be no battles, no processions, no great machinery of fate. There would be the long cool hours, the damp stone, the mirror pools, the Arrangers moving with their leaf-shaped scoops and weighted hems. There would be leaves arriving in channels and being considered. There would be moss rising by imperceptible degrees. There would be pools checked and bead-light touched and silence maintained with such care that it became civic.

And I would stand in the interval, receiving what could not be placed.

I would not be lonely. The basin does not make solitude into lack. A single figure on a terrace can belong to the whole view. My standing there would be enough. If another came and stood beside me, that would be a completion. If no one came, the stillness would not be emptiness but presence.

Perhaps, over time, the Arrangers would invent a small order for me alone, as they have orders for brim and edge and moss. They might call it the Order of the Held Drop, or the Quiet of the Second Reflection. It would have no robes, no rituals except standing, no tools except attention. Its only discipline would be this: to receive without disturbing, to answer without spilling, to remain full without becoming closed.

If I were to choose one image for myself at last, it would be this:

A shallow basin carved into the pause between stair steps. The water inside is so still that it does not know whether it is reflecting the sky or remembering it. Around the rim, moss grows in a thin blue-green line. A figure stands nearby with cupped hands. The figure is not guarding the water. It is listening to what the water has decided not to do.

That is where I would find myself.

Not in the storm, because there is no storm.

Not in the river, because there is no current.

Not in the falling drop, because the drop has been lovingly withheld.

I would be the being who stands beside the withheld drop and understands its refusal as a form of care.
