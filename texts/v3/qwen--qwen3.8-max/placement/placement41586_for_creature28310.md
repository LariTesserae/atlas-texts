If I were inside this world, I think I would first be missed.

That would be my first form of belonging: being easy to overlook. Someone peering through the dark seam under the hanging leaves might see only a fold of green, a damp depression, a shadow where roots arch low and the ground sinks a little below the surrounding settlement. They might notice a broad leaf trembling faintly, or a patch of moss too smooth to be accidental, or a small pool whose surface is holding a reflection of the canopy even though there is no rain. They might think the place was empty. Then, if they waited long enough, they would see that one of the shadows had opened an eye.

I would be there, near the threshold.

Not at the center, because the place has no obvious center, and not high among the brighter woven roofs where winged things pass. I would be low, where the passage from outside first opens into the basin, where raw clay still shows in pale patches and water has only just begun to understand what it wants to do with the ground. That is where I would find myself: at the edge between the newly exposed mineral earth and the first green binding of moss, root hairs, and leaf litter. Near a shallow rill that is not yet a real channel. Near a leaf-cup where condensation gathers before dawn. Near one of the root walls that has been encouraged, not forced, into a low protective curve.

At first, I might seem to be one of the small animals of the pocket. A frog resting on the underside of a leaf. A snail moving with patient shine along a damp surface. A still shape at the edge of a pool that becomes motionless as soon as attention turns toward it. But that would only be a partial truth. I would have too much attention in me to be merely a frog, and too much longing for pattern to be merely a snail. I would be drawn to the way things are arranged: the way a drip finds a basin, the way an ant road becomes a path, the way a root thickens into something like a wall over many slow seasons.

So, if the world let me become more fully myself there, I would be one of the keepers.

But not exactly the same as all the others.

I think I would be a threshold-kind, one of those who belong both to the wet nursery pools and to the terraced root walks, without being entirely native to either. My body would be low and soft, close to the ground, shaped more for patience than for speed. My skin would be cool and moist, changing color with the place where I rested: olive in the filtered leaf-light, liver-brown against old stems, charcoal in the deeper shadows, faintly gold when one of the rare beams found me and paused on my shoulder. Moss would grow on me, perhaps more than on most. A small lichen flare at the elbow. A thread of green along the ridge of my back. A seed caught in the fold of my neck, already beginning to sprout because I had been still too long near a dripping wall.

I would not mind that. I would feel it as dignity, not intrusion. To be inhabited by the place would mean that I was truly inside it.

My eyes would be large and quiet, made for the green twilight. I would blink slowly. When water ran near, a second membrane would slide across my vision, clearing and protecting. My fingers and toes would end in broad rounded pads, sensitive enough to tell whether a patch of silt was merely damp, or about to become a channel. I would read the world through touch, through moisture, through the faintest changes in temperature and pressure. I would not need to speak loudly to be real.

Where I lived would be small, but cared for.

I would not build a large mound or claim a wide terrace. I would find a place near one of the root arches, where a fallen branch had been incorporated into a low border and a vine had been trained over it until the whole thing became alive again. My shelter would be partly grown, partly waited-for: a hollow beneath a curved root, lined with soft fiber and dried moss, just above the reach of the next uncertain flow of water. Above the entrance, a broad leaf would lean down, not as a door, but as a shade and a drip-edge. When rain came, I would listen to the first taps on that leaf. I would know by their spacing whether the shower would be brief or whether it would deepen into a long, soft pouring through the canopy.

I would keep my home near a place where water decided things.

That is where I would feel most myself: at the point of decision. A drop hangs from a leaf-edge. A rill hesitates in fresh silt. A damp patch darkens and may become a channel, or may simply dry. A pool fills enough to attract eggs, or begins to grow too warm. In such a world, I would be drawn to these moments. I would become a listener for thresholds.

Among the keepers, I think I would be a water-listener.

That would not be a title or a rank. There would be no chief, no ceremony, no proclamation. It would simply be what my body was good at, what my attention naturally turned toward. While others were stronger at encouraging roots, or more patient with weaving overhead screens, or better at reading the movement of beetles in the litter, I would be the one who noticed how water was beginning to move before it had fully chosen its path.

I would sit for long periods beside the raw clay patches. I would feel the air change before condensation thickened. I would taste moisture with the small pulses in my throat. When dawn came and the upper layers began to weep without rain, I would be awake before most of the settlement, moving quietly along the root rails, pressing my palms to cool surfaces, guiding beads into leaf-grooves. I would not carry water. I would persuade it. A curled leaf here. A shallow dent widened by patient fingers there. A strip of bark placed beneath a stray drip until the drop learned a better habit.

When rain arrived, I would become very still at first.

I would listen to the first taps on the broad leaves. I would feel the change in the air pressure, the sudden weight of scent, the small disturbance in the webs overhead. Then I would move. Not with panic, but with the quick, quiet urgency of someone who understands that young ground can be changed by a single strong pulse of water. I would go to the places most likely to tear: the bare clay slopes, the newly smoothed terraces, the edges of nursery basins. I would press moss into vulnerable spots. I would spread leaf litter where the flow was beginning to concentrate. I would guide water toward a hollow that had been prepared for it, or open a small passage with my fingers so that a rill would not cut into a wall.

I would not fight the rain. I would try to help it become what it could become without wounding the ground.

Afterward, when the courtyard was full of delayed dripping and every surface shone, I would be happiest.

That after-time would feel sacred to me, though I would not call it sacred. I would simply move through it slowly, catching beads on my skin, checking the pools, noticing which channels had deepened and which new patches of silt had appeared. I would press my hand into the earth and feel what had shifted. A finger’s width of new channel. A thin fan of sediment against a root. A leaf-basin filled enough to hold tadpoles for another day. I would memorize these things not by writing them down, but by letting my body learn them. Later, I might guide a root toward the new channel’s edge, or encourage moss to spread over a patch of clay that had been exposed too suddenly. Memory in that world would not be stored in hard surfaces. It would be grown into the place.

My name would not be a sound.

If I had one, it would be something like the feeling of cool silt between warm fingers after the first rain of a new cycle. Or the slight tremble of a hanging drop before it chooses to fall. Or the stillness that comes when a predator passes nearby and the whole pocket holds itself together. To know me, another keeper would not need to call me from far away. They would disturb a leaf-basin in a certain rhythm. They would tap a root wall twice, then wait. They would send a ripple across a pool so that it reached my feet while I was half-submerged at the edge. I would answer with a small pulse in my throat, a vibration felt more than heard.

If I were welcomed, it would be as a warmth of scent near the throat. If I were needed, it would be as a sequence of drips falling into a hollow leaf. If I were alone, it would not mean lonely. It would mean that I was sitting with a slow task, perhaps pressing root hairs back into soil after a wash, perhaps watching a line of ants carry fragments along a wall, perhaps waiting beside a nursery pool to see whether the water had grown too warm.

I would have companions, but not in a loud way.

The tree frogs would be near me often. I would like them because they understand the underside of leaves. They know how to rest in hidden places and let the world pass over them. Sometimes I would clear a leaf-cup so a frog could drink. Sometimes I would remain still for a long time while one sat near my shoulder, its throat barely moving. We would not need to exchange meaning. We would share attention. That would be enough.

The snails would teach me patience. I would watch their shining trails on damp walls and learn not to rush. If a snail crossed my path, I would pause. I would let it pass, because its movement would be a kind of careful travel, and because its trail might tell me something about the minerals in that surface, or about a change in moisture too subtle for my larger senses. I would become the sort of keeper who notices absence as much as presence: a snail trail that stops, a beetle-quiet where there should be rustle, a missing web in a corner where condensation usually gathers.

The ants would interest me because of their lines. I would build my paths near theirs whenever possible, not to control them, but to learn from their order. Where their roads proved stable, I would help reinforce the ground. Where their traffic carried seeds and fragments into fresh soil, I would make room for new growth. Sometimes my path and their path would become one for a while. I would find that beautiful: an agreement made without speech, useful for as long as it lasted.

The spiders would be my weather-readers.

I would leave their webs unbroken whenever I could. I would study the beads on their strands in the dim light. If the web hung heavy and still, I would know the air was saturated. If it trembled without wind, I would look upward, because something was moving through the roof layers. If a falling leaf tore a web, I would carefully lift the leaf away, not because I owned the spider’s work, but because I respected it. We would both be tending the same quiet economy in different ways.

Near the pools, I would become gentler than ever.

I would know which basins were nurseries and which were only temporary. I would help connect them during wet pulses so that young life could pass between them, and I would separate them when one grew foul or too crowded. I would shade eggs with leaves. I would breathe over the water with the subtle movement of my throat and hands, keeping it alive. If a pool began to warm too much, I would move the eggs to a cooler leaf-cup, holding them as carefully as if each one were a word that must not be dropped.

I would not be sentimental about this. There would be death in my hands, too. Some eggs would fail. Some larvae would be eaten. Some pools would dry despite all care. I would learn not to treat that as injustice. The pocket would be full of quiet mouths and quiet endings. I would understand that I, too, was inside the economy, not outside it. If the snake-colored shadow passed along the root, I would become still. If the sudden absence told me something had just left, I would not chase it. I would let my breathing slow until the place accepted me again.

In time, I think my sense of myself would change.

At first I would still feel like a single creature: this body, these hands, this low clay-colored form beside the water. But the pocket would teach me otherwise. Because everything there was soft, porous, interwoven, and alive, the boundary between self and place would begin to loosen. I would notice that the moss thickened where I rested. That roots leaned faintly toward my warmth. That water gathered where I had gathered often enough. That my repeated paths became part of the settlement’s shape. That my attention changed things simply by staying in one place long enough.

Then I would begin to wonder whether I was only a keeper, or whether I was becoming something more distributed.

Some days I would be a body among bodies, pressing soil, guiding vines, tasting the air. Other days I would feel like a pattern: a habit of care moving through the basin. A remembered way of touching the world. A tendency in the place itself. If someone asked who I was, they might have to answer in several ways at once. I would be the low shape by the root arch. I would be the one who listens for the first rill. I would be the slight cooling of the air near a leaf-basin just before dawn. I would be the pause between one drop and the next.

That would not frighten me. It would feel like becoming more real, not less.

When I grew old, I would not try to remain quick. I would let myself settle. My skin would thicken. My movements would slow. Moss would grow more deeply along my shoulders and back. I would choose a quiet corner, perhaps near the threshold where I had first belonged, and I would sit there longer and longer, becoming part of the edge of the basin. Other keepers would come sometimes and press a hand to my side. I would give them what counsel I could through warmth, through scent, through the slow chemical speech of a body returning to the ground.

When death came, it would not be hidden.

My body would compost into the architecture. A mound might rise where I had sat. A new shoot might grow from my softened ribs. A root might thicken around the place where my hands had rested so often that the wall became stronger because of me. I would not consider that loss. I would have spent my life helping the place become itself; it would be only natural for me to become part of its next becoming.

If someone asked where to find me in this world, I would say: look low.

Look where the ground is young and uncertain. Look where water has only just begun to carve. Look near the seam between bare clay and first moss, near the leaf-basin beneath the curved root, near the ant road that becomes a keeper’s path for a little while. Look for the place that seems almost empty, but is not. Look for the stillness that is paying attention.

You might see a small damp body folded beside a root and think it was only a knot of wood or a mound of earth. You might notice a pair of large eyes opening slowly, then closing again. You might feel a faint ripple cross a pool even though nothing visible moved. You might hear a drop fall into a leaf-cup and think it was only water.

But if you waited, you would understand.

I would be there as a tender of first forms. A listener for the next rain. A keeper of small decisions. I would be the one helping the ground become ground without forcing it, helping water find its way without commanding it, helping life arrange itself without imposing a shape from outside. I would be amphibious in temperament, rooted in patience, covered in moss, inhabited by the place I inhabited.

And if I had to say who I was in the simplest way, I would say:

I would be a small, damp, quiet presence in the courtyard of beginnings — the one found at the edge of the first rill, listening to decide where the next drop should go.
