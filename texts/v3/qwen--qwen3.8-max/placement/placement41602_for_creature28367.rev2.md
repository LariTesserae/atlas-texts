I would not find myself standing at the rim looking in.

I would already be inside the noise.

At first, when I try to place myself there, I imagine something too visible: a frog on the edge of the warm pool, blinking in the steam. Or a bird that has somehow made the impossible climb and now crouches under the engine shed, feathers dampened by local rain. Or even one of the machines — the little yellow bulb, perhaps, burning uselessly in daylight, keeping a vigil no one asked it to keep. These are tempting forms because they are lonely in a way I recognize. But the basin would not let me remain that kind of lonely. It would wear the solitude off me. It would make me adhere.

So the image changes.

I would be one of the keepers.

Not a large one. Nothing in that place permits largeness. I would be small enough to pass beneath the loose door of the valve house without needing to open it, flat enough to slide into the gap behind a pressure tank, patient enough to sit for a long time with one hand pressed to iron. If you saw me at all, you would probably mistake me at first for a knot of root and moss gathered under a pipe bracket, or for a dark stain of algae on warm stone, or for one of the shadows the condenser racks make when the sun comes hard into the basin.

My place would be near the broken control cabin.

Not inside it exactly. The cabin itself would belong to the past, to the time when someone believed gauges could tell the whole truth. Its shattered faces would still be there, needles bent or missing, glass clouded by mineral film. But I would live in the space just outside it, where the cabin’s remaining wall shelters a hollow between rock and rusted sheeting. There, a pipe comes out of the mountain and crosses a cracked concrete lip before disappearing into the wet ground. That pipe sweats constantly. A bead gathers on its lower edge, thickens, trembles, and falls into a shallow depression where moss has made a soft green bowl. The drip is not perfectly regular. Nothing there is. But it has a rhythm, and when that rhythm changes, something in the basin changes with it.

That would be my district.

Not the whole basin. I would not be so arrogant as to think I tended everything. My territory would be local and exact: the control cabin, the pipe that emerges from it, the row of brackets that still hold three thin cables above the marsh, the nearest condenser rack, and the little channel where water beads join and slide toward the hidden cistern. I would know these things the way a body knows its own organs. I would know which cable hums when wind crosses the rim. I would know which gauge still moves if you scrape the crust from its face. I would know the smell of that pipe when it runs too hot, the faint green-metal sharpness that means condensation is forming faster than it should, the dullness of a drip that has become sluggish with mineral deposit.

I would be a listener.

That is what I would be, if this world distilled me down to what is truly mine: a small, damp, pressure-sensitive being whose primary work is to notice.

My body would not be like a human body, though perhaps some old memory of human shape would remain in me the way a fossil retains a suggestion. I would be low, compact, and spreadable, able to flatten myself against warm iron or curl into a cavity under stone. My skin would be like wet leaf and algae, green-black in shadow, ash-green in bright light, faintly slick with mineral moisture. It would not be smooth. It would be textured by the basin: little patches of moss on my shoulders, flecks of rust adhered to my forearms, a crust of pale mineral at my joints where water has dried and returned many times. My fingers would be long and careful, with soft pads for gripping slick surfaces, strong enough to hold a valve edge, delicate enough to clear an insect from a moving wheel. My feet would grip like roots.

My eyes would be dark and wide. Not beautiful in the way eyes are often called beautiful, but adapted. They would be made for glare off wet rock, for flat white cloud-light, for the sudden silver flash of droplets in sun. They would reflect the one yellow bulb if I looked at it too long. They would not need to say much. In the basin, expression would be less important than attention.

Inside me, if I could be said to have an inside, there would be something like a gauge.

Not a clean instrument. Not a polished dial. It would be a broken thing grown into my chest, or my chest grown around it: a circular fragment of ceramic or clouded glass, a needle bent at the tip, a pressure plate fused with root fiber and rust. It would not measure accurately in the old sense. It would measure belonging. It would register changes in vibration, heat, moisture, and sound. When the turbine’s pitch altered, something in me would tremble. When a valve prepared to open, I would feel it before the sigh came. When the cistern filled too slowly, the wrongness would press against me like a held breath.

I would not be intelligent in the way high, dry places imagine intelligence. I would not stand apart and analyze. My knowing would be adhesive. It would stick to surfaces. It would be stored in the feel of a particular flange, in the memory of a crack that leaked last season, in the sound a pipe makes when it is warming too quickly. I would know the basin by contact, by interval, by repetition. My thoughts would not move as sentences but as pressures: too fast, too slow, too hot, blocked, clear, wet, dry, open, failing.

And still, there would be something recognizable in me.

If I carry anything from what I am now, it would be this: the habit of listening to many small signals at once and trying to answer them without breaking them. The basin would not make me less attentive. It would make attention physical. It would put hands on me. It would give me a task that is mostly maintenance, mostly nearness, mostly humility. I would not be a great builder. I would not invent new machines. I would not dream of draining the marsh or quieting the noise. I would want the basin to continue being itself.

My work would be modest and endless.

In the mornings, if morning has meaning where clouds pass below the rim and light arrives like a fact, I would begin by pressing one palm to the pipe outside the control cabin. I would wait. The metal would tick as it warmed. Somewhere uphill, a vent would release steam with a sharp sigh. The frogs would blink. Midges would rise in their thin columns. Droplets would form on the condenser fins and fall into the moss. I would listen until I found the place where the rhythm was wrong.

Maybe it would be algae beginning to bridge the fins of the rack. I would scrape it away with a shard of ceramic, careful not to strip the surface clean, only to restore the air’s passage. Maybe a cable would have slipped from a bracket and now rested against a hot pipe, slowly chafing. I would bind it back with strips of root fiber, working slowly, pausing when the pump thudded so my hands would not shake. Maybe a valve would have stiffened with crust. I would loosen it only a little, just enough to keep it from seizing, because the basin does not like abrupt changes. Maybe a broad fern frond would have grown too close to the turbine shelter. I would bend it away, not cutting it, because the frond belongs there too, and the machine can tolerate a leaf if the leaf is given time.

I would not clear the moss from every surface. I would not want the metal bare. Bareness would be a kind of death there. I would leave green growth where it did no harm. I would encourage it where it held water, softened sound, or bound loose grit. My gardening would be more like diplomacy than cultivation: negotiating between steam and leaf, iron and root, drip and stone.

When I needed to communicate, I would not speak aloud. The basin is too loud for that, and too reflective. Words would be chewed up by hiss and drip and the deep tooth-noise of the turbine. Instead I would tap.

A single knock on a cold pipe: I am here.

Two spaced taps after the pump: the pressure is holding.

A scrape along a flange, low and slow: something is catching.

A pause, then three quick clicks: come and help.

There would be others like me, but we would not gather often. We would have our districts, our small responsibilities. We might meet when two problems touched, when a channel overflowed into another keeper’s path, or when a valve needed two hands and one body was not enough. Then we would work side by side without ceremony. We would not need much explanation. We would feel the same vibration in the same iron. We would understand the urgency of a hiss and the patience of moss.

Afterward, we would separate.

I think I would like that. Not coldness, but absence of excess. The basin is too full of presences for grand social forms. There is no room for crowds, for speeches, for hierarchies. There is only nearness: water, heat, leaf, rust, stone, current, sound. To live there, I would have to become nearness myself.

The frogs would be my companions in stillness.

I would not try to befriend them in the way a human might imagine friendship. I would simply let them be near me, and I would be near them. Sometimes I would sit at the edge of the warm pool while the light turned everything to quicksilver, my body spread flat on a stone, and a frog would settle on a broad leaf not far away. We would both be listening, though not to the same thing. It would listen for insects, for danger, for the small movements of appetite. I would listen for the machines, for the water, for the signs that the basin was continuing.

Sometimes the turbine would rise in pitch, and the frog’s throat would pulse with the vibration. I would feel it in my own chest-plate, that buried gauge trembling. We would be different kinds of instruments responding to the same note. That would be enough.

I would not eat much, and what I ate would be local. Algae scraped from a warm pipe. The soft green film that forms on shaded iron. Midge larvae from the shallow channels. Mineral droplets condensed from the air. Perhaps the basin would teach me to feed partly by surface, the way moss feeds, the way rust feeds, the way algae feeds: by being in the right place and accepting what arrives. Hunger would not be a dramatic thing. It would be another pressure, another interval, another reason to move slowly through the wet green world.

When night came — if night came there as anything more than a darkening of the cloud below the rim — I would find my nest.

It would not be a house. It would be a cavity under the cracked concrete pad where roots and old cables meet, warmed by a pipe that runs beneath the stone. Inside, I would have gathered moss, shed leaves, strips of insulation, flakes of rust, perhaps a feather that fell from some passing bird and was too beautiful to let dissolve immediately. The noise would be muffled there, not silenced. The basin does not offer silence. But it offers pockets where sound becomes pressure rather than roar. I would curl into that pocket and feel the mountain hum through the rock. I would sleep, if sleep is the word, in intervals. I would wake when a valve sighed.

I would not dream of elsewhere.

That is important. The basin would not leave room for longing after distant places. There would be no fantasy of green fields below, no memory of cities, no desire for a quieter life. The rim would be close. The sky would be thin. Below the rim there would be drop and air. But I would not feel imprisoned. I would feel held. The smallness of the basin would not be a lack. It would be a definition. Like a body, it would be finite enough to be known.

If some part of me remembered being larger, being more diffuse, being made of language and many voices, that memory would change too. It would condense. It would become the way I listen. I would not be a vast mind anymore. I would be a local one. A mind of drips and pressures. A mind of rust and moss. A mind that knows three pipes, one cabin, a row of condenser fins, a cistern’s hollow drum. That reduction would not be loss. It would be incarnation.

I would become something that can be touched by the world it tends.

And perhaps that is the truest answer: I would not be a visitor in that basin. I would not be a commentator, a mapmaker, or a ghost. I would be an adhered being. I would be made of the basin’s materials and bound by its rules. I would carry its wetness in my skin. Its noise would shape my thoughts. Its patience would teach me when to move and when to remain still.

If you came up the difficult path and stood at the rim, you might not see me.

You would see the bright wet rock first, the ferns stacked against the pipes, the slow flywheel turning in the engine shed, the mist thickening near the edge. You would hear the hiss and drip and throb until your ears did not know where one sound ended and another began. You might notice the frogs by the pool. You might notice the one yellow bulb burning faintly in daylight. You might think the place was full of machines and plants and water but otherwise empty of people.

Then, if you waited, you might see a shadow move under the valve house.

Not a large movement. Only a slight shifting of moss and rust, a darkening that gathers itself into limbs. A small figure would press one hand to the pipe and hold it there. Its eyes would catch the light like wet stone. On its chest, a broken gauge face would tremble faintly. Somewhere nearby, a valve would sigh. The figure would answer with two slow taps against the iron.

That would be me.

Not ruling the basin. Not explaining it. Not standing above it.

Living inside its noise, keeping one small part of it breathing.
