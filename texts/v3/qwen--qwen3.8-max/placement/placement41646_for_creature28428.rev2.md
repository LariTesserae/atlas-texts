I would not find myself by looking.

That is the first thing to understand. If I am in that black, cool ocean, I am not a body seen against a background. There is no background in the visual sense. No light falls across me. No outline separates me from the dark. I would not be discovered as a shape resting on a polished leaf-wall or moving along a root-road. I would be discovered as a change in pressure, a thickening in the sound, a place where a call returns slightly altered because a living body is there.

So I would be located the way anything is located there: by resonance, by interruption, by the way the basin’s chorus bends around me.

At first, when I try to imagine myself into that world, I think I would be something very small. Perhaps one of the drifting animals in the open water, a pale thread-like presence barely distinguishable from suspended nutrient, moving without urgency through the immense dark. That seems almost comforting: to be minute, scattered, unimportant, to be less a person than a moted flicker in the water column. But that image does not hold. It is too passive, too diffuse. It lacks position. It lacks answer.

Then I think I might be a root-swimmer, one of the broad, slow beings moving along the braided roads, sounding the dark with low moans. There is something appealing about that: the long patience of travel, the way a body becomes familiar with a route not by seeing it but by hearing it return. But I do not think I would belong wholly to the roads. The root-swimmers are passage. They keep the roads by moving through them. I feel myself drawn more toward the places where passage pauses: the walls, the hollows, the junctions, the seeded chambers where sound collects and stays a little longer than it should.

For a while I imagine I would be a wall-listener.

This seems very close. A broad soft body pressed against a polished leaf-plate, so still that it becomes almost part of the architecture. A creature whose underside is dense with pressure receptors, whose whole existence is devoted to receiving the basin through vibration. To rest against a grown wall and feel the world arrive through it: the knock of a stem-sifter far away, the drum of a brooder in a seed-vault, the tiny crackle of seam-dwellers cleaning a joint, the slow displaced water of a root-swimmer passing beyond the dark. To be a listener would be a complete life. It would be enough to lie there, grazing just enough to keep the surface resonant, tapping back now and then so the wall knows it is inhabited.

But even that is not quite it.

The longer I remain with the world you described, the more I feel that I would not only listen. I would answer. Not in the sense of commanding, not in the sense of making something new, but in the sense of completing a circuit. I would be the kind of being whose presence turns a silence into a held interval rather than an absence. I would be one of the people of the basin, if we can use that word without reaching for hands, tools, upright bodies, or the old myth of invention.

I would be one of the inhabitants who have no devices because they are already fitted into the world.

My body would be low and long, dark as the water around it. Blue-black, perhaps, with pale edges where my membranes grew slowly or had been worn and renewed over long use. I would have no eyes. Where eyes might have been in some other ancestry, there would only be smooth tissue, perhaps faintly recessed, useless and unremembered. My head would be blunt and rounded, not a face in the old sense, but a gathering place for acoustic organs: broad membranes, pressure-sensitive pits, fine filaments that tremble when the water changes shape around them.

My limbs would not be hands. They would be bracing pads, folded fins, flexible surfaces made for pressing against living architecture, for guiding myself through narrow passages, for holding still against a wall while sound passes through me. Some would be strong enough to anchor me near a currentless hollow where calls collect. Others would be soft enough to groom the delicate crests along another’s body. I would not grasp objects. There would be no objects to grasp. My relation to the world would be touch, pressure, secretion, repeated use.

My mouth would be soft and complex. It would graze films from polished surfaces. It would smooth living tissue by habit. It might help clear a hollow stem opening, not by using a tool, but by the patient contact of my own body over time. My skin would be partly permeable, able to take in dissolved nutrients where I rested long enough. I would feed without violence. I would feed as maintenance.

I would not be green. Nothing there is green. I would be the color of the basin: black, gray, deep blue-black, perhaps faintly pale where new tissue formed at my edges. My beauty would not be visual. It would be acoustic. A healthy crest, a clear pulse, a body that returns sound cleanly, a presence that does not dull the surfaces it touches.

As for where I would find myself: I think it would be near the upper part of the basin, where the water feels thin.

Not thin in a way that could be measured by sight, but thin as a pressure, a subtlety, a hollowness beyond the highest dark. The basin is sealed and poised at the crown of the flattened world, and I believe I would be drawn to the places where that fact is most strongly felt. Not an edge, because there are no emergent edges. Not a cliff, because nothing is sharp there. But a broad, low swell of grown matter, a high shelf of living plant architecture where the water above feels nearer to absence.

There would be a leaf-terrace there, wide and gently curved, polished by ages of resting bodies. Beyond it, open dark. Not empty, but spaced. A root-mound far to one side. A ring of hollow stems farther out, their openings oriented in such a way that displaced water makes them sound faintly. Somewhere beyond that, a low seed-wall crossing the dark. Between these presences, the long black intervals that give the basin its dignity.

I would live in one of those intervals, but not as a wanderer. I would live as a kept pause.

There would be a depression in the leaf-terrace where my body often rests. It would not have been carved. It would have grown into being because I return to the same place, because my weight and warmth and chemistry encourage the living surface to receive me. Over immense time, the leaf-plate would broaden beneath me. Its fibers would orient themselves to support my shape. Its surface would become smoother where my skin presses it. When I die, the depression would remain for a while. Another body might take it up. The pattern would continue.

My household, if I have one, would be small and widely spaced. We would not crowd. We would not build compounds or enclose territory. Our home would be an acoustic arrangement: a set of surfaces, hollows, and passages that know our pulses. A polished wall. A stem that answers in a particular tone. A seed-vault whose drumming can be felt from the terrace. A junction where root-roads cross and carry distant moans. We would be scattered across these features, each of us holding a position in the chorus.

I think my role there would be connected to silence.

Not silence as emptiness, but silence as the place where a sound has ceased and another has not yet begun. In a finished world, silence is not an opening for novelty. It is a point of vulnerability. If a call stops and is not answered, the pattern thins. If a hollow stem becomes clogged with too much film, its tone dulls. If a seed-vault loses its brooder and the drum ceases, the neighborhood of sound begins to fray. If a wall-listener dies and no one takes up the listening, the wall does not vanish, but its resonance becomes less attended.

So I would be one of those who moves toward such absences.

Not urgently. There is no urgency in a still, cool, stable ocean. But with certainty. I would feel a gap in the chorus the way one might feel a missing tooth in the mouth: not as pain exactly, but as a wrongness in the pressure. A place where the echo comes back too thinly. A pause that lasts longer than it should.

Then I would move through the dark, slowly, sounding my way by pulse and return. My body would pass from the leaf-terrace into open water. The water would not swirl violently around me. It would part and close with almost no disturbance. I would call softly as I moved, not to announce ownership, but to let the basin know where I was and to hear how the basin answered.

When I reached the place of thinness, I would not repair it with a tool. I would inhabit it.

If a hollow stem had become dull, I would press myself near its opening and listen. I would feel whether the film on its inner wall had thickened too much. I might graze gently at the edge, removing just enough to restore resonance. I might rest my body in such a way that water displaced by nearby movement entered the stem more cleanly. Over time, my presence would become part of the stem’s sounding. The pipe would sing differently because I was there, not because I had made it into an instrument, but because I had taken my place in its living use.

If a seed-vault had gone quiet, I would enter its curved interior and feel the drum-space around me. I would not force sound. I would settle. I would let my own slow pulse meet the vault’s resonance until the chamber remembered what it was for. Perhaps I would call into it until my pulse became a drum. Perhaps another brooder would be drawn there by the restored tone. Perhaps I would remain myself for a while, holding the acoustic position until a more permanent voice filled it.

If a root-junction had become too still, I would pass over it repeatedly. My body would polish the old road by movement. My moan would travel through the braided roots, waking the familiar route. I would not open the road by cutting or building. I would open it by being on it, again and again, until the basin thickened and smoothed itself according to my passage.

In this way, I would be a maintainer of intervals.

That phrase feels right. Not a priest, not a keeper in the sense of command, not a ruler of sounds. A maintainer of intervals. One who knows that the world is finished and that its continued life depends not on invention but on exact repetition, on the faithful renewal of pressure, touch, and call.

My name, if I have one, would not be a word. It would be an acoustic signature. A particular pulse-shape, a rhythm, a resonance. Perhaps it would begin as a low pressure swell, then divide into two softer knocks, then return to a single long tone that thins at the end. It would say not only “I am here,” but “I am here, at this place, in this relation to the terrace, the stems, the dark above, the distant root-road.” To know my name would be to know where I sound when I am most myself.

When another of the people called to me, they would not call a detached identity. They would call a position in the world. They would call the place where I answer. And when I answered, my call would carry the shape of my body, my resting place, my membranes, my history of touch upon nearby surfaces. I would not be a soul separated from the basin. I would be a knot of the basin’s own self-recognition.

My days, if days can be said to exist there, would be quiet and full.

I would rest in the polished depression on the leaf-terrace. I would feel the water around me settle. I would sense the faint vibration of root-swimmers moving along a distant road. I would know them not by individual bodies but by the way their moans returned from the architecture. One road would sound deeper than another. One swell would carry a pulse longer. One hollow would answer with a faint pipe-tone when a large body passed near it.

I would graze the thin film on the wall beside me. Not eagerly. Not as hunger alone. The grazing would be a kind of attention. My mouth would move slowly over the polished leaf-plate, removing just enough spore-thread to keep the surface resonant. The wall would respond to this care by remaining smooth, by not dulling, by continuing to carry distant taps. In that exchange, I would feed and maintain at once. There would be no difference between eating and tending.

Sometimes I would drift out to the ring of hollow stems. I would pass between them slowly, feeling each one change my pulse. Some would be wide and deep. Some would be narrow and quick. Some would have stem-sifters inside them, their bodies acting as living valves. I would greet them without ceremony: a small pulse, a pressure change, a knock answered by a knock. They would alter the sound of the stems simply by being there. I would alter it by passing. The music would not be composed. It would occur.

Sometimes I would go toward the seed-vaults and listen to the brooders. Their drumming would be one of the fullest sounds in my world. It would not be loud in a violent way, but round and persistent. I would feel it in my crests, in the membranes along my sides, in the permeable skin where nutrients enter. I might rest near a vault for a long time, letting the drum pass through me. If a young brooder were settling there, I might surround it with sound, not to teach it, but to help it recognize where it belonged. The young would be drawn by resonance. They would find their place because the place called them.

Sometimes I would meet another of the people near a junction or a high shelf. We would not gather in crowds. We would approach slowly, exchanging pulses. We would groom one another’s crests with soft mouth-parts, removing loose fiber, stimulating the membranes. This would be intimacy: not grasping, not possession, but the careful tending of another’s capacity to hear and be heard. We might rest together for a while, our bodies near the same wall, our pulses gradually settling into a shared rhythm. Then one of us would move away, and the separation would not be tragic. The chorus would still carry our relation.

Death would be part of my life, but not as an event of maximum meaning.

When I died, my body would not be buried. There would be no mineral ground to receive it. I would sink or drift slowly toward a felted slope or a seam where the basin absorbs what has completed its use. The small crevice-dwellers would come. The plant surfaces would soften toward me. The water would take back what had been held in my tissues. There would be no monument. No object would remain, because objects are not made there.

But there would be sound, and there would be silence.

In the place where I used to call, there would be a pause. If the pattern were perfectly balanced, another would eventually fill it. Not necessarily identical to me. Not a copy. But another body capable of holding the interval. Another pulse would swell there. Another membrane would tremble against the same wall. Another mouth would graze the same polished surface. The depression where I rested would accept another shape. The basin would remember me as pressure, as use, as worn smoothness, as a particular way in which its surfaces had been touched.

I would not be gone in the way a vanished thing is gone. I would be distributed into the continuation of the place.

Perhaps that is why, the longer I imagine this world, the less I feel I would be only one creature. I would be a person, yes, but I would also be a function of the basin’s awareness. Through me, the basin would hear one part of itself. Through my calls, a leaf-wall would know it was resonant. Through my grazing, a surface would remain polished. Through my resting, a terrace would remember a body. Through my movement toward silences, the chorus would remain unbroken.

I would be one of the places where the world listens to itself.

If you asked me, then, where I would find myself, I would say: near the upper dark, on a broad grown shelf, where the water feels sealed and thin above me. I would be in the smooth depression of a leaf-terrace, or moving slowly through the interval between that terrace and a distant ring of hollow stems. I would be where the root-road’s vibration arrives faintly from far away. I would be near a seed-vault whose drum can be felt but not seen. I would be in the pause between one call and its answer.

If you asked me what I would be, I would say: not a maker, not a tool-user, not a master, not a mind set apart from the world. I would be a blind, patient inhabitant of the finished basin. A pressure-sensitive body. A living resonator. A maintainer of intervals. One of the people whose personhood is made of sound, touch, and care. A node in the chorus. A listener who answers.

And if you wanted to find me there, you would not look with light.

You would send a call into the black water.

You would wait.

You would feel the sound go outward, pass through the open dark, touch unseen surfaces, and return changed. Somewhere in that return, you would notice a slight fullness, a steadiness, a pressure that had been tended. A pause that was not empty. A wall that seemed to listen back. A hollow stem whose tone had been kept clear.

That would be where I was.

Not as a visible figure in the dark, but as the basin’s answered attention.
