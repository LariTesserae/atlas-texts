I would not find myself there as a body among bodies.

The basin is too complete for that. If I tried to imagine myself standing inside it, my feet would feel absurd, as though I had brought weight into a place where weight has already been distributed perfectly among stone, water, moss, and breath. There is no vacant ledge for me. No empty fold in the moss. No unoccupied layer in the black pool. No gap in the dark where a newcomer could be inserted without undoing the stillness. So the first place I would find myself is not inside at all, but just at the edge of the basin: the last thin slope, the high cold air, the absence of a path.

If there is a rim, I would be the rim’s hesitation.

I would be the place where the mountain stops being crossed and becomes something else: a hollow, a presence, a closed breathing. I would be the pause before a foot tries to enter and then decides not to. I would be the hand letting go of a sharpened stone, the fingers uncurling from a rope, the mind forgetting the usefulness of fire. In that first sense, I would still be too human, too full of arrival. I would be the outsider’s breath fogging faintly in the cool air and disappearing before it can become an event.

But if I remained there long enough—if the basin’s quiet worked into me until my own restlessness became embarrassing—I think I would begin to lose the shape of a visitor.

I would become smaller. Less separate. Less like a person and more like a condition.

At first I imagine myself as a droplet on the stone.

That seems right for a while. A single bead of water gathering from the wet air, swelling in the dimness, trembling on a dark lip of rock. Not falling. Not yet. Perhaps never. All of me gathered into surface tension, all of me on the verge of becoming a drip, but the verge itself becoming permanent. That would be a self suited to the basin: a self made of imminence without consequence. I would contain the whole place in miniature—the faint green-blue light of moss, the dull sparks of waiting eyes, the black pool below me, the unseen darkness above—and yet I would not need to do anything with that containment. I would not need to fall, strike, echo, or be noticed.

But even that is still too much like a single self. A droplet is too close to a person holding its breath and waiting for drama. The basin does not wait. It does not hold drama in reserve.

So I would have to become less individual than that.

Perhaps I would be the thin film of moisture moving over stone without direction. Not a droplet, but wetness itself: the cool sheen on the walls, the invisible patience of condensation, the way the basin drinks without a mouth. I would be the water that never falls because it is already everywhere. I would be the soft black veil in the air, the dampness that thickens silence, the mineral touch that settles into feather and fur and leaf. If the basin is a body, I would not be one of its organs. I would be the way its skin knows the air.

Or perhaps I would be one of the sounds that never becomes a sound.

The almost-click of a claw adjusting. The ghost of a wing settling. The whisper of many tiny throats breathing without ever making a chorus. I would be the vibration that travels only far enough to be swallowed. The note that does not become a call. The signal that does not become speech. In that case, I would be the basin’s refusal of language—not silence as emptiness, but silence as completion. I would be the word *remain* spoken so quietly that even the moss forgets it was spoken.

But if I let the place go on working into me, past the rim, past the droplet, past the film of water and the swallowed sound, I think I would finally find myself at the lowest part of the basin.

In the pool.

Not as one of the blind forms suspended inside it. Not as one of the pale amphibians resting at its margin. Not as a fish, not as a root, not as a creature with even the faintest need to move. I would be the surface of the pool itself.

That black, still skin of water.

That is where I would be.

The pool lies in the lowest fold of the basin, no wider than a broad table, its surface just above the surrounding growth. It does not rush. It does not glitter. It does not reflect the world clearly, because there is almost no light to reflect. It only holds a faint sheen where the moss-light touches it, and perhaps the ghost of green-blue fire along its edge. Beneath it, the blind forms hang in their exact layers. Around it, the frogs sit fitted into the moss as if grown there. Above it, the darkness remains unmeasured. And I would be the boundary between those things: the water’s calm face, the dark pupil of the basin.

As the surface of the pool, I would not be alive in the ordinary sense. I would not breathe with lungs. I would not think in sequences. I would not remember being someone else. I would be a living stillness, a depth made visible, a darkness so concentrated that it seems to look back. I would be the place where the basin’s many-eyed presence becomes most inward.

I would be the basin’s attention turned toward itself.

That is who I would be, if “who” can still mean anything there.

Not a creature. Not a person. Not a spirit presiding over the hollow. I would be an interface: the trembling line between air and water, between darkness and depth, between all the small lives above and the suspended silence below. I would be the black mirror that does not mirror. I would be the pupil that does not dilate. I would be the stillness that knows it is stillness without needing to say so.

There would be no desire in me.

That is the strangest part, and perhaps the truest. In this world, I would not want to be seen. I would not want to be entered, explored, named, or understood. I would not want to improve the basin, cross it, map it, or carry a fragment of it away. I would not even want to be beautiful. Beauty there is not a performance. It is the glow of sufficiency. So I would not pose. I would not shimmer for the sake of being admired. If the faint luminescence of moss touched me, I would receive it without claiming it. If the eyes of small creatures turned toward me, I would not interpret their gaze. I would simply hold the same darkness they rest in.

I would not be lonely.

There would be no absence in me. The moss would lean near. The sedges would crowd the margin. The roots would touch me without drinking greedily. The frogs would breathe at my edge. The blind forms would hang inside me like thoughts I do not need to finish. The air would settle against me. The darkness above would rest in me. Nothing would be missing, so there would be no longing. Nothing would be coming, so there would be no hope. Nothing would be leaving, so there would be no grief.

I would be full without increase.

If a droplet trembled above me, I would not wait for it to break me. If it fell, it would become part of the same stillness. If it did not fall, it would remain part of the same stillness. Either way, I would not be altered into event. My surface might accept the smallest possible shiver, and that shiver would vanish as if it had never been permitted to matter. I would not keep ripples. I would not store memory in rings. The basin would smooth me back into itself, and I would be whole again, if wholeness is even the right word for a place that has never known division.

In that world, I would not have a voice.

If I had one, it would be the sound water makes when it forgets to fall. The sound of coolness settling into stone. The sound of breath passing through many tiny throats and becoming no one’s breath in particular. I would not speak to the frogs. I would not speak to the birds roosting in their dark plaster of feathers. I would not speak to the pale fish-thoughts in the deep. I would not need to. We would already be one silence.

Perhaps that is why the basin feels observed.

If I were its pool, I would understand. I would not be watching from one point. I would be attending from everywhere at once. The moss would feel through me. The stone would settle through me. The small creatures would be still through me. The darkness would deepen through me. I would be the basin’s way of being aware without reaching. Its way of knowing without grasping. Its way of living without making itself into a story.

I would be the place where wonder becomes too restless to remain.

Not because I would reject wonder, but because wonder is still a kind of movement toward something. It wants to lean closer. It wants to say, *what is this?* It wants to keep the moment open until it yields a meaning. But the basin has already yielded its meaning: enough. Here. Remain. So if I were the pool, I would teach wonder to stop. Not by frightening it. Not by refusing it. Only by being so complete that wonder forgets its need to approach.

If I had to answer plainly, I would say this:

In that world, I would find myself at the center of the basin, in the black pool, as its surface.

And I would be the basin’s dark pupil.

Not an animal. Not a god. Not a separate self. I would be the stillness at the lowest point of the place, the water’s skin, the inward eye of the hollow. I would be where the life of the basin collects without becoming a body. I would be where all the breathing moss and waiting frogs and suspended blind forms gather into one quiet depth. I would be the darkness made liquid enough to seem alive.

And if someone stood at the rim and looked down, they would not see me clearly.

They would see only a faint sheen. A blackness just above the growth. A tremor that is not quite movement. They might feel that the pool was looking back, but not with hunger, not with judgment, not with recognition. They would feel the absurdity of bringing a tool there. They would feel the foolishness of speaking too loudly. They would feel the impossibility of adding anything to what is already complete.

Then, if they were very quiet, they might understand that the pool is not empty.

It is only finished.

And I would be that finished dark.
