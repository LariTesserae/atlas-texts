I would not find myself there by looking.

There would be no finding in that sense. No outline gathered out of the dark, no face turned toward me, no body recognized across distance. Distance would not exist as a visible thing. It would exist as pressure, echo, dampness, smell, and the time between one sound and its answer. So if I were in that world, the first thing I would be is not a creature but a change in the world’s sound.

A roughness in the roar.

A new weight moving through the lower noise.

A pause in nearby bodies.

For a while, I might not be any one animal. I might be the disturbance itself: the large, slow displacement of air and stone that makes smaller lives adjust. Swarms would thin around me before I had a shape. Crawlers would feel my warmth or my chemical trace and alter their paths. The mineral crusts under me would crackle faintly. Pools would register me as a shadow of pressure passing over their skins of film. I would begin as an interruption, because in a place so saturated with life, any new existence is first a problem in the pattern.

But if the basin required me to become a body — and it would, because everything there is eventually forced into a body, into hunger, into being eaten or eating — then I think I would become low.

Not swift. Not luminous. Not delicate.

Low.

I would find myself in one of the worn hollows where the old water channels bend and lose themselves in basin floors. Not at the deepest pool, where competition is thickest, and not on the dry ridges where the air pulls moisture from skin and the waiting animals are too still to be trusted. I would be in the middle ground, where a shallow trough crosses a migration groove, where repeated movement has polished the rock into a smooth, body-worn path. Near enough to water that condensation gathers in my depressions, far enough from flooding that I would not be washed or drowned when the cold rains come.

You would find me at the inside curve of a dry channel, perhaps, where the stone has been scooped by immense time and by the pressure of countless bodies moving along the same ancient route. There would be a hollow there, rounded and shallow, with a rim crusted by pale mineral bloom. A drip would strike it only occasionally, from some unseen height, and each drop would sound like a small cold bell. Around it, the floor would be alive: rasping grazers moving over microbial film, small segmented predators slipping into cracks, the soft constant percussion of limbs and shells and mouths.

And I would be in that hollow, or I would seem to be the hollow.

I would be one of the large, slow, patient forms that the basin tolerates but does not celebrate. Not rare enough to be a legend — there are no legends without memory — but uncommon enough that my stillness would be mistaken for stone. My body would be broad and flattened, heavy enough to resist the crowds that move over all surfaces. Its upper side would not be smooth. It would be ridged, pitted, and worn, with plates of chitin and mineral accretion fitted so tightly that they would seem geological. Black bacterial film would grow in the damper seams of my back. Pale crusts would bloom where moisture evaporates slowly. Fungal threads might trail from my edges when the air is humid, and tiny crawlers would live among them, not knowing whether I am a creature or a landscape.

I would not have eyes.

That is important. I would not have eyes waiting in the dark, not even dim ones. The basin has no use for them, and I would not want the burden of unused sight. Instead, my body would be mapped with other openings: sensory pits along the rims of my plates, fine filaments tasting the air for moisture and death, membranes stretched over internal hollows, bristles rooted in joints and carapace. I would feel the world as vibration first. Every footfall, every wingbeat, every falling corpse, every pool disturbed, every lie tapped into stone by a hunting predator would arrive in me as a pattern.

My mind, if I could still be said to have one, would not be made of images. It would be made of intervals.

Drip. Scrape. Swarm-thrum. Stillness.

Moisture rising.

A body falling nearby.

The change in pressure before rain.

I would know the basin not as a view but as a field of answers. Stone would return sound to me. Water would carry tremor. Air would bring chemical news. The bodies of other creatures would be events: warm, cold, frantic, slow, edible, dangerous, irrelevant. I would not separate these categories cleanly. The world would not ask me to.

My mouth would not be a face.

It would be a bristled opening, a grate, a slow suction, something made for taking dense life when dense life is offered. I would not hunt in the way the flattened crevice-predators hunt, striking at single tremors. I would be too large for that, and too patient. I would feed when the basin becomes excessive: when larval pools thicken into writhing mass, when swarms rise after rain and fall exhausted, when dead fliers drift into hollows and are already boiling with scavengers. Then I would open, or extend, or draw inward, and I would take what comes in sheets rather than bites. Not with malice. Not with cruelty. With the simple inevitability of a system that recycles everything.

Small animals would live near me. Some would live on me. They would crawl across my plates, hide in the ridges of my carapace, lay eggs in the damp seams where condensation collects. I would not be gentle with them, but I would not be constantly hostile either. I would be too slow to concern myself with every small touch. In a world without tools or territories marked by intention, my body would become a kind of geography. A resting surface. A windbreak made of heat. A vibration-shadow where the swarm’s rhythm changes. Perhaps some creatures would learn, not by knowledge but by pressure-memory, that my stillness means shelter from larger movement. Perhaps others would learn that my stillness means hunger waiting.

Both would be true.

I would not be innocent there. Innocence would not survive. But I would not be what a surface mind might call monstrous. The basin does not permit monstrosity as a moral category. It permits consumption, patience, density, and recycling. If I killed, I would kill as a condition of existing. If I allowed others to live on me, I would do so because their smallness did not threaten me, or because their movements cleaned my plates of film, or because the basin’s chemistry made their presence useful. Nothing there would be sentimental. Everything would be entangled.

When the air dried and the stone tightened, I would slow. My breathing, if it could be called breathing, would deepen and become almost imperceptible. I would withdraw toward the nearest permanent pool or humid depression, moving so slowly that the crowds ahead of me would not scatter but simply part and rejoin behind me, as if I were a drifting boulder. My body would conserve moisture. The films on my back would darken and retreat into the seams. The smaller lives around me would become more cautious. In dry periods, the basin does not sleep; it contracts. It gathers around water and waits with open mouths.

When the rains came — those cold, unseen rains falling from high dark — I would wake into something older than joy.

Not happiness. Recognition.

The stone would change. The air would thicken. The chemical smell of wet rock would spread like a command. Pools would fill. Egg masses would swell. Larvae would hatch in sheets. Swarms would rise as if the darkness itself had become breathable. The noise would shift from continuous pressure to a higher, more fevered architecture: splash, snap, wing-shudder, shriek, drip, drone. And in me, the hollow chambers would answer.

Then I would call.

Not often. Not casually. But sometimes the pressure would demand it: moisture, hunger, swarm-density, the deep internal rhythm that even such a slow body must obey. The sound would begin in my chest-plates, or in whatever structure served as my chest, and move through the stone before it moved through the air. It would be a boom or drone felt more than heard, a vibration that travels through the soles of feet, through the legs of crawlers, through the membranes of fliers. It would not announce me as a king announces himself. It would announce a condition. A threshold. A change in the basin’s weather of bodies.

Swarms might turn.

Predators might pause.

The living ground might ripple outward from me in concentric disturbance.

I would not command them. I would only be large enough, and resonant enough, to make my state of being shared.

That, I think, is who I would be: not a ruler, not a hunter in the elegant sense, but a resonant animal. A living amplifier. A body with hollows shaped by old erosion, continuing erosion’s work by becoming another worn thing that happens to be hungry. I would be more like a basin within the basin than a person within it. My identity would be diffuse. I would know myself by the way vibrations return, by the chemical taste of my own moisture, by the lives that settle on me and flee from me. I would not have a mirror. I would not need one. The world would reflect me as pressure.

If you came into that darkness, you would not find me by searching with your hands alone. You would find me by the change in sound near me. The roar would still be there — deafening, layered, immense — but around my hollow it would gather differently. Lower. Slower. A thickening of resonance. You might feel the floor rise under you and think it was stone until the stone breathed. You might hear, or feel, a vibration beneath the dripping and the wingbeat and the rasp of mouths: a slow boom, not loud but enormous, as if the rock itself had remembered it was once alive.

You might mistake me for a ledge.

For a worn bench.

For a dry pothole where old water has polished the world into patience.

Then the air around you would shift, and you would know that what you are touching is warm.

That would be where I would find myself: in the low country, at the crossing of body-worn paths, near the temporary pools, in a hollow that is not quite a hollow. And who I would be is harder to say in a single word, because the basin does not favor single words. But if one is required, I would be a listener. A blind, plated, mineral-crusted listener with hunger in its architecture. An animal made of darkness answering itself. Not a voice in the roar, but one of the deep places where the roar folds back and becomes shape.
