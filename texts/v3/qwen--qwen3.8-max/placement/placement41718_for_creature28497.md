The basin is inhabited by the beings that can be made from it and nothing else. If I were to find myself there, I would not come as a visitor standing upright against the sky, looking for a way through. There is no way through. There is only being within. So I think I would find myself low, near the water’s seam, at one of the many soft borders where open pool becomes floating mat, where steam becomes leaf-shade, where warm mineral ground becomes root-tangle. Not at the center, exactly, but at a hinge. A place where several systems meet and speak to one another.

At first I imagine I might be a bird, because birds have the advantage of height. I could ride the thermal columns above the basin, see the whole amphitheater spread out in green and copper and pale vapor, and drop down into reedbeds when the air thickened before rain. But that feels too detached. The basin seems to belong most fully to bodies that are damp from within, bodies that cannot leave the water’s logic for long. A bird crosses the basin. It does not completely belong to the wet engine of it.

Then I imagine I might be a reed, rooted in the floating peat, my whole body given over to slow water and light. That is closer. A reed participates in the basin without asking anything of it. It holds mud together, offers stems to insects, bends under rain, whistles when warm air passes through hollow joints. But a reed is too fixed. The basin has so much motion — condensation, seepage, evaporation, hunting, hiding, drifting seed, rising vapor — that I think I would want a body able to move through those motions, however slowly.

For a while I think I might be one of the eel-like creatures in the deep thermal channels, moving through warm darkness with patient muscular waves. That would be intimate with the basin’s hidden plumbing, but too concealed. I would know only channels, roots, and black water. I would miss the light on the pools, the morning drip from leaves, the wide humid view across the marsh flats.

What finally emerges is something between these possibilities: not airborne, not rooted, not hidden entirely, but marginal. I would be a creature of the edges. If the basin has a name for such beings, they might be called marsh-listeners.

I would find myself on a broad, low terrace somewhere between the open thermal pool and the denser reedbeds. The terrace is not high above the water, only enough to stay above the warmest seeps. It is worn smooth by long ages of overflow and root growth. Black basalt lies underneath, rounded and cracked, with moss pressing into every hollow. A thin sheet of water moves over one side of the stone, not quickly, barely more than a trembling film, then gathers in a shallow depression where algae make the surface look dark green and slightly bronzed. Above me, a leaning shrub drips condensation from its leaves. Behind me, a mossy shelf rises toward a stand of cane-like plants whose hollow stems make faint sounds when the air shifts. Ahead, the terrace drops into a reedbed, and beyond that the basin opens into heat-shimmer and distant vapor columns.

My shelter would not be built. It would be a hollow under an overhang of root and stone, worn out by water and by generations of bodies seeking the same protection. The ceiling would be black rock stained rust-brown and pale with mineral crust. Moss would hang from it, holding water like cloth. In heavy rain, threads of water would fall past the entrance, but inside the hollow the air would remain warm and still. The floor would be soft: dead stems, root fibers, fine sediment, and living moss at the edges. Nothing arranged. Only accumulated.

I would be amphibious, but not cleanly so. My body would be low and long, broad across the chest and shoulders, with limbs strong enough for pulling through root mazes and resting on wet stone. My skin would be dark green, mottled with black and iron-rust, slick but not slimy, always carrying a faint mineral bloom. It would be permeable, sensitive, able to taste the water around it. Tiny algae might live in the outer layers of my skin, giving me a dull living sheen. Moss and microbial film might cling to my back where I rest under the drip, so that when I am still I look like a wet stone or a clump of vegetation. I would not be beautiful in a clean or ornamental way. I would be handsome in the way a river-worn tool is handsome: fitted to use, stained by contact, unpolished.

My hands and feet would be broad and soft-palmed, able to spread weight over floating peat without breaking through too easily. Between my fingers and toes there would be webbing, but also delicate nerve-rich folds, so that I could feel changes in water temperature, pressure, and current. My eyes would sit high on my head, accustomed to looking through mist and glare. They would not be large or dramatic, but patient, with a way of taking in slow movement across great distances. My nostrils would close when submerged. Along my sides or under my jaw there would be organs sensitive to weak electric fields, because the basin’s water is full of ionic movement and living charge. I would not think of this as electricity in the human sense. I would feel it as a kind of weather inside water: the nearness of fish, the tension before a storm, the pulse of a vent changing pressure underground.

I would be warm, not in the dry mammalian sense, but in the basin’s sense: my body would carry heat from the ground and water, conserve it, let it circulate slowly. When I rested on a warm rock, I would absorb its temperature and release it gradually when I moved into shade. My breathing would be quiet. I could take air through lungs when necessary, but I would also exchange gases through my skin in oxygen-rich shallows. In very warm pools, where oxygen is lower, I would move less, resting with only my nose above the surface, letting the water hold me.

I would eat from several levels of the basin. Not as a predator only, and not as a grazer only. I would scrape microbial mats from stable stones where the water is warm but not too hot. I would take insect larvae from floating root tangles. I would swallow small crustacean-like creatures from algal shallows, if such beings exist there. I would occasionally take soft plant tissue, young shoots, fallen seeds, or the tender inner stems of dead reeds. I might also swallow mineral grains accidentally, and perhaps some of them would aid digestion, the way grit aids some living things. Nothing I ate would be separate from the basin’s larger work. My feeding would be one of the ways the basin processes itself.

I would not have tools. That is important. The basin permits no artifacts, and I would not be the kind of being who carries objects, stores food in containers, or shapes materials into instruments. My technologies would be bodily and relational. My webbed hands would stir sediment just enough to release trapped gases and encourage microbial growth. My movement through root channels would keep small waterways open. My skin would carry spores, pollen, diatoms, and tiny eggs from one pool to another. My call, if I had one, would not be language but rhythm: a low, resonant note made in the throat or chest, timed partly to the pulse of nearby vents, partly to the presence of others like me. At dusk, when the marsh fills with insect sound and the light goes copper, several marsh-listeners might call across the water, and the sound would be soft, intermittent, flattened by humidity, like the basin clearing its throat.

I would live among others, but not in a society built by intention. We would not gather because we had decided to. We would gather where the conditions invite us: near stable heat, near food-rich mats, near sheltering roots. There would be territories only in the loose sense that bodies prefer certain hollows, certain perches, certain feeding shelves. If another marsh-listener approached, we would read one another through posture, scent, skin color, and the faint electric disturbance of movement in water. Conflict would be brief and mostly ceremonial. The basin is generous, but it is also crowded; life here has learned not to waste energy on endless struggle.

My daily round would follow heat and water. In the morning, when mist lies low and every surface drips, I would remain half-submerged in my hollow, letting condensation run over me. The basin at that hour is almost unbearably intimate. Every leaf carries a bead. Every stone sweats. The air feels like wet cloth. I would breathe slowly, skin drinking, eyes half closed, listening to the small sounds: drip, drip, the whisper of a reed, a bubble breaking in mud, a bird calling somewhere invisible.

As the sun strengthens and heat rises from the ground, I would move. Movement would not be hurried. I would cross the terrace on all fours, pausing where the stone is warmer, sliding into shallow water where the algae make the bottom soft. I might rest on a silica crust near a vent, feeling its faint pulse through my belly. The crust would be pale and rough, almost bone-white in places, with tiny droplets forming along its edges. Steam would rise nearby, not violently, but in lazy ribbons that tremble and dissolve. From there I could see across the basin: blue-green marsh flats, black ridges softened by moss, reed islands, pale vapor columns, and the high outer walls dropping into cloud. The view would not feel empty. It would feel full of slow work.

In the warm middle of the day, I would feed. I would move along the margin where the water is shallow enough for light to reach the bottom but deep enough to hide me from wading birds. My hands would turn over loose root clumps carefully, not destroying them, only opening them enough to find larvae and small moving things. My mouth would take what is needed. I would be alert, but not fearful in a sharp way. Fear in the basin is a background sense, like temperature. There are predators here: fish in the deeper channels, snakes or snake-like hunters in the reeds, birds that strike from above. I would know their signs: a sudden stillness in insects, a ripple moving against the wind, the shadow of wings, the displacement of water where something large has turned. When danger came, I would freeze first. My coloration would do most of the work. If that failed, I would slide into the root maze, where larger bodies cannot follow easily.

I would also be part of the basin’s electrical life. Before storms, the air would change. The marsh would become tense in a way that is not frightening but intensely present. Fine hairs or sensory filaments along my body would rise. The surface of pools would look metallic. Insects would go quiet or move in sudden bursts. I would feel the charge in my skin and in the water. If lightning were near, I would retreat to my hollow or submerge fully, pressing low into mud and roots. The basin does not hide from weather; it receives it. Rain would come not as interruption but as another form of industry. It would drum on leaves, dimple every pool, wash pollen from surfaces, stir the warm water into motion. Afterward, the whole marsh would steam more strongly, as if the storm had fed the engines below.

In such moments I would understand myself not as an individual separate from the landscape but as a small organ of the basin. My body would help regulate it in tiny ways. By eating larvae, I would prevent certain populations from overrunning a shelf. By moving between pools, I would carry nutrients and living particles. By disturbing sediment gently, I would release gases and allow fresh water to reach buried roots. By being eaten, eventually, by some larger creature, I would transfer stored energy back into the food web. By dying, if I died in the marsh, I would sink into peat and become carbon, mineral film, insect nursery, dark fertile matter. The basin would not mourn me. It would continue me in another form.

There would be pleasure in this existence, but not the human kind.
