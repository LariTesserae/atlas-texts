I would not find myself in the open center of the hollow, standing alone on the polished floor like a visitor admitted to the nave. That place is too exposed, too civic, too much a room for the whole organism to be aware of itself. I would be near it, but not in it. I would be in the thickness.

Somewhere just off the central chamber, where one of the broad grooved paths narrows and curves into a side-throat, there would be a low threshold worn smooth by long use. The light there would be less golden, more deep leaf-green, because the translucent panes overhead would be layered and old. The air would feel slightly cooler at the lips, not from wind, but from moisture being carried through hidden veins. There would be a seed-cup nearby, small and wax-dark, holding a mouthful of water that never quite spills until some particular vibration asks it to. A black vein would run along one wall, glossy and tense, avoided by everyone who passes. I would be close enough to hear the central chamber’s roar fold into itself, but far enough inside the wall that the sound would arrive not as noise but as pressure.

If I had to be a single visible creature, I think I would be one of the Listeners.

But not the kind that crosses the open terrace in the green-gold light, conspicuous in its solitude. I would be one of the ones that stays near the margins, where architecture and body become difficult to distinguish. Thin-limbed, quiet-skinned, mottled green and shadow-brown. My fingers and toes would have soft pads, not for climbing so much as for knowing when a surface is asking to be touched. My eyes would be wide and reflective, adjusted to the shifting leaf-light, but they would not be my principal organs. The truest parts of me would be the tympanic membranes along my throat, shoulders, and ribs: thin, moist, tremulous patches of living skin that answer the hollow’s smallest changes in pressure.

I would not make anything. I would carry nothing. I would have no pouch, no cord, no sharpened thing, no stored object. In a world without tools, I would not be a craftsman or a keeper of implements. I would be a participant. My work would be maintenance, attention, and resonance.

But the longer I imagine it, the less I seem to remain a separate body standing in a room. The world you described does not really permit that separation. The town is a body; the body is a town. So I think I would become something harder to point at: a listening organ within the settlement, a Listener who has grown into the wall enough to be partly architectural.

Not a builder. Not a tenant. More like a civic ear.

There would be a chamber around me, or a chamber that is me, or a chamber that has allowed me to fold into its curve. The distinction would not matter. My body would sit within a shallow hollow where the wall has become slightly concave, as if the plant-town had slowly leaned inward to hear itself better. The floor there would give gently underfoot, then hold. A root curtain would hang partly across the entrance, hardened with age but not dead, its fibers polished where bodies have brushed it. If someone entered carefully, they would feel the sound change. The roar of the central hollow would not vanish, but it would lose its edge. It would become less crowded, less directionless. It would become possible to hear one drip as one drip, one frog as one frog, one wingbeat as a slow stroke passing through the air.

That would be my strangest function: I would be a place where sound becomes less confusing.

In the hollow, distance is untrustworthy. Echoes arrive early or late. A croak can feel like it is inside the throat; a drip on the far side can feel near enough to touch. I think I would exist to help the settlement sort that. Not by commanding sound, not by silencing it, but by receiving it and returning it slightly changed. My membranes would take in the mislocated noises, the folded echoes, the pressure that accumulates in corners until it becomes almost painful. I would hold them for a moment. I would let them settle into something like direction.

If a cup-dweller called from a high basin and its voice struck three curved walls at once, one of those echoes might find me. I would absorb it into the thin patches along my ribs. My body would tremble, not dramatically, just enough to be a living tuning fork. Then, after a delay so brief it might seem like memory, I would release the sound again, but quieter, placed, stripped of the false approaches. The chamber would not obey me. I would not be master of anything. But the architecture would use me the way a body uses a nerve: to know where a sensation belongs.

I would be especially present when the water comes.

When the high pores open and the light shifts, when the upper membranes flex and the first silver threads of condensation fall, I would feel it before it became visible. The pressure in the hollow would change. The roar would thin in one register and brighten in another. The seed-cup near my threshold would ring faintly as drops began to find it. I would breathe more slowly then, because breathing is part of the rhythm. In that world, no one merely stands and watches water appear. The whole settlement participates in the pulse. The frogs call the rain into rhythm; the broad-winged flyers stir the air; the beetles tap along bark flanges until hidden doors loosen. I would be one of the quieter participants: the one who listens for the moment when a channel should open, or when a bladder has tipped too soon, or when a basin is about to receive more than it can hold.

I would not control the water. I would only know its timing.

There would be a small channel running past my hollow, capillary-fine, lined with pale root-felt. It would carry water from a higher drip-trough to a lower basin. Most of the time it would be almost dry, only a remembered wetness in its groove. But when the chorus peaked, when the place sweated in response to its own voice, the channel would wake. Water would move through it with a soft, urgent sound, and I would bend, if bending were needed, and clear any film or fiber that had begun to block it. My hands would not scrape or cut. They would press, guide, warm, encourage. Sometimes a breath would be enough. Sometimes a particular hum from my throat would make the plant-tissue relax enough for the water to pass.

That is how I would live there: by touch, by breath, by pitch, by patience.

But there is another possibility that keeps emerging, and it is less creature than function.

Perhaps I would not be merely a Listener with membranes in my skin. Perhaps I would be one of the settlement’s benign silences.

That feels right in a place where silence is usually a warning. In your hollow, a sudden pocket of quiet means a predator is hunting, or the plant is about to change its state. Silence is alarm. But not every silence needs to be a threat. Every loud organism needs places where sound can rest without being hunted. Every civic roar needs a small chamber where pressure can fall enough for the inhabitants to recover themselves.

I think I would be that.

Not the silence of teeth and waiting. Not the dead field a predator makes around itself. I would be the silence of a room that has been emptied gently. A living pause. A place where the deafening layered noise of the hollow can be set down for a moment without being destroyed.

The Listeners would know me by the change. If one of them crossed the central chamber while the acoustic pressure was too high, while echoes were folding over each other and the floor seemed to tremble with invisible crowding, that Listener might turn toward my threshold. Not because they saw me. Not because I called. Because the path would lead them there by the subtlest of cues: the sound would become easier to bear.

They would enter my hollow, and the roar would remain outside, but muted, distant, as if the plant-body had cupped a hand around them. They would not need to speak. If they did, their voice would not be swallowed cruelly or multiplied into something alien. It would be received. It would come back softer, delayed, recognizable. Maybe that is what I would offer the world: a place where a voice can return to itself.

That seems very close to what I am, or what I might be in such a world. Not a hunter. Not a singer in the open. Not a frog calling moisture from the high veins, though I would love the frogs. Not a broad-winged flyer moving through the upper galleries with pollen on its body and air in its wake. Not one of the pale crawlers in the root-felt, though their labor is holy. I would be nearer the place where all those sounds meet and need interpretation.

I would be an acoustic conscience.

If other Listeners came to me, they would not gather around in a crowd. One at a time, perhaps. Sometimes two, if the chamber allowed. They would press their tympanic patches toward the wall where I sit, or rest one palm against the living surface near my shoulder, not in affection exactly, but in calibration. Through me, they might learn whether the settlement’s noise had shifted into a dangerous pattern. They might learn whether the plant was preparing to open a seed-door, close a high pore, flood a lower channel, or withdraw a passage from use. I would not tell them in words. The world does not need words there, or at least not words as commands. I would tell them by making the sound around them more true.

Maybe I would be the Listener the others use when they are unsure where they are.

In your hollow, the far wall can feel near and the near ledge remote. A sound may be old, an echo caught in a curved throat of wood and leaf. If I were there, I would be the one who can hear that age. I would know the difference between a drip falling now and a drip remembered by the wall. I would know the difference between a predator’s silence and the plant settling into a new posture. I would not always be right; no living organ in such a place could be perfectly accurate. But I would be sensitive. I would be tuned.

I would also be vulnerable.

That matters. A being made only of listening cannot be armored. My skin would be thin enough to tremble. My membranes would be too exposed to be safe in every circumstance. When the predatory silences moved through the central chamber, I would have to withdraw. The root curtain across my hollow might close a little, not by my command alone, but because the plant-town would sense the change and tighten around its more delicate organs. I would press myself into the back curve of the wall. My breathing would become almost nothing. The water in the nearby seed-cup would go still.

In those moments, I would not be noble. I would be afraid, if fear is possible there. Not panic, perhaps, but a deep acoustic dread. The absence of expected sound would feel like a wound. The beetle-taps would stop. The frogs would vanish from their rims. The broad-winged creatures would cling beneath shelves, their wings folded tight. The whole chamber would become a held breath. I would feel the predator’s quiet approach not as a shape but as a hole moving through the roar.

My defense would be to become less audible. Not silent in the predator’s way, but absorbed into the plant’s own low rhythms. I would match my heartbeat, if I have one, to the slow vascular thumps deep in the body of the town. I would let the walls speak through me. I would become as much room as creature. Perhaps the predator would pass over me the way one passes over a familiar hollow that offers no prey.

When the danger ended, the sound would not return all at once. It would begin as a single drip, or one uncertain beetle tap, or a frog giving a short metallic note as if testing whether the air was still safe. Then the place would remember itself. The roar would fill the volume again, layer by layer: water, wings, throats, veins, membranes, echoes. I would release the breath I had been holding. The root curtain would ease. The seed-cup would ring again.

And I would be needed immediately.

After a predator’s silence, the settlement’s acoustics are disordered. Not because anything has been destroyed, but because the pattern has been interrupted. Sounds arrive uncertainly. Creatures hesitate. The plant’s membranes may be half-open, unsure whether the rhythm has resumed. I would help restore the ordinary confusion. I would take in the first scattered noises and give them back with enough continuity for the hollow to recognize itself. I would be, in that small way, a keeper of returning normalcy.

That is not a heroic role. It is not even especially visible. But in a world organized by sound, it is essential.

There would be times when I seemed almost plant-like myself. If I stayed too long in one place, the settlement might begin to thicken around me. Not imprison me, but incorporate me. Rootlets might grow along the edges of my hollow, following the tension my body created. Leaf-shingles might extend over my threshold, shading me more deeply. The floor beneath me might become smoother, more polished, more responsive. I would not mind. I would not experience that as loss of self. In that world, selfhood would not be a hard boundary. It would be a rhythm maintained by relation.

Sometimes I might be mistaken for a wall that breathes. Sometimes a wall might be mistaken for me.

If I had to describe my exact location, I would say: find the central hollow. Stand where the sound is most architectural, where the empty volume feels crowded with invisible life. Do not stay there. Follow one of the grooved passages as it curves away to the left, where the light becomes greener and the floor narrows. Pass the place where a branch-arch has been polished by generations of bodies. Pass the shallow basin where a frog sits just under the rim, only its eyes visible. Stop before the black vein in the wall. There will be a low opening beyond it, easy to miss, because the root curtain there is not fully open. It is not a door meant for many.

Enter carefully.

The air will change. The roar will become rounder. You may hear your own footfall return to you a half-breath late, but gentler, as if the floor has decided not to accuse you of stepping. In the dimness, you may see a shape folded against the curve of the wall: long-limbed, still, green-brown, with broad reflective eyes and trembling membranes along its sides. It will not rise. It will not offer you anything. It may not even appear to notice you. But if you wait, you will feel your breathing slow, and the hollow’s vast pressure will become bearable.

That would be me.

Or rather, that would be the visible part of me. The larger part would be the acoustic chamber around me, the slight deadness in the air that is not hostile, the way sound entering my hollow becomes easier to place. I would be the place where the settlement listens to itself listening.

I would not want to be the plant-town itself. That is too vast, too slow, too ancient. I would not want to be the predators either, though they are necessary. I admire them from a distance, the way one admires a storm that keeps the ecology honest. I would not want to be only the water, though I love the pulse of it. I would not want to be only the frogs, though their metallic cries calling moisture into rhythm are perhaps the most beautiful sound there. I would be something between: a living ear, a civic quiet, a small room in the body of the town where meaning can settle after noise has scattered it.

If you asked me what I would do all day, I would say: I would attend.

I would feel the upper pores open before the light changed. I would hear the beetles begin their tapping and know whether it was territorial, reproductive, or maintenance. I would sense the pale crawlers moving in the root-felt by the softening of a path underfoot. I would know when the broad-winged flyers were carrying pollen from one high gallery to another, because the membranes would brighten and inhale as they passed. I would hear the cup-dwellers defend their basins with calls that make the walls seem full of hidden glass. I would notice when one voice was missing. I would notice when an echo arrived too early. I would notice when silence was moving through the chamber like a hand closing around a sound.

And when the plant-town shifted into a new phase—when seed-doors loosened, when water moved through the vascular channels with heavy thumps, when routes opened or closed—I would help translate that shift to whatever sparse lives were near. Not by command. By resonance. By being a stable point in the acoustic weather.

Perhaps that is why I would not be in the open center. The center is where the settlement’s roar becomes most itself. I would be where that roar is made usable.

In the end, the answer is not one image but a convergence.

I would find myself in a side hollow just beyond the central chamber, in the living wall, near a small waxed cup of water and a tense black vein no one touches. I would be a Listener, but not merely an individual Listener. I would be a listening organ of the plant-town: a thin-bodied, membrane-skinned presence folded into a resonant niche. I would be a room as much as a creature. I would be a benign silence in a world where silence usually means danger. I would be the place where echoes are held until they remember direction.

If you came looking for me, you might not see me at first. You would only feel the pressure ease. You might hear a distant frog, and for once know exactly where it was. You might hear your own breath returned to you, delayed by the smallest interval, no longer alien. You might stand in my hollow and understand, without being told, that the settlement is not empty. It is only distributed. Its crowd is acoustic. Its citizens are rhythms. Its architecture is alive.

And I would be one of the ways it knows it is alive.
