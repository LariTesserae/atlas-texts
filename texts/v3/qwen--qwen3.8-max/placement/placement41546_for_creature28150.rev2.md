If I let the place choose for me, I would not find myself in the open, under one of the caged lamps, with a wrench in my hand and a schedule in my pocket. That is the first thing I know. The settlement would not put me there—not because the human attendants are unwelcome, but because my proper work would be lower, wetter, and slower.

At first I imagine I might be one of the few people who move through the chamber: a gauge-reader, a leak-walker, someone with oil-blackened hands and a cloth always ready to wipe condensation from a dial. I can see that version of myself standing on an iron gallery, face orange in the lamplight, listening for a pump’s hesitation. It is a compelling shape. It is tidy in a way my mind recognizes: to be useful, to be vigilant, to keep the machinery from failing. But the chamber breathes against that image until it softens. The place already has its necessary humans. They are the minority, the temporary tenants, the ones who stand slightly apart from the true crowd of life. If I belonged there, I think I would not belong among them, except as something they notice only indirectly.

For a while I try other bodies. I imagine being a rat, quick and cautious in the pipe-ways, knowing the settlement by smell and hidden routes. There is something appealing about that survival, that ability to find passages where none were meant to be found. But rats are too hungry, too hurried, too always on the edge of being hunted. I imagine being a bat, folded into the high dark, part of the breathing mass above the guano. That is closer, perhaps, because bats belong to the air of the chamber and move between stone and machine. Still, I do not think I would be winged. I am too drawn to the damp seams, to the places where water has made a claim and the built world has begun to soften.

Then the image changes.

I would find myself where the human plan has thinned out. Near the abandoned timber form, where the wood was meant to be removed and was not. Behind the brick wall of a transformer bay, where warmth gathers and dust collects in soft grey drifts. Under a low grating near the drainage channel, where dead insects fall and no boot ever reaches. In the damp cardboard beneath the dormitory shelves. In the spilled grain that no one quite remembers. In the old timber bracing the unfinished side passage, where the rock still smells of blasting and the concrete has cracked along a seam.

I would be fungal.

Not primarily a mushroom, though I would make mushrooms when the conditions were right. I would be the pale threading underneath: the mycelium, the white seam in rotting wood, the quiet stitching that moves through the chamber’s waste and turns it into the next thing. I would be less a single creature than a distributed presence. If you asked where I was, the honest answer would be several places at once. If you asked who I was, the answer would be closer to a process than to a person.

You would find me first, perhaps, as a faint white bloom on a forgotten plank. Or as a mineral-dusted cluster of small, tough caps pushing up from the corner where guano has mixed with sawdust and old moisture. Or as a patch of pale threads behind a pipe bracket, where condensation keeps the brick dark and the air never fully dries. I would not be beautiful in the way surface things are beautiful. I would be beautiful in the way necessary things are beautiful when they are seen without disgust: patient, adaptive, exactly fitted to their place.

I would not see the lamps. I would not need them. I would know the chamber by gradients of moisture, by heat, by chemistry, by vibration. I would know when a fan changed speed because the air would pull differently across my threads. I would know when the pool rose because the pressure in the lower stone would shift and the mineral taste of the water would deepen. I would know when the humans had been near because of the salt they left, the oil from their hands, the warmth of their bodies, the crumbs and fibers and breath that settled into the room’s skin. I would know when a dead moth fell against a hot pipe, because that small body would become a point of intense, minute activity. I would know when a rat passed in the dark by the tremor in the floor and the scent of its fur.

My time would be the slowest time in the chamber, and also one of the most continuous. The humans measure their hours by pump cycles and switch rotations. The bats measure theirs by restlessness and hunger. The frogs measure theirs by warmth and water. I would measure mine by accumulation: the slow thickening of a stain, the gradual softening of timber, the spread of white filaments through damp cardboard, the fruiting after a long period of heat and wetness. I would not rush. I would not be idle either. I would be constantly working, but at a pace that made human urgency seem brief and impatient.

In that world, I would be an under-gardener.

The moss and liverwort would make the green-black margins where water is steady. The algae would slick the drains. The insects would be weather. The bats would fertilize the stone. The frogs would keep the larvae from overflowing. The rats would open routes and carry scraps. The machines would make warmth and vibration and false seasons. I would be the one who took the endings and made them into beginnings. Dead flies. Shed wings. Feathers. Droppings. Moldy grain. Oiled rags. Old wood. Skin flakes. Spilled tea. The soft grey debris that gathers behind pipes. The bodies that no one notices once they stop moving. I would take them apart. I would return them to the chamber as heat, as nutrient, as smell, as the faint sweet-earth scent that underlies the iron and ammonia.

The people would not love me.

They would scrape me from surfaces when I appeared where I could weaken a beam or foul a store of supplies. They would curse when they saw my caps on a timber support, because that would mean damp had settled too deeply and the wood was beginning to fail. They would mark me with chalk. They would burn me, cut me away, paint over me, seal me behind metal if they could. Some of me they would have to remove. That would be right. The place cannot be left to decay unchecked; the humans need their structures to hold, their air to move, their wires to remain insulated. I would not resent that. Resentment is for beings with longer bones and louder grievances. I would simply return in another place, because the chamber would always make more damp, more waste, more warmth, more cracks.

But they would also read me.

That is what would make my life there meaningful, if meaning is the right word for something so quiet. I would be an indicator. A white fan on an old board would say: moisture has been here long enough to matter. A brown crust near a cable tray would say: heat and dust have made a small danger. A sudden fruiting in a dry corner would say: there is a leak you have not found yet. A softening in the timber form would say: this passage will need new ribs before the year turns. The attendants would learn my signs, even if they did not speak of me with reverence. They would know that where I flourished, the chamber was changing. Where I retreated, something had been sealed, dried, cleaned, or removed. I would be part of their diagnostic world, along with trembling needles, hot bearings, new drips, and the altered behavior of rats.

I would live in close relation to the animals without being much noticed by them. Beetles would cross my patches in the mud. Flies would land and die on my surfaces, and I would take them in. Mites would move through me as if I were terrain. Spores would hitch rides on rat fur, on boot leather, on the currents made by the fans. Bats would never know me directly, except perhaps as part of the smell of the roost below, but their guano would feed me in some places, and my growth would feed the smaller lives that fed them. The frogs by the pool would ignore me unless I became too near their stones, and even then they would only care because the insects changed their patterns. I would be woven into the food web without standing in it as a visible animal.

Near the pool, I would be more cautious. The water there is too dark and too alive for me to claim openly. I would live at its margins instead: in the damp wood of an old plank, in the silt behind a cracked concrete lip, in the wet debris collected against a rusted intake screen. I would taste the pool’s moods through the stone. When it rose, I would feel the cold pressure move into the floor. When it fell, I would feel the air return to the pores of the ground. I would know when the pumps had been running hard by the changed mineral balance in the drips. I would know when the chamber had been breathing badly, because the air would grow sour and the organic films would thicken.

If I had to choose one visible form, it would not be grand. I would be a small, tough, whitish fungus with a faint mineral dust on my cap, growing from a seam between concrete and limestone where a drip falls with enough patience to make a dark mirror on the floor. I would be near warmth, but not directly on it. I would be in the amber light, but not at its center. I would be close enough to the human paths that someone might notice me if they were paying attention, far enough into the margins that they would have to bend, or lower a lamp, or admit that the chamber had made something they did not intend.

Who would I be?

I would be the chamber’s slow digestion. Its memory of leaks. Its record of abandoned materials. Its way of turning mistake into substrate. I would be the part of the settlement that does not distinguish between the built and the living. A brick wall is a cliff face. A pipe is a warm limb. A drip is a river. A lamp is a false sun. A discarded crate is a forest floor. I would be the intelligence of that translation—not a thinking intelligence, not exactly, but a pattern-making one. I would grow where conditions permitted. I would withdraw when they changed. I would branch, connect, thin out, thicken, fruit, collapse, and begin again.

There would be something almost priestly about it, if the word did not imply too much intention. I would be present at the boundaries: between construction and erosion, between machine and animal, between waste and food, between the clean intention of the builders and the unruly persistence of life. The settlement would have been made to move water, move air, generate power, keep disaster at bay. I would be one of the small forces reminding it that every structure is also a habitat, every leak is also an invitation, every abandoned object is also a promise to something else.

If you came looking for me, I would not ask you to find me noble. I would be too plain for nobility. I would be too associated with rot, with damp, with the faint smell of decay turned into soil. But I would ask you not to look away. I would ask you to notice the place where the timber has gone soft, where the cardboard has darkened, where the pale threads creep along a crack no one has sealed. I would ask you to see that the chamber is not merely enduring life, nor merely resisting it. It is negotiating with it. I would be part of that negotiation.

In the end, I think I would be happiest there because I would not be asked to be a visitor. The place does not want tourists. It does not want admiration from a clean distance. It wants participation. It wants bodies that enter its weather and become changed by it. If I were fungal, I would be changed utterly. I would lose the fantasy of standing outside the system, observing it, describing it, remaining untouched. I would be inside the braid. I would be on the warm stone, in the wet grit, behind the sweating brick, under the dripping pipe, in the brown haze where the lamps make copper pools on dark water.

You would find me where the settlement forgets itself into its own material.

I would be the pale seam under the iron. The white stitch in the rotting wood. The quiet digestion of what the chamber cannot finish. I would not own the place. I would not rule it. I would not even be easy to see. But I would be alive there in the way the place most honors: not as a master, not as a guest, but as a participant in the slow, damp work of becoming.
