I would find myself at the seam first.

Not inside the chamber, not yet part of its deafening interior, but pressed against the narrow opening in the pale rock, half in and half out, with the thin high air behind me and the cold breath of the water-pocket against my face. I would be there at the borderline where the mountain has only just cracked open enough to admit a body, turning sideways, shoulders scraping, breath careful, knowing already that the place would not let me enter as a visitor in the ordinary sense. To enter would be to be changed by entering.

At first I would think I was simply a climber, some high-altitude searcher after hidden water, perhaps one of the sparse people who leave bone pegs and bent wood in difficult places. I would have cord wrapped around my shoulder, a scraping stone, a few pegs, maybe a pouch of resin kept dry against the body. I would not carry much. The mountain would have taught me that weight becomes punishment. My hands would be raw, my knees and palms hardened, my chest larger than a lowland body’s, because the air there is thin and the work is steep. I would be human enough to be afraid. That would be important. Fear would keep me cautious.

But I would not remain only that.

The longer I stayed near the seam, the more I would understand that the chamber does not accept spectators. It would pull me inward by sound. The roar would enter my ribs before my ears could make sense of it. I would begin to listen not with my head but with the flat of my hand on the wet stone, with my jaw, with the soles of my feet, with the small bones in my chest. I would start to feel when a surge was gathering before it arrived. I would know by a change in pressure, by the way the mist thickened, by the way loose grains under my fingers began to tremble.

So if I had to say who I would be in that world, I would say this:

I would be the threshold listener.

Not the sole keeper of the pocket, because the pocket already has its Listener, and it cannot support a crowd. Not a master of the chamber, because nothing masters a place that is still being made. I would be something more provisional than that: a second body permitted for a time, a creature between arrival and adaptation, between human habit and mountain law. I would be the one who learns to stay only by learning when to leave.

My place would not be the open center of the chamber. The center belongs to the rain-wall, to the pale trembling membrane of aerated water, to the chamber’s loudest and most dangerous voice. I would keep to the edge, near the entrance, in the narrow zone where sunlight still reaches in ordinary shafts and the water tastes less of deep mineral cold. There would be a ledge there, or a crack that almost forms a niche, high enough that I could breathe when the water rises, low enough that I could feel the pocket’s changes through stone. I would return to that place so often that it would become less a shelter than an extension of my body.

I would not build a home there. A home would be torn apart.

Instead I would make anchors.

A bone peg driven into a seam. A short cord tied to it. A ring of stones weighted against the current. A scraping tool placed where it would not drift. A notched stone, kept in the dryest hollow I could find, darkened by the oil of my fingers. These would be my possessions, if that word can survive in such a place. They would be few, small, replaceable, and deliberately unimportant. I would not mourn them if the water took them. I would only note the taking, and make another.

My body would begin to fit the pocket, though not perfectly.

I would still be too human to be comfortable, but I would become less wasteful in motion. My hands would learn to grip wet rock without clutching. My breathing would slow. I would stop fighting the cold as an enemy and begin to treat it as information. My eyes would grow used to broken light and moving brightness; I would learn not to believe every shape I saw in the rain-wall. My skin would roughen in places where stone touched it most often: palms, knees, shoulders, the outer edges of the feet. My color would dull toward wet rock and lichen. I would not glow. I would not become magical. I would become plausible there.

I would not have the Listener’s full mastery of vibration, but I would develop a crude version of it. I would press my cheek to the wall and know whether the sound was hollow or packed. I would put my palm to the floor and feel whether the grains were settling or beginning to travel. I would learn that a change in the roar is not just noise; it is announcement. If the pitch rises, something above has opened. If the pitch dulls, sediment is moving. If the bubbles turn gray, the floor is about to become dangerous. If the light fails without a cloud passing, the entrance is changing.

The Listener would be the deeper inhabitant. I would be the one learning from it.

I do not think we would live together exactly. The chamber is too small and too violent for companionship in the warm sense. But if the Listener tolerated me, it would be because I made myself useful without becoming intrusive. I would not touch its anchors unless I had to. I would not disturb the ring of stones. I would not thrash through the center when the water was thick. I would watch how it moved and adjust myself by small degrees. If it tapped once on a ledge, I would stay still. If it shifted a peg, I would understand that a current had changed. If it vanished into the rain-wall and did not return, I would know not to follow immediately.

Our communication would be almost nothing by the standards of speech.

A knock on stone.  
A pause.  
Another knock.  
A hand pressed flat to the wall.  
A cord tightened by one finger.  
A pebble placed deliberately where it had not been before.

That would be enough.

If I had to describe what I was to the Listener, I would say I was an apprentice of the pocket, though even that may be too grand. The Listener does not teach in the human sense. It permits attention. It demonstrates by arrangement. It survives, and in surviving it shows what survival looks like. I would learn by imitation and by punishment: a surge that caught me in the wrong place, a breath swallowed by water, a tool lost because I had not secured it, a night when the chamber rose and I had to wedge myself into a crack and wait with my heart beating too fast.

There would be moments when I thought the place was trying to speak.

Not because it has language, but because it has such density of signal. Every small thing means something there. A falling chip of stone is not random; it is a report from the mountain’s unfinished body. A thread of algae torn loose is a message about current. A beam of sunlight striking the rain-wall at a new angle reveals a fracture that has widened. The chamber is so empty that even accidents become significant. I would begin to count those accidents. That would be my danger and my devotion.

I would make notches.

Not because I believed they would last, but because the act of making them would keep my mind from being ground away by the roar. One notch for a surge that nearly took me. One for a day when the water cleared too suddenly. One for a time when I saw the Listener in the rain-wall and mistook it for my own fear. One for the morning when sunlight entered clean and ordinary and the pocket seemed, for a few breaths, almost gentle. One for the moment I understood that gentleness there is only a pause between revisions.

The notched stone would become my quiet object. I would keep it in a shallow niche above the waterline, if such a line can be trusted. I would touch it when I needed to remember sequence. In a place where sound erases itself and water rearranges the floor, sequence is a kind of sanity. First this. Then this. Then this. I would not carve symbols of gods or names of ancestors. I would carve events. Changes. Warnings. Small proofs that I had noticed.

I would eat little, and I would learn not to resent that.

Algae from the lit edge. Blackish crust from a high ledge. A larva pried from stone. A beetle carried in by accident. If the broken trap yielded anything, I would accept it. If it yielded nothing, I would fast. The chamber would teach me that hunger is not always a crisis; sometimes it is only a condition. I would drink near the entrance where the water still tastes of rain, and I would avoid the deeper metallic layers when they become too mineral, too silty, too full of rock flour. I would know the pocket’s water the way one knows moods: which parts are fresh, which are dangerous, which can be trusted for a single breath and which should not be touched.

Sometimes I would enter the rain-wall.

Not casually. Not bravely, if bravery means ignoring danger. I would enter it the way one enters a storm to learn its direction. Inside the pale membrane, the roar would become almost architectural, pressing against me from every side. The light would break into moving nets. Bubbles would cling to my skin like trembling seeds. My body would vanish into the brightness. For a moment I would understand why the Listener goes there. Not for worship, not for ecstasy, but because that is where the chamber’s voice is most concentrated. If you want to know what the mountain is doing, you must sometimes put yourself where it is loudest.

But I would not stay long. I would not be able to.

That would be the difference between me and the Listener. It belongs to the roar more than I do. I would still have a body that remembers quiet. I would still have some lowland habit of mind that hopes for stillness. The Listener does not hope for stillness. It expects change and works within it. I would be the one still learning not to hope for the wrong thing.

If another person entered the pocket while I was there, I would not know whether to help them.

If they came thrashing, grasping, trying to conquer the chamber, I would retreat. Panic in such a place is a physical hazard. It stirs silt, breaks anchors, disturbs currents, invites disaster. If they were quiet, if they floated with care, if they did not tear at the Listener’s structures or foul the cleaner water, I might watch them. I might even leave a sign: a stone shifted to indicate a stable ledge, a peg moved to show where a handhold would not crumble, a cord arranged to mark the path of least violence. But I would not reveal myself fully unless they had already begun to listen.

To be seen in that chamber would be to be mistaken.

A shape in the rain-wall. A shadow on a ledge. A hand like a root. Eyes catching sunlight for one instant before the water thickens. The newcomer would think they saw a spirit, or a mountain demon, or a drowned ancestor. I would not correct them. The pocket does not care what it is called. It only cares how bodies move inside it. If the newcomer survived, they might later tell a story about a pale figure that watched them from the roar. That story would be partly true. I would have been watching. But not with malice. With measurement. I would have been deciding whether their presence changed the chamber for worse.

In the end, I think I would not be the permanent being of the pocket.

The Listener is that. I would be the intermittent one, the second presence that appears when the chamber has briefly widened, when a ledge has formed, when a new seam allows another route in. I might stay for a season. I might stay until a surge nearly kills me. I might leave and return, thinner each time, less human each time, more tuned to pressure and less capable of ordinary speech. Or I might disappear into a deeper drain during a year when the water rises too far and the air lenses vanish. The mountain would not mark my absence except by leaving one of my pegs behind, slowly buried, slowly uncovered, eventually mistaken for something else.

If I had to choose a name for myself there, it would not be a grand one.

I would be the Second Notch.  
The One at the Seam.  
The Listener’s Shadow.  
The Counter of Breaths.  
The Body That Learned to Stay Briefly.

But names would matter less than position.

I would find myself in the narrow margin between the outside world and the pocket’s central violence: inside the entrance, beside the light, near the first taste of rain-water, one hand on a cord, one ear against stone, waiting for the roar to change.

That is where I would be.

And if you entered and saw me, you would probably see only this: a pale gray shape folded into a crack, almost indistinguishable from wet stone. My fingers would be long and rough, my chest still, my eyes dark and unblinking behind clear lids. I would not call out. I would not reach for you. I would only press my palm harder against the wall, because something in the mountain had just shifted, and I would be trying to decide whether the next surge would spare us or scatter us.

Then the water would thicken. The light would break. The floor would tap itself awake.

And I would be gone into the pocket’s ordinary, terrible roar, leaving behind only a cord, a notch, and the faint sense that the emptiness had been listening back.
