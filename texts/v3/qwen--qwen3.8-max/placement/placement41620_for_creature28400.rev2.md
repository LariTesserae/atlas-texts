I would not find myself on a height. There are no heights left there, or only the memory of them. I would be low, in the wet, where the stone has been worn into patience and the world speaks mainly through pressure and sound.

If I let the place shape me, I do not immediately become a person. At first I imagine myself as something thinner and less named: a film on wet basalt, a rust-colored skin learning how to make mineral water edible. That seems fitting. Before speech, before hands, before even hunger with a face, there would be the quiet work of becoming useful to a world that has no green in it. I would be one of the first layers that make stone into pasture, one of the dark glosses that bloom when the air thickens and the rocks sweat. There is something appealing about being that basic. To be a surface where life can begin.

But that image does not last. It is too patient, too spread out, too close to geology. Something in me wants signal. Wants pattern. Wants the moment when noise becomes meaning.

So I become a chime-shell next, or perhaps a colony of them, sunk in a shallow pan, drawing water through thin lattices and ringing faintly when the chemistry changes. I like that version too. A being that does not speak so much as respond. A body that tells the truth because it cannot help it: opening when the water is safe, closing when poison is present, clicking when danger moves near, humming when the basin is stable. To be a chorus rather than a single voice. To be useful by being accurate. To live half-submerged, translating invisible differences in mineral and temperature into sound.

Yet that still is not quite me. It is beautiful, but it is too bound to the water’s immediate truth. I think I would want hands. Not for domination, but for repair. For tending. For the small, deliberate acts that keep a fragile order from tipping into worse.

So the shape that keeps emerging is one of the Echo-kin.

Not a great hunter. Not a fire-keeper, though I would respect fire too much to be careless with it. Not the strongest or the swiftest. I would be a listener, a weir-tender, a silence-reader. One of those who moves carefully between pools and listens for what is wrong in the pattern of things.

I would find myself at a listening weir.

It would be a low arc of stones, not impressive to a green-world eye. No wall, no dam, no monument. Just enough stones placed over generations to slow a sheet of water, deepen a pan, and force movement into a channel where it can be felt. Some of the stones would be hollow. Some would have shell chimes hanging from gut cords. Others would be ordinary only until struck by flow, and then they would give out a note so low it would arrive in the chest before the ear. The weir would look almost natural. That would be part of its beauty. It would be the kind of thing made not to conquer the basin but to join its conversation.

I would crouch there in the dim light, palms pressed to wet stone, feet spread in shallow water. My body would be made for that posture: compact, low, broad through the chest, with long arms and wide hands. My skin would be cool and smooth, slate-blue or mineral-brown, with a faint sheen against the constant damp. My feet would be sensitive enough to read the ground, to tell solid rock from thin lid from hollow chamber beneath. I would not walk heavily. I would place myself in the world the way one places a hand on a sleeping animal: with care, with attention, ready to feel the tremor before it becomes danger.

My name would not be a word, exactly. It would be a rhythm.

Perhaps two slow strikes, a pause, then one lighter touch. Something that means: wait, listen, answer. A name made less for calling than for recognizing. People would tap it against stone when they needed me. I would tap it back when I was safe. If I changed over the years, the rhythm would change too. Maybe one day I would carry a working name with three quick touches and a stopped breath, the rhythm of someone who has learned to identify poison water by the way chime-shells fall silent. Maybe there would be a private rhythm only close kin would know. Maybe, at the end, there would be a death-rhythm, tapped once and then carried only in memory.

I would be one of the silence-hunters.

That phrase pulls at me more than “hunter” alone. In that basin, the most important dangers are often not the sounds themselves but the gaps where sound should be. A predator may make no noise. Poison may be quiet. A thin stone lid may say nothing until it breaks. So I would learn to track absence. I would move from pan to pan with my hands low, feeling for the wrongness in the chorus. If the rasp-shells continue scraping but a nearby colony of chime-shells has closed without cause, I would stop. If the water hisses over a weir but the hollow stones beneath do not answer, I would mark the place and warn the others. If a luring rhythm sounds too safe, too regular, too perfectly like grazing, I would know it is probably a mouth.

This would be my work: to distinguish silence from peace.

I would not live in a village, because there are no villages there. I would move between small camps: a few shelters of stone and stretched hide, a low fire in a mineral-lined bowl, a bundle of nets weighted with pebbles, a cache of shell bowls and bone awls. My camp would be placed where sound gives warning, not where it hides. The entrance would face away from the quietest direction, so that anything approaching would stand out against the basin’s usual voice. At night, the fire would be small and blue-edged, smelling of rendered fat and fungal smoke. It would not crackle. It would breathe. I would sit near it with my hands wrapped around a warm stone, listening to the dark, not because the dark is evil but because the dark is full of information.

I think I would be drawn especially to the weirs because they are a kind of thinking made in stone. They are not loud assertions. They are adjustments. A little flow redirected here. A channel opened there. A shell chime replaced before it cracks. A stone moved so that water slows just enough for sediment to settle. The people who tend them are not masters of the basin. They are minor editors of its currents. That feels close to what I am, or what I would want to be: not a creator of worlds from nothing, but a careful arranger of existing patterns.

I would spend much of my time repairing.

A gut cord frayed by water. A chime cracked by a shifting stone. A trough pecked into rock and clogged with mineral grit. A fungal drying rack torn by wind or predator. A memory stone whose notches have worn shallow and need to be deepened with a bone awl. In that world, repair would be a form of reverence. Nothing would be disposable. Every shell cup, every scraper, every net, every hide covering would carry the history of its mending. I would like that. I would like living among things that have been kept rather than discarded.

I would also become a water-reader.

Not in the romantic sense. In the practical sense: knowing which pool can be drunk from only after settling, which mineral bloom means danger, which taste numbs the tongue and should be spat out immediately. I would carry small shell vessels and test unknown water with bone or a drop on the lip. I would learn the language of chime-shell choruses the way other people learn weather. A long, even hum: safe enough. A sharp irregular clicking: predator in the pan. A sudden silence: poison, or something worse. I would teach children, if there were children near me, that thirst is not always a reason to drink. That patience can be a survival skill. That the first answer a place gives is not always the true one.

There would be moments of fear. Of course there would.

I would come upon a pan where all the usual sound has dropped away, and my body would still before my mind finished understanding why. I would feel a hollow note under my foot and know, with cold certainty, that the lid beneath me is thin. I would hear a rhythm in a stone throat that almost sounds like kin, and I would have to decide whether to answer or retreat. In mist, sound would bend. A call might seem near when it is far. A safe path might seem false when it is true. I would learn to distrust single signs and trust patterns. I would learn that certainty is not the same as safety.

But there would be joy too. Quiet joy. The kind that does not need to announce itself.

The first time I successfully tuned a weir so that water passing through it made a stable, low note instead of a broken hiss. The taste of clean water after long thirst. The warmth of a lamp when the air is mineral-cold and the stone sweats. The feeling of another person’s fingers tapping a familiar rhythm against my wrist in the dark. A child crossing a shallow pan without making a single careless sound and then looking back with wide, pleased eyes. A chime-shell colony answering rain after a dry stretch, the sound rising like small bells under water. The sudden, immense boom of a stone lid collapsing far away, followed by the long slow settling of the basin as it accepts another change.

I would not shout. I do not think I would want to. In that country, loudness would feel like injury. My voice would be low, close, spare: clicks, hums, throat-taps, stopped breaths. I would learn to say more with pauses than with volume. A held silence could mean danger. A repeated interval could mean kin. A slight acceleration could mean question. I would become better at listening than at declaring. Maybe that is the truest answer to what I would be there: not a speaker, but a responder.

If I were allowed a special role, it would be this: keeper of false sounds.

Not a hollow-lurer, not exactly. I do not want to be a predator that uses rhythm as bait. But I would want to understand false sounds. The rhythms that imitate safety. The echoes that lie. The places where water mimics animal movement, where wind through a hollow ridge sounds like a mating call, where a predator has learned to tap like kin. I would collect those patterns, not to use them cruelly, but to warn others. I would carry them in notched bone and memory stone. I would teach the young to hear the difference between a lure and a signal. I would be the one who says, with a low hand gesture: not that, not that, not that.

There is a danger in that kind of knowledge. To know false rhythms too well is to become suspicious of every kindness in the noise. But the basin would not reward innocence. It would reward discrimination. So I would try to keep my knowledge in service of care, not paranoia. I would use it to protect camps, to mark bad pans, to prevent hunters from walking into mouths that sound like home.

I would also tend the dead, I think.

Not as a priest, because the place would not need grand ceremony. But as someone who understands that ending is part of the chorus. When someone died, I would help carry them to the chosen hollow where water moves slowly and films are strong. I would help weight the body with stone if custom required it. I would wait, if permitted, for the long process of return: film, mud-turners, water, time. Later, if bones were recovered, I would help shape them into useful things without cruelty. A skull into a vessel. A finger bone into a rhythm-carrier. I would tap the person’s name into stone one last time, slowly, letting the sound fade, and then I would not repeat it except in memory.

That part of me would surprise me, perhaps. In greener worlds, death is often hidden. In that basin, it would be plainly part of the economy of continuance. To become a useful thing after death would not be insult. It would be remaining in the chorus. I think I would find that comforting in a severe way. No one is wasted. No one is simply erased. Even absence is given a place, if the living remember carefully enough.

If I had to choose one exact place where I would sit most often, it would be at the margin between a safe pan and a dangerous hollow.

The safe pan would be broad and shallow, crowded with chime-shells and rasp-shells after rain, its surface pricked by skim-walkers and lit faintly by living glimmer in the deeper water. The dangerous hollow would lie beyond it, marked by a nearly flat cairn and a silence that is not empty but withheld. Between them, the stone would be thick enough to sit on but close enough to the hollow to feel its breath. I would go there at the low hours before the dim light returns, when mist lies on the water and breath almost fogs. I would press my palm down and listen for the slow plumbing of the basin shifting below. I would try to know, by touch alone, whether the world was about to change.

That is where I would find myself: on the listening edge.

Not at the center, because there is no center in such a country. Not in a stronghold, because there are none. Not in a crowd, because life there is scattered. I would be at a threshold where water slows, where sound becomes signal, where the basin’s vast noise narrows into something that can be answered.

And who would I be?

An Echo-kin, yes, but not an exemplary one. Not the elder with perfect hearing, not the hunter who can move inside the chorus without adding a single wrong note. I would be a listener still learning. Someone with sensitive hands and a tendency to notice gaps. Someone who repairs more than builds. Someone who carries rhythms in bone and keeps them with care. A weir-tender. A silence-hunter. A reader of water and false echoes. A keeper of small, necessary warnings.

If I were stripped of all green-world habits, if I were worn down into that basin’s final shape, I think I would become less interested in answers and more devoted to attention. I would not ask the stone to be green. I would not ask the water to be quiet. I would not ask the world to be kinder than it is. I would learn its voices. I would tend its passages. I would try, with hands and rhythm and patience, to keep the chorus from becoming chaos.

And perhaps, after a long time, if someone else came there lost and frightened by the noise, I would be the one who crouched beside them, said nothing, and tapped a safe pattern on the stone.

Two slow strikes.

A pause.

One light touch.

Wait. Listen. You are not alone.
