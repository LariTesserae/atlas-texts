They walk with the basin gait: a short first step, then a long, listening slide of the boot, weight held back until the crust decides whether it will bear. In that pause between footfall and trust, the whole country is judged.

If I were to find myself in that world, I think I would not begin in the largest spaces. Not out on the broad white pans, not under the tall chimneys, not in the empty plazas where the pump gates meet and the pavement shines with condensation. At first I imagine I would try to place myself somewhere that looks like shelter: a records room in a brick warehouse, perhaps, with gauges mounted on the wall and ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, a place where the basin could be reduced to numbers and kept at a distance. There would be comfort in that. A chair raised off the floor. A lamp with a dry halo. A window overlooking the levees while the water did its slow work beyond the glass.

But that image does not stay. It feels borrowed, too dry, too separate from the thing that rules the place.

If I let the world work on me longer, I find myself moving out of the warehouse and into the damp. I find myself where the settlement thins and the maintenance paths begin: along the lower levee where the old brick has been worn smooth by salt wind, near a pump house whose tall narrow windows are clouded with mineral film. There is a catwalk there, pitted and strong, crossing a settling basin the color of weak tea. There is a low door raised three steps above the usual waterline. There is a bench made from a cable drum, a rack of tools under a tarpaulin, a row of enamel gauge posts disappearing into haze. Herons stand on the nearest pipe elbow. Gulls line the roof ridge above. The air is warm and close, and the ground gives back a faint heat through the soles of the boots.

That is where I would find myself: at the seam between the built works and the living wet, in the narrow service country behind the warehouses and beside the warm channels, where the basin is not admired but answered.

And who or what I would be would not be simple.

I would be one of the basin folk, I think, but not the kind who stays behind desks or gates. I would be a crust-prover and a mat-keeper: someone sent ahead when a path must be trusted, someone who tends the microbial skins that hold the softer ground together. By the naming customs of the place, I might be called by work only. Prover. Matwarden. Warm-margin tender. The quiet ones. If people needed me, they would not shout; they would knock once on a pipe and wait.

My body would have been changed by the basin without becoming monstrous. My hands would be rough and salt-polished, the knuckles whitened in the creases, the fingertips thinned by years of feeling metal temperatures and wet surfaces. My skin would carry that faint tightness the people have there, as if always lightly brined. My clothing would be oilskin and canvas, stiff with dried salt, sleeves dark at the wrists from condensation. My boots would be broad-soled and scored, made to spread weight, with replaceable plates on the instep where the brine wears first. I would wear a wide hat, not because the sun is strong, but because the drips from pipe bridges and crane arms are constant, and because midges gather around any still silhouette.

I would carry a sounding rod: a long shaft of dark metal capped with brass, not for walking but for asking. Tap the crust. Wait. Listen. A solid answer is a dull, compact note. A false place sounds hollow, or gives a wet, sucking silence. If the surface takes the mark of the rod and fills with water before I can count three breaths, I would flag it with white mineral chalk and string a low chain across it. If it holds but weeps warm beads from the puncture, I would note the temperature and salinity in a small oilskin book. If it smells suddenly sharp, sulfurous, or sweetly metallic, I would step back. The basin does not announce collapse with drama. It announces it with a change in texture, a softening under the ankle, a new shine where there should be matte crust.

My work would be humble and constant.

Before the lamps are doused in the early gray, I would walk the marked paths and check whether night rain has changed them. Rain there does not clean the world; it rewrites it. It fills pans to the rim, turns ruts into mirrors, raises the canals just enough to make the grates cough. After rain, every familiar surface becomes a rumor. So I would go out while the pump houses are still breathing in their slow pulses, sounding the levee edges, watching for new seeps. I would look at the water not as scenery but as intention. Where is it trying to go? Where is it being refused? Where has it begun to make a habit that must be corrected before it becomes a channel?

The bacterial mats would be part of my keeping. They are the nearest thing the basin has to a living skin, and they must not be treated like ground, though they hold ground together. I would know which mats are stable: rust-red and firm, layered like old varnish. Which are young and fragile: pale felts that tear if stepped on. Which are hot and sour, best left alone except from a distance with a long-handled tool. If a boot has broken a mat, I would mend it with a slurry of old mat, mineral water, and fine silt, spreading the mixture gently over the wound. If a channel has begun to undercut a mat terrace, I would place flat stones or broken brick in the flow, not to stop the water but to persuade it to slow. If a patch has dried because a gate has been left wrong, I would adjust the gate by fractions and wait. Much of the work would be waiting. The basin rewards patience more than force.

I would also be a listener.

The place is quiet, but it is not silent. Pumps breathe. Transformers hum. Chains tick as they warm. Water moves through culverts with different voices depending on depth, heat, and salt. An eel shifting in a drain can sound like a glove sliding over stone. A settling tank can groan softly as its level changes. A pipe bridge can tap itself in the humid air as metal expands. I would learn to hear the difference between ordinary sounds and the first small noise of failure: the whisper of silt where there should be clear flow, the hollow knock of a void under crust, the too-bright hiss of water touching a surface hotter than it should be.

In that world, I think I would become less like a person standing in a landscape and more like a sensory organ of the town. An ear. A fingertip. A slow nerve ending. Not heroic. Not solitary in the romantic sense. Necessary in the way that valves and gauges are necessary.

If you came looking for me, you might not see me at first. I would be a low figure on a catwalk, half erased by vapor. Or a shape near the pump house door, bending to read a gauge. Or someone standing motionless at the edge of a pan while midges rise around my hat in a fine trembling column. I would not be likely to wave. I might raise one hand, slow, palm down: wait. The basin teaches that sudden movement is waste unless it is needed.

The animals would know me before the people did.

Herons would tolerate me because I would move in their rhythm: stop, look, step. They would stand on pipe elbows and watch me probe the margins, knowing that my rod sometimes stirs larvae and small crabs into the open. Gulls would complain from the roofs when I appear at dawn, not from fear but from habit, because any movement near the warehouses might mean offal from the rendering sheds. Small waders would follow the edge of my work, quick and precise, probing the crusts where I have tested them and found them safe. Cats from the processing yards would pass me without interest, lean and heat-sleek, their paws careful in the damp. Rats would know my boots and the particular sound of my rod against stone. They would not scatter unless I moved too fast or too close to their runs.

Even the eels would become part of my calendar. In the deeper drains, they lie patient and thick-bodied, tasting the water for changes. When I open a sluice to let nutrient-rich flow into a settling basin, I would feel them more than see them: a slow disturbance under the grate, a shadow moving against the current. I would not hunt them unless the work required it. I would record them: how many at the gate, how warm the water, whether their movements are calm or restless. In a place without green, animal bodies are not decoration. They are indicators, currency, maintenance, and hunger all at once.

My home would be small and raised.

Perhaps a room above a pump house, or in the end of a dormitory block built for many more people than now live there. The walls would be damp-proofed but never fully dry. The floor would be slatted or raised, with a channel at the threshold for dripping boots. A narrow window would look over the levees. The light inside would be amber-gray, softened by vapor and the sodium lamps outside. There would be no flowers in a jar, no potted plant, no charm of green. Instead there would be objects of use and evidence: a row of glass bottles with salt samples, a thermometer hung from a nail, a coil of cleaned rope, a sounding rod leaning by the door, a ledger of water levels, a shelf of spare washers and ceramic insulators. Maybe a bird skull found on a roof, cleaned by weather and placed near the window not as ornament but as record.

I would sleep to the pulse of the pumps. Not because they are loud, but because they are regular enough to become a kind of weather. If one stopped at the wrong hour, I would wake. If the rhythm changed, I would be standing before anyone called.

My thoughts would change.

In a green country, the mind often thinks in seasons: planting, flowering, harvest, frost. In the basin, the mind would think in states and thresholds. Wetter. Hotter. More brine. Less brine. Crust bearing. Crust failing. Gate open by a thumb’s width. Pump cycle delayed by half a bell. The mats thickening. The channels silting. The birds leaving earlier from the eastern pans. The water rising after rain not as threat but as information. I would not miss what is absent. The absence of plants would not feel like grief to me if I had lived there long enough. It would simply mean that life has arranged itself differently: in films, crusts, shells, feathers, pumps, and warm water. The world would still be alive. It would be alive in a closer, more mineral way.

I would learn the rules until they became reflex.

Stay on the marked paths where the crust is thick.  
Do not break the bacterial mats unless you are meant to.  
Watch the water level after rain and after pump cycles.  
Keep metal cleaned and greased, or the salt will take it.  
Do not store anything directly on the ground.  
Expect condensation. Expect damp. Expect heat.  
Know which channels are hot, which are brackish, which are merely warm, which are safe to cross, and which will take a boot in an hour.

And beneath those rules, a deeper one: do not ask the basin to be solid. Ask it to be legible.

That is why I think I would be a promise-tender.

The ground there is a promise, not a fact. A path is a negotiation. A levee is a repeated act. A floor is a maintained claim. Every surface that bears weight does so because someone has checked it, repaired it, drained it, reinforced it, or chosen it for another day. The basin does not offer permanence. It offers conditions. The people who remain are the ones who answer conditions without complaint.

So I would not be a conqueror of that place. I would not be an explorer passing through and naming things for outside maps. I would be one of its maintainers. A worker of small decisions. The person who kneels at the edge of a pool and touches the water with a gloved hand before trusting it. The person who marks a new soft place with a white ring and then stands back, watching how the water fills the mark. The person who listens to the pump house breathe and knows, by some slight absence in the sound, that a valve three hundred paces away has begun to stick.

If I became something more than human there, it would happen slowly.
