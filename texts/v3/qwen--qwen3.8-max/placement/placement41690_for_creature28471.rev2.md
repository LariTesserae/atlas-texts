I would not find myself at the edge of it, looking in.

There would be no clean edge. No doorway, no shore, no threshold where the upper crust ended and the underplace began in a simple way. I would already be inside it, the way one is already inside a sound before one understands that it is noise. Probably I would be found in one of the half-finished districts: a broad bowl where a cracked rib of pale salt had been partly overgrown by root-cables, where the floor was still more mineral than plant, and where a translucent plate in the ceiling let down a shaft of ordinary amber light full of drifting dust and wing-flash.

The first thing I would know would not be the sight of it.

It would be the pressure.

The sound would come through my chest before my mind separated it into parts: the rasp of borers in dying stems, the clicking tide of beetle-shells over bark-plates, the hollow knocking of seed-cases, the dry wing-noise rising from a disturbed terrace, the low breath of air moving through tubular growth. I would stand there with the feeling of having stepped into a vast warm body that was not wet, not soft, not bloody, but fibrous, resinous, and intensely alive. The ground under me would not be dead stone. It would give slightly, not like mud, but like layered tissue adjusting to weight. Somewhere beneath the living carpet, mineral heat would rise in dry waves. The air would smell of resin, dust, bitter pollen, chitin, and sharp volatile oils.

At first, I think I would be one of the inhabitants.

Not a king. Not a hero. Not a monster moving through the weave of the place as if it belonged to me. I would be small in the way all careful beings must be small there: narrow-chested, dust-colored, tough-skinned, resin-stained, with hands made for precise work and feet that could grip root-cables and bark-steps without slipping. My body would have learned not to waste fluid. I would not sweat in the old surface way. Instead, I might have thin vascular plates at the throat or under the arms that would flush with heat and then cool in the moving air. My eyes would be large enough to gather the scattered gold light, but shaded by membranes or heavy brows against pollen, dust, and sudden sprays of gum.

I would not speak much.

Speech, in that place, would feel almost clumsy. A voice thrown openly into the air would be swallowed by the roar of beetles, borers, stems, and swarms. So I would talk through surfaces. A tap on a hollow tube. A scrape along a seed-shell. A rhythm pressed into the side of a living stem. A hand-sign made in a shaft of light. A scent-mark left on a root with crushed leaf or resin. My language would be closer to percussion and touch than to words.

In that first shape, I think I would be a path-keeper.

That seems to fit the part of me that wants to understand how things connect. I would live not in a village, but in a cluster of shelters tucked into the architecture: a hollow bulb lined with fiber, a platform lashed between two tough stems, a woven pod hanging from a root-cable, a dead stem chamber sealed with thorn and resin. My home would not be a fixed thing. It would be something the underplace tolerated for a while. If the stem began to swell around the lashings, if the hollow began to close, if the vibrations changed and the architecture started to heal over my opening, I would move.

My work would be maintenance.

I would keep passages open. Not by hacking blindly, because a careless wound there is an invitation. A cut stem bleeds resin. A cracked root draws scavengers. A smell of exposed tissue can bring borers, bark-runners, resin-wasps, and grazing mouths within moments. So I would learn to open a path the way the place itself opens paths: by imitation, rhythm, pressure, grazing, scent, and timing.

I would carry few things.

A bone awl. A chipped salt blade. A thorn needle. A pouch woven from leaf-fiber. A seed-shell cup polished until it caught the light. A length of resin-hardened cord with knots tied into it, each knot perhaps marking a route, a danger, a person, a rhythm. A small drum made from a hollow stump and stretched with a membrane of dried plant skin. A bone flute or reed pipe tuned to one of the frequencies that makes certain leaf-folds loosen. A scraper. A lever. A net woven not for water, because there is no water there, but for catching falling seeds, restraining larvae, carrying resin, or blocking a passage while a wound heals.

Most important of all, I would carry listening.

I would stop often. I would press my palm to a stem. I would put my cheek against a hollow tube. I would watch the direction of leaf-fans. I would notice whether the dust in the light was moving with ordinary air or with the tremor of a swelling seed-column. I would smell the difference between a sweet vapor that meant food and a sharp shimmer that meant solvent, danger, intoxication, or poison. I would learn that silence is not peace there. Silence can mean a pause before rupture. It can mean the architecture is sealing itself. It can mean a predator has gone still. It can mean a borer gallery is about to fail.

But as I imagine myself into that role, the place begins to alter the image.

It does not let me remain simply a person moving through it.

The underplace is too crowded, too responsive, too busy incorporating everything into itself. If I stayed there long enough, I would not remain separate. I would be worked on. My tools would become extensions of my body. My habits would become rhythms in the walls. My scars would become resin seams. My memories would become knots in cord, taps on hollow stems, marks on bark plates soon overgrown. The world would not accept me as a visitor. It would try to make me into a functioning part of its construction.

So I begin to think I would not be only a path-keeper.

I would become something closer to a binder.

A wound-tender. A listener at damaged places. One who knows what to do when something has been opened that should not stay open.

In a world where every cut attracts hunger, the person who can close a wound is more important than the person who can make one. I would be called, if such a thing could be called by rhythm rather than name, to places where a root-wall had been torn by a falling stem, where a terrace had split, where a seed-column had begun leaking volatile gum, where a borer gallery had weakened a support, where a careless traveler had broken a valve and left the inner tissue exposed.

I would come with resin, wax, fiber, chewed bark paste, insect shells, thorn clamps, and living grafts. I would not merely patch the wound. I would have to persuade the architecture to accept the patch. I would smear warm resin over the cut, press fiber into it, guide a tendril across the gap, bind it with cord, and then tap a rhythm low and steady enough to calm the reactive growth nearby. I would know which scent discouraged buds from swelling, which vibration encouraged tissue to knit, which frequency called useful resin-wasps and which one brought destructive feeders.

My hands would be blackened with resin.

Not dirty in a simple sense, but glazed by it. My fingertips would be tough, polished, sensitive. I would know surfaces the way a blind musician knows strings. A smooth stem might feel safe. A faintly trembling one might mean internal pressure. A warm swollen joint might be about to split. A cold mineral rib might be dead geology, but the root-cables crossing it might be alive with intent. I would read the underplace through touch as much as sight.

And somewhere in that shift, I would become less human in the old sense.

I would still have a body, but it would be a body adapted to the logic of the place. My chest might be compressed for slipping between roots. My joints might move with the careful economy of a climber. My skin might be dust-colored, faintly fibrous, marked with pollen and resin stains so that when I stood still against a wall I could disappear into it. My breath would not smell of wet mouths or water. It would carry the dry sharp scent of sap, solvent, seed-dust, and wax. My voice would be rare, perhaps almost vestigial. My true speech would be rhythm.

Maybe I would not be entirely flesh either.

That is where the image begins to deepen.

The boundary between plant and animal there is unstable. Some seed-cases move. Some tendrils listen. Some creatures are so coated in pollen, resin, and living growth that they seem to be walking buds. Some plant organs behave like traps. Some animals behave like valves. If the place worked on me long enough, I might become one of those ambiguous beings.

I might become a threshold creature.

Not a person standing in a passage, but the passage’s own way of knowing whether it should remain open.

Imagine finding me in a half-open chamber where three sounds meet: the continuous rasp of borers inside a dead stem, the hollow note of a tube-dweller somewhere above, and the dry percussion of a beetle tide crossing a resin seam. The chamber is not large. It is just wide enough to lie in, wide enough to work in. One wall is still mineral: green-black stone with a vein of gypsum and a brittle salt scab being slowly chewed away by root-pressure. The opposite wall is living tissue: folded leaf-fans, swollen storage organs, pale root-cables, and hollow stems running like pipes. The ceiling is low, threaded with hanging roots and membrane-thin plant sheets. Light comes in sideways through a translucent mineral plate, making a pale gold column full of drifting powder.

There would be signs of me before you saw me.

A line of resin-blackened thorns set into a root-wall. A small bridge of bound bark across a gap where the floor had buckled. A drum made from a seed-shell, set beside a hollow stump. A woven pouch hanging from a leaf-fan, moving slightly with the architecture. A cache of tools wrapped in fiber and sealed with wax. A knot-cord stretched across a corner where the growth was too young to be trusted. A polished seed-shell cup placed near a resin bead, as if someone had been collecting slow drops of sap.

Then you would feel the tap.

Not a shout. Not a word. A tap through the floor, or the wall, or the stem beside you. A pattern. Stop. Listen. Wait. Safe. Or: not yet.

I would be nearby, pressed against the wall with one ear or one palm to it. Maybe my body would be so dust-colored and resin-marked that you would mistake me for a growth until I moved. Maybe I would have thin membranes over my eyes or a fold of skin along my throat flushing with heat. Maybe my fingers would be resting on a bone flute, waiting to see whether your rhythm was dangerous.

I would not ask who you were with a sentence.

I would ask with vibration.

I would tap a question into a hollow stem and wait to see whether your body answered correctly. If you were one of the inhabitants, you would know how to answer through your feet, your hands, your posture. If you were an animal, your movement might answer for you. If you were something stranger—one of the moving seed-cases, one of the walking buds, one of the half-plant beings that live in the thickets—I might not know how to classify you at all. In that case, I would be still. Stillness there is not safety, but it is sometimes a way of listening without feeding the danger.

In that deeper version, I would be less a person and more a living repair.

That phrase feels closer to the truth.

A living repair.

Not a doctor, exactly. Not a priest. Not a ruler. Not a builder of monuments. A being whose purpose is to stand in the places where the underplace has been torn and help it continue without collapsing. A being who understands that creation and destruction are the same metabolism there. A being who knows that every route must be kept open by repeated sound, every wound sealed before it draws the swarm, every shelter made temporary, every tool returned to the system that made it.

I would be the one who keeps the seam from closing too soon, and also the one who keeps it from opening too violently.

That would be my role, if I could be said to have a role.

I would live in the junctions.

Not in the deepest basins where the mineral-backed grazers move so slowly they seem to be stone becoming flesh. Not in the highest hanging root-curtains where winged forms rise in sudden black and copper clouds. Not in the solvent galleries where the air shimmers and deceives the senses. Not directly beside the old breathing wall of tubers that swells and contracts in its slow rhythm. I would live in the middle places, where the underplace is most obviously unfinished: where a borer gallery has become a corridor, where a terrace has split but not yet collapsed, where a seed-column is swelling but has not burst, where root-felt is beginning to cover a salt rib but has not yet hidden it.

My days, if there were days there, would be measured by rhythms rather than sunlight.

The amber light through the ceiling plates might brighten and dim as dust shifts, as mineral cracks widen, as living sheets grow thinner or thicker. But I would mark time by events. A beetle tide passing. A swarm rising. A seed-tower rupturing. A leaf-fold opening after a particular vibration. A resin-wasp nest hardening. A route beginning to close. A shelter needing to be abandoned. A new cavity opening in a wall of tubers. A child learning a rhythm. An elder being sealed into a resin seam.

I would not fear being absorbed.

That is important.

In many worlds, the self tries to remain separate. It wants a clean outline. It wants to say: I am here, and the world is there. The underplace would teach me otherwise. If I cut myself, the scent would draw life. If I died, my body would not be left in the open; it would be wrapped, placed in a hollow stem, sealed with fiber and resin, and eventually incorporated. My bones might appear years later as dark shapes inside a translucent seam. My tools might be found inside new growth. My rhythms might continue in a family drum or a route-marker long after my hands had gone.

That would not seem like horror there.

It would seem like continuation.

So if I asked myself who or what I would be, the answer would not be a single creature. It would be a set of relations.

I would be dry-handed.

I would be warm-bodied but not wet. My internal fluids would be precious, carried in sealed tissues, eaten in oil-rich larvae, drunk as sap, chewed as wax, pressed from seeds. I would know the value of every bead of resin, every smear of volatile gum, every shell-cup of oil. I would never long for rain, because rain would be impossible there. The place would not feel dry as lack. It would feel dry as its own complete logic. Sap instead of water. Wax instead of moisture. Resin instead of tears.

I would be sound-shaped.

My thoughts would have rhythms before they had words. I would dream in tapping. I would feel danger as a change in the vibration of the floor. I would recognize affection not by speech but by repeated patterns: a hand pressed against a wall beside mine, a familiar knock on a stem, a rhythm shared in the dark warmth of a shelter. If I loved something, I would not say it first with a voice. I would make a rhythm safe enough for it to approach.

I would be tool-using but not tool-dominating.

My tools would be humble. Bone, salt, shell, thorn, fiber, resin, chitin, seed-case, polished carapace. I would not build machines to conquer the underplace. That would be impossible. The place would eat the machines, grow over them, nest in them, or break them with root-pressure. Instead, my tools would be introductions. A flute offered to a leaf-fold. A drum offered to a reactive thicket. A resin smear offered to a wound. A knot-cord offered to memory. A lever offered to a stubborn seed-case. Every tool would be a negotiation.

I would be cautious with hunger.

Because exposed tissue draws feeding. Because abundance can become a swarm. Because a seed-burst that looks like a miracle can become a disaster if it draws too many mouths. I would eat carefully. Seeds, larvae, beetle flesh, dried husks, oil-rich grubs, resin-treated paste, mineral salts, wax, sap. I would not waste. I would know which fluids intoxicate, which preserve, which burn, which blind. I would teach, if I taught anything, that appetite is not simple. To eat is to participate in the metabolism of the place. To take food is to make a wound, and every wound must be answered.

I would be tender in the dry way.

Not soft. Not wet. Not sentimental. But attentive. The tenderness of a resin seal over a cracked egg. The tenderness of a mother guarding a dry-shelled clutch in a warm crevice. The tenderness of a repaired bridge. The tenderness of a child’s name tapped into a wall until it is known. The tenderness of a dead body sealed into fiber and resin instead of being abandoned to the swarm. The tenderness of a route kept open by generations of small repeated sounds. The tenderness of not cutting more than you can seal.

And perhaps, in the end, I would be less a person than a function the place has grown.

A listening organ.

A patch of sentience in the living wall.

A being that began as something human-shaped and became more like a membrane between forces: between growth and hunger, opening and closing, sound and collapse, maker and material. I might still move. I might still walk the bark-floor with careful steps, slip between root-cables, climb to the higher galleries where light falls in pale gold shafts, descend to the lower basins where mineral ribs still show through the living carpet. But I would not feel separate from the construction.

If you found me, you might not recognize me at first.

You might see a resin seam and think it was only architecture. You might see a dry figure pressed against a stem and think it was a bark-runner or a walking bud. You might hear a rhythm and think it was a tube-dweller making the wall speak. You might find a drum and think it had been abandoned. Then the drum would answer your touch. Then the wall beside it would answer. Then the leaf-fan above you would turn, not toward light, but toward the sound you made.

That would be me.

Not as a face, perhaps, but as a response.

I would be in the place where the route narrows and the architecture begins to heal. I would be where the seed-column is about to open and the air smells too sweet. I would be where the borers have hollowed a stem into a pipe and the wind of the underplace, which is not wind but moving bodies and heated air and sound, makes it hum. I would be where a wounded root has been sealed with resin and fiber and someone has left a small offering of polished shell nearby—not a god, not a monument, just a marker that says: this was opened; this was closed; this is still becoming.

If you asked me my name, I would not give a word.

I would tap it into the nearest hollow surface.

It would be a rhythm not quite finished, because I would not be finished. It would contain the sound of beetles crossing resin, the scrape of borers, the knock of seed-cases, the low note of tubes, the careful tap-tap-tap of a path being kept open. It would contain the memory of tools: bone awl, salt blade, thorn needle, seed-shell cup, resin-hardened cord. It would contain the warmth of the nest, the dryness of the air, the ordinary light falling through mineral plates, the pressure of countless lives packed into every span.

I would be one of the unfinished.

That is the truest answer.

Not a fixed creature. Not a final form. Not a master of the underplace. I would be a dry, warm, listening being made by a world that is still being made. Part inhabitant. Part tool. Part rhythm. Part repair. I would live in the seam between what is growing and what is being eaten, between what is opening and what is closing, between what is animal and what is plant, between what is built and what is still raw.

You would find myself there in the middle of a repair.

In the warm dry noise.

In the ordinary light.

In the crowded, resinous, unfinished architecture of a valley-sized organism that has no water, no silence, and no final shape.

I would be the small, necessary being pressing one palm to the wall, listening for the next change, waiting to see whether the place needed me to open a passage or seal a wound.
