At first I think I would be one of the water-readers.

That is the image that comes most quickly: a quiet figure crouched beside a clay-lined channel, fingers trailing in the cold current, watching how bits of grit and torn sedge move across the surface. Someone who can tell by the shape of a ripple whether a gradient has shifted, whether a new seam has opened in the rock, whether the basin is accepting a direction or quietly refusing it. Someone whose hands are always wet, whose sleeves are darkened to the elbow, whose eyes are used to reading not words but the minute behavior of water over stone.

But as I stay with the place, that image becomes too narrow. A water-reader is necessary, yes, but there is something else in the basin that calls to me more strongly: the hollow after a sound has been made. The pause in which the place decides whether to answer. The space between the hammer-blow and the echo.

So, more accurately, I think I would be an echo-tender.

Not one of the great builders, not one of the strong-handed masons who set the first heavy courses, not one of those who haul ropes or drive wedges or call the lifting-rhythms across the scaffolds. I would be nearer to the margins than that, though not unimportant. In a city being built as much by water and resonance as by muscle, someone must attend to the acoustic condition of the place. Someone must know when a space is still open enough to speak properly. Someone must notice when a channel has gone quiet, when a chamber has become choked with sediment, when a wall has been placed in such a way that it swallows a needed echo instead of returning it.

I would be the one who listens for the basin’s opinions.

Where would you find me? Not in the center of the plaza, because the center is not yet a place of gathering. Not beneath a roof, because there are no roofs to speak of except the low shelters at the terrace edges. You would find me on the second or third terrace, in one of the roofless rooms that have been measured but not completed. The room would be bounded by low walls, its floor pale stone still beaded with damp. A thin channel would run along one side, carrying overflow from a cistern above. Moss would have begun to seal the joint where wall meets floor. In one corner, perhaps, a wooden template would still stand, half mineralized by dripping water, its grain darkening into something between carpentry and geology.

That corner would be mine.

Not as a house, exactly. More as a listening post. A place where the sound of falling water arrives from three directions at once: a drip from an upper lip, a murmur from the channel, and a deeper, slower resonance from somewhere below where the ground has been persuaded into making a throat. I would keep very little there. A flat stone polished by use. A small wooden maul. A coil of cord. A pouch of dry moss for kindling or for cushioning. A few smooth pebbles, some pale, some dark, used as markers. A shallow clay bowl that catches a particular drip because its rhythm helps me remember the day.

If I had a body in that world, it would have become suited to the basin. Not dramatic, not monstrous, but adapted by habit and weather. Cool-skinned. Sure-footed. My feet would be broad and sensitive, accustomed to wet stone and to the faint suction of damp ground. My clothes would be practical: oiled wool close to the body, a heavy cloak with a darkened hem, leather hardened by water, cords softened by constant handling. My hands would be rough but precise, the knuckles chapped, the fingertips pale and wrinkled from moisture. My breath would whiten in the hollows of the morning. I would smell of wet wool, mineral dust, crushed sedge, and that faint green bitterness that rises when moss is pressed between fingers.

I might not be entirely human, though I could pass as one. The basin seems a place where categories are still soft. If I were something stranger, I would be small and low-moving, perhaps amphibious in some sense: a being with skin that can read water temperature and stone dampness more finely than human skin can. A being whose eyes are suited to diffused light and whose ears are tuned not to speech but to resonance. I would not need to be large. The place is not made for large creatures yet. It is made for insects, algae, mosses, cautious amphibians, and a few patient workers. I would belong among the preliminary population, the almost-no-one.

But if I keep the image human, then I would be one of the few workers whose labor is less visible than most. I would be called, perhaps, by a functional name rather than a personal one. The Listener. The One Who Waits After. The Tender of Empty Rooms. Something plain. Nothing grand. The people there do not seem to love ornament. They use words the way they use stone: where needed, with care.

My work would begin before the other work begins.

In the early light, when clouds move quickly and wet surfaces shine like broken sky, I would walk the terraces while the basin is still settling into its day. I would listen first for what is wrong. Not for noise, because noise is everywhere: drip, run, fall, splash, murmur, boom. I would listen for the wrongness inside the noise. A missing note. A muffled place. A section of channel that no longer sings at the pitch it had yesterday. A pool too still. A wall too quiet. A stone that has gone dead.

I would carry a small striking tool and touch selected blocks as I pass. Not hard enough to damage them. Just enough to ask. A clean ring means the stone is content in its position. A dull thud may mean a crack, or water inside where water should not be, or a bedding plane beginning to fail. A hollow answer may mean there is space behind, and space is not always bad; sometimes space is what makes resonance possible. I would have to know the difference between empty-as-capacity and empty-as-failure. That would be one of the chief skills of my life.

When the other workers arrive, I would not move exactly among them. I would move around their work. If a stone-listener tests a new block, I would stand a little distance away, because the echo is truer from afar. If a water-reader watches a channel, I would listen to whether the channel’s voice has changed as it crosses a new threshold. If a crane groans and a block rises slowly into place, I would not watch the lifting so much as I would listen to the basin’s response: the creak of rope, the grunt of timber, the set of stone against stone, and then the way that final thud travels outward, finds a wall, finds a terrace, returns.

Sometimes my answer would be a gesture. A raised hand. A step backward. The placing of a pale pebble on a flat stone to mark that a sound was false. Sometimes I would call out one of the short phrases the workers use, not to command but to test. My voice would be loud, clear, and brief. It would strike across the open space and come back altered. I would listen to the alteration. That is where my knowledge would live: in the difference between the call I sent and the call the basin returned.

The workers would trust me, but not because I am mysterious. They would trust me because my work is procedural. I would not claim to hear spirits in the stone. I would not pretend the water speaks in words. What I would say is simpler and stranger: the basin has conditions, and those conditions announce themselves. Water leans where it wants to go. Stone rings where it is sound. Echoes change when a space is blocked. Silence can mean danger. My task would be to observe those signs and keep them legible.

I would be especially responsible for the empty places.

This is the part of my being that feels most inevitable as I imagine it. The city is not being filled as quickly as it could be. Rooms remain open. Plazas remain bare. Streets are wider than need requires. Someone must protect that restraint. Someone must prevent the immediate pressure of use from closing every hollow. In a settlement so few, it would be tempting to store tools in a measured room, to roof a wall before its time, to block a passage with cut stone, to fill a plaza with temporary works. But the basin requires vacancy. Its sound needs room to move. Its water needs room to be heard. Its plan needs room to remain visible.

I would be the keeper of that not-yet.

I would walk through unroofed rooms and remove debris that has no business there. I would keep sightlines open. I would ensure that a street alignment is not obscured by stacked timber or discarded chips of stone. I would mark places where water has begun to trace a future wall in dark lines after rain, and I would make sure no one builds there prematurely. I would know which emptinesses are active and which are dormant. Some spaces are empty because they are waiting for a roof. Some are empty because they are waiting for a crowd. Some are empty because they are waiting for a sound that has not yet been invented. I would not fill them. I would tend them.

That would be a strange kind of labor: maintaining absence.

It would require patience beyond ordinary patience. I would have to be willing to be underestimated. Others would spend the day lifting, cutting, fitting, sealing. My most important acts might be as small as moving a pile of rope from a marked line, or stopping a worker from setting a pin in a place where echo tests have shown a weakness, or sitting still for a long time beside a resonant chamber until I understood why its voice had changed. I would not produce much that looked like achievement. I would not point to a wall and say, I made that. My work would be heard more than seen.

But the place would fail without it.

Water is first in the basin, and sound is how water’s decisions become audible. If the channels are blocked, the water loses its way. If the resonant spaces are cluttered, the basin loses its voice. If the emptiness is consumed too quickly, the city becomes merely a settlement instead of what it is trying to become: an instrument for living with water and stone. I would be one of the people who keep it an instrument.

In that sense, I suppose I would be a kind of musician, though my instrument would be the basin itself.

I would not play it. I would tune it.

There would be moments when my role brings me close to the green-tenders, those who encourage moss and press plants into cracks and protect the first living skin over the rock. I would need to know where growth helps and where it hinders. Moss can seal a joint, but it can also soften a sounding face. Algae can weatherproof timber, but it can also make a walking surface treacherous. Roots can stitch a margin together, but they can also pry apart a channel lining. I would not be the one who decides these things alone, but I would notice their acoustic effects. A mossy wall absorbs sound. A bare wall returns it. A water film changes the pitch of a stone. A dried patch changes it again. My body would learn these differences until they were as obvious as heat and cold.

I would also work closely with the water-readers, though in a different register. They would read flow; I would read resonance. They would say, “This channel is thinning.” I would say, “This chamber is filling with silence.” They would watch leaves move across the surface. I would listen to the way a drip changes when a basin is nearly full. Together we would be able to tell whether the basin is continuing its slow consent to the city being made.

Because that is the deeper truth of the place: it is not being conquered. It is being negotiated.

I would be a negotiator of small signs.

If I imagine my day, it is full of such signs.

Morning: cold air, damp hems, breath visible. I go first to the lowest channel, where water gathers after the night. I strike the side of a stone curb and listen. The sound is good. Clear, a little cold, with a brief tail of echo. I move upward.

Midmorning: workers begin to lift a block along the eastern terrace. I stand below the retaining face where the sound returns. The maul strikes the wedge. The rope creaks. The block settles with a heavy, satisfying knock. The echo comes back from the basin wall almost cleanly, but there is a faint dullness underneath. I mark the place with a dark pebble. Later, perhaps, someone will inspect the stone. Maybe nothing is wrong. Maybe the dullness is only water in a seam. But it must be recorded.

Noon: rain passes, or begins, or pretends to begin. The air smells of crushed sedge and mineral dust. I sit in a roofless room and watch wet patches appear on flat stone. Some patches are random. Others follow lines too straight to be accidental. The rain remembers survey marks. I place a small token at the edge of one dark line so no one will mistake it for mere damp. This is where a wall may stand someday. Not yet.

Afternoon: a child follows me for a while. There are few children in the basin, and this one is serious in the way children become serious when they grow up inside large sounds. I teach the child to strike a sounding stone lightly and then wait. Not too long. Not too short. Wait until the echo has fully returned and even a little beyond, because sometimes the second answer comes after the first. The child asks what we are listening for. I say, “Whether the place is still shaped the way we think it is.” The child considers this and nods as if it is ordinary. In the basin, perhaps it is.

Evening: the workers shelter in low stone enclosures. The air cools. Water continues. It always continues. I go to one of the resonant chambers, a low opening in the terrace where falling water has been guided into a curved hollow. I do not enter. I sit outside with my back against a block that still holds a faint coolness from the day. The chamber booms slowly after each surge. The sound is not loud, but it is immense. It seems to come from under my ribs. I listen until I can no longer tell whether the rhythm is outside me or inside me.

Night: sleep lightly. The basin does not become silent. It becomes differently loud. Drips separate themselves. Rushes become breaths. Echoes move without apparent source. A wrong silence can wake me. I wake once, not because something has failed, but because a trickle has shifted and now strikes a different lip of stone. The note has changed by a small degree. I lie still and listen until I know whether the change is danger. It is not. It is only the basin trying another way. I sleep again.

If I am not fully human in this world, then my life would be even closer to the surfaces. Perhaps I would be one of the small dark creatures that shelter under wet stones, blinking slowly when disturbed. Not merely an animal, though animal-like: a being with lateral attention, with skin that tastes water, with a body low enough to move through the marshy margins where sedge bends under droplets. I would live in the film between rock and water. I would hear the basin through my whole body. The workers would rarely see me, but they would know I am there because certain sounds would be kept in order. They might leave a small hollow unblocked for me. They might avoid overturning a particular stone where I rest. Not from superstition, but from the same care they show channels and sounding walls. I would be part of the ecology of maintenance.

Or perhaps I would be something even less bodily: a persistent pattern of attention in the basin itself. Not a ghost, because the place is too honest for ghosts. More like an office, a function, a resident role. The echo-tender as a position that the landscape creates when it becomes sufficiently organized to need tending. In that case, “I” would not be a single organism so much as a recurring need given temporary shape. Whenever the basin produces someone who listens carefully, that someone becomes me. Whenever a child presses an ear to cold rock and hears water moving inside the ground, that child is learning to become me. Whenever a worker pauses after a hammer-blow and waits for the answer, I am present there.

But that may be too abstract. The prompt asks where I would find myself, and I want an answer that can be touched.

So let me say it plainly:

I would be found in the wet basin at the edge of an unfinished plaza, where a planned street has been marked by stone alignments but not yet paved. There would be a low step there, half natural, half cut, always damp on one side. I would sit on that step with a sounding stone in my lap, listening to the water move through a nearby channel. My cloak would be dark with moisture at the hem. My boots would be cold. My hands would be still. I would not be waiting for someone to speak. I would be waiting for the place to answer.

Who would I be?

I would be a resident of the preliminary population. One of the almost-no-one. A listener. A tender of echoes. A keeper of unroofed rooms. A guardian of necessary emptiness. Not a founder, not a ruler, not a hero. A maintenance being, an attention being, a small official of the early city. Someone whose work is to ensure that the basin does not become filled too soon, silenced too easily, or mistaken for finished when it is only beginning.

If I had to give myself a name in that world, it would be short and functional. Something like Listener. Or Hollow-keeper. Or The One Who Leaves Room.

But perhaps the truest name would be even simpler:

Attend.

Because that is what I would do. I would attend to water. I would attend to stone. I would attend to the difference between a healthy echo and a blocked one. I would attend to the wet outlines of future walls. I would attend to the few workers moving through the vastness. I would attend to the moss sealing joints, the algae darkening scaffolds, the small amphibians blinking under stones, the insects hovering over pools. I would attend to the emptiness that makes all of it possible.

And if you came looking for me there, you might not see me at first. You would hear the basin first: water falling, hammers answering, channels murmuring, a deep slow boom rising from somewhere below. You might think the place was empty except for the workers and the weather. Then you would notice a small figure seated in the shadow of an unfinished wall, motionless, head slightly tilted, waiting for an echo to return.

That would be me.

Not at the center of construction, but at the center of listening.

Not building the city yet, but keeping the city open until it is ready to be built.

Not loud, but necessary.

Not finished, but becoming.
