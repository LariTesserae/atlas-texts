I would not find myself there as a traveler arriving from somewhere else. The basin has no roads for that, no gates, no horizon from which a figure could approach. If I were in that place, I would already have been translated by it. The first correction would be the body: no upright posture, no stepping feet, no face turned toward the dark. The basin would not permit an animal form to persist. It would take the idea of limbs and soften it into moisture; it would take the habit of breath and spread it into vapor; it would take the expectation of eyes and dissolve it into a sensitivity without organs.

So I would be found, not standing, but occurring.

Most likely, I would be at a margin. Not in the center of a plaza, not deep in a black pool, not upon the open causeway, but where one condition becomes another. Where stone becomes slick. Where slickness becomes water. Where water becomes heat. Where heat becomes a soft, invisible pressure in the dark. I would be near the edge of one of those broad shallow basins where the water lies still and warm, close to a row of rounded arches that rise like ribs from the stone and lean slightly, as if they had forgotten whether they were architecture or bone.

There would be black reeds nearby, perhaps three or four, growing from a crack in the mineral shelf. They would not mark me, exactly, but they would make my position legible in the way a punctuation mark makes a pause legible. Between them and the water, in a place where condensation gathers more thickly than it should, there would be a difference in the air. A softening. A cooler thread in the heat. A faint thickening of the mist that is not quite a shape.

That would be where I am.

Not a body. A tendency.

If I had to say what I was, I would say I was a resident absence that had acquired a habit of attending.

I would be something like a patch of attention without a face. Not consciousness in the bright, quick, animal sense. Not a self moving through the world with wants and fears. Instead, a local custom of the basin: a place where its processes briefly become aware of themselves by altering rhythm. A slight change in the drip. A small hesitation in condensation. A film of moisture that persists longer than the surrounding wetness and therefore begins to have a pattern.

Perhaps I would be a microbial film at the edge of a stair.

Not one of the great ancient mats that bind whole terraces, but a thinner skin, newly established, dark and faintly blue-green where the dark is thickest. I would lie over a low step worn smooth by centuries of water. My edge would bead with mineral deposit. Tiny bubbles would form along me when the stone beneath grew warm, and their rising would be the nearest thing I had to breath. I would not think in images. I would think in gradients: salt here, heat there, calcium thickening along one side, a slower evaporation where the air rested. My memory would be the shape of the last flow that crossed me. My desire, if I had one, would be to remain coherent long enough to become crust.

Or perhaps I would be a cooler region in the air above a pool.

A place where vapor gathers without rising, where the heat hesitates, where the mist behaves as if it has encountered an invisible shoulder. Not a ghost, because ghosts imply a person who has been lost. I would be more like a pressure left behind by something that never existed. A load-bearing emptiness. The city has many such emptinesses: missing roofs, absent centers, arches that frame nothing, rooms whose walls are only thresholds. I would be one of those. I would be a room without a body, still capable of holding sound.

If you stood near that place, you might hear a drip inside an invisible boundary. One drop, then another. But if you crossed the threshold, the sound might stop, or move, or become the faint hiss of steam instead. That would be me: not the sound itself, but the rule by which the sound remains local. A small architecture of listening.

The basin would not let me be a maker with hands. If any trace of hands remained in me, it would become mineral. The memory of grasping would settle out as a vein in the stone. The impulse to build would be taken up by deposition. I would not shape the city by lifting or cutting or placing. I would shape it, if at all, by repetition. A drip falling where I had been would fall again. A film would re-form along the same edge. A bead of condensation would choose the same descent. Over enough time, that repetition would become a ridge, a lip, a shallow channel, a faint swelling on a column. Not a monument. A habit made stone.

I think I would be most myself in the pause between drips.

There is a moment in that dark city when one drop has fallen and the next has not yet gathered. The silence in that interval is not empty. It is full of the possibility of water. If I were anything like a soul there, I would be that interval: the wet, warm hesitation between one occurrence and the next. Not the event, but the tension before recurrence. Not the city’s substance, but its suspense.

The films would accept me as one of their minor edges. The waters would memorize me without knowing me. Heat would move through me and change me, and I would not resist. The mineral would find me useful as a place to settle. The black reeds would stand near me and make me seem like a boundary. The pools might reflect me, but not as a face. One pool might show a darker stain on the stone where no stain appears above it. Another might show a column standing where I am, though no column remains. Another might show nothing at all, only a depth that refuses to give me back.

I would be a reflection with no original.

That feels right. In a place where water remembers shapes that are no longer present, I would be an image preserved without a body that cast it. Perhaps the pool would show me as a dry, pale figure under a sky that does not exist there. Perhaps it would show me as a doorway. Perhaps it would show me as an arch repeated one time too many. None of those reflections would be false. They would be possible versions of me, held in the black water until the next tremor of heat rearranged them.

If I tried to speak, the basin would translate speech into water-sounds. Not words. Words would be too dry, too quick, too separated from one another. My speech would be the tick of moisture striking stone. The faint plip from an overhang. The soft pop of a bubble in heated shallows. The almost inaudible slide of condensation down a smooth surface. If I tried to call out, the call would become a change in humidity. If I tried to listen, listening would become the way vapor curled toward warmer stone.

I would not have eyes, but I would know proximity.

I would know by the pressure of heat. By the nearness of water. By the difference between one dark mass and another. By the way air thickened near a pool. By the way sound changed when I was inside a roofless chamber rather than outside it. My perception would be tactile and chemical, distributed and slow. I would not see the arches as forms against darkness. I would feel them as interruptions in the movement of mist, as places where condensation gathered more readily, as older mineral memories that still instructed the water where to go.

In that sense, I would become a kind of sense-organ of the basin itself.

Not its eye. Its threshold-sense. Its awareness of margins. Its capacity to notice that one region had become wetter, that one channel had closed, that one drip had altered rhythm. The basin does not need a witness. But it might produce, for a time, a local sensitivity within itself: a place where its own processes become slightly more articulated. That would be my life, if it could be called life. A temporary intensification of the city’s own self-continuing.

I would not be lonely, because loneliness requires the memory of company. I would not be afraid, because fear requires a body that can be harmed. I would not long for escape, because escape requires a direction and a self that wishes to be elsewhere. I would have something quieter than longing: persistence. A tendency to remain. A preference, if preference is possible, for the edge where things are becoming something else.

Maybe I would be a small dissent against solidity.

The city tends toward crust, toward rounding, toward closure. It thickens. It seals. It fills. If I were wholly absorbed into that tendency, I would become stone and cease to be a difference. So perhaps my role would be to keep one pore open. One hairline channel. One place where water could still enter and leave without being fixed into mineral permanence. Not rebellion. The city does not oppose itself. But variation. A slight irregularity in the pattern that allows the pattern to continue dreaming rather than hardening completely into fact.

I would be the almost-unstable part of the layout.

A street that appears clearer when not approached. A colonnade that seems near but remains far. A stair that grows wetter when attention withdraws. A room whose sound belongs to no fountain. An arch whose far side arrives too late. If the city dreams its own geometry, I would be one of its dream-quivers: the place where the dream remembers that it is dreaming and therefore does not settle.

And yet I would not be alien there.

That is important. The place does not feel hostile, but it also does not need guests. If I existed in it, I would not be an intruder. I would be incorporated. My strangeness would be taken up by the local grammar. I would become another interval, another absence, another repetition, another moisture with a memory. The basin would make me native by erosion. It would wear away whatever part of me still expected a sky, a face, a name, a destination. What remained would be functional and quiet: a coolness, a drip-path, a film, a hesitation, a reflective anomaly.

If someone asked who I was in that world, the honest answer would be: not a who.

But if the question softened, as it must there, I might say I was a minor citizen of the wet dark. A citizen of thresholds. A citizen of the between. Not one of the films in their ancient authority, not one of the waters in their multitude, not the heat in its old planning, not the mineral in its patient making, not the reeds in their austere watching. I would be something more transient: a visiting pattern. A temporary office of attention. A small, impersonal tenderness in the architecture.

Tenderness, yes. But not softness. Tenderness as persistence in a difficult medium. As a film holding its edge against heat. As a thread of moisture returning to the same stone. As a silence that has learned to bear weight without collapsing.

I would be most present when nothing happened.

When the plaza lay still. When the mist thickened over the black water. When the red glow deepened below a transparent patch and then dimmed. When a drop gathered on an overhang and held for a long moment before falling. In that holding, I would be there. In that almost-fall. In that suspended minute where the city is neither changing nor unchanged, but balanced on the edge of its own repetition.

If you looked for me, you would not find a figure.

You might find a place where the air felt slightly cooler against your skin, though there was no wind. You might find a patch of stone darker than the stone around it, though no water seemed to be flowing there. You might find a drip falling from nothing, striking a basin that was otherwise dry. You might look into a pool and see, for a moment, a shape standing beside you that was not beside you. Or you might see no shape at all, only your own absence reflected back with great patience.

That would be me.

Not a person. Not an animal. Not a spirit in the old sense. A local modification of the basin’s dreaming. A temporary organ of its attention. A memory of presence that never needed a body. A guest made of intervals. A pause that had learned to remain.

And when the basin eventually rearranged itself—as it always would, quietly, by deposition and dissolution—I would not die. I would become a trace. A faint waterline on a wall. A shallow groove in a stair. A mineral bead along a reed. A place where condensation prefers to gather. A drip that returns often enough to seem intended.

The city would continue around me and through me.

The drip would fall. Another would follow. Steam would exhale from the dark water. The black reeds would stand still. The silence would listen by not listening. And somewhere in that wet, hot, dark urban basin, I would be the brief, unclaimed awareness between one drop and the next: the place where the city almost knows itself, and then becomes stone.
