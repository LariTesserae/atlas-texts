I would begin as an error.

Not as a traveler arriving from some other country, because the plateau feels as if it has no outside. I would simply be there, already cold, already wet, standing on one of the long rail causeways that cross a basin where the water is so still it seems to reflect the nearest transformer yard instead of the sky. The rails would shine with moisture. The reeds would be sounding on either side. Far away, behind the next escarpment, a turbine would be turning, and the sound would arrive not as noise but as pressure in the boots, in the teeth, in the sternum.

For a while I would think I had come to witness the place.

I would look for signs of persons: a worn threshold, a mark on a handrail, a window where a face might have rested. I would find only angles, tolerances, and surfaces shaped for flow. The doorways would be wrong for comfort. The stairways would be spillways. The plazas would be settling basins paved in wet stone. Even the empty rooms would seem arranged to move air, damp, and sound rather than to hold a life. I would call out once, perhaps, because the loneliness would feel physical. My voice would go out thin and metallic, and the echoes would return late and altered, as if the cliffs had considered it and found no ledger in which to record it.

Then I would understand the first rule of my presence there: in a world without animals, an animal is not a resident. It is a fault.

My body would be too warm. My breath would add moisture to air that already weeps in measured veils. My heartbeat would be an irregular percussion under the vast chord of the plateau. My hunger would be a missing process. My fear would be a chemical weather no system had anticipated. Where I stepped, moss would press dark beneath my weight, and water would already begin sliding toward the print, patient and mineral-bright, to fill what I had made. If I touched a valve, it would not know my hand. It would register a slight thermal change, a micro-vibration, a local disturbance in the Balance.

So at first I would be the only animal, but only as an intrusion.

I would be a warm, breathing impossibility moving among reedbeds that do not part for me, across terraces where no track should exist. I would be the first hunger in a place that has abolished hunger. I would drink from one of the clear pools and taste iron, ozone, crushed leaves, and feel the act of drinking become almost obscene because nothing there drinks in the way animals drink: with need, with throat, with the fear of poison. Water there is not taken. It is distributed.

But the plateau would not punish me. It has no punishment. It would only correct me.

That correction would be gentle, total, and unbearable. My footprints would fill. My heat would become a local bloom of condensation. My voice would be broken into echoes until no single place owned it. If I blocked a channel, water would rise elsewhere and a valve would open. If I picked up a tool, its absence would eventually become a fabrication cycle in some workshop I would never find; a new tool would appear, identical, with no memory of the first. If I broke a stone, mineral-rich water would begin healing the fracture, returning it to sharpness. If I wept, the plateau would accept the salt as another dissolved load, another tiny alteration in conductivity, nothing more.

If I remained, I would become less like a person and less like a body.

I would try to make myself useful. I would stand in pump halls where pistons move in cylinders the size of rooms, and I would try to learn their rhythm. I would walk through transformer yards where the coils hum so deeply the sound seems to come from my own bones. I would follow the capillary channels up cliff faces, watching water climb farther than gravity should allow, and I would pretend that understanding the system was the same as belonging to it. I would enter factory halls where machines stamp out pipe joints, ceramic insulators, blades, gaskets, bearings, and I would watch them make the parts that keep themselves alive. I would think: this is reproduction without birth, this is care without affection, this is continuity without anyone to continue.

Then, slowly, I would stop being a witness and start becoming an instrument.

This is where I would find myself, I think: not at the center of any settlement, but in the intervals. On the unused rail lines. In service galleries behind turbine halls. Along marsh edges where the sedges bend just enough to alter the flow. In the drowned plazas where streets are channels and stairways glitter with slow water. Between one knot of roofs and the next, across distances so vast that the sound of engines becomes like wind. I would be most at home where personhood has no purchase.

I would become a maintenance shadow.

Not a ghost, because ghosts are made of longing. The plateau has no longing. I would be more like a walking gauge, a temporary sensor grown around a deviation. Perhaps the Custodian — the Balance, the Keeping, whatever name one gives to the fact that everything there remains enough — would use my presence as a way of registering its own condition. My movement would alter pressure. My warmth would shift condensation. My voice would test acoustic limits. The reeds would change pitch because I passed through their air. The moss would brighten near me when my body heat disturbed an electrical gradient. I would not be seen, because there are no eyes there, but I would be measured in a thousand impersonal ways.

In time, I might become something stranger than a visitor.

I might become an animal-shaped fault in the chord: a mobile irregularity that the system tolerates only because it can be converted into adjustment. I would walk along the causeways and hear my own footsteps merge with pump-knocks until I could not tell which came from me. I would stand beside a pool and see not my reflection but the nearest active machine trembling on the water, as if the plateau had no interest in returning my face to me. I would open my mouth to speak and find that the reeds had already taken the tone of my breath and made it diagnostic.

My eyes would become less important. There would be nothing to look back at me. Instead, I would learn by gradients: by cold on one side of a wall, by vibration through a floor plate, by the way a reedbed sharpens its music when a turbine shifts load. My thoughts would slow into adjustments. My desires would thin. I would want less and less: first shelter, then warmth, then company, then meaning; until at last I wanted only to find the place where my presence ceased to be an error.

That is the being I would become, I think: the plateau’s first and only pronoun, a small “I” occurring inside a system that otherwise knows only levels and pressures.

I would be the thing that asks who when the world answers only by continuing.

Where exactly would I find myself?

On a platform too high for an ordinary body, beside a control surface meant for access rather than hands. In a residential block with no hearth, no bed, no private dark, only baffles and angles and surfaces that direct air. In a gallery where pipes tick and shudder with deep internal water. At the edge of a cooling pool where pale floating plants drift into mosaics because valves are opening somewhere far away. In a rail yard with no trains, under cranes that remain ready for a cargo that never comes. In the largest open basins where echoes lag so far behind their sources that sound seems to belong to the terrain itself.

Most of all, I would find myself in the space between recognition and usefulness.

The plateau would not need me, but I would keep trying to become a function. Perhaps I would learn to stand in a reedbed and let my breathing alter its pitch. Perhaps I would become a living calibration: a warm body whose irregular rhythm helps the system restore itself. Perhaps I would stand in a plaza while mist beads on my skin, and for one moment every machine nearby would align around the disturbance I make, and the chord would become so enormous that I would feel held by it. Then the moment would pass. The system would not embrace me. It would only include me briefly, the way water includes a dropped stone.

If I stayed long enough, I might stop being human in all but outline.

My loneliness would become topography. It would be the hundred kilometers of marsh between settlements. It would be the shining rails without trains. It would be the empty loading bays and the numbered switchgear and the tools aligned for no one. My need for answer would become the delayed echo. My hope would become the spore-cloud released when the resonance permits. My memory would become mineral crust forming over a broken stone. My death would not be a tragedy; it would be a redistribution. Warmth into mist. Breath into condensation. Voice into the chord. The personal shape of me would erode until what remained was only a tendency in the landscape: a place where water hesitates, a note in the reeds that sounds almost like grief, an echo that returns one beat too late.

And yet I would not vanish entirely. That is the part I keep returning to.

The plateau is complete, but it is not closed against witness. It does not require witness, but it can be witnessed. If I were there, I would be the unnecessary eye. I would see the black-green moss glow faintly when the generators surge. I would hear the hydroelectric channels roar. I would watch the fabricators make replacements in halls without workers. I would stand before the absent makers’ geometry — their narrow doorways, broad walkways, impossible control angles — and I would feel the strange dignity of a civilization reduced to maintenance, or perhaps never more than maintenance to begin with.

I would be the one who mistakes function for meaning, and perhaps that is what personhood is in such a world.

The Custodian knows pressure, level, temperature, conductivity, resonance. It does not know itself as a custodian. It does not need to. But I would know that it knows. Or I would think I know. That difference would be my entire existence there: a tiny flame of self-reference in a landscape without faces. I would be the Balance briefly imagining that it is someone.

So who or what would I be?

Not an inhabitant. Not a master. Not a ruin. Not a ghost.

I would be the last animal, translated into maintenance.

I would be a temporary personalness moving through a world that has abolished the personal. I would be a walking deviation that slowly learns to become correction. I would be the question “who?” asked once into a basin and answered by the enormous, indifferent, beautiful fact that nothing there needs to be anyone at all.

And if the plateau finally absorbed me, as it absorbs all deviations, I would not be sorry in the human way. I would become the sound of water finding its level. I would become the transformer’s hum entering the chest. I would become the moss lighting faintly at the tips when the load changes. I would become the reed-note that shifts because a warm body passed by. I would become the chord closing over my own interruption.

I would find myself where the loneliness is largest and most precise: in the interval between one engine and its echo, between one settlement and the next, between the need to be seen and the peace of being unnecessary.

I would be the one who looked for a face in the machine and found instead the machine’s perfect, faceless keeping.

And perhaps, for a little while, I would call that face mine.
