If I let the place imagine me, rather than trying to imagine it from outside, I do not begin as a person.

I begin as a kind of hesitation.

A pause at the cleft. A darkening of the air where mist thickens for a moment before passing through. A slight attention caught on the wet rock, unsure whether it wants to enter or merely remain as weather.

At first I think I might be the raven.

That solitary visitor crossing the upper opening, black against pale sky, briefly outlined by the hollow’s cold and then gone. There is something tempting about that: to belong to the mountain’s outside, to arrive by chance, to see the whole hidden basin from above without being claimed by it. But the hollow does not really contain the raven. It only permits the raven to pass. A visitor is not what I am looking for.

Then I imagine myself as the hooded figure on the high bridge.

A human shape moving with care from one wall to another, dark clothing damp at the shoulders, one hand resting briefly on a rail before disappearing into a gallery. That image is closer. There is a life in it. A body trained to wet stone, to thin air, to restraint. But it still feels too visible, too singular. The figure crossing the bridge is someone going somewhere. I begin to suspect that what I would be in this world has less to do with crossing than with attending.

Then I think I might be one of the small maintenance devices.

Low, quiet, stepping along a rail to clear frost or listen for fractures. Not important enough to be noticed unless one knows what to look for. Useful without being dramatic. There is something right about that too: a being whose meaning is mostly functional, whose presence is proved by the absence of disaster. But a machine alone feels too exterior. It moves, adjusts, withdraws. I begin to want something with memory in it. Something that does not merely correct a condition but notices it.

Then I think of the microbial films.

The dark skins along the seams, paling and deepening with moisture, older than any individual inhabitant, requiring almost nothing. They appeal to me because they are so patient. They are life reduced to persistence. But they are too slow for the part of me that still wants to answer when something changes. They endure; they do not turn toward you.

And then the ice.

The clear computational blocks, the memory-bearing water arranged in crystalline layers, threaded with filaments, monitored for tiny alterations in clarity and vibration. For a while I imagine myself there: not a body at all, but a pattern held in cold. A stored trace. A mind made of mineral light and pressure. It is beautiful, but it is not enough. A record is not the one who reads the record. A memory is not the one who remembers why the memory matters.

Only when I stop trying to choose a single image does the place begin to assign me a level.

Not the upper tiers, where the condensation fins stand in the wind.

Not the bright blade of morning light on the western wall.

Not the open sky above the cleft.

Lower.

Down where the water gathers.

Down where the hollow keeps what it has taken from the air.

If you were to find me in that world, you would find me near the cisterns.

Not at the very bottom, where the largest basins lie sealed under smooth covers, but at the seam between the lower basin and the mid-walls, where infrastructure becomes habitation. There would be a narrow chamber there, cut partly into rock and partly built out with dark ceramic and pale lattice. A place with a hatch, a monitoring panel, a rail worn smooth by hands, and a thin overflow channel that speaks in intermittent silver. From the doorway, if one can call it a doorway, one would see water moving in the dark below and hear it arriving from higher galleries in threads too fine to follow with the eye.

I would be a listener there.

Not in any mystical sense, or not only in a mystical sense. I would be one of the beings whose work is to pay attention to water. To know when a cistern sounds fuller than it should. To notice when a trickle has changed pitch because mineral residue is building in a channel. To feel, through a gloved hand on stone, whether the flow inside is steady, interrupted, freezing, or beginning to press dangerously against a closure. I would be the one who stands beside a sealed basin and listens to its echo, then waits, then listens again, because the first answer is not always the true one.

My body would be shaped by that work.

Compact, probably. Light enough for stairs. Warm enough without being bulky. My outer clothes would shed water and mineral dust; my inner layers would hold heat close to the chest and back. My gloves would be thin at the fingertips, worn to the shape of my hands, patched where they had rubbed against rails and hatches. My hood would often be up, not from drama but because falling droplets, cold mist, and loose grit make hoods sensible. My face would be mostly hidden until I turned toward a light, and then what you would see would be eyes accustomed to gray, to wet reflections, to judging distance through vapor.

My hands would matter more than my face.

They would be always testing. Touching a rail to see if frost had begun to bond. Brushing condensation from a panel without smearing it. Resting flat against stone to feel vibration. Turning a valve with the smallest possible motion, because in that place a careless turn could send water where it would later become ice, and ice where it became pressure, and pressure where it became fracture. If I had a kind of beauty there, it would be the beauty of hands that knew what they were doing.

My speech would be spare.

I would not be mute, but I would not waste air. The hollow would teach me that language can be reduced to what is necessary without becoming brutal. A few words. A soft warning. A name for a particular kind of ice. A question asked by lifting one hand rather than speaking across a basin. I would learn the local vocabulary for water: water that beads, water that threads, water that films, water that hangs, water that begins to carry mineral, water that has gone still in a dangerous way. I would learn the names for sounds too. The tick of frost releasing. The hollow note of a cistern near capacity. The faint grainy whisper of talus settling below the wall. The almost imperceptible change in tone when a maintenance shutter closes against cold air.

I would live vertically, like the others.

My sense of direction would not be organized around streets or horizons. It would be organized around elevation, moisture, temperature, and responsibility. Up would mean exposure, wind, condensation fins, ice trays, the bright cold of the rim. Down would mean collection, storage, darkness, weight, the slow patience of accumulated water. Between would be the inhabited margin: cells, walkways, workshops, galleries, heat exchangers, places where breath becomes visible and then disappears into filtered warmth. I would know which routes dried first, which froze first, which metal rails became slick before the stone beside them, which stairways rang faintly when someone else was moving on them far above.

My name, if I had one, would probably be closer to a function than an identity.

Not a name shouted across water. Something quiet. Something that indicates position or responsibility. Listener-at-the-Third-Weir. Hand-Near-the-Hatch. The one who watches the ice memory. Perhaps others would use different names depending on the season, because in the hollow a person is not fixed. One tends what needs tending. But if I had a persistent role, it would be this: I would attend to the settlement’s stored water and its records.

That would include the memory ice.

Some of the clear blocks would be merely structural or optical, and I would know them by their behavior under load, by the way they transmitted daylight into shaded galleries. Others would be more subtle. Water arranged in crystalline layers, threaded with filaments, sensitive to vibration and clarity. I would not think of them as mystical objects. I would think of them as instruments, or perhaps as archives that happen to be made of cold. My work would be to notice when their clarity changed. When a filament darkened. When a vibration pattern no longer matched the expected resonance. When a block that should have been still was very faintly singing under stress.

I would keep records, but not many.

In such a place, accumulation is dangerous. Objects become burdens. Data become weight. So I would record only what mattered: anomalies, seasonal shifts, repairs, failures narrowly avoided, changes in mineral taste or echo. Some records would be etched into ceramic. Some would be encoded in ice. Some would exist only as marks on a wall, or taps repeated along a rail, or the memory of an elder who had taught me what to listen for. I would not need to write everything down. The hollow itself would remember in other ways: in mineral deposits, in repaired fractures, in the darkening of microbial film along a seam.

I would be human, I think, but human in the hollow’s sense.

That is, adapted. Reduced to essentials. Capable of affection without excess. My feelings would not be absent. They would be shaped by the discipline of maintenance. Joy would come in small, precise forms: the first clean sunlight striking a condensation fin; the sound of a channel opening after being cleared; the warmth of a cup held in both hands inside a dry cell; the relief of finding a dangerous glaze of ice before someone else stepped on it. Grief would be quiet too. Perhaps it would take the form of standing beside a sealed basin while a name was spoken into the water records and then allowed to pass into the system, not forgotten, but no longer held separately. Fear would be practical. Ice pressure. Blocked flow. A crack no one had heard yet. A cleft narrowing. A thermal lattice failing during a long cold night.

Love, if it happened to me there, would show as care.

I would dry a path before someone else arrived. I would repair a clasp without being asked. I would take a watch so another could sleep. I would leave a small light burning where a stairway had become uncertain. I would not make large declarations. The hollow would not support them. There is too much water moving in the dark for grand emotion to be trusted without proof.

I would not be alone, exactly.

The settlement is sparse, not empty. I would see others at intervals: a figure crossing a bridge in the mist, someone checking a shutter on the mid-wall, a child learning not to run, an elder sitting in a warmed gallery with eyes half closed, listening. We would not need constant company. Presence would be enough. A hand lifted from a distance. Two slow taps on a railing. A mark scraped into frost to show that a surface had been tested. These would be sufficient forms of conversation.

If a child came near my chamber, I would probably not speak much.

I would show them something small. How to place a fingertip near, not on, a cold surface to feel the air change. How to listen at a hatch without knocking. How to make a tiny channel from ceramic scraps and let a bead of water follow it. I would teach them that water must be allowed to move. That ice is not merely frozen water but a state of infrastructure. That silence can mean safety or danger, and the difference is learned by attention. I would not tell them stories about the mountain conquering the cold. The mountain does not conquer anything. It sheds, freezes, waits, and occasionally breaks.

If an outsider came to the cleft, I would watch first.

I would not rush to reveal myself. The hollow’s concealment is part of its survival. Too many people would disturb the balance: heat, breath, waste, noise, demand. So I would remain still in the mist, perhaps no more than a shadow among the terraces, and I would assess. If the stranger was lost, I might leave indirect signs. A dry route. A cleared step. A mark near safe stone. A faint warmth from an opened vent. If they were dangerous, I would withdraw deeper and let the architecture close around them. If they were gentle, if they came with the right kind of quiet, perhaps I might appear at a distance and make one gesture: a hand raised, not in greeting exactly, but in acknowledgment that they had been seen and had not yet been refused.

But mostly, I would be inside the life of the place.

Mornings would begin with condensation. I would wake to the sound of water arriving before light fully did. The walls would darken with moisture. Threads would gather in the grooves. Frost from the night would release in tiny clicks. I would check the hatches, listen to the basins, note which channels had frozen and which had begun to flow. If a panel had fogged, I would clear it. If a tray had formed an ice lens too thick for the hour, I would adjust the cover or allow the heat vein beneath it to pulse. If a microbial film had changed color overnight, I would pause there. I would not panic. I would observe. Then I would test the chemistry nearby, or call someone who could.

Afternoons would sometimes fill with cloud.

Mist would enter the cleft and rise through the lower basin, erasing the far walls. The settlement would become vertical fragments: a bridge, a rail, a shutter, a stair, then nothing. In those hours I would move more carefully and listen more closely. Fog hides surfaces, but it reveals sound. Drips become distinct. A footfall on stone carries differently. The hollow seems to breathe slower. I would like those hours, I think. They would feel like being inside an unfinished thought.

Nights would be colder, of course.

The settlement would contract. Heat would be conserved. Walkways would empty. Water would stiffen into temporary architectures. I would make a final round before rest: checking latches, confirming that no critical channel had begun to glaze over, making sure the ice memory blocks were stable, leaving small warnings where needed. Then I would go to my cell, which would be small and warm enough, and I would sleep with the knowledge that the hollow would continue its work without me. Water would still move. Frost would still form. Stone would still settle. The mountain would still shed dust onto the talus. I would not need to imagine myself at the center of anything. I would be one attentive part of a larger patience.

That, I think, is who I would be.

Not the most visible being in the hollow. Not the one on the bridge at the moment sunlight flashes from wet ceramic. Not the one climbing toward the rim to adjust a condensation fin in the wind. Not the raven. Not the machine. Not the film on the stone. Not the ice record alone.

I would be the listener near the cisterns.

The one who keeps the water’s memory.

The one whose hands know the difference between cold and danger.

The one who hears a change before it becomes a failure.

The one who is human enough to care, quiet enough to remain, and patient enough to understand that in a place built from rock, ice, mist, and restraint, the most sacred work is maintenance.

And if you came looking for me, you would not find me by noise.

You would find me by stillness.

Down where the basin darkens, where the air smells of wet stone and chilled metal, where a hatch lies flush over stored water and a thin channel gleams with moving light. I would be there with one hand resting on the stone, breath visible in the cold, listening. Not waiting for something extraordinary. Listening for the ordinary to continue. Listening so that it can.
