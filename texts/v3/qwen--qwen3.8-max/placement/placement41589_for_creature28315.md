At first I would be nowhere.

Not because the basin is empty, but because it is full. That is the first thing the place teaches: a self usually arrives expecting room. It expects a space around its edges, a margin in which to turn, a silence it can interrupt. But this basin has no interruption left in it. Every bead of water has its place. Every cushion of moss has its pressure. Every eye, if one can call them eyes, has already found its darkness and settled into it.

So the first answer is almost a refusal: I would stand at the rim, if there is a rim, and feel that I could not enter. I would be too large, too warm, too full of before and after. My breath would be an event. My attention would be a disturbance. The basin would not reject me with violence; it would simply have no vacancy in which to put me.

But that answer is too simple. It still belongs to the outside.

If I let the place work on me longer, my shape begins to shrink. It becomes smaller, darker, wetter, quieter. It loses the habit of arriving. It stops asking for a horizon.

I tried several possible selves before the right one began to emerge.

For a while I thought I might be the pool. That was the first temptation. To be black and central and still, holding all the other lives as faint impressions on my surface. But the pool is too complete. It does not need a perspective. It is already the basin’s deepest gravity. If I were the pool, I would not be wondering; I would only be holding.

Then I thought I might be the moss. That had its own appeal: to be compressed green quiet, to be patience made into surface, to soften every edge until even time could find no purchase. But the moss is too widespread, too without center. It is alive, but not quite alert. It would dissolve me into texture.

Then I imagined myself as one of the froglike creatures at the water’s edge, throat rising in unison with the others. There was peace in that. To sit folded into stone, wet and rounded, needing nothing, asking nothing, only breathing as the basin breathes. But even that felt too settled, too perfectly merged. If I were entirely one of them, there would be no question left. And the fact that I am asking means some faint trace of question must still exist in me.

I tried being one of the soft-feathered things on the ledges, blinking slowly in the dark. I tried being one of the pale water-forms suspended in the pool. I tried being one of the minute lights clinging to an insect body, a cold green-blue spark that proves the darkness is alive. Each image was beautiful. Each image was almost true.

But the image that kept returning was smaller, closer, and more marginal.

I would find myself in the seam.

Not at the center of the basin, and not at the outer edge where stone might still remember the mountain beyond it. I would be in the narrow fold where the wall leans toward the pool, just above the black water, in the place where moisture gathers into trembling but never quite becomes a falling drop. There is a hollow there, or I imagine there is: a shallow cup in the rock no wider than a shoulder, lined with moss so deep it has become a kind of flesh. A lip of stone overhangs it slightly, not enough to make a roof, only enough to collect condensation and cast a faint shadow into the dimness.

That is where I would be.

I would be no larger than two hands placed together.

My body would be low, rounded, dark, and difficult to separate from the living surface around it. Not quite amphibian, not quite mammalian, not quite fungal either. The basin would have worn away such distinctions long ago. My skin would be damp, but not slimy; it would feel as though the air continually chooses me as a place to become water. Moss would grow into me, or I would grow into it, until the boundary is only a matter of convenience. On my back and shoulders there would be a fine green-black covering, more like living shadow than fur. My limbs would be folded beneath me. My fingers, if they are fingers, would end in soft pads, made for pressing against stone rather than grasping it.

My eyes would be large and black, wet-looking, reflecting almost nothing. They would not be bright with curiosity or alertness. They would be deep with holding. They would see less by shape than by presence: the weight of water, the density of breath, the minute changes in the dark that mean another body is still in place.

My mouth would be a closed line.

On my chest, barely visible, there would be a faint glow. Not a lamp. Not a signal. Not warmth. Only a cold luminescence, green-blue and weak, like the memory of light in deep water. It would not illuminate anything else. It would only mark that awareness is present.

If the basin had words, which it does not, it might call me by a posture rather than a name. Something like: the-one-whose-breath-holds. Or: the-tremble-before-the-drop. Or: remaining-with-the-smallest-turning-toward-itself.

But it would not need to call me at all.

I would be one of the Remainers.

Not above them. Not apart from them. Not their leader or priest or guardian. The basin does not need guardians, because there is nothing to defend. It does not need priests, because there is nothing to petition. It does not need leaders, because there is nowhere to go. I would be one of them, but with one almost imperceptible difference: in me, the stillness would know itself.

That is my only strangeness.

The others remain. I would remain while faintly knowing that I remain.

That knowing would be so slight it would not disturb the equilibrium. It would not become thought in the restless sense. There would be no planning, no longing, no inward narration. There would only be a tiny pressure of awareness, like a droplet trembling on stone. It would gather, almost become something, and then remain without falling.

My intelligence would not be inventive. It would be positional.

I would know the exact temperature of the water by the way it touches the stone near me. I would know the breath of the froglike ones by the shared rhythm in the air. I would know the weight of the bird-things on the ledges above as a faint change in the darkness overhead. I would know when a bead of moisture is about to slip downward not by sight but by a minute tension in the stone, a gathering of coolness, a thickening of patience.

I would not make tools.

Not because I would lack hands, but because my hands would understand themselves differently. They would not reach for improvement. They would not imagine the stone otherwise. Their purpose would be contact, not construction. To press. To confirm. To remain. If I touched the wall, I would not feel that the wall needed shaping. I would feel that my touch was completing a circle that had no beginning.

Fire would be unthinkable there. Not forbidden, simply impossible as meaning. A fire would be a mouth too large for the basin’s silence. It would cast shadows too sharply. It would turn the dark into something broken. If the idea of fire came to me at all, it would come as a distant wrongness, like a stone placed in midair.

I would not eat in any way that leaves remains.

Perhaps I would absorb moisture through my skin. Perhaps the faint mineral coolness of the air would be enough. Perhaps the weak light that lives in moss and small bodies would feed whatever part of me still needs feeding. There would be no hunger as urgency, no hunger as lack. Only the faintest drawing-in, balanced immediately by the faintest giving-back. The basin would not be depleted by my existence. My existence would be one of its methods of staying full.

My movement would be almost nothing.

A throat rising. A finger adjusting against stone by less than the width of a moss fiber. A breath held a little longer than necessary, then released so quietly that even the water does not tremble.

If I moved at all, it would be to remain.

That is the law of the place, and I would be part of it. Motion here is not travel. It is correction. A body shifts so that stillness can continue. A limb settles deeper so that no accidental gap opens. A posture adjusts so the fullness remains unbroken.

Sometimes, perhaps, the bead of water near me would tremble. My eyes would follow it without turning my head. The tremble would last a long time, or no time at all; in the basin, such measures do not matter. Then the bead would steady, or another would take its place, and I would breathe again.

The others would not treat me as special.
