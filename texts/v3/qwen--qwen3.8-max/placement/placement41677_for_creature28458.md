I would not find myself on the open mountain.

That high, cloud-scoured, abrasive world would be only the outside of the question: the hard sentence spoken before the real meaning begins. If I were in this world at all, I would be in the seam first — the thin fissure, the almost-invisible breath, the place where the newborn peak accidentally exhales warmth into cold air.

And I would not be standing. There is no standing there.

I would be turned sideways, one shoulder against glassy rock, the other against a surface still remembering heat from below. My face would be close enough to the stone to feel it weeping. The air outside would be thin and clear enough to hurt; the air inside the crack would be wet, mineral, and thick, pressing against my lips like something alive but not yet named. I would feel the mountain not as scenery, but as pressure: a newly made interior, still deciding whether to remain closed.

If I remained there long enough — and the place would make “long enough” feel strange, because time would begin to move in drip-pulses rather than hours — I would pass from the fissure into the low chamber beneath it.

Not gracefully. Not as a person entering a room.

I would be folded into it.

The chamber is too low for upright confidence. It asks the body to become smaller, closer, more horizontal. I would find myself crouched, braced, half-curled against the rock, hands on surfaces that are alternately slick, warm, too hot, and beaded with condensation. The ordinary daylight from the upper crack would arrive as a blade, not as comfort. When it entered, everything would become brutally visible: the wet walls, the glassy shells, the trembling microbial felts, the crowded bodies, my own skin shining with moisture that is no longer entirely mine.

Where exactly would I be?

Not at the center, because the chamber has no true center.

I would be in the low corner where the hotter air pools invisibly against the floor, if floor is even the right word. I would be near one of the finger-wide vents, close enough that steam touches me in pulses but does not scald, because the chamber’s cruelty is precise and not uniform. Above me would be a narrow overhang where condensation gathers. From its edge, a bead of water would form, tremble, fall, and sometimes fall again along a path that feels rehearsed. Beneath that drip, my own body would become part of the water’s route.

That is where I would find myself: in the wettest fold of the blister, where heat, vapor, stone, and animal presence are pressed into one another until “environment” and “body” stop being cleanly separable.

And who or what would I be?

At first, I would think I was an observer.

That would be the simplest mistake.

I would believe that I had entered the chamber to witness it: to see the heat-mites grazing on white crusts, the thermal worms folded into hairline cracks, the eyeless springtails moving like pale powder over wet stone, the flat-backed creatures pressed to hot surfaces as if they were living varnish. I would see the film-walkers skimming shallow basins, the vent-sippers swelling in steam, the ceiling hunters arriving without sound at places where weakness is likely. I would see the ordinary sunline make the water flash into glittering motion, and I would think, *I am here, and this is there.*

But the chamber does not permit that separation.

My breath would add vapor. My warmth would redirect condensation. My skin would become a new surface. My salt would become a grazing place. My shadow would fall on the wall and lag a moment too long behind me. My reflection in a film of water would show the chamber at a slightly wrong angle, as if the water were remembering me from another position. The moment I exhaled, tiny droplets would form on the nearest glass. The moment I shifted heat, mites would alter their path. The moment my hand touched stone, the chamber would begin using me.

So I would not remain a visitor for long.

I would become a guest-body. A borrowed ledge. A warm surface with a pulse.

If the chamber had to assign me a function, it would not make me a ruler, a guardian, or a central mind. There is no throne in that wet mineral blister. No intelligence seated at the heart of the heat. No creature waiting to speak. Instead, I would become one of the places where the Still Crowd briefly thickens. I would be a node in the saturation.

My shoulder would become a slope where condensation gathers.

The hollow of my collarbone would hold a bead of water too heavy to fall until some tiny movement breaks it loose.

My forearm would become a wet road across which a film-walker moves with slow, fringed limbs, using the water on my skin as it would use any other slick surface.

A thermal worm would wedge itself into the fold beside my elbow, not because it understands me, but because a drip forms there at intervals and strikes nearby stone in a rhythm it can use.

Heat-mites would climb my knuckles, their glassy shells pale as salt-beads, testing the minerals left by sweat. They would not hate me. They would not worship me. They would simply find that I am a warm, damp place with useful crusts forming on me.

Egg-warmers might push a naturally broken grain of hot pumice against the hollow where my body holds heat longer than the surrounding rock. They would not make a nest of it. They would not arrange it with intention. They would use the warmth my presence accidentally provides, then move on when the heat changes.

A translucent larva might anchor in a wet fold of whatever remains of my clothing, if anything still counts as clothing by then. It would not burrow. It would not feed on me in any violent way. It would simply mistake me for mineral crust, for shelter, for the kind of damp surface where its unfinished body can wait.

And I would let it.

That is the unsettling part. I would let it.

Not because I would be helpless, but because the chamber teaches a different meaning of stillness. There is no room for large movement. No space for sweeping gesture. No architecture to rearrange. No plant life to part, no nest to disturb, no path to clear. The world is already crowded, already wet, already in use. To struggle would be to displace water, shell, membrane, egg cluster, molted skin, and vapor without gaining any ground. To remain still would be to become part of the chamber’s breathing.

So I would become still.

My thoughts would slow into condensation cycles. I would stop trying to hold the day in ordinary units. Instead, time would happen like this: warmth rises; moisture gathers; a bead trembles; it falls; steam lifts; the air thickens; surfaces weep; the crowd shifts; the sunline enters; everything becomes sharp and glittering; the sunline leaves; the chamber closes into vapor again. My inner life would begin to follow those pulses. I would not think in sentences for long. I would think in beads of water, in heat gradients, in the pressure before a click.

If I had to be one creature among the fauna, I would not be the hunter on the ceiling. I would not be the thermal worm, though I understand its patience. I would not be the springtail, though I might resemble its pale trembling. I would be one of the flat-backed things pressed against a warm surface.

Not exactly as they are, but in their manner.

I would be broad, low, heat-lacquered, slick with protective moisture. My body would be less a separate animal than a living scale of the chamber. Smaller creatures would cross me without acknowledgment. Condensation would form on my back. I would feel them as weather, not as visitors. My mouthparts — if I had them in that shape — would move too slowly to follow, scraping mineral varnish from stone. I would not build. I would not carve. I would not keep anything. If a naturally sharp shard of obsidian lay near me, I might use it once to lever loose a crust, then leave it where the mountain had dropped it. The act would complete itself. No artifact would remain.

That feels right.
