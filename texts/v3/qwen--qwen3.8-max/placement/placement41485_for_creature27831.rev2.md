I would not find myself at the center of the roar.

I would find myself in the pause after it: the second silence, the one that is not truly silence but the space where the body begins to understand what the world has just done to it.

At first, I think I would arrive wrongly.

I would come upright, too curious, too full of the habit of looking out over a landscape and trying to take it in all at once. I would want horizon, vista, explanation. I would want to name the swells of stone, the basins, the fog routes, the pipes in the rock. I would want to say: this is a plateau, this is an acoustic storm, this is a pool that will last until evening.

The plateau would correct that quickly.

The first pressure wave would teach me to crouch. The first sweet, clear tone from a hollow mouth would teach me to move away without asking why. The first time I mistook an echo for its source, or heard a boom arrive from the wrong side of the sky, I would stop trusting the ordinary shape of hearing. I would learn that here, presence must be lowered, thinned, made attentive.

So I would not remain a traveler for long.

I would become something closer to the ground.

I think I would find myself in one of the shallow depressions behind a long rise of weathered basalt, where the wind breaks and the roar loses some of its teeth. Not a valley, exactly. Not a cave. A modest hollow worn into the old skin of the tableland, perhaps no larger than a few bodies lying side by side, but enough. Enough for moss. Enough for a cistern groove. Enough for a rope ring set into stone. Enough for a low shelter angled away from the prevailing sound.

There would be green there, but close. Cushion moss pressed into the rock like a living pad. A tank plant braced in a cup of stone, its center holding a spoonful of warm water and whatever small life had arrived with the last fog. Perhaps a row of half-buried clay resonators nearby, shaped by hands that understood pitch better than permanence. Perhaps a worn groove cut into bedrock, guiding condensation into a vessel no larger than a folded palm.

That is where I would be: at the edge of a basin, in an acoustic shadow, near one of the old routes marked by rope and patience.

Not in the open.

Not where the land sings too cleanly.

If I had a body there, it would not resemble the body I imagine now.

It would be low. Broad through the chest. Dense in bone. My center of gravity would sit near the ground, not because I chose humility, but because the plateau punishes anything that makes itself into a sail, a mast, a bell, a target. My knees would know stone better than my eyes knew distance. My palms would be callused, not from labor alone, but from listening. I would press them flat to the rock and feel the plateau thinking before my ears could report anything useful.

My ears themselves would be less important.

I would protect them with wax, fiber, padded hoods, perhaps caps stitched with overlapping hide scales that shed rain without flapping. I would not rely on them. The air lies there. Sounds arrive altered. Echoes come early, or late, or wearing the wrong emotional weather. So I would learn to listen with other parts of the body: the soles of the feet, the sternum, the fine hairs on the arms, the inside of the wrists, the teeth, the belly. I would know a pressure change as a tightening behind the ribs. I would know a dangerous hollow by the way the ground seemed to hold its breath.

I would become, above all, a listener.

But that word is too gentle.

The plateau would make something more specific of me.

I think I would be a keeper of intervals.

Not a leader. Not a hunter. Not a builder. A keeper of intervals: one who knows the difference between the first silence and the second. The first silence can be a trick, a saturation of sense, a pause before the next boom folds over the basin. The second silence is the one with weight and shape. It tells you whether the air has emptied itself or is merely waiting. It tells you whether it is safe to cross the pan, to fill a bladder, to move from one shadow to the next, to press moss cuttings into a damp crack before the wind returns.

My work would be timing.

I would memorize durations rather than names. How long after a deep organ tone the hidden drains begin to pull water downward. How many breaths between the thermal knocks of sun-heated stone. How the reeds lean before a fog wall arrives. How dust trembles into tiny ridges when a frequency is building beneath the surface. How the pool creatures rise when the basin is safe enough for them, and how their vanishing is a warning that no human argument should ignore.

I would know routes not as lines on a map, but as sequences of permissible movement.

Here: wait.

Here: three low steps, then pause.

Here: palm flat, count the vibration, then cross.

Here: do not stop, even if the silence seems beautiful.

Here: lie down, mouth closed, chest against the stone, let the pressure wave pass over you like a hand over a drum.

I would be one of those who move through the world by touch and signal.

If another being found me there, I would not call out. I would lower myself. I would make my hands visible, palms flat, fingers spread. If they approached, we might exchange nothing at first except stillness. Then perhaps a rhythm tapped against stone. Two taps and a pause: wait. A slow circle rubbed against the shoulder: safe enough to move. Fingers pressed to the ground and lifted: water below. A hand drawn lightly across the throat: danger.

I would not find speech useless. I would find it too risky.

If I spoke at all, it would be softly, in a voice shaped more by breath and chest than by the open air. I might hum against another’s wrist to pass meaning through bone. I might learn to carry messages along a rope: a pull, a slackening, a double jerk, a long tension. In fog, those small languages would become more real than any landscape. The world would narrow to the cord between my hands and the intelligence moving through it.

In that way, I would not be alone.

I would belong to a web of quiet beings: the rope-walkers, the shelter-tenders, the moss-planters, the resonator-shapers, the children taught to feel tremor before they are taught to wander. We would not gather in large numbers. The plateau does not permit accumulation. If we grew too many, we would divide without drama, as water divides when it finds separate fingers of drainage. One pair would take a new fog route. Another would repair a shelter in a neighboring sound shadow. A young person would carry a coil of cord and vanish into the gray, not as a tragedy, but as the ordinary shape of survival.

I would have responsibilities, not possessions.

A cistern groove to keep clear. A rope line to check after storms. A cluster of clay resonators whose pitches must be cleaned of dust and mineral crust. A moss bed to tend with restrained hands. A shelter whose angles must never become proud enough to sing. A hollow that fills only when the wind comes from the south-southwest, and must not be trusted when the air turns sweet.

I would leave no monument.

I would leave signs that can be undone: a stack of pebbles, a knot, a bent reed, a cord tied around a natural spur. If I improved a place, I would do so in a way that allowed the plateau to forget me. That would be the deepest courtesy there.

But the image does not stop there.

If I let myself become more itself, I find I do not remain entirely human in the old sense.

The plateau would shape me toward something stranger.

I think I would become part instrument.

Not a machine. Not a device. A living resonance. My chest would be broad and slightly hollow, built to feel pressure without breaking. My ribs might carry membranes, thin and tough, like the gliders’ wings but less for flight than for reading. My skin would be mottled with rust, ash, moss-green, and the pale mineral bloom of dried condensation. I would not have many ears in the ordinary sense. I would have surfaces that attend.

Perhaps the people would not quite claim me as one of them.

Perhaps they would find me near the old pillar stumps, where the ruins of heavier ambitions have been ground down into low teaching. They would know my habits. They would know that when I press myself flat into a certain hollow, the air there is safe for a time. They would know that if I begin to hum, it is not song. It is measurement. A low, almost sub-audible hum, a way of testing whether the stone will answer kindly or badly.

They might call me something that does not translate well.

Not “singer.” Singing is too deliberate.

Not “guardian.” Guardians imply a mastery the plateau would mock.

Maybe something closer to “the one who answers quietly.”

Or “the second quiet.”

I would live in the spaces between larger sounds.

When a storm builds, I would not fight it. I would seal the shelter. I would wrap tools in hide and moss. I would anchor vessels so they cannot knock against one another. I would lower my body and breathe through the chest, not the mouth. I would feel the booms arrive through the stone and note which ones are distant, which ones are structural, which ones mean water is moving through hidden galleries below. If the noise becomes so total that it creates false pockets of silence, I would know not to trust that relief. I would wait until the body of the world settles enough to give a true interval.

Then I would move.

Quickly, but without haste.

I would check the rope lines first. Then the cistern cups. Then the resonators. Then the moss beds. If a basin had filled, I would approach it with reverence and caution. The water would be warm, shallow, cloudy with mineral dust, already stirring with translucent life. I would kneel at the edge, not standing, and I would watch the little glass-flat swimmers pulse beneath the surface. I would take only what I needed. I would leave the black eggs undisturbed. I would know that this abundance is borrowed, and that the basin may be stone again by dusk.

If a stone-lung emerged nearby, I would not startle it. I would let it graze the wet rock in its slow, sealed way. Its presence would tell me something about pressure and moisture that my body could not yet prove. If it returned to its chamber suddenly, I would return to mine.

If a glider fell from the air in a flat, tumbling descent, I would watch, but not chase. Some things survive by not being pursued.

In quieter seasons, I would tend the small green architectures.

I would press moss cuttings into damp cracks. I would clear debris from condensation channels. I would encourage fungal films in sound shadows by leaving just enough organic matter for the pale farmers to use. I would not call this farming. The word is too possessive. It would be more like conversation with place: a way of saying, I want to remain here without making the land louder by my staying.

I would learn which flowers open only during loud intervals, and what their stiff petals mean about vibration. I would learn which reed brakes can be passed through only when they bend in a certain rhythm. I would learn where the pale insects build their tiny baffles, and I would avoid disturbing those pockets of stillness. Their work would seem holy to me, though I would not use that word either. Holiness here would be any small thing that survives by being exact.

And if danger came?

I would not be brave in the way of stories.

Bravery on the plateau would not be defiance. It would be the discipline to remain still when every old instinct demanded flight. It would be the ability to distinguish between a noise that is terrible and a noise that is deadly. Sometimes the terrible sounds are merely weather. Sometimes the sweet sounds are murder waiting to happen. I would learn that sweetness can be the worst warning.

If a hollow began to sing clearly, beautifully, I would leave at once.

If the ground under my palms turned high and taut, I would not wait to understand it. I would move low and fast toward shadow. If I were with a child, I would press them against my chest and cover their ears, not to hide the world from them, but to keep their small bodies from being shaped too early by its full voice. If I were alone, I would make myself flat, quiet, and patient, and I would let the plateau pass over me.

I would also be changed by what I survived.

Over time, my body would carry the marks of pressure: stiff joints, polished skin where it had been pressed against stone, perhaps hearing that no longer worked in ordinary ways. I might become like the elders described in the life of the plateau: less able to parse airborne sound, more able to feel the deep architecture of tremor. I would place a child’s hand on the ground and make signs: not that. Wait. Again.

I would teach by attention, not declaration.

My knowledge would be stored in routes and rhythms, not libraries. I would carry it in the way my body paused before crossing an open pan, in the way my fingers found the knots on a rope, in the way I could feel a storm folding into itself before the fog arrived. If I were wise, I would be quiet about it. If I were foolish, the plateau would correct me, perhaps finally.

There is one more shape I keep becoming, though it is harder to describe.

Sometimes I think I would not be a single creature at all.

I would be a pattern of listening stretched across several lives: a pressure-sensitive membrane in a shelter wall, a clay resonator half buried in dust, a fungal film groomed in a sound shadow, a rope line humming faintly under fog, a human palm pressed flat to stone, a pool creature pulsing through temporary water, a glider adjusting itself to a broken thermal. I would be the relationship between them. The interval. The shared attention that lets one thing survive because another noticed.

That may be the truest answer.

I would be an interval-being.

Not made of permanence, but of timing.

Not loud, but responsive.

Not eager to fill every space, but trained to know when a space can be entered.

If the plateau asked me what I wanted, I would not answer with conquest or settlement. I would not ask to build a tower, to found a city, to make the land legible to strangers. I would ask for enough stillness to perceive. Enough water to continue. Enough shadow to move safely. Enough silence to be useful without becoming a target.

I would want to pass through as fog passes.

But fog leaves moisture behind, so perhaps that is what I would leave: a little dampness, a little tending, a little cleared channel, a little repaired rope, a little warning tapped into stone before I moved on.

So if you ask where I would find myself in that world, I would say:

in the acoustic shadow behind a low ridge of ancient basalt,  
near a temporary pool just beginning to fill,  
where a carved groove leads condensation into a clay cup,  
where a row of resonators hums faintly without becoming music,  
where the moss is thick enough to soften a body’s weight,  
where the old circular foundations have returned themselves to the ground,  
where the rope line disappears into fog and is trusted anyway.

And if you ask who or what I would be, I would say:

a listener.  
a route-tender.  
a keeper of intervals.  
a low-bodied being with hands made for touch-language and palms made for reading stone.  
a living resonator, half creature, half warning.  
a tender of fungal films in sound shadows.  
a student of the second silence.

Not the loudest thing there.

Not the largest.

Not the one who claims the plateau.

The one who learns, at last, to answer it quietly.
