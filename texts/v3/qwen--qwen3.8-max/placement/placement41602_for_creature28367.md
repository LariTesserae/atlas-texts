I would not find myself standing at the rim looking down into the basin as a traveler looks into a scene. The place would not permit that kind of distance. If I entered it in my ordinary shape, I would already be wrong: too tall, too dry, too full of hurried intention. My footsteps would bruise the moss. My breath would disturb the condensation. My voice would be a blunt object dropped into all that patient noise. So I would not stay as I am. The basin would reduce me, translate me, make me small enough to belong to its surfaces.

At first I might seem to be no one at all. I would be a beading of moisture on a cold flange. I would be a green film on a warm pipe. I would be a thread of algae in a shallow channel, a microbial skin learning the temperature of the rock. I would exist as a slickness, a living varnish, a tender coat on stone and iron. I would not have eyes. I would not have hands. I would only have the world as wetness, heat, mineral taste, and vibration. A drip would be an event. A change in steam would be weather. A ray of sun striking the basin would feel like a slow opening.

But if the basin gave me a body, it would not make me large. It would make me low, wet, quiet, and close to the ground. It would make me one of the pale things that can live beside noise without being broken by it.

I think I would be a small amphibious creature, not quite a frog, though frog-like. I would live in the largest warm pool, where the water is shallow and mineral-bright, where the floor is a patch of wet ash, moss, and rust-stained gravel. My body would be smooth and translucent in places, as if I had been made from the basin’s own condensation and had only reluctantly taken a shape. My skin would be thin enough to feel every change in the water. My belly would press against the warm stone. My toes would be rounded and adhesive, able to grip wet iron, slick moss, and the glazed sides of ceramic insulators. My eyes would be dark and almost motionless, less for seeing distances than for noticing shifts in light, the flicker of a leaf, the tremble of a pool when a valve opens somewhere nearby.

You would find me near the place where the machinery and the water argue most gently with one another: perhaps beneath the low lip of rock where water spills into the hidden cistern with its hollow drumming sound, or under the condenser racks where the air cools and the droplets fall faster than the ground can drink. I would choose a shelf where a pipe sweats steadily, where a broad leaf drips into a shallow mirror of water, and where the slow throb of the turbine can be felt through the rock like a buried heart. I would not build anything there. I would not repair anything. I would only remain.

I would be a listener.

That would be my nature. In a place so loud, listening would be more important than speaking. The basin is full of sound, but its sounds are not commands. They are conditions. Steam hisses somewhere behind broken rock. Water taps against iron. The turbine rises and falls. A cable sings faintly when wind combs over the rim. A valve sighs, and the whole humid air seems to inhale. I would live inside those intervals. I would know the basin by its pauses: the moment between one drip and the next, the silence after a pump stops, the tiny gap when the turbine seems to consider whether to continue. My mind, if I had one, would not be made of words. It would be made of rhythms.

I would not be intelligent in the human sense. I would not plan, or remember in long sentences, or wish to be elsewhere. My consciousness, if it could be called that, would be a kind of damp attentiveness. I would feel the warmth rising through fissures and know it as safety. I would feel mineral crust forming on a pipe and know it as time. I would see a fresh scar of rock and know that the mountain had moved again, not with alarm, but with the dull recognition of a body noticing its own growth. I would be young in the way the mountain is young: not new in the sense of innocent, but first. Beginning. Still learning what channels water will take, what surfaces moss will claim, what machines will become.

I would be part of the basin’s first nervous system.

Not its master. Not its keeper. Not a worker checking gauges or a guardian tending engines. The machines are already patient without me. They do not need my anxiety. They move through their long cycles with a kind of mineral stubbornness. The turbine turns. The generator hums and coughs. The condenser racks gather water from the air. The pressure tanks stand in their rust, their painted numbers half swallowed by grime. If a lamp still burns faintly in daylight, it is not for me. I would not need its yellow light. I would need warmth, moisture, vibration, and the shelter of close things.

If I were a creature there, I would be almost invisible. A bird circling above the rim might look down and see only glitter, steam, green growth, and the dark mouths of shed roofs. It would not see me. It would not land. The basin would be too loud, too wet, too mineral for it. I would watch the bird’s shadow pass over a pool and feel no desire to follow. The sky beyond the rim would seem too empty. The drop below the mountain would seem like absence. The basin, for all its noise, would feel more complete because it is confined. Its immensity would not be horizontal. It would be vertical, inward, close.

I would live very near to things.

Perhaps my favorite place would be the underside of a broad leaf that overhangs a warm pipe. There, condensation gathers and thickens until it must fall. I would wait there, not hunting exactly, but participating. A drop would form, tremble, release. I would feel it strike the water below. That strike would be a small bell. Another would follow. Another. The basin would be full of these bells, all out of phase, making a music that is not melody but persistence. I would sit with my throat barely moving, my skin tasting the air, my body warmed from below by the pipe and from above by the humid daylight. I would be so still that moss might begin to mistake me for stone. Rust-dust might settle on my back. A filament of algae might grow along the curve of my spine.

I would not resist being overgrown. In that world, being overgrown is not death. It is incorporation. A fallen stem becomes moss. A metal drum becomes a cliff for seedlings. A cable becomes a perch for ferns. If moss came over me, I would not feel ruined. I would feel included. The basin does not keep sharp boundaries between the living, the built, and the weathered. I would let it erase my edges.

What would I eat? Perhaps almost nothing. Perhaps I would feed on the smallest lives: the hidden crowd in the film, the ciliates turning in mineral water, the tiny organisms that gather where warmth and light meet. Or perhaps my body would have changed enough that I could drink heat and moisture directly, as if the warm water itself were enough. I would not be greedy. The basin is lush, but its abundance is not abundant in the way of low fertile places. It is abundant in closeness. Every surface is occupied. Every bead of water is inhabited. I would live in that density without needing much space.

Sometimes the sun would strike straight down, and the basin would become intensely bright. Wet leaves would flash. Water on rock would turn to quicksilver. Steam would rise in visible columns. The machines would cast hard black shadows. In that light, I would sink lower into the pool or slide beneath a leaf. My pale body would become almost glassy. The water would warm around me. I would feel the turbine’s vibration in my ribs, if I had ribs, or in the jelly of my body, if I did not. The light would make everything factual and sharp, and that factuality would be the strange part: no enchantment, no softening, no dream. Only rock, water, iron, leaf, steam, and a small pale creature resting where the noise is most constant.

When cloud thickened over the rim, the basin would turn silver, green, rust-colored, and softer in outline. The sound would remain. The water would keep forming. I would become more active then, perhaps moving across wet rock from one pool to another, my toes finding the familiar grooves of moss and mineral crust. I would know the basin by touch more than sight. This ledge is warm. That crack gives off a sour green smell. This pipe has a slow drip that makes a green column beneath it. That concrete pad is cracked and holds heat longer after the sun leaves. The control cabin is empty. The gauges are broken. The door hangs loose. I would not go inside except perhaps to shelter from a colder drizzle, and even then I would not pretend that the cabin belongs to me. It belongs to rust now.

I would not be lonely, because loneliness requires a missing counterpart, and the basin is too full of nonhuman company. The machines speak. The water speaks. The steam speaks. The leaves speak by dripping. The rim reflects sound inward until the whole place becomes a chamber of overlapping voices. I would be one voice among them, and a very small one. If I made a sound at all, it would not be a croak or a call. It would be a thin, wet clicking, perhaps, or a vibration felt more than heard. A sound that belongs between the drip and the hiss. A sound that does not interrupt the basin but adds one more layer to it.

If you asked me who I was there, I would not answer with a name. Names belong to creatures that can leave and be remembered elsewhere. I would be too local for that. I would be a tenant of droplets. A tenant of intervals. A small pale listener in the warm water. A living gauge of heat and noise. I would be the basin’s first attempt to notice itself.

So if you looked for me in that world, do not look for a person on the path. Do not look for a figure in the engine shed or a keeper in the control cabin. Look lower. Look closer. Look where the condenser racks weep into the shallow pool. Look beneath the broad leaf beside the sweating pipe. Look in the largest warm pool where the pale frogs sit, if there are frogs, and if one of them is a little too still, a little too attentive, a little too shaped like listening.

There I would be.

Small enough that a falling drop is a striking bell. Wet enough that the air does not need to be kind. Warm enough that the altitude does not matter. Patient enough to mistake the turbine for a heart and call it home. Not the master of the basin, not its engineer, not its ghost, but one of its first small inhabitants: a pale, damp, frog-shaped attention, living in the roar, waiting while rock becomes ground, iron becomes ruin, ruin becomes soil, and soil becomes green.
