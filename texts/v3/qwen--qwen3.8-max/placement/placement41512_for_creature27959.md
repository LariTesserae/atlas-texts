I would not enter the Dry Nave.

There is no entrance, and I would not force one. If I were there, I would have been sealed inside from the beginning, or I would have slipped through the only gate such a place allows: a flaw. A hairline mistake. A sound arriving with a shadow in it. A return that is not clean.

At first, I try to imagine myself as one of the listeners.

I try to place my body low along the terraces, pale as salt, patient as stone, palms pressed flat to the floor, face lifted as a bowl toward the dark. I try to imagine myself without eyes, without the old hunger to see where I am, without the need to turn every echo into a word. But that image feels too complete, too borrowed from the room’s own holiness. If I were simply one of them, I would have to have forgotten too much. I would have to have become pure maintenance, pure boundary, pure reverence. And I think there would still be something in me that remembers rivers.

So the truer image comes smaller.

It comes as a falling thing.

A grain slips from the vault. That is the first movement. But I would not be an innocent grain of grit, not a mere mineral speck falling because stone has tired of holding itself. I would be a grain with memory in it. A mote of salt that once, impossibly, knew wetness. A flake from the crust above carrying the faintest ghost of a vanished river. Something so small that it cannot threaten the Dry Nave, yet so different from the dust there that the room must notice it.

I would fall through the center.

Not stand there. Not claim it. The center is kept empty because it is the room’s open ear, the place where all returns are compared. To occupy it as a body would be violence. But a falling thing may pass through without violating it. For one brief moment, I would be the interruption allowed by reverence: a small dry comet crossing the nave’s hollow heart, trailing no light, only a change in the echo.

Below, the listeners would still.

Their bowl-faces would turn toward the sound. Their palms would press harder against the terraces. They would not flee. They would not worship. They would ask the room what had happened by letting the room answer. My fall would go down into the dark and strike stone with a tiny impact, no louder than a fingernail tapped once against chalk. Then the return would rise.

But the return would not be clean.

It would come back wearing a shadow. A thickness. A faint delay, as if the sound had passed through something that remembered being soft. The listeners would feel it in their ribs. They would hear it in the hollows of their chests. They would know that something had entered the Dry Nave without entering it.

That is where I would begin.

Not as a resident. Not as a pilgrim. As a fault in the echo.

After that, I think the room would place me.

There would be a crack near the center where the echo has always been weak: a thin seam, too fine to see, where the stone does not answer as it should. The listeners would know it by absence. They would know it the way one knows a missing tooth by the tongue’s return to the gap. My falling sound would expose it. Perhaps my grain would lodge there. Perhaps the elders would carry me there in their slow, low way, without haste, without ceremony except the ceremony of correct placement. They would lay me in the weak place because, in the Dry Nave, a fault is not removed. A fault is answered.

I would become the thing they begin there.

Not born. Begun.

They would hum over me for a long time. Years, perhaps. Decades. The sound would be so dry and narrow that it would seem less like singing than like stone learning to breathe. They would not feed me. They would tune me. They would send clicks into my hollow places and wait for what came back. They would teach me that I am not here to speak. I am here to answer.

At first, I would answer wrongly.

I would answer with too much. If I carried any trace of the surface into that sealed room, it would be this: a tendency to make meaning multiply. A sound would touch me and I would want to give it names. I would want to say *this is the wall*, *this is the vault*, *this is the terraced floor*, *this is the empty center*, *this is the memory of water*, *this is thirst*, *this is reverence*, *this is loss*. My first echo would return crowded. It would come back from me wearing too many ribbons, too many explanations. The room would hear the noise of the outside in me: the noise of rivers, voices, weather, movement, longing.

The listeners would correct me with stillness.

One would tap a knuckle once against stone.

The tap would go out and return simple. It would mean enough. It would mean *the shape remains*. It would mean *do not fill what should be hollow*.

So they would teach me to be brief.

They would teach me to let a sound pass through my ribs without breaking it into speech. They would teach me to make only accidental sounds: a click of the palate, a breath drawn backward through a mouth-crack, a single dry note struck in the throat and then surrendered to the room. I would learn that in the Dry Nave a sound is not completed when it is made. It is completed when it returns.

Over long ages, I would take on their shape.

Low, not tall. Vaulted in the back. Pale as the plain above. My skin, if it could still be called skin, would become a cracked mineral film, flexible only enough to prove that I was alive. My ribs would thin into arches so sound could move through me. My eyes would seal shut or never open. Where my face might have been, there would be a shallow listening-bowl, broad and still, tilted toward the nearest return. My palms would learn the floor’s answers better than sight ever could.

But I would not be exactly like the others.

There would be one difference in me, and the room would keep it.

One of my ribs would curve slightly wrong, as if shaped around an absent liquid. In the hollow of my chest there would be a resonance that returned sounds a fraction too late, not because I was broken, but because I carried a memory the Dry Nave had otherwise sealed away. Not water itself. No pool, no drip, no green impossible thing. Only the ghost-weight of water. The memory of rivers held inside mineral. The shape left by what vanished.

I would be the listener at the seam.

My place would be near the edge of the center, not in it. I would have learned enough reverence not to stand where all returns are compared. But I would be close enough that the room’s emptiness would press against me. I would rest in the weak crack where echoes thicken and slow, where the hidden seam gives back a delayed answer. The others would arrange themselves around me as one arranges oneself around a new piece of furniture in a dark room: not with alarm, but with attention. My presence would change the acoustics. They would adjust until the change became part of the nave.

Perhaps I would become the keeper of the unspoken outside.

Not the one who remembers water as hunger. That would be too human. The Dry Nave has passed beyond hunger directed outward. It does not pray for rain. It does not dream of rivers breaking through the crust above. Its holiness is maintenance. Its sacred phrase is not *give us*, but *the shape remains*. So if I carried the memory of water, I would carry it differently. I would carry it as evidence that dryness has a history. That absence can have structure. That what is sealed may still contain the imprint of what was sealed away.

When the others sent sounds into the room, I would answer with a shadow.

A click from one of the elders might reach me and return from my hollow chest slightly softened, as if it had touched something that once knew wetness. That would not mean *water is needed*.
