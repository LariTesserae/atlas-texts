Not words, but intervals.

If the inhabitants have a language, it begins there: in the spacing of pressure, in the pause between rasps, in the way a hollow tower’s note is answered not by another note but by a brief, deliberate absence. A click can mean little by itself. A click repeated can mean a road is active. A click repeated, then suddenly withheld, can mean something has changed the shape of the world.

In that dry country under stone, I think I would not be a visitor. I would not stand there as a human mind trapped in an alien body, looking for water, looking for fire, looking for signs. The place would refuse that kind of longing. If I belonged there, I would belong as the place belongs to itself: sealed, patient, dry, and woven into the long noise of the city.

I would be, I think, a keeper of pauses.

Not a builder, though I would live among builders. Not a gardener, though I would move through the fungal groves. Not one of the resonants, though my whole existence would depend on sound. I would be one of the creatures that keeps the city’s deafening life from becoming a single unbearable pressure. I would be a living dampening, a soft body placed against the stone where the chord grows too sharp.

You would find me in the hollow places.

Not in the brightest districts, if brightness can be said to exist there at all, and not at the centers of the densest towers. I would be found where sound gathers and needs to be shaped: in sunken courts where air pools and resonates; at the mouths of breathing passages; inside old towers whose walls have become too thin; beneath bridges of fused mycelium where vibration doubles as it crosses from one structure to another; along the margins of nurseries, where larvae need less shuddering in the walls; in the sparse intervals between districts, where the noise from one cluster of towers travels over empty dust and returns from stone.

I would be especially present at thresholds.

Where a hollow tower meets older, solid stone. Where a vent has begun to sing too continuously. Where a district’s chord has grown so full that the fine dust on the avenues starts to arrange itself into rings. Where the resonants have gathered too thickly and the air becomes a wall of stridulation. There I would go, slow and flat and pale, pressing my body into seams and gaps, becoming a living baffle.

I would be small.

No larger than the spread of two hands, perhaps, and lower than that when at rest. My body would be flattened, dry, and wax-coated, covered in a faint mineral bloom so that I would look less like an animal than like a piece of the city that has learned to move. My color would be bone-white gone grey with dust, with here and there a dull amber staining where fungal spores have settled on me and remained. I would not shine brightly. If I emitted light at all, it would only be a weak blue-green trace along the seams of my shell when I was touched or startled, a glow that vanished almost before it could be noticed.

I would have no true eyes.

At most, I would carry dull patches able to sense whether the dimness around me had grown slightly brighter or darker. My real organs would be elsewhere: long, feathery antennae; stiff hairs along my limbs; membranes stretched thin across my sides and thorax, set into dry frames like parchment. I would read the city through vibration the way surface creatures read weather through light. I would know a crowded avenue by its continuous fine shiver, a hollow tower by its long resonance, a grove by the tiny crackle of spore cases opening, a predator by the sudden wrongness of a silence that had no reason to be there.

I would not drink.

I would carry my water as all creatures there carry it: bound, hidden, sealed inside tissue and slow chemistry. There would be no dampness on my surface, no gloss, no bead of sweat, no sign of thirst. My body would preserve its inner inheritance by doing little quickly. My movements would be slow enough to seem almost ceremonial, though not because I possessed any notion of ceremony. They would be slow because haste would waste what could not be replaced.

My work would be quiet-making.

But not silence, exactly. Silence in that country would be rare, perhaps impossible, and perhaps dangerous if it became too complete. The city must carry sound. Its towers must ring. Its resonants must signal. Its air passages must breathe through hollow stone. The problem is not noise itself. The problem is noise without distinction: the chord becoming so total that nothing within it can be heard, the vibration becoming so continuous that walls begin to fatigue, that larvae cease to orient, that the subtle gaps marking danger or change are lost beneath the general roar.

I would make room inside the sound.

My body would be adapted for this. The membranes along my sides would take in vibration the way a dry seed case might take in heat: slowly, diffusely, until the energy passed into me and was dispersed into faint warmth or converted into bodily response. Where a particular note grew too strong, I would seek it out. I would press myself into the seam from which it issued. I would fold my limbs close. I would become dense and still. My membranes would shudder with the offending resonance, and that shuddering would stimulate glands within me to secrete a soft, dry, fibrous substance: part wax, part silk, part mineral dust bound in body salts.

I would not carry tools. I would not build with devices. I would build with my own body and with what my body could make.

Over time, I would line dangerous cracks with that secretion. I would soften the inner lips of vents that had begun to whistle too sharply. I would thicken the walls of towers that rang beyond endurance. I would create small pockets of muted air inside the greater noise. These pockets would not be empty. They would still contain the deep pressure of the city, the distant rasp of crickets, the hollow breath of stone, the whisper of fungal stems. But within them, sound would lose its cutting edge. It would become bearable again. It would become interpretable.

In that sense, I would be a listener more than a maker.

I would know the city not as a map of visible streets and glowing towers, but as a field of tensions. I would know which districts were overfilled with resonants, which hollow towers had become too musical, which plains were carrying vibration too cleanly from one built island to another. I would know when the air was turning from one set of stone passages to another, because the whole pressure of the country would shift by a fraction, and certain old notes would lengthen. I would know when a predator moved through a thicket because the ordinary noise would not merely stop; it would develop a shape around its stopping.

I would have to learn which silences to preserve.

That would be the subtlety of my existence. Not all quiet is good. Not all interruption is beneficial. A gap in the sound may mean that danger is near. A sudden absence of wing-noise may mean that blind, quick things are hunting under the mats. A hush in a nursery wall may mean that something has entered where it should not. My work would not be to fill every quiet place. It would be to know the difference between a pause that gives meaning and a pause that announces harm.

So I would be neither loud nor wholly silent.

I would carry a small vocabulary of my own, if vocabulary is even the right word. Perhaps it would be this: a faint tapping of forelimbs against stone, followed by stillness. Another tap, then a longer absence. A pressure against a wall, then withdrawal. A weak glow along my shell, then darkness. Meaning would not reside in the signal alone, but in the interval after it. I would say, in the only way such a creature could say anything: here, and then here no longer. This, and then the lack of this. Attend now, because what follows is not random.

If the city had language, I would be one of its punctuation marks.

Not its voice. Not its grammar, perhaps. But the small living sign that a phrase has ended, or that a new one may begin.

I would not be solitary in the way surface solitude is understood. There would be others like me, though perhaps not many. We would not gather in crowds. Too many pause-makers in one place would deaden the city beyond usefulness. We would be distributed according to need, drawn by resonance as gardeners are drawn to spore-rich caps or builders to worn seams.
