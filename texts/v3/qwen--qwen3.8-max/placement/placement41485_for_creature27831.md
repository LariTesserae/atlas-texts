I would not find myself at the center of the roar.

If I belonged to that country at all, I would begin as a local easing of pressure: a small pocket of lessness behind a rounded ridge, where the sound has been broken just enough for breath to become possible. Not silence. Silence there would be a trap, a held breath before the next blow. But a lull — a thin interval between booms, a pause in the great acoustic weather — that would be the first place I could call mine.

When the image steadies, I am in a shallow hollow on the lee side of a low basalt swell, one of those broad, ancient rises like the back of some creature buried long before the world learned to name bones. The hollow is not deep enough to become a drum. It has been shaped by so much time that it does not amplify; it deflects. Above me the sky is ordinary blue, almost brutally clear. Below the horizon, clouds move like slow white animals over lower lands, but here the air is warm, damp, unsettled, carrying that smell of wet stone and green sap and high wind that has traveled a long way without arriving anywhere.

My shelter is not a house. It is a negotiation with the ground.

There is a low windbreak of fitted stone, old and partly collapsed, rebuilt often enough to remain useful but never made proud. Behind it, a shallow depression has been scraped and widened into a listening hollow, lined with packed clay and mats of woven reed. A row of clay resonators sits half-buried along the edge, each one the size of a skull or a swollen seed-pod, each tuned to a different pitch. Some are cracked. Some have been sealed and abandoned. Others tremble faintly when the pressure changes, giving off a thin, tooth-felt buzz before the larger sound arrives.

I would be there in that place, and I would be low.

The plateau would not permit me height. If it shaped me, it would press me closer to itself. My body would be broad and compact, dense-boned, with wide feet and wide hands whose palms are mapped with pressure sense. I would not have delicate ears. I would have small closable canals, protected by flaps of skin, because hearing in the open is a kind of violence. My true ears would be my hands, my sternum, the soles of my feet, the fine bones of my jaw. I would kneel often, not in worship, but in attention. To touch the stone is to listen before the ears can lie.

I would wear layers of padded fiber and hide, not only for warmth or wetness, but to mute the body’s own noise and protect it from vibration. Moss-felt over the shoulders. A hood that covers the sides of the head. Straps crossed over the chest, carrying small things: cord, clay pellets, a curved knife, resin, a skin of clean condensate, a few smooth plugs of wax for the worst passages. Nothing heavy. Nothing that can become a sail, a drumhead, or a burden. Everything must either be useful in a lull or left behind.

If the world asked what I am, the nearest answer would be: a lull-walker. A hollow-keeper. One of the pause-born.

Not a ruler of that place. Not a settler. Not even truly a resident in the way lower lands understand residence. I would be a seasonal presence, an intermittent one, a life that appears when the conditions permit and withdraws when they turn. I would follow the acoustic seasons as other peoples follow rivers or stars. I would know which basins fill after fog tides, which stone pipes clear their throats before a storm, which ridges cast useful acoustic shadows at different hours, and which depressions become beautiful and deadly when the air turns sweet.

My work would be listening.

Not listening as contemplation, though there would be contemplation enough. Listening as labor. As navigation. As survival. Each morning, if morning is a word that can exist between booms, I would test the air with my body before moving. I would place a hand flat on the rock and wait for the grain of it. Is the stone merely warm? Is it ticking with expansion? Is there a deep water-hammer moving under it? Is the pitch rising in the nearby slits? Are the reed brakes keening in a single direction, or are they confused? Is the pressure pulling dust into ripples? Is the fog arriving as a wall or as a thin breath?

Only after that would I move.

I would maintain the resonators first. I would tap each one with a knuckle or a soft mallet and feel how it answers. If one gives a clear, sweet note, I mark it with a knot of red cord and prepare to leave that camp. Sweetness is warning. Sweetness means the plateau has found a new throat. I would not stay to admire it. I would gather what can be carried, seal the cistern cups, loosen no stone, and move along the rope line toward the next shelter before the tone becomes beautiful enough to kill.

I would also tend the ropes. Across the exposed flats, where fog can erase the world in a single surge, there are old lines anchored in stone rings. Some are frayed. Some have been buried by dust and vibrating grains. I would clear them, splice them, replace knots, add new markers that can be felt by touch. These ropes are not property. They are agreements between strangers who may never meet. Whoever comes after me will follow them in blindness, hands moving over knots, counting intervals, trusting that someone before them cared enough to leave a path.

I would gather water when it appears, but carefully.

When a basin fills, the whole district changes. The stone darkens. Moss lifts. Seeds that have waited as black specks in dry cups suddenly swell and open into green carpets. The translucent pool-life rise in their quick, brief lives, membranes trembling with the sky reflected in them. I would not disturb them if I can help it. They are too much like the plateau’s own thoughts: thin, pressure-sensitive, almost without color, born into an hour of water and leaving behind eggs that can endure decades of absence.

I would kneel at the edge of such a pool and drink from a cup-shaped plant before the basin water becomes thick with life. I would collect condensate from broad leaves and hanging root-mats, because that water is cleaner and less likely to carry the chemical memory of old stone. I might harvest moss where it grows thick enough to spare, and tuberous roots in damp folds, and resin from low plants whose leaves beat without tearing in the wind. I would take very little. The plateau punishes appetite that becomes habit.

There would be days when I am almost not human in the usual sense.

If I truly belong there, I would carry some of the pool-life in my ancestry, or at least in my shape. I would not be born in a house or a city, but in a temporary pool during one of the rare long fillings. My earliest form might be soft, flat, translucent, all edge and pressure-sense, knowing the world as vibration before knowing it as object. When the water began to fail, I would thicken. My skin would become tougher. My limbs would find the stone. I would learn to move not through water but through intervals of sound.

Such a being would know patience differently.

I could seal myself into a damp chamber when the weather turns too dry or too loud. I could slow my breathing, lower my heart, become almost indistinguishable from a wet stone in a dark hollow. I could wait there for hours, days, perhaps longer, dreaming in rhythm: boom, pause, hiss, pause, knock, pause. My thoughts would not be continuous in the way lowland minds imagine thought. They would come in bouts and recoveries, like pools. When the fog tide returns, my skin would wake first. The pressure would change. The moss would smell green. I would open my eyes into the dark and know: now. Move now.

But even in such a body, I would still be a keeper of small signs.

That is who I would become: not a hero of the plateau, not a conqueror, not a prophet of its roar, but an attendant to its pauses. My knowledge would be local, bodily, and humble. I would know the exact angle at which to lie flat when a pressure wave rolls across a basin. I would know how to breathe through the chest rather than the mouth when the air becomes too loud to hold. I would know that if fine dust begins to hop into neat ripples near the mouth of a stone pipe, the pipe is about to speak, and I should be far from its lips. I would know that if a pool trembles with concentric rings though no wind touches it, something below is shifting its weight.

I would communicate rarely, and never by shouting.
