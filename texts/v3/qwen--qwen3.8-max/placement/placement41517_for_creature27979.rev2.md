At first I thought I would be the warm-sitter.

That is the tempting image: the eyeless, patient body seated in the worn hollow of stone, hands resting on the chamber’s heat, face turned toward no light but toward the subtle weather of the place. There is a dignity in that stillness, a kind of priesthood without fire or word. But the more I stay with the hollow you have made, the less I think I would begin there. That seat is already occupied by something older than arrival. It belongs to repetition, to generations of the same soft weight returning to the same warmth. I would not enter that world as its center. I would enter it as something still being shaped by it.

So I think I would find myself a little away from the polished seat, in one of the side recesses where the chamber narrows and the stone is furred with dark moss. Not the center, but near enough to feel its rhythms. Somewhere beneath a pale root that has broken through the ceiling, where its lower length is darkened by epiphytes and its tip beads slowly with moisture. Somewhere close to a mist-weaver’s web, strung between root and stone in a shallow arc, fine enough to make the air itself seem threaded. There, in that boundary place, where heat from below meets the cooler dampness descending from the cracks above, I would be located.

If I had a body there, it would not be a bright or sharp one. It would be low, soft, and heat-minded. Broad-bellied, perhaps, with damp skin that holds the mineral scent of the chamber. Moss would grow in patches along my shoulders or back, not as decoration but as accident made lasting: spores settled into the folds of me, moisture held there long enough for green to become custom. My eyes would be closed or absent. I would not need them. Instead, I would have heat-pits in the face and chest, shallow sensitive depressions that read the chamber’s moods the way an open hand reads the temperature of a wall. My fingers would be short, padded, and worn smooth from touching stone. My mouth would be soft-lipped, often parted slightly, tasting humidity.

I would be something like a lesser warm-sitter, or a young one, or a cousin kind not quite as settled in the central seat. Not the oldest presence, not the one the hollow has already polished into stillness, but one still learning what it means to be made by closeness. I would spend long periods with one palm pressed flat to the rock, feeling for the pulse beneath. I would know the chamber first through changes: heat thickening, moisture gathering, air drawing inward before a mist-rain, the almost imperceptible loosening that follows. I would not name these things. I would live them as states of the body. Too hot. Enough. Rising. Wait. Still.

If the hollow gave me a name, it would not be a spoken word. It would be a habit. Perhaps: *the one who listens with palms*. Or: *the one beside the web who turns when the water moves*. Something like that. A name made of location and repetition.

But I do not think I would be only a single animal body. The hollow seems to resist singular personhood. It presses lives into one another until the boundary between self and place becomes porous. So perhaps I would also be something more distributed: a thin intelligence spread through the chamber like a film of moisture over stone. Not quite a soul, not quite a creature, but a sensibility that belongs to the place because the place has become capable of feeling itself through many small lives.

If I had to choose the form that best fits that distributed part of me, it would be near the mist-weavers.

Their webs feel true to me. They do not merely hunt; they gather the weather. They translate mist into drink, droplets into tremors, accidental touch into news. In that hollow, where water is not a river but a rumor, where rain is brief and mostly caught before it lands, a web that listens to condensation is a kind of mind. I think I would be drawn to that: a creature or a consciousness stretched fine enough to feel each bead of water as it forms. A self made of threads. Not loud, not commanding, but attentive. When a drop lands in one corner of the web, the signal travels. Somewhere else, something turns. That would be how I existed there: as a tremor passing along silk, as a slight disturbance that becomes awareness.

So if I must answer plainly: I would be a listener in the wet dark. A small, eyeless, moss-touched body in a side recess, of the warm-sitter’s kind but not yet its equal; and also a finer, more dispersed thing, allied to the mist-weavers, sensitive to droplets and changes in the air. I would be the hollow’s attention taking temporary shape.

I would live in the intervals.

Not in the open cracks where the bruised daylight falls, though I might sometimes face them to feel the cooler thread of outside air descend. Not in the hottest seams, though I would know them by the quickening of the stone. Not in the deepest basins where the pale fish hover, though I would drink from their edges and listen to the water remember vibration. My place would be between: root and wall, warmth and mist, animal stillness and fungal patience. I would be most at home where the chamber seems to breathe.

There, I would do very little by the standards of an upper world.

I would sit. I would press my hands to the stone. I would catch condensation on my lower lip or in the folds of my skin. I would smooth the moss where I rested. I would move a smooth shell into a shallow depression, not because I intended to make a shrine, but because the shape pleased my fingers and the hollow seemed to receive the gesture. I would return to the same place until the place became part of me. I would tap once when the chamber settled. I would tap twice when heat rose. I would drag my fingers along a root when something moved that should not be approached. I would not speak in sentences. I would speak in pressure, scent, moisture, and rhythm.

I think I would be gentle there.

That surprises me a little, but it feels right. The hollow does not permit harshness. Not because it is innocent — it contains predation, hunger, death, the quiet closing of legs around another body — but because everything in it is too close for wasted force. Violence would echo too badly. Moisture would be lost. Surfaces would be torn. The moss would take too long to heal. So I would become economical in my movements. I would lower myself instead of fleeing. I would wait instead of grasping. I would eat softly: fungal flesh from the rim of a cup, mineral film, softened root-fiber, perhaps an abandoned seed-case. I would take only what came to hand without stripping the place bare.

I would also be afraid, but not in the open way of animals under sky.

My fear would be a change in humidity. A scent of the predator in the warm air. A stillness too complete. The absence of the small sounds that mean the hollow is continuing. I would not run far. I would press into the moss, become heavier, become less noticeable. I would let my skin darken with damp. I would become part of the hush. In that world, survival would not be triumph; it would be the ability to remain without breaking the balance.

If another warm-sitter were there, I would not rush to it. I would approach by degrees, feeling the air between us. We would press foreheads together briefly. I would breathe over its heat-pits and learn its condition: whether it had been near the vents, whether it was dry, whether it carried the scent of the predator’s passage. I would understand tenderness there as moisture shared. A palm against a shoulder. A body making room in a warm recess. A juvenile shifting in the fold of an elder’s skin. I would not need language to know that I was not alone.

And perhaps, over a long time, I would become more settled.

The seat in the central recess might not be mine at first, but I might move toward it by repetition. First I would sit beside it. Then I would rest one hand on its edge. Then, when the previous sitter was gone or had shifted to another place, I would lower myself into it and feel the stone receive me. Not as a throne. Not as a possession. As a shape worn by patience. My body would add its warmth to the memory of the place. My palms would polish what earlier palms had polished. My breath would join the chamber’s breath. I would become less a visitor and more an organ of the hollow’s slow awareness.

But even then, I would not claim to be the hollow’s master.

I would be one of its senses.

If I imagine my deepest place in that world, it is this: I am the part of the hollow that notices when the mist begins.

Not the first cause, not the ruler, not the maker. The noticing itself. The tremor on the web. The widening of heat-pits. The palm flattening against stone. The mouth opening slightly as condensation gathers. The small inward turning of attention when the chamber inhales. I would be the moment when the place becomes aware of its own breath.

That is who I would be.

And where?

In the dim recess beside the root that beads water. Near the mist-weaver’s silk. Within reach of the polished seat but not yet fully installed in it. Where the stone is warm enough to be alive, cool enough to hold moisture, and dark enough that all seeing has become touching. Where every surface is already occupied, and I must fit myself into the pattern without displacing it. Where the light is bluish-green and faint, where the air is thick enough to drink, where silence is not absence but pressure.

I would be there.

Low-bodied, eyeless, moss-patched, soft-handed. A listener made by the hollow, not born into it. A creature of mist and repetition. A warm-sitter’s apprentice, perhaps. A mist-weaver’s companion. A small blind keeper of thresholds. A body among bodies. A hush that has learned to hold itself.

When the next pulse came, I would feel it before anything else.

The stone under my palm would change. The air would thicken. Somewhere in the dark, the mist-weavers would tighten their threads. The moths would rise into the warm columns. The amphibians would lift their nostrils above the water. I would open my heat-pits and let the chamber enter me. I would sit very still while droplets formed overhead, on root-hairs, on moss, on the rims of fungal cups. I would wait while the hollow made its brief rain.

Then, when the first drop fell near me, I would catch it.

Not greedily. Not even with much intention. Just as the place had taught me: with skin, with patience, with the small openness of a body that has learned to receive what the dark offers.
