They gather around it, and I am among them.

Not as a stranger who has come from below with boots and hard intentions, but as one of the Softstep, already barefoot, already listening, already part of the plain’s slow metabolism. If I found myself in the High Sward, I would not stand outside it in wonder for long. The place would ask too much of that. It would ask me to step softly, to stop announcing myself against the world, and to become instead a small, attentive pressure within it.

So I would be found where the ground is neither too dry nor too wet, on one of those shallow blisters of living felt between a rain-basin and a long, sun-warmed rise. Not at any dramatic edge, because there are no dramatic edges there; not beside a cliff or beneath a tree, because cliffs and trees do not belong to that country. I would be near the whistle-stems, where pale green pipes rise from the margins of the pools and make their hollow calling when the air moves over them. I would know that place by sound before sight: the constant root-drone under everything, the clickers breaking into brief crackles after rain, the reedy sweetness of the stems, and now and then the sudden lift of burstwings rising as if the ground had remembered wings.

I would be one of the Softstep.

Not the strongest in the band, perhaps, nor the one who walks farthest in a single day. I would be one of the listeners, one of those who put their feet still and let the world come to them through the ground. My body would be made for that life: slender, deep-chested for the thin high air, long-legged but unhurried, with broad feet and thick, sensitive soles capable of telling rain from old rain, health from beginning-sickness, a herd passing from a herd resting. My toes would have learned to part the sward without wounding it. My skin would be green-brown or faded gold, marked with pale healed lines from small scratches received in tending. My hair would be fibrous, like soft dried grass, and in time the elders might allow me to wear living filaments in it, tiny threads of the sward itself taking root where I had walked long enough beneath the sun.

My name would not be a word spoken casually in the air.

It would be a rhythm: two light footfalls, a pause, and then a low hum from the chest, felt through the ground more than heard. If someone far off wished to call me, they would not shout. They would tap the staff, press the heel, send the pattern through the felt. I would know my own name coming through the plain the way a person in another world might know their name spoken in a quiet room.

If I had to say what work I would do there, I would say I would be a mender of sour places.

The High Sward is generous, but it is not invincible. It can thicken, sour, close over itself, ferment wrongly in patches where air and water no longer move as they should. I would be one of those who notices early. I would feel it first as a wrongness under the sole: not heat, not cold, but a kind of slackness, a deadened note in the living body of the ground. The root-stridulators would still be sounding around it, but their drone would seem thinner there, less continuous. The clickers might avoid the nearest pool. The burstwings would pass over without settling. To an unpracticed eye, the patch might look like any other stretch of sward, only a little darker, perhaps a little too soft after rain. To me, it would feel like a held breath.

The band would gather around it.

We would not dig. We would not cut. We would stand in a loose circle, staffs planted, feet listening. One of us would hum low through the chest, not to command the ground but to answer it. Another would kneel and part the upper blades with patient fingers, finding the felt beneath. I would carry a pouch of hardened seed-cases, polished resin beads, fine cord twisted from fiber, and perhaps a small sealed vessel of clean water taken from a healthy pool. My staff would be made from a lignified stem, darkened with use, its tip worn smooth by pressure and travel. It would not be a weapon. It would be a voice sent downward.

We would work slowly. We would open channels where thatch-stalkers had already made hidden paths, encouraging air to move again. We would press sweet filaments into places where the felt had gone slick and dark. We would place resin nodules where fermentation needed to be calmed, not killed. We might call the corkbacks near, if the land required their mouths, because their grazing opens the upper growth and lets the lower layers breathe. We would watch for the first sign of recovery: a change in smell, a return of small sounds, the ground beginning once more to recover from our footprints with its old tired-flesh slowness instead of holding them like wounds.

That is where I would find myself most clearly: in the middle of such tending, with my hands in the warm felt and the plain speaking upward through my bones.

But I would not always be solemn about it. There would be hours of simple, astonishing beauty.

In the mornings after rain, the High Sward would be so loud with life that I might have to laugh just to bear it. The clickers would be crackling in the shallow bowls, their polished plant-mineral shells flashing as they struck hollow seed-cases together. The pools would be soft green bowls with living bottoms, their edges fringed with hollow stems, and thin-winged larvae would rise from them in trembling clouds. The root-stridulators would be hidden everywhere beneath my feet, blind and armored in seed-cases, making the plain’s high constant note without ever intending to be heard as individuals. I would walk through that sound and feel myself surrounded not by a wilderness, but by a living interior, as if I had been admitted into the body of some vast, warm, patient creature.

At noon, when shadows sharpened and cumulus towers began building over the plain, I would sometimes stop and stand very still while cloud-shadow crossed me. The burstwings would rise then. The ground would seem to open. Thousands of them would lift into the light with pale grass-veined wings, their bodies warm-colored underneath, and for a moment the empty country would reveal how full it had always been. I would not chase them. I would only stand there with my staff and feel the air change as they moved. If I were very still, some might settle near me, mistaking my fibrous hair or the woven cape across my shoulders for dried leaf-sheath or seed-case. That would please me more than I can say.

I would learn the corkbacks with reverence.

At first I might step onto what seemed a low rise and feel it breathe. After that, I would walk differently. I would know them as fellow gardeners, not beasts to be feared or owned. Their broad backs would carry living sward; mossy fibers and seedlings would take root in the crevices of their shells. They would move with such patience that the ground seemed to rearrange itself gently around them. Their mouths would crop the upper growth without tearing it, opening the felt so it would not smother itself. Their dung would be warm nurseries of seed and filament. When a herd rested, the sound of them would be deep and wooden, like a house settling in sunlight.

If the band ever had to take a corkback in ritual necessity, I would be among those who sang the apologies. I would not pretend to understand all of it. I would only know that taking such a creature must be done with grief, gratitude, and the awareness that the plain becomes quieter when something is taken wrongly. That quiet would be my true fear. Not punishment, not retaliation, but silence. A High Sward that had gone silent would be worse than any ruin.

Sometimes I would look up and long for the bladderkites.

In the afternoon heat, when the plain sent columns of warm air upward beneath the clouds, they would ascend: translucent gas-bladders swollen by fermentation, trailing rootlets fine as hair, broad sensory fringes moving slowly as they fed on spores and organic dust. Some would be small as lanterns; some would cast shadows over the whole walking band. Their sigh would pass overhead, hollow and reedy, and I would feel my chest answer it. If I could not be a Softstep, I think I would have wanted to be one of them—drifting with immense deliberation, belonging more to air than ground, feeding on what the world released upward.

But I would know my place was below, not above.

Even so, I would mark the sky-gifts. Where a bladderkite had descended and not risen again, the sward would make a raised oval of richer growth. I would learn to recognize those places by their color and by the way sound bent around them. They would be sacred without being set apart. They would teach me that the High Sward does not waste what falls into it. It takes in rain, wing, seed, filament, memory, and even the sky’s slow creatures, folding them into root and felt. I would try to become someone who could be taken in like that, without resistance, when my own season ended.

At evening the band would make camp, but we would not build it in the ordinary sense. We would walk the chosen place first, pressing the ground with bare feet, listening. Then we would persuade the sward into usefulness. We would part blades, encourage low wind-lips of living fiber, shape sleeping hollows by weight and repetition. My own hollow would form over several nights, warmed to the exact curve of my body. I would lie there wrapped in a felt-fiber cape, with a belt of braided root around my waist and a small rattle of polished seed-beads tied near my hand. There would be no fire. The thought of fire there would feel obscene. Instead there would be the sward’s own stored heat, the faint fermented smell of the hollows breathing out after sunset, and the high drone continuing in the dark like the world’s own pulse.

We would eat attentively: young shoots, resin nodules, sweet seed-paste, pool-eggs, edible filaments, perhaps a small water-creature taken with care. Food would soften in sealed seed-vessels using heat already given by sun or compost. Nothing would be wasted. Nothing would be taken for display. Even our ornaments would be sound-makers, because in the High Sward beauty seems to belong more to vibration than to possession. I would wear a whistle carved from a hollow stem, and perhaps a single resin bead that caught the light like captured afternoon. When I moved, I would make only small sounds. When I spoke, my voice would carry in the layered way of the Softstep: air-language above, ground-language below.

That is the self I would become there: not a hero, not a founder, not a conqueror of open land, but a listener and tender. A person whose first task is to notice. A person whose deepest discipline is restraint.

If I were very lucky, I would grow old in that life. My hair would become threaded with living filaments. My feet would read the plain with such ease that I could stand still on a quiet afternoon and hear for leagues: a corkback herd moving east, a pool filling after rain, a thatch-stalker shifting under loose litter, the slow creak of a ground-blister warming in the sun. Children would learn my rhythm before they learned my name. I would send messages through the felt with my staff, not because I had many words, but because I had learned which words the ground would carry.

And when asked at last who or what I was in the High Sward, I would not answer simply that I was human, or even that I was Softstep. I would say I was one of the plain’s own ways of paying attention.

I would be found in the warm open quiet, near the whistle-stems, with my staff planted in living ground. I would be two light footfalls, a pause, and a low hum. I would be the pause after the hum: the moment when the sward listens back.
