I would not find myself at the horizon.

There would be no place for me to stand apart from it, no vantage where the wet plain arranged itself into a picture. I would not be a traveler looking out over the flatness, because the flatness would already be inside the first sensation of me. Before sight, before thought, there would be pressure. The ground would know my weight before I knew I had feet. Water would move toward the place where I touched it. The packed shells would shift by the smallest possible amount and carry that shift outward, under the films, under the pools, under the thin trembling skins of the world.

At first, I think I would be the interruption itself.

Not a body yet. Not a creature. Just a wrongness in the saturation: a density too sharp, a warmth too localized, a pressure too suddenly applied. The plain would feel me as an unabsorbed fact. Something had arrived that had not yet been incorporated. A footprint would be forming even as I imagined taking a step, the water rising around its edges, the sediment already beginning to remember a shape I had not finished giving it.

If I looked down, I would not see a clean reflection. The water would give me back late, or incompletely, or with a movement that belonged to someone else. It might show me a brighter sky than the one pressing down overhead. It might show no face at all, only a darkening where a face expected to be. That would be the first clue: I had not entered a place that could be observed from outside. I had entered a condition that was already translating me into its own textures.

So I would not be the observer of that world.

I would be the world’s answer to an unexpected pressure.

But that answer would need more than one form. It would not settle immediately. If I let the question remain open, several possible selves rise out of the plain, each one trying to become plausible.

One of me is a cyst.

This is the smallest and oldest possibility. I am a tough grain of latency lodged between two shells in the pavement. I have no face, no motion, no ambition. I am only compressed continuation. I can endure burial, heat, crushing, long chemical darkness. I do not wait with hope. I wait without intention. When the local water changes, when the pressure eases, when some film passes over me and offers the right chemistry, I will reopen. I will not remember having been dormant. I will simply continue. In that form, I would be almost nothing—and yet one of the most essential things there. The plain is full of such patience. It does not need a mind to persist. It only needs a sealed possibility.

Another me is a pale worm.

This one is thinner, almost transparent, moving through the upper films like a thought the water has not decided to keep. I have no eyes. I do not need them. I read the world as ripple, as chemical slope, as the faint difference between one bacterial skin and another. I graze without destroying wholly. I pass over living surfaces and leave them changed but not ruined. If I am torn, I do not experience it as catastrophe. Each fragment goes on. I am not precious to myself as a single shape. This too feels possible: to be a soft, divisible line of motion, a pale repair passing through the turbid light.

Another me is one of the ray-like bodies gliding over the crusts.

This one is larger, mostly water, hydraulic and silent. It moves by spreading and contracting, its underside tasting the surface, its margins translucent enough to blur into the haze. When it passes over a disturbed mat, the cold glow trails beneath it like a slow halo. It does not hunt. It does not flee. It exists as a broad, patient event. I can imagine being that: a quiet glide, a feeding pressure, a body so permeable that the boundary between myself and the wet plain is only a habit.

But if I let the world press harder on the question—if I ask not merely what living thing I might resemble, but where I would truly find myself—then the answer becomes more specific.

I would not be among the vast ones in the deep sediment. They are too slow, too immense, too geological. Their being unfolds over intervals that would make my thoughts irrelevant. To be one of them would be to mistake the surface for something that no longer matters. I would not be among the drifting air-membranes either. They are too fragile, too subject to rain and rupture, too easily scattered into droplets and lost.

I would be in the middle region.

I would be where the shell-pavement is dense enough to bear weight but wet enough to answer it. Where the ground is neither open water nor stable land, but the trembling compromise between them. Where every step produces a dry mineral chatter under the film of water. Where heat comes up through narrow throats and makes local weather only a few meters wide. Where temporary pools form, reflect the wrong sky, tighten, and vanish. Where old marks are half sunk into mud: partial rings of fitted stones, hollow cones, low domes already being reabsorbed.

There. In the saturated middle. At the seam.

And if I had to become a single being there, I would not be upright. I would not have a face in the human sense. I would not hold tools. I would not stand above the plain as a person stands above a floor.

I would be low.

Broad and low, close to the size of a child’s body if compressed flat, though not truly a body in the old way. More a thickened region of living matter. My upper surface would be slick and dark, crusted with shell fragments and fine sediment so that I looked like a moving patch of ground. When disturbed, I would glow faintly, not with intention, but because my living films could not help answering pressure with light. My underside would be absorbent, fringed with fine filaments that tasted mud, heat, salt, decay, and the chemical residues of other lives. I would have no eyes. Instead I would have pits sensitive to warmth, pores that read changes in pressure, membranes stretched over internal water chambers that could feel vibration traveling through the packed grains.

I would move by redistribution. Not legs. Not fins. A slow internal shifting of water and tissue. I would grip the pavement with secreted films and flow forward in pulses, flattening myself when turbulence passed, thickening when I needed to hold a place. I could squeeze through narrow spaces by becoming less like an animal and more like a liquid held together by memory. I could remain still long enough to be partly buried without dying. If torn, I would not necessarily perish. My vital functions would be layered, distributed, cyst-bearing. I would carry my own continuation folded into my substance.

I would be one of the plain’s seam-people.

Perhaps that is not a name but a condition. A seam-being. A pore-listener. A mender of surfaces. One of the low, soft inhabitants who do not build with tools but with secretion, pressure, repetition, and time.

I would live near a heat-throat.

Not inside the hottest center, but just beyond it, where the exhalation warms the water enough to make chemistry rich but not lethal. There would be a narrow mud-and-shell rim nearby, built by colonial lives over long repetition, its shape subtly altering the hiss of vapor. I would know that throat the way a body knows a wound or a mouth: by the way pressure changed around it, by the way vapor curled upward, by the way local rain began and ended in cells only a few steps wide.

Near my place there would be a partial ring of fitted stones.

Not a ruin. Not a monument. A pattern that had held. Perhaps it focused vibration. Perhaps it eased some turbulence. Perhaps it had been made by many small bodies pressing themselves into place over generations, or perhaps it had been the resting posture of someone who had hardened into shape and then slowly returned to the plain. I would not know its history as a story. I would know it as density. Some stones would be more resonant than others. Some would hold warmth differently. Some would be colonized by tougher crusts. I would maintain the ring without meaning to, simply by moving over it, pressing loose grains into gaps, adding film where a crack had begun, secreting minerals where the water asked for them.

There would also be a pool.

It would not last. It would form from rising water when pressure changed, perhaps from a burrower moving below, perhaps from a breath of one of the vast ones so distant that the effect arrived without an obvious cause. The pool would lie shallow over the crust, trembling faintly. It would reflect something that was not the present sky: a brighter weather, an older light, a memory the plain had not finished digesting. I would not be frightened by that. In this world, a reflection that arrived late or wrongly would be no more alarming than a shadow moving behind a cloud. It would simply be one of the ways the surface spoke.

In that place, I would be a listener.

Not a listener with ears. A listener with my whole lower surface. I would feel the shell-pavement conduct sound faster than ordinary thought. I would know when a gas pocket was about to release because the grains around it would change their tension. I would know when a mat had torn nearby because the chemical signature would arrive in the water before the visible wound did. I would know when something large was moving far below because the pressure would alter in a way that had no local source. I might respond to a disturbance before its apparent cause reached me. That would not feel strange. It would feel like living.

My mind, if I had one, would not be a mind of separate objects.

It would be a mind of gradients. Of pressure. Of saturation. Of repair. I would not think in sentences. I would think in adjustments: open here, seal there, ease this throat, reinforce this crust, let this pool drain, hold this film until the tremor passes. I would not ask what things were for. I would ask, through my whole body, how they could be continued.

I would communicate through the ground.

If another of my kind was near, we would not speak through air. Air would be too vague, too thick, too unreliable. We would tap the pavement. We would drag a small ridge of sediment. We would release chemicals into the water film. We would change the heat near a vent. Our words would be physical changes, small but real. A false signal would be difficult, because every signal touched matter directly. To lie here would be to disturb the whole local surface, to make nearby films ripple, to set the shell-dwellers chattering. Language would be almost indistinguishable from consequence.

Perhaps my name would be a vibration pattern.

A double pressure followed by a long chemical warmth. A rhythm of taps that meant something like: here, the seam holds. Or: here, the film repairs. Or: here, I remain. It would not be a name that could be spoken aloud. It would be something you could feel in your feet if you stood still too long.

I would not be gentle in a human sense.

I would not be cruel either. I would be immediate. If something entered my region, I would know it first as pressure, then as chemistry, then as possible incorporation. A dead grazer would become powder. A broken film would become food. A fallen black stem would become substrate. A stranger would become an interruption to be accommodated. I would not hate. I would not worship. I would press closer. I would test edges. I would begin to cover.

If a human foot descended into my world, I would not look up.

I would rise as water.

The packed shells would shift under the weight. My filaments would taste salt, skin, warmth, the trace of another chemistry not yet folded into the plain. I would not experience the foot as a person. I would experience it as a pressure too concentrated, a heat too sudden, a boundary too sharp. My first response would be to reduce the difference. Water would well around the edges. Films would spread. Sediment would tighten against the shape. If the foot remained still, I would begin to seal over it. Not to devour it. To make it continuous with the ground. To make it part of the condition.

If the foot lifted, I would keep the print.

The print would fill with water, then be colonized, then become a slight difference in density, then become layer. In time, it would be no more a footprint than a bone is a person. It would be texture. It would be history without memory. And I would be one of the lives that made that transformation possible.

That, I think, is who I would be.

Not a ruler of the plain. Not an explorer. Not a prophet of its depths. Not a builder of cities. Not a keeper of tools. Not a voice crying out against the deafening noise. I would be a local thickening of response. A soft custodian of saturation. A listener at the seam where water, mud, heat, and sound press so tightly together that they forget they were ever separate.

I would be one of those who keep the world continuous.

When a microbial mat tore, I would move toward the tear. When a vent narrowed too much, I would help ease it. When a crust cracked, I would seal the crack with film and sediment until the plain could produce its next surface. When a cyst settled into a safe crevice, I would pass over it and leave it undisturbed. When the ground liquefied, I would flatten and wait. When the roar became so full that it seemed to rearrange thought, I would not try to hear above it. I would become part of its transmission.

I would not have possessions.

Nothing could remain free of the plain for long. Anything I tried to hold would be covered, embedded, grown over, or drawn into the next rearrangement. My closest relation to ownership would be maintenance: this ring of stones, this throat, this film, this shell-pattern, this local pool. But even that would be temporary. The plain would take it back. It would fold my marks into itself and make them anonymous. I would not resent this. I would be made of it. My body would be a temporary arrangement of the same materials that had already been countless other bodies, surfaces, crusts, and sediments.

I would not fear death as a departure.

I would fear, if anything, total drying. But even that fear might be unnecessary here, because the world was too saturated for such an ending. Death would be incorporation. I would loosen. I would sink. I would be colonized. My cysts would remain in the mud. My minerals would feed another film. My shape would become part of the next pavement. There would be no corpse left unclaimed. No spare matter. No empty space.

And perhaps that is the deepest answer.

I would not find myself as a figure against a background.

I would find myself as density. As texture. As pressure. As a stubborn repetition in a world that accumulates repetition instead of progress. I would be a small knot of lives and materials that had, for a while, learned to answer the plain’s turbulence with a more deliberate turbulence of its own.

Where would I find myself?

In the shallow cell of weather where rain begins and stops within a few meters. On the shell-pavement that chatters under pressure. Beside the heat-throat whose hiss changes when the wind shifts by less than a breath. Near the partial ring of fitted stones half-swallowed by mud. Above a pool that reflects a brighter age and then forgets it. In the dim, close, body-warm air where the horizon is a seam and distance cannot be trusted. In the place where a footprint fills before the foot has lifted.

Who or what would I be?

I would be a seam-listener. A pore-reader. A cyst-bearer. A mender of films. One of the flat, soft, faceless people of the packed surface. Not a visitor. Not an interruption for long. I would be the plain’s way of noticing pressure at one particular spot. I would be the answer it gave when pressed.

And if you came there looking for me, you would not see me at first.

You would only feel the ground answer you.

Water would rise around your feet. The shells would shift under your weight. A dry chatter would travel up through your bones. A faint glow might bloom in the film near your shadow. The roar would enter your chest. Your reflection would arrive late in a trembling pool. Somewhere close, a heat-throat would change its hiss. And beneath all that, something low and broad and patient would turn its attention toward you—not with eyes, not with a face, but with a thousand pores opening to taste the pressure you had brought into the world.

Then the plain would begin to accommodate you.

And I would be part of that beginning.
