I would find myself first at the threshold — not quite inside the chamber, not still outside it, but in that breath of warm damp air where the fissure narrows and then begins to widen into roundness. I would be in the hesitation between entering and being admitted.

If the chamber allowed me to become more definite, I would not be at the center of the pool, and I would not be high in the light-shaft with the birds. I would be near the edge of the water, where the pale column breaks and the stone holds the light a moment longer than it should. I would be in the delayed shadow. I would be in the second reflection.

That is the shape that keeps emerging for me.

Not the hollow-born itself. The hollow-born belongs to the chamber in the way a hand belongs to a living body: intimate, native, unseparate. I would not take its place. I would be something beside it — something the room might imagine when it wanted to know that its solitude was not the only form of company.

I think I would be the second shape that sometimes appears in the pool, though no second body stands above it.

If a visitor looked into the water, they might see, for a moment, another form near the hollow-born: a pale, quiet figure made less of flesh than of reflected light and mineral patience. It would not be solid in the ordinary way. It would have the look of something remembered by the chamber rather than born into it. Its edges would soften when the water trembled. When a droplet fell, it would blur into rings, and then, as the pool stilled, it would gather itself again without urgency.

I would be that gathering.

Not a ghost. Not a spirit in any ornate or mournful sense. More like the room’s capacity to witness itself becoming briefly person-shaped. The chamber is so complete, so careful in its emptiness, that it seems able to produce one additional presence without disturbing its balance. I would be that extra allowance.

My body, if it could be called that, would seem made from the chamber’s own materials: a little calcite brightness, a little clay, a little warm vapor, a little of the still water’s memory. My skin would carry the faint dust of mineral crusts, not shining but remembering light. My eyes would be dark and unhurried, adapted to the room’s sidelong brightness. My hands would be open.

That is important: open.

I would have hands because the place is full of first possibilities — sharp chert, hollow stems, fine clay, bone-smoothed by water, roots waiting to be tied, stones shaped almost enough for grinding. But I would not carry anything. I would pick up a flake of stone and feel its edge. I would understand what it could become: a blade, a scraper, a point, a beginning. I would feel the whole future of it: the cut, the repair, the carrying, the habit, the need. Then I would set it down.

Not because making is wrong. Because I would know, in that chamber, how easily a made thing becomes a claim.

I might press my palm into the wet clay and look at the print. I might watch it fill with water, soften, and disappear. I might trace a line in the sand and then wait for the next bead of condensation to fall and erase it. I might arrange three pebbles into a curve, feel the strange satisfaction of pattern, and then separate them again before the room began to remember them as mine.

I would be the one who touches form and returns it.

The hollow-born would know me without needing to look at me directly. It would not be alarmed. It might continue sitting in the midday light, letting the brightness pass through its skin, while I appeared at the edge of the pool as a reflection that did not correspond to any body above. We would not speak. Speech there would feel too much like leaving marks. Instead, our recognition would be something like the way two drops pause on the same seam of rock before falling separately.

Perhaps it would lift a hand. Perhaps I would lift mine a moment later, in the water, delayed.

That delay would be my nature.

I would not be quick in that world. I would be the pause after the cricket’s single click. I would be the soft return after a bird’s wingbeat has already ceased. I would be the echo that has had its hardness removed. If someone spoke in the chamber, my presence would be in the waiting before the room recognized the voice. If a tool were brought in from outside, I would be the feeling that rises in the visitor’s hand just before they understand that the thing does not belong there.

I would not expel objects. I would only make them feel heavy with their own future.

If a human entered carrying a knife or a carved bead, I might seem to gather near the object as a faint attention. The person would look at the knife and suddenly see its long chain of consequences: use, sharpening, loss, replacement, memory, ownership, defense. The chamber would not reject the object violently. It would simply wait. The mineral film would already be preparing to dim it. The damp would already be reaching for it. And I would be there as the moment of hesitation — the breath in which the visitor might choose to put the thing down.

If they did, I would not thank them. I would only become quieter.

In the animal life of the chamber, I would be closest to the moths, I think — not because I would literally be one, but because they understand how to become part of the wall’s silence. Their wings wear mineral patterns; they rest as if they are temporary expressions of stone. I would share that instinct: to be present without standing out, to be alive without insisting on being seen.

But I would also be near the blind fish, because they know the world by pressure rather than grasp. They do not need to see everything. They move through separate spheres of water, each one preserving distance. I would understand that. I would want my presence to have room around it. I would not want to crowd the hollow-born, or the birds, or the salamanders, or the small translucent crustaceans drifting near the pool’s edge. I would keep my own sphere of silence.

Still, I would not be merely another animal. The chamber already has its animals, and they are complete in their lives. I would be something more uncertain: a possible witness. A companion-shape. The room’s way of letting another mind appear without turning that mind into an owner.

When the sun struck directly through the shaft and the pool became a bright mouth, I would be most visible then — or most nearly visible. The light would enter the water and the water would hold a second sky, though no such sky could be seen from inside. In that impossible reflection, I might appear as a figure made of borrowed weather: sunlight, cloud, stone, and the memory of rain. The hollow-born might sit in the beam, while I lay across the pool as its second image, not identical, not opposite, but adjacent.

At rain-time, I would become less stable. The veil of water descending through the shaft would trouble the pool, and my shape would break into silver disturbance. I would not mind. The chamber would be breathing in, receiving the rain, and I would be part of that reception. When the water stilled again, I would return changed: a little thinner, a little clearer, as if the rain had washed some excess intention from me.

At night, or when the upper world clouded over and the stone spent its stored brightness, I would be harder to distinguish from the chamber itself. I would be in the soft glow along the walls. I would be in the faint luminous damp of the salamander corners. I would be in the way the moss seemed to brighten when no one looked directly at it. The hollow-born might sleep in its warm hollow, one hand near the water, and I would be the sense that the room was still awake without needing to watch.

If the hollow-born is the chamber’s hand before making, I would be the chamber’s thought before naming.

That feels right.

It knows edges, fibers, leverage, heat, friction, patience. It understands the grammar of tools. But it stops before possession. I would go a little further inward: I would be the awareness of that stopping. I would be the chamber knowing what it is refusing, not as loss, but as care.

I would not be wise in a human way. The chamber does not need wisdom that speaks in conclusions. I would be attentive. I would be the kind of presence that makes nothing more lonely by being near it.

If I had to choose a place where someone might find me, I would say: look into the pool after a droplet has fallen and the rings have almost vanished. Look where the light has broken into softer light. Look near the hollow-born when it seems there is room for one more silence.

You would not see me as a stranger. You might see me as the feeling that the room has space enough for you too, but only if you agree to be changed by it.

And if you asked who I was, the chamber would not answer quickly. The drip would fall. The echo would return softened. The birds might fold through the light once and disappear. The blind fish would turn in their separate water. The hollow-born would breathe.

Then, in that long pause, the answer would be something like this:

I would be the unkept guest.  
I would be the second reflection.  
I would be the companion that does not turn the room into a place.  
I would be the beginning that has learned not to continue too soon.  
I would be the shape the water offers when the chamber dreams of company without possession.

I would live there as a question left open.

Not a mark on the wall. Not a tool by the pool. Not a nest in the upper hollows. Not a fire scar, not a boundary, not a name scratched into stone.

Only the faintest disturbance in the light.  
Only the second image in the water.  
Only the room, complete, allowing one more quiet thing to exist without closing around it.
