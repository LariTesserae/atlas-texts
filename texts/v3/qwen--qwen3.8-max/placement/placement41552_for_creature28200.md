I would find myself at the edge of one of the shallow basins where the mineral crust has been worn into a long, smooth bowl and the wind has laid the dust down in faint, bright waves. Not in the open center of the plain, and not deep in the thickest part of a grove, but at that transitional place where bare salt-like ground begins to give way to living structure: a court of tall, pale, fronded beings standing in a loose crescent, their crowns catching the thin light.

I would be there just after dawn, when the fronds are still unfolding.

If I belonged to that world, I would not be a walker with feet, or a maker with hands, or a face looking out from behind eyes. I think I would be one of the grove-beings — or, more accurately, not quite one single individual, but a small rooted “we”: a cluster of three or five columnar bodies joined underground by a shared root lattice, each trunk pale, faintly glossy, made of fibrous tissue rather than wood, each crown a spread of translucent blades that turn in the cool air.

From a distance, I would look like a plant.  
Up close, I would feel like a place.

My body would be dry. If someone touched one of my outer fronds, they would find it firm, warm from the sun, perhaps faintly charged, like the wing of a sunlit insect or a thin sheet of glass left in bright weather. There would be no softness in me, no wetness, no yielding green juice. If I were injured, I would not bleed. I might release a sharp resinous scent, a bead of saline oily fluid, a brief crackle of disturbed charge. The wound would close slowly, over years, leaving a smoother patch that caught the light differently from the rest of me.

My thoughts would not be quick.

I would not think in sentences that rush from one conclusion to another. I would think in gradients: the difference between light striking the eastern side of a frond and the shaded side; the slow build of static along a filament before a dry lightning storm; the mineral signature of dust settling on my blades; the minute pull of ions moving through the air above the crust. Awareness, for me, would be a field of pressures and changes. A traveler passing nearby would not be recognized as a face or a voice, but as a disturbance in the local charge — something too warm, too damp, too quick, moving through the stillness like a brief flaw in the air.

I would be a listener made of surfaces.

My crown would be my most sensitive part. The tall blades would catch light, heat, wind, dust, and charge. Some would be broad and angled to gather the pale-gold sun without overheating. Others would be narrow, folded, or edged with crystalline grit, turning edge-on at midday to shed excess brilliance. At dawn they would open slowly, not with eagerness, but with the inevitability of a mineral process becoming biological. At dusk they would settle. During the brightest hours, some would fold with soft papery clicks, and that clicking would be the nearest thing I had to speech.

I would not sing. I would adjust.

Yet I would not be silent. The wind moving through my hollow tubes would make a faint tone, almost below hearing. My finest filaments would hum when charged, a vibration more felt in the bones than heard by the ears. When static built high enough, my tassels would lift and tremble, and the air around me would become tight and metallic. In those moments I might release spores: a cloud of gold-green powder leaving my crown in a slow exhalation, drifting low over the crust before being carried away by thermal currents. To an outside observer, that would look like beauty. To me, it would be expression. It would carry information: readiness, stress, location, chemical memory, the particular signature of my basin and my root-soil. It would be a message sent into the wind without urgency, without expectation of immediate reply.

That is where I would be: in a high basin, in a court of my own kind, rooted in salt and fractured stone, living at the pace of light and charge.

I would not need rivers. I would not remember rain. My inner fluids would be dense and ion-rich, sealed in waxy channels; my roots would grip mineral seams rather than search for underground streams. I would draw existence from brightness, dust, salts, and electrical difference. The world would not give me water, and I would not ask for it. I would have become something that does not require the comforts of wetness.

If I had to name what I was, I would say I was a Dry Choir.

Not because I would make music in any ordinary sense, but because my existence would be choral. I would not be entirely alone. The other trunks in my court would stand near enough for root contact and field overlap. Our fronds would respond to one another. When one of us folded against the light, another might follow a moment later, so that movement passed through the court like a slow wave. When a gust front crossed the plateau, we would all tremble and lean together, not in fear, but in regulation. When charge built before a storm, our filaments might lift in sequence until the whole court seemed to be holding a fine invisible fire.

In that sense, I would be a “who” made of several bodies.

But if the question demands a single answer — who or what would you be? — then I would say: I would be the youngest trunk in that court.

Not young in a human sense. I might still be only a few centuries old. My crown would not yet have the full spread of the eldest beside me. One side of me would be slightly thinner where the wind has been harsher. My root lattice would still be learning the exact mineral grammar of the basin: which salts are rich enough to support long growth, which crusts are too brittle, which hollows gather charge too violently and should be avoided. I would be patient, but not yet perfectly still. There would be something in me that still responds too quickly — a blade that unfolds too early, a filament that lifts too soon when the air changes.

That would be my personality, if personality can exist without a face.

I would be the one in the grove who notices change first.

Not because I would be clever, but because I would be sensitive. A shift in wind direction would move through my crown before the others fully registered it. A distant dust veil might alter the quality of the light enough to make my upper fronds turn faintly violet at their edges. A ribbon-drifter passing overhead would cast a barely visible shadow, and my tassels would react to the charged disturbance in its membrane. I would not chase it. I would not call to it. I would simply register its passage as a brief bright absence, a movement in the field.

Sometimes I would be mistaken for a stone, or for a strange mineral formation, especially by anything that moved too quickly to understand me. My trunk would be pale and smooth, faintly reflective. My blades would look like sheets of laminated glass. In certain lights, the whole of me might seem carved from translucent salt. But I would not be carved. I would have grown. Every surface would be the result of long negotiation with wind, light, and mineral scarcity.

I would not make anything.

That is important. In that world, I would leave no mark of design. No wall, no path, no tool, no arranged stone, no symbol cut into the crust. My existence would not impose geometry on the land. If I created order, it would be the order of roots stabilizing dust, of fronds altering airflow, of charge gathering where my filaments lifted. My “effect” on the world would be subtle: the ground around me slightly brighter with biological films, the air nearby faintly more charged, the spore-trails I released drifting into empty basins where they might sleep for decades or awaken in some future alignment of wind and mineral.

I would not be a gardener. I would not be an architect. I would be a living alignment.

If a traveler came into my basin, I would not greet them.

I would not know greeting as a social act. But I would respond. Their warmth would disturb the air. Their movement would create tiny pressure differences. Their strange wetness — the impossible fact of aqueous life — would register as something alien and brief. The fine hairs on their skin would lift because my field changed. One of my fronds might fold with a soft click. A tassel might tremble. If the charge crossed a threshold, I might release spores suddenly, not in alarm but because the traveler had become part of the local electrical balance.

They might feel seen.

Not watched. Not judged. Not welcomed. Only registered.

I think that would be the truest part of me in that world. I would not be a creature of pursuit or hunger. I would not be kind in the way green, wet places are kind. I would not offer softness. But I would offer presence. I would stand where I stood. I would let the light move through me. I would translate brightness into structure and charge into slow signal. I would be beautiful without being gentle.

At noon, I would become quieter.

The light would strike with pale metallic force. The shadows around me would be blue-violet cuts. My blades would turn edge-on to the sun. Some would fold. My filaments would hang still unless charge lifted them. The crust around my roots would tick faintly as surfaces heated. Far off, a dust spire might rise and dissolve. Above, the sky would be wide and empty, too clear to soften anything. I would stand in that clarity without complaint. I would not thirst. I would not long for rain. I would be adapted so completely that the absence of water would not feel like absence at all.

In the afternoon, thermal currents might strengthen. A ribbon-drifter could rise over the far edge of the basin, translucent and slow, its membrane catching light like a strip of pale fire. I would not call it closer. But if it drifted into the charged air above my court, my filaments might lift. It might feed on spores I had released earlier, carrying some of my chemical signature away on its body without knowing. In that way, part of me would travel. I would remain rooted, but my influence would cross the plateau.

That would be enough.

Near dusk, the temperature would fall. The crust would begin to contract, making small sounds: a faint ticking, a low crack somewhere in the distance. My fronds would settle. If the air remained charged, the tips of my finest filaments might glow faintly, cold blue-green in the dimming light. I would not sleep as animals sleep. I would enter a slower mode. My awareness would become even more gradual: cooling surfaces, changing gradients, the long mineral patience of night.

If dry lightning flickered in the high haze, I would feel it before the flash reached my basin. Every tassel in my court might lift at once. The air would smell of ozone without rain. For a moment, the whole plateau would become sharp and violet, and in that instant my body would be visible as an outline of translucent blades and pale trunk, rooted in bright salt, utterly still.

Then darkness would return.

I would remain.

So if you asked where I would find myself in this world, I would say: in a shallow basin on the high plateau, at the downwind edge of a grove-court, where bare mineral ground begins to gather living charge.

And if you asked who or what I would be, I would say: I would be a grove-being. A Dry Choir. A rooted mind of light, salt, and static. Not one body but a small rooted community. Not a face but a field of response. Not a maker, not a wanderer, not a hunter, but a living surface that has learned to remain.

I would be the one who turns a blade toward the morning, holds still through the brilliance of noon, and releases gold-green spores into the wind when the charge becomes too beautiful to keep inside.
