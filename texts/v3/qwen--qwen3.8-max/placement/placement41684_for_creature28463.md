I would not find myself at the center of that world. I would find myself at one of its seams.

Not on the highest watch-cairns, though I would know them by sight and signal. Not out on the outer fog-walls where the cloud banks drag themselves white and wet over the stone. Not down in the deepest undercrofts where ice is kept in the dark, and not among the great herd lanes at the hour when the woolly beasts are moving in slow mass through the city. At first, when I try to imagine myself there, I do not become one person. I become several possibilities, each standing in a different part of the plateau, each with a different kind of patience.

For a while I think I would be a fog-tender, standing at dawn beside the broad angled pans with my sleeves damp at the cuffs, cleaning grit from the channels, watching the first bead of moisture gather on stone. That life appeals to me because it is receptive. It does not seize. It waits for weather to give what it can.

Then I think I would be a water-counter, kneeling beside a covered cistern with a notched rod, listening to the pitch of a jar filling in the dark. That life appeals to me because it is exact. In a place where water arrives by drip and seep, exactness becomes a form of care.

Then I think I would be one of the herders, moving with the broad-hoofed beasts through the terraced lanes, smelling of wool and animal warmth, guiding with body weight rather than force. That life appeals to me because it is warm in the literal sense: because bodies there are architecture, and heat is shared before it is owned.

Then I think I would be a watcher, wrapped in hides on a roof platform, eyes trained to the smallest change in cliff, cloud, or bird-flight. That life appeals to me because it is quiet, and because distance there is not emptiness but a field of signs.

But the image that keeps returning, the one that settles deepest, is not any of those alone.

I would be a keeper of marks and signs.

I would live on the middle terraces, in the part of the city where the outer fog-catching walls begin to give way to the cistern districts and the more stable inner benches. Not so high that weather is only something looked down upon, not so low that it floods the lanes and makes every doorway a wet threshold. My district would be one of those hinge places where damp becomes countable, where the city turns cloud into storage, and where stone is watched carefully because it is still young enough to fail.

There would be a basalt shelf there, broad enough for several households and pens, protected on one side by a cliff that has been judged stable by generations of stone-listeners, and open on the other to a long view of terraces, windbreaks, and distant roof platforms. In the cold season, fog would enter the lower lanes in slow white movements. In the clearer hours, the light would come down hard and blue-shadowed, making every crack and gutter sharply visible.

My home would not be large. It would be thick-walled, low-ceilinged, and entered through a bent passage so that wind could not rush straight inside. The doorway would be hung with a heavy hide, worn soft at the edges, darkened by years of hands and weather. Inside there would be a sleeping platform against the warmest wall, perhaps sharing heat with an animal pen on the other side. There would be niches cut into the stone: one for folded wool, one for tools, one for small tokens of memory. A bone needle. A feather. A shard of old plaster with a painted line on it. A notched tally stick from a winter when the cisterns nearly failed.

On the roof above my room there would be a shallow frost-catching surface, grooved toward a covered jar. In the brief thaw, that roof would be watched almost constantly. In winter, snow would be banked against a shaded corner of it, then harvested as ice when needed. Nothing about the roof would be ornamental. It would be a working top, a small extension of the city’s body.

I would not be a person of dramatic importance. I would be necessary in the way that memory and maintenance are necessary, which is to say quietly, constantly, and with consequences that only become visible if neglected.

My work would begin after frost nights.

While the lanes are still blue with early cold and the fog has not yet lifted from the lower passages, I would go out with a pouch of pale plaster, a little charcoal, a scrap of pigment, a bone stylus, and a cord for measuring. I would walk the routes assigned to me: the wall faces beneath the safe benches, the corners of cistern courts, the edges of terraces where older works meet newer additions, the places where last season’s repairs must be checked before anyone trusts them again.

I would look for the small signs.

A plaster seal fallen from a crack.  
A hairline that has widened by the breadth of a fingernail.  
A dark streak of seepage where there was none before.  
A slight bulge in a retaining wall.  
A fresh scatter of stone chips at the base of a cliff face.  
A drainage channel blocked by rime or windblown grit.  
A rope anchor loosened by repeated frost.  
A door lintel that no longer sits the way it should.

I would not panic. Panic belongs to lower places where mistakes can be outrun. In this city, panic is waste. Instead I would mark what I found. A line of pigment. A fresh plaster seal. A small cairn of chips. A knot of cord tied in a particular way. If the sign was serious, I would send for a stone-listener or a mason. If it was only weather working at the surface, I would record it and move on.

Part of my task would be comparison. I would carry in my head, and on notched sticks, the memory of how things stood before. This wall was dry at the last thaw. This gutter flowed evenly when the fog was heavy. This corner iced over lightly but did not crack. This passage sounded solid underfoot three days ago. In a world built by accretion, where changes happen by millimeters and seasons, memory is not a luxury. It is infrastructure.

So I would become one of the city’s careful rememberers.

Not a priest, exactly. Not a ruler. Not even a specialist in the way that lower cities might imagine specialists, set apart from ordinary labor. I would still mend wool, carry dung fuel, help clear a blocked lane, and stand with others when a heavy stone had to be levered into place. But I would also be trusted with signs. People would ask me which wall had been marked last year, which cistern had been low after the long cold, which terrace had shed stone in the season before I was born. Elders would tell me things while we sat in weak sun, and I would be expected to hold those words in place until they mattered.

My body would fit this life not by elegance but by adaptation.

I would be compact, as the people there are compact, with deep lungs and unhurried breath. My face would be wind-darkened, my lips often cracked, my eyes accustomed to glare from pale stone and ice-rimmed walls. My hands would be the truest evidence of my life: roughened by plaster, wool, rope, and cold water, nails short, knuckles thickened, palms callused in patterns belonging to tools I used every day. I would not move quickly unless there was reason. I would place my feet. I would know without looking when a slab was slick, when gravel was loose, when a step had heaved slightly in the night.

My clothing would be layered and repaired. Wool next to the skin, softened by wear. A heavier outer garment, dense against wind, darkened at the shoulders where fog settled. Hides across the shoulders and knees where strength mattered. Fastenings of bone or carved stone, perhaps one small metal toggle so old and thin that it was more inheritance than ornament. I would smell of cold wool, faint dung smoke, wet rope, and mineral dust. In fog my sleeves would darken at the cuffs. In sun I would seem suddenly vivid, every fold of cloth throwing small shadows, my breath no longer visible, my eyes narrowed against clarity.

My voice would be quiet.

Not because I lacked feeling, but because the air and stone would teach me better. I would use short phrases and low calls. I would learn the gestures that carry where speech fails: a lifted hand across a terrace, a marker shifted on a parapet, a certain arrangement of stones on a roof edge. I would understand that a warning spoken too loudly can be less useful than a sign seen clearly at a distance. When I laughed, it would be brief and warm, quickly swallowed by wool. When I grieved, I would probably hold it in the body for a long time, the way cold is held in a north wall.

I would not live alone, though I would sometimes work alone.

My household would be one of several clustered around a sheltered court or passage. There would be shared surfaces, shared chores, shared heat. An old water-keeper might live nearby, severe about small things, tender in the way she taught children to drink slowly. A wool-worker might occupy the next room, hands quick with carding and twisting, smelling of grease and fiber. A herder’s family would share the court, and their animals’ warmth would be part of the architecture of winter. I would know my neighbors by the sounds of their doors, the smell of their fuel, the rhythm of their work.

There would be a herd beast I knew individually, perhaps one that always paused near my corner of the lane because the wall there gave off stored sun. I would not think of it as mine in the lowland sense of ownership. It would be one of the city’s living presences, and I would be one of the humans responsible for not frightening it, not blocking it, not wasting what it gave. I would know the sound of its breath, the way its coat darkened with moisture, the particular patience of its wide muzzle.

There would also be ravens.

I think I would love them, if love in that place could be shown through attention. They would follow the stone-workers and mark-keepers, inspecting every opened cache, every fresh heap of chips, every length of newly laid cord. They would be too clever to trust fully and too useful to drive away. If I had a companion that was not human, it would likely be a raven that tolerated me because I was in the habit of noticing things. I would leave it scraps when I could. It would teach me to look where nothing seemed to be happening.

If I were allowed to be something not quite human in that world, perhaps I would be one of those ravens. I would live in a cliff niche or under a roof edge, riding the uplift along the plateau rim, watching the city with cold patience. I would see districts change by tiny degrees. I would know which roofs collected frost first, which pens were warmest, which watchers fell asleep at dawn, which children were learning to be still. I would be part of the city’s alertness without being part of its obligations.

But if I had to be human there—if I had to have hands, debts, duties, and a place in the web of labor—I would be the keeper of marks and signs.

My authority would be modest, but real.

I could not command a wall to stand. I could not command weather to be gentle. But I could say: this passage should be closed until a mason checks it. I could say: this cistern level does not match the count from the last thaw. I could say: this crack was sealed last freeze and the seal has failed. I could say: this lane must not be blocked tonight because the young animals are being moved below. My word would not be final in every matter, but it would be trusted because it was based on observation and memory rather than desire.

I would be answerable to others. Stone-listeners would correct me when I misread a sound. Water-counters would correct me when I confused a seep with a useful flow. Elders would correct me when I thought a thing was new because I had not lived long enough to know it had happened before. Humility would not be a virtue in that place so much as a practical condition. The plateau would punish anyone who mistook confidence for knowledge.

My days would be shaped by repetition.

After hard frost, walk the marked routes.  
After fog, check the gutters and pans.  
After thaw, look for new seepage.  
Before winter, inspect seals and coverings.  
Before a building project, remember what the ground has done.  
Before speaking, remember what has already been recorded.
