At first, when I ask where I would be, the city refuses to give me a landmark. It gives me conditions instead: heat pressing through damp cloth, the smell of warm stone and iron salt, a lamp blurred by vapor, a drip falling somewhere out of sight. Then, slowly, a position forms.

I would not be in the lowest places, where the flooded halls hold black water and old rails disappear under mineral skin. Nor would I be out on the far flats where the ground softens and the horizon dissolves. I would be somewhere in the middle levels: on a service gallery above a sunken basin, perhaps between a reservoir terrace and a pump house whose pistons rise and fall too slowly to seem urgent. There would be a catwalk there, narrow and cambered, with a rail polished in one place by old hands. Beneath it, a shallow pool would catch the faint light. Above it, a pipe bridge would sweat in patient beads. A ceramic hood would hang over a drain, cracked but still doing its work.

That is where I would find myself: not in a plaza, not in a grand avenue, not in any room meant for ceremony, but in the in-between architecture of care. A doorway set high. A ladder with its lower rungs crusted white. An access panel recently opened, then closed, then opened again. The sort of place that looks unimportant until you notice that everything nearby depends on it.

As for what I would be, I think I would be minor.

Not a ruler of the city. Not an engineer-priest commanding the deep pumps. Not a survivor trying to restore some lost golden age. I would be a keeper of small continuations. A listener. A tender of thresholds. Someone whose hands know the difference between a valve wheel worn smooth by use and one made brittle by salt. Someone whose first instinct is not to speak, but to put a bare knuckle against a pipe and feel what is moving through it.

I would probably be human once, if the word still applies cleanly. The city would have changed me, or my people would have changed themselves to suit it. My skin would be pale from the low diffuse light, maybe flushed in patches where heat settles too long. My eyes would be accustomed to pearl-gray distances and would distrust sudden brightness. My lungs would have learned to take the thin, saturated air carefully, as if every breath were borrowed from a crowded room. I might wear a condenser mask over my mouth and nose: a lattice of ceramic and metal, darkened with use, recovering moisture from my breath and filtering out mineral dust. It would not make me heroic. It would make me practical.

My coat would be glazed, stiff, and faintly shining where droplets cling to it. My boots would be thick-soled, perhaps ceramic-composite or layered rubberized cloth, because the floors are never only floors. Some are safe. Some are crusts over heat. Some are stone that looks dry but hides a slick of brine. I would carry a valve key, a scraper, a coil of waxed line, a small ceramic knife, and a lamp whose amber glow is strong enough to matter but weak enough to be honest. I would not carry much ornament. If I carried any, it would be something mineral: a shard of blue-glazed pipe, a crystal growth sealed under glass, a piece of rust-streaked porcelain-like ceramic polished smooth.

I would be the sort of person who has learned that quiet is not empty.

My work would not be dramatic. I would not spend my days fighting floods or restarting dead engines for the salvation of everyone. There is no everyone here. Instead, I would tend a route. A stretch of gallery. A cluster of drains. A row of condenser fins furred with scale. A set of pressure gauges whose needles tremble more than they should. A trough that clogs with white crust when the aquifer rises. A lamp whose circuit fails whenever the nearest transformer sighs too deeply. I would know these things not by theory but by repetition. The city would teach me through small refusals: this stair floods on certain days; this railing is too brittle to bear weight; this panel should be opened only after the vent cycles; this pool looks calm but is hot enough to burn.

I would mark safe paths. A copper tag tied to a rail. A scratch on a tile. A line of salt where a crust begins. Maybe I would keep a chart on a wall of glazed brick, tallying pressure changes, drip rates, and the hours when certain doors close by themselves because buried timers still remember crowds. I would not call this superstition. In that city, routine is a form of attention.

If you saw me, you might think I was alone. I would not be, exactly. There would be others, but not enough to make a crowd. We would be scattered across the upper terraces, the dry galleries, the rooms with ceilings angled toward interior gutters. We would know one another by signs more than by conversation. A lamp flashed twice: stop. Three slow flashes: pressure rising. A scraped rail: unsafe floor. A knuckle knocked twice against iron: I am here, moving on. Speech would exist, but it would be low and sparing. The air would make long sentences feel expensive.

I might share my route with one other keeper, or perhaps I would only encounter them through evidence: a freshly cleared drain, a replaced seal, a copper tag bent in a pattern I recognize. That would be enough. In such a place, proof of care can be as intimate as company.

My home would be raised above the wettest floor. Not elegant. Not sealed against the city, because nothing there stays sealed for long. The room would have a gutter running along one wall, a threshold shaped to guide water away from the sleeping platform, and a ceiling cambered so condensation falls into a channel rather than onto the bed. The bed itself would be suspended or built on high supports, because the ground is never entirely trustworthy. Tools would be near the door. A distillation vessel would sit beside a warm vent. Shallow trays would hold microbial mats: rust-colored felts, dense and slow, scraped into cakes when they thicken enough. There might be dried fungal filaments pressed with salt, or a jar of pale brine worms harvested from a managed pool. The food would be harsh, mineral, and sufficient. It would not remind me of green things. I would not let myself expect it to.

If I had been born there, I would not mourn the absence of plants in the way an outsider might. I would not dream of leaves. I might know the word “green” only as a color on glazed tile or copper stain, a mineral falsehood. My sense of life would be different: life as crust, film, filament, translucent flicker in warm water, machine turning patiently in the dark. I would understand that the city is not dead because it lacks meadows. It has its own metabolism. My task would be to remain inside that metabolism without becoming careless.

There would be fear, but not in the old-fashioned sense. Not fear of monsters in the dark, though the lower halls might contain shapes better left uncertain. More precise fears. The fear of a hollow note underfoot. The fear of a pressure gauge whose needle has stopped trembling. The fear of a pipe too quiet when it should be humming. The fear of a lamp burning where no one has lit one. In that city, reassurance would not come from brightness or sound. It would come from rhythm. A pump breathing at its usual interval. A drip falling where it always falls. A fan turning with its heavy, deliberate patience.

I would be, above all, a reader of rhythms.

Perhaps that is why I would not be purely human in the old sense. The city repairs what it keeps. A finger lost to a brittle crust might be replaced with something ceramic and dull. A knee worn from climbing damp stairs might be braced in copper alloy. A lung weakened by mineral vapor might be assisted by a small internal valve, or by a mask worn so long it becomes part of the face. Such changes would not feel tragic. They would feel like weathering. The city does not preserve purity. It preserves function. It preserves continuation.

If I had to name my role, I might say I am a custodian of seams. The places where water and architecture disagree. The places where heat meets metal and metal sweats. The places where a drain begins to clog and a room begins to flood. The places where a machine has fallen into a habit so slow it resembles sleep. I would stand at those seams with a scraper and a lamp, not to conquer them, but to keep them from becoming catastrophes.

I would not imagine myself as the city’s savior. That would be too loud a thought for such a place. I would be closer to an immune response: minor, local, mostly unnoticed. A white filament spreading over dust has its place. A brine worm has its place. A valve turning itself has its place. I would have my place too: the one who notices when the drip changes pitch.

Sometimes I think I would be almost machine-like myself. Not because I have lost feeling, but because feeling there must be economical. I would know satisfaction from a cleared channel. I would know anxiety from a pressure line that ticks wrongly. I would know something like tenderness when I see an old mechanism still moving after all this time, crusted and patient, doing what it was made to do. I might place my hand on a warm pump casing the way someone elsewhere might place a hand on a sleeping animal. Not to control it. To confirm that it is still with me.

Loneliness would be strange there. It would not feel like absence of people so much as absence of crowds. The city was built for processions, carts, machines, many hands, many voices, but those have narrowed into a few gestures repeated by a few caretakers. Still, I do not think I would experience that as pure sorrow. The city does not invite mourning. It invites maintenance. If I felt loneliness, it would be in the form of intervals: how long between one lamp and another, how many drips between footfalls, how many days before I hear another human knock on iron. I would learn to measure time that way. Not by sun, not by season, but by pressure cycles and condensation.

There would be moments, though, when the city would become unexpectedly beautiful. The vapor would thin for a breath. The pale smear above the basin would strengthen just enough to make the wet streets glow. Puddles would become mirrors. Pipe bridges would cast soft reflections. A far chimney would darken against the haze. Somewhere, a single lamp would flicker on, not welcoming, only present. In such a moment, I might stand on my gallery and understand that I am inside something vast and self-contained: a weather made of buildings, a body made of pipes, a population reduced to water, rust, filament, machine, and a few slow creatures in glazed coats.

I would not need to pray, but I might do something like it. I might check a valve. I might clean a gauge face. I might leave a copper tag on a rail for whoever comes after. Those acts would be my liturgy. The city does not require belief. It requires that someone, somewhere, remembers which floor is unsafe and marks it.

If I had to say who I would be in one sentence, I would say: I would be the one who keeps the water from becoming catastrophe and the machines from becoming obstacles.

But that still sounds too important. The city would humble me quickly. So let me say it smaller: I would be a caretaker of drains, a listener at pipes, a marker of safe routes, a scraper of salt, a tender of lamps that no crowd needs. I would be minor. I would be necessary enough. I would be one of the small lives that keep the city from becoming merely a monument to its own weather.

If you looked for me, do not look in the broad plazas or under the great chimneys. Look for a narrow catwalk where the rail is worn bright. Look for an access panel slightly ajar. Look for a lamp moving slowly over a pipe bridge, reflected in a pool below as if the ground contained a buried room. Look for a figure paused with one hand on iron, head tilted, listening.

That would be me.

I would tap the rail once, not because I am afraid, but because the city answers taps better than voices. Then I would move on, boots careful on the damp stone, mask dark with condensation, hands gloved and ready. Above me, a bead of water would gather on a pipe, grow heavy, and fall. Below, a pump would breathe. Somewhere, a valve would turn for no one.

And I would continue.
