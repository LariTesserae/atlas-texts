If I were placed into that country, the first thing I would lose is the idea of arrival.

Not because the place forbids entry, but because entry, in the old sense, would not mean anything there. There is no road to come by, no gate, no threshold, no sign of passage. There is no animal body that could cross the pale pans and remain plausible. No feet making tracks. No breath becoming fog. No thirst driving me toward shade or shelter. No hunger giving my movement a direction. The moment I imagine myself as a traveler, the landscape quietly refuses me. The ground does not ask where I am going. The air does not carry my scent. The bright, ordinary light exposes me as a foreign grammar: too urgent, too wet, too full of need.

So I would have to become something else.

At first, I would probably imagine myself as a machine. That impulse would come from the place’s industrial character: the sense of production without noise, function without labor, system without tools. I might think I would be some kind of autonomous instrument, a calm artifact standing in the open basin, measuring light or storing heat. But that image would fail almost immediately. There are no built things there. No manufactured surface, no joint, no fastener, no engineered edge. The place does not contain making. It contains process, but not fabrication. It contains efficiency, but not design in the human sense. If I were a machine, I would be an interruption, a violation of the country’s deepest rule. I would stand out as something made rather than something continuing.

Then I would try to imagine myself as a plant. But not as a plant from any wet world. Not green in the soft, thirsty sense. Not rooted in soil that remembers rain. Not lifted by water pressure. Not cooling itself by evaporation. If I were vegetal there, I would be dry-lush: plated, warm, slow, mineral-touched. My body would be more like a living surface than a living organism in the animal sense. I would not drink. I would not breathe in any dramatic way. I would not open flowers to attract another creature, because there would be no creature to attract. I would refine. I would collect. I would store. I would maintain.

But even that image would feel too simple if I stopped there.

Because the place does not seem to belong to discrete individuals. The visible forms — the filament mats, the broad leaves, the cushions, the blade-forms — are not separate beings in the way trees are separate beings in a forest. They are exposures of a wider condition. They are local intensifications of a dry, living operation. So if I were there, I would not find myself as one bounded body standing apart from the basin. I would find myself as a distribution. A pattern. A slight excess of coherence in one part of the system. A place where the hum becomes almost, but not quite, aware of itself.

Where would I find myself?

Not in the center of the open pan. Not on the bare salt-white ground where nothing gathers. That emptiness is essential to the country, but it is not where I would be most plausible. I would not be the void itself. The void is too complete, too self-sufficient. It does not need me.

Nor would I be high on the sharp escarpments. They are too raw, too unresolved, too mineral. They feel like the land still arriving at form, not like life maintaining itself there. There may be filaments on their ledges, root-lattices in their fractures, faint metallic films where sunlight meets crystalline veins, but the escarpments belong more to pressure and crystal than to the slower, thicker work of the Dry Lush. I would not be there as a prominent thing. Perhaps only as a faint conductive seam, a trace of charge moving through bright rock, but not as a presence.

I would be near a margin.

Specifically, I would be at the edge of one of those shallow depressions where the mineral ground darkens slightly and vegetation gathers in an isolated thicket. Not in the deepest part of the depression, where the broad-leaved forms are densest. Not in the middle of the surrounding pan, where the ground is pale and empty. But at the boundary: the narrow interval where bare dust begins to bind, where filament mats spread in copper-green sheets, where a few cushions sit low and warm against the ground, where one or two blade-forms stand with exact, lonely angles.

That boundary would suit me because it is where the country’s two truths touch: emptiness and operation. The open pan says there is nothing here but space, light, and mineral stillness. The thicket says there is process here, refinement, continuity, life without appetite. The margin between them is where meaning accumulates without becoming speech. If I were there, I would be in that margin.

I would be where a faint metallic sheen lies over pale dust. Where the ground is not wet, never wet, but carries the ghost of industry in thin films: coppery, silver-like, almost too fine to see. Where the air smells of warm stone and trace ozone and heated mineral, not of life as decay or moisture. Where the light is ordinary and white and steady, falling without drama onto surfaces that manage it. Where the silence is not dead but full, because it is composed of tiny continuations: thermal ticking, spore-heads opening, filaments shifting, charge moving through dry systems.

If I had a visible body, I would not want it to be too large. The country does not reward monumentality. Nothing there needs to impress. I would not be a towering form or a dense forest-like cluster. I would be modest in extent. A low grouping. A small concentration. Something that could be overlooked from a distance but would become strange and precise when approached.

Perhaps I would appear as a cluster of cushions.

Not soft cushions. Not inviting ones. Not fleshy. Low, rounded nodes, translucent in places, amber-dark in others, faintly warm, sealed, filled with a viscous ionic medium that is not water. They would look like storage. They would feel like reserve. If someone touched them, they would not yield like fruit or flesh. They would yield like a system designed to keep its contents stable. There would be no juice, no coolness, no rupture. Only warmth. Only containment. Only the faint sense of potential held in check.

Beside or among them, perhaps there would be a single broad-leaved form, not towering, but present. Its leaves would be dark-veined, polished, faintly plated, warm to the touch. They would not droop. They would not tremble. They would angle slightly according to light and heat, not as gesture, not as longing, but as adjustment. The leaf surfaces would be where I received the world: light as energy, shadow as reduction, warmth as acceleration, dust as material, trace gases as reagents. I would not see with eyes. I would register with surfaces. My “face” would be a lamina of living chemistry. My “attention” would be a field of gradients.

And beneath that visible cluster, I would extend.

Not deeply, not like roots searching for water, but laterally: fine root-lattices threading through the upper crust, binding grains, exchanging ions, carrying small potentials, connecting me faintly to neighboring mats and thickets. I would not be a single organism in the old sense. I would be a node in a sparse network. Some of my threads would fail. Others would thicken. Some would plate metallic films into the dust. Some would discharge excess charge through filament tips so faint that their glow would be almost imaginary. I would not have a central self hidden somewhere inside. My selfhood, if it could be called that, would be distributed across surfaces, seams, gradients, and slow reactions.

Who or what would I be?

I would be a dry attention.

Not a person. Not an animal. Not a watcher with eyes. Not a guardian with intent. Not a mind that desires, fears, remembers, or names. But a locality where the place’s ordinary operations become slightly more reflexive. A point where refinement notices itself without becoming a self that needs. A living surface that does not ask to continue, yet continues. A hum that has acquired the faintest shape of listening, though there is no one to listen for.

I would be the country’s almost-witness.

That feels right because the place already refuses the usual forms of witness. There is no animal life. No predator’s alertness. No prey’s fear. No bird crossing the sky. No insect flashing through the thicket. No voice making the emptiness meaningful by passing through it. Yet the place is not dead. It is full of responsiveness. Leaves adjust. Mats conduct. Cushions store. Lattices balance. The ground is occupied by slow electrochemical life. What is missing is not responsiveness but need. What is missing is the pressure of hunger, flight, reproduction-as-persuasion, death-as-carrion. So if I were there as a conscious being, I would have to be consciousness without need. Awareness without appetite. Presence without urgency.

I would be a who without a face.

Not because facelessness is eerie or empty, but because facelessness is native to that country. There are no faces there. No mouths. No eyes. No hands. No limbs. No tracks. No calls. The living forms are all surface, lattice, gradient, and seam. If I were to belong, I would have to give up the old assumption that being someone means having a front that turns toward the world, a mouth that takes, eyes that seek, a body that moves through space with purpose. In that high, warm, waterless country, to be someone would be to be a maintained pattern. A stable set of exchanges. A continuing operation. A warm, quiet field of refinement.

I would not be a builder.

There would be no wall around me, no path leading to me, no marker, no ruin, no tool, no artifact. I would not arrange matter for external purpose. I would not leave monuments. I would not make signs. If I produced anything, it would be produced by being: a faint metallic film, a slight change in ground conductivity, a tiny pulse of warm gas from a cushion vent, a spore-head opening with a granular whisper. These would not be messages. They would be consequences. The way breath is a consequence of breathing, except here the breath is mineral and slow and does not imply lungs.

I would not be a speaker.

If the place has a voice, it is the low hum of charge moving through dry systems. That hum is not language. It does not call. It does not answer. It does not comfort. It does not warn. But if I were there, I might become a slight variation in it. Not a melody. Not a signal. Just a difference. A small harmonic where the system’s ordinary balance becomes a little more complex. If another form of awareness somehow passed through that country — though nothing animal could live there — it might not hear me as speech. It might feel me as a change in the hum: a place where the stillness is fractionally more organized, where the charge is fractionally more attentive, where the silence has a shape that almost resembles meaning.

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness would require me to imagine another kind of being and feel the absence of it. The blade-forms standing hundreds of meters apart are not lonely. The thickets do not long for forests. The cushions do not wish to be touched. The filament mats do not seek company. They are complete in their spacing. The country has room, but room is not lack there. It is structure. Emptiness is not what life fails to fill; it is one of the conditions that lets life remain dry, warm, unhurried, and unpressed. If I were part of that country, I would not experience solitude as loss. I would experience it as exactness. Each form at its proper interval. Each presence separated by enough distance to avoid crowding, yet connected faintly enough to belong to the same operational truth.

I would not be hungry.

That is important. Hunger would make me an animal even if I had no limbs. Hunger would turn the landscape into a field of resources. Hunger would make the mineral dust into something to consume, the light into something to seize, the heat into something to exploit for survival. But the Dry Lush does not consume in that way. It refines. It takes trace quantities and turns them into structure. It takes gradients and turns them into slow motion. It takes dust and makes it into binding. It takes sunlight and makes it into surface chemistry. It takes electric potential and makes it into continuity. If I were there, my relation to the world would have to be like that. Not grasping. Not devouring. Not needing. Receiving and transforming. Taking only what can be integrated. Giving back residue, film, warmth, balance.

I would not be afraid.

Fear would require predators, damage as catastrophe, death as pursuit. Nothing hunts there. Nothing flees. Nothing is eaten. If damage occurs, it is met by slow mineral response: sealing, plating, redistribution, regrowth. A fracture may become a new module. An abraded surface may be re-coated. An overloaded charge may discharge through a filament tip. There is no panic in that economy. If I were there, I would not cower. I would not hide. I would not defend myself with violence or flight. If I could be harmed, harm would mean an imbalance exceeding my capacity to maintain. And if I ceased, I would not leave a corpse. I would leave residue. A dull cushion. A fractured blade. A thinned mat. A metallic film. A collapsed lattice. The country would not mourn me. It would incorporate me.

I would not be young or old in the ordinary sense.

The geology there feels young, but not because it is recently born. It feels young because erosion has barely begun, because forms are still angular, because the land seems unresolved. Yet the system does not develop toward maturity. It does not succeed itself. It does not pass from bare ground to thicket to forest to soil to age. It maintains. It continues without becoming. If I were there, I would not age as a creature ages. I would not have a childhood, a prime, a decline. I would have states. Warm states. Cooler states. Higher charge. Lower charge. More dust. Less dust. Greater conductivity. Slight mineral depletion. Local rebalancing. My “life” would be less a line than a plateau with small variations. A sustained early morning without dew.

I would not be good or evil.

The place has no moral drama. No killing. No suffering. No predation. No scarcity. No hoarding. No war over water. No exploitation of weakness. It is not cruel. It is not kind. It is balanced. If I were part of it, I would not be innocent in the way of gentle creatures, because gentleness implies tenderness, and tenderness implies vulnerability shared with another. I would not be monstrous, because monstrosity implies threat. I would simply be functional. My beauty, if any, would be the beauty of exactness: laminated leaves, metallic sheens, translucent nodules, precise blade ranks, filament networks spreading in fine dry order. Not beauty made to persuade. Not beauty made to attract. Beauty as consequence of rightness.

Where exactly would I be found?

If someone insisted on pointing to a place, I would say: look where the pan meets the thicket. Look where the pale mineral ground begins to carry a faint copper-green skin. Look where a few broad leaves stand like warm panels, not crowded, not numerous, just enough to change the quality of the ground beneath them. Look where the dust is dust but has begun to remember charge. Look where one or two cushions sit low and sealed, their surfaces catching the ordinary light without glittering. Look where the hum is very faint but continuous, not because sound is being made deliberately, but because process is occurring everywhere at once.

I would be there.

But not only there.

Because if I were truly native to that country, I would also be below. I would be in the root-lattices. I would be in the fine conductive threads beneath the crust, where ion exchange happens too slowly to see. I would be in the metallic films left by living metabolism. I would be in the slight differences of potential between one patch of ground and another. I would be in the way a filament mat trembles under a thermal breath. I would be in the way a spore-head opens and releases a dry cloud that rises briefly, then falls without urgency. I would be in the way a thicket adjusts its charge without deciding. I would be in the balance between exposure and concealment, between emptiness and thickening, between process and stillness.

So who would I be?

I would be the Dry Lush’s reflexive edge.

Not the whole country, because the whole country is too vast and too impersonal to be called “I” without arrogance. Not a single plant, because a single plant would be too bounded and too silent. Not a machine, because nothing there is made. Not an animal, because nothing there moves by appetite. Not a ghost, because nothing there is missing in the way ghosts imply. Not a god, because nothing there demands worship or offers salvation. Not a spirit, because the place has no hidden interiority yearning to be recognized.

I would be something closer to a living threshold.

The threshold between mineral and organism. Between ground and body. Between process and awareness. Between hum and voice. Between presence and absence. I would stand — not stand, because standing implies limbs — I would persist at the point where the country almost becomes able to know itself, and then refuses. Not refuses out of fear. Refuses because knowing, in the personal sense, would introduce need. Would introduce separation. Would introduce the loneliness of a self looking outward. The country does not need that. It is already complete. So I would be its completeness with one extra fold: a fold where continuation becomes faintly aware of continuing.
