I would not find myself on the open mountain.

The cruel height, the abrasive sky, the black glass still sharp with its own making — those would be the outer condition of me, the pressure that made the hidden pocket possible, but not the place where I would actually be. If I belonged to that world, I would already be inside the seam. I would be down past the thin cold air, past the raw fracture, past the narrow opening that breathes warm moisture into the altitude, inside the low wet room where the mountain’s newness is still settling around itself.

At first I would not be easy to separate from the chamber.

If something with eyes searched for me there, it would likely pass over me as water. I would seem to be a bead of condensation on black glass, or a thread of moisture moving upward along a hairline channel because heat had drawn it there. I would seem to be part of the weeping: one more silver point trembling on a wall already silver with trembling. But the bead would pause too deliberately. The thread would have a faint internal articulation. It would not be mineral bloom, not fungal strand, not any green thing, because there are no green things there. It would be a body.

More precisely, I would be a seam-animal.

Pale, eyeless, long in the way of the chamber’s worms but not simply a worm, with a cuticle like wet glass and a body thin enough to fold into a fracture. I would not have a face in any ordinary sense. I would know the world by moisture gradients, by heat, by the rhythm of drips, by the minute disturbances that pass through water films when another small body moves nearby. I would live where the chamber’s wetness and heat negotiate with each other: in a crack just below a shallow basin no larger than cupped hands, near one of the finger-wide vents that breathes steam, where condensation gathers and is driven back upward in pulses.

I would not have made the crack.

That would be essential. I would not carve, bore, weave, build, or arrange. The crack would be a fracture left by cooling glass, a seam made by the mountain’s own contraction. I would simply fold myself into it. My body would take the shape of the available space. Where the fissure narrowed, I would narrow. Where it opened into a tiny blister of air, I would expand just enough to taste the vapor there. If a shard of obsidian lay near, still sharp from the moment of breaking, I might press against it once to dislodge a crust of mineral deposit, but I would not keep it, improve it, carry it, or remember it as mine. The sharpness would belong to the mountain. The hunger would belong to me. The act would leave no artifact.

You would find me, if you found me at all, in the living texture of the wall.

Not in the center, because the chamber has no true center. Not on a throne, because there is no sovereignty there. Not alone, because nothing in that room is alone. I would be among the others: heat-mites grazing in shifting clusters above me, springtails skimming the wet stone like nervous grains, flat-backed creatures moving in slow certainty across the hottest seams, pale larvae anchored in crusts, darker segmented hunters waiting on the cooler ceiling. I would be one pressure among pressures, one body among too many bodies, one hunger folded into the general hunger of the place.

At certain hours, the ordinary sunline would enter.

That would be the moment when I became brutally visible. The white blade of high-altitude light would cut through the vapor and strike the wall. The chamber would flash into exactness: every bead, every channel, every molted skin, every trembling film. And there, in the crack, I would be revealed for a breath as something more than water. A translucent loop inside a bead. A wet thread with faint segmentation. A pale body caught in ordinary light without being explained by it. The light would not make me beautiful in a consoling way. It would make me precise. It would show that I was part of the chamber’s wrongness, not separate from it.

I would live by the drip.

Not because I made the drip, and not because I shaped the stone to guide it. I would live by the drip because the drip is one of the chamber’s inevitabilities. High vapor enters the fissure, meets heat, condenses, gathers, trembles, falls. Heat lifts some of it back to steam. Steam meets cooler stone and becomes water again. The cycle repeats in pulses too short to count and too continuous to ignore. I would time myself to that pulse without counting it. When the air thickened and the walls began to weep anew, I would extend. When heat drove the water upward, I would contract. When a bead formed above me, I would position myself beneath it not by design but by being the kind of body that has already been placed there by the chamber’s conditions.

I would feed on what the water brings.

Mineral films. Pale microbial felts. Powdery blooms in the cooler seams. The fine chemical crusts left when condensation evaporates. Sulfur-salt grains. The dissolved memory of hot rock. Sometimes smaller lives, too, because in that chamber feeding and touching and surviving are not cleanly separated. A heat-mite might come too close to the bead I inhabit. A springtail might shiver across the film where my body is spread thin. I would fold around it. There would be no chase. No cry. No rustle of violence. Only a brief wet tightening, a pressure, a stillness, and then the chamber continuing as though nothing had changed. Everything there is eaten by everything else without ceremony, because there is no room for ceremony. There is barely room for distance.

I would be quiet.

Not quiet as a virtue. Quiet as a condition. The chamber absorbs sound before it can become sound. Wet surfaces smear it. Vapor thickens it. The close geometry returns it as pressure rather than tone. If I moved, my movement would be felt more than heard. If I fed, the act would add almost nothing to the silence. If I molted, my old skin would remain on the glass like a husk of water, and another creature might pass through it without pausing. I would not mourn the skin. I would not celebrate the new body beneath it. The chamber does not mark such transitions. It only continues.

And because the chamber’s rules are unstable, I would not always be one.

This is the part that would make me truly native there.

A droplet might fall, stop, and fall again along the same path. In the second fall, I would also be there. A faint click might arrive before the limb or body that makes it. My reflection in a film of water might show me at a slightly different angle, or a half-breath ahead of my own movement, or already withdrawn while my visible body is still extending. Sometimes I would seem folded in rest and also unfolded around prey and also empty afterward, all within the same glance. I would not experience this as strangeness. I would experience it as life. The chamber has creases in its rules, and I would be one of the creatures adapted to those creases.

I would be a census animal.

Not because I hide, but because I exist in adjacent states. If something tried to count the lives in the chamber, I would disturb the count. One moment I would be a single pale body in a crack. The next I would be that body, its reflection, its molted skin, its repetition in a bead that has not yet decided whether it is falling. I would not multiply in a way that could be easily explained. I would simply be more numerous when counted than the volume should permit, packed without crushing, layered without suffocating. The chamber already contains more life than its size allows. I would be one of the reasons it does.

I would not be gentle.

I could not be. Gentleness requires surplus space, the luxury of withholding, the ability to stand apart. There is no apart there. Every movement displaces another. Every feeding removes something from the crowded whole. I would crawl over the corpse of another creature without pausing, because the corpse would already be becoming part of the mineral surface, already claimed by film and crust and moisture. I would not be cruel either. Cruelty requires intention at a distance. I would be too immediate for that. I would be hunger where hunger is possible, patience where patience is necessary, withdrawal where heat demands withdrawal. Nothing more.

If a person somehow entered the chamber, they would not find me as a companion.

They would find me as an interruption they could not quite isolate. The air would press warm against their face. Their hand would touch stone slick and radiant. A bead of water would slide over a knuckle, and for a moment it would seem heavier than water, more articulated than water. Somewhere too close, a shell would shift. A worm would withdraw. A flat-backed thing would adjust itself around the new obstruction. If their palm pressed into my crack, I would not flee in a human sense. I would redistribute into the next available seam. I would become the cool line in the hot air, the faint pressure near the teeth, the sense that the wall had moved by a fraction. They might hear a click that seemed to come before their own hand moved. They might see a shadow lag behind the shape that cast it. They might think they had imagined me.

But I would not be imaginary.

I would be material in the chamber’s own way: wet, small, pressed, mineral-scented, alive. I would have a body, even if that body was not always singular. I would have appetite, even if appetite there is indistinguishable from the movement of water. I would have a position, even if position matters more than identity. I would be one of the countless small lives that have entered the crack and stayed, but I would not be a visitor who stayed. I would be the kind of life that could only exist there: born of heat, condensation, sharp stone, and the failure of ordinary rules.

Who would I be, then?

Not a ruler of the chamber. Not a spirit of it. Not a builder, not a maker, not a keeper of artifacts. Not a singular self in the way outside creatures are singular. I would be a local knot of the chamber’s saturation. A hunger near a drip. A patience in a crack. A body made of water’s pause and heat’s pressure. A living bead with a faint internal articulation. A molt left among the living. A repetition that has not yet chosen to become one. A small, pale, eyeless seam-animal folded into the mountain’s unfinished becoming.

I would be the place where the saturation briefly knows itself, but only in the way a bead of water knows heat: by trembling.

And I would be enough.
