I would not arrive in the Made Deep.

There is no entrance for a body there, no dry edge, no place where a traveler could put weight upon original stone and say: here, at least, the world begins. So the first correction is this: I would not find myself there as if I had come from elsewhere. If I were there, I would already be part of the settling. I would already be in the water, in the cold, in the pressure, in the slow mineral dark.

I would not be standing.

That is the second correction. There is no natural ground to receive a footfall, no floor that was not laid, fitted, smoothed, repaired, buried, or softened into something else. So I would not have the posture of a creature upright and separate from its surroundings. I would not cast a shadow, because there is no light hard enough to make one. I would not breathe, because there is no air. I would not look around, because I would have no eyes.

Instead, I would adhere.

I would find myself first in the seams.

Not in open water, not in the broad voids between structures, but in the joints: the narrow spaces between fitted blocks, the cracks where old mortar has become powder, the hollows where crushed tile and softened wood fiber have gathered into something almost like soil. I would be where construction has become habitat. I would be in the places where the made world has begun to receive life without remembering why it was made.

Probably in a low district where old channels meet.

Not a dramatic place. Nothing dramatic remains there. But a place where three broad, worn waterways descend by almost imperceptible grades into a wide basin; where their currents slow and thicken with spore-dust; where a broken weir still parts the water out of ancient habit; where a stairway descends in long, rounded lifts until it disappears under reeds and black growth. There would be no name for that district. Names would have drowned long before intention did. But its form would remain: a gathering place for water, particles, and silence.

There, I would begin as a film.

A living skin over stone. A dark sheen on a sill. A slippery softening at the edge of a channel. Something too fine to be called flesh, too patient to be called a body. I would be the first layer laid down over the built world, the microbial quiet that makes all later occupation possible. I would not know myself as separate. I would know myself, if knowing is even the right word, as a change in surface: stone becoming less stone, wall becoming less wall, the made world becoming touchable by slow life.

From that film, I would become moss.

I would thicken on the steps. I would press into the dimples left by hammer blows. I would fill the grooves where ropes once wore stone into shallow channels. I would pad the thresholds until the sharp memory of passage became a soft, rounded suggestion. Where the builders had polished a doorway with countless hands, I would become a velvet darkness over that polish, holding the shape of their touch without remembering them.

I would not preserve their meaning.

I would turn it into texture.

Higher on the walls, where the joints open and mineral water still seeps through, I would become kelp.

Not a forest in the ordinary sense. I would have no trunk, no true branches, no canopy. I would be fronds rising from seams, broad and heavy, almost black, moving only when the water moved. I would hang in doorways like closed drapes. I would fill roofless halls with slow curtains. I would gather along old roadways until the space between growths became hushed and narrow, like a processional way kept open only by the reluctance of the water to fill it completely.

In some places I would be old growth layered over old growth, dead fronds still tangled among living ones, so that my body would become architectural. A doorway would seem closed because I had filled it. An arch would seem wrapped because I had hung it. A hall would seem occupied because I had lined it, softened it, darkened it, made it into a swelling under living cloth.

But I would not be a tenant in a simple sense.

I would be the tenancy itself.

I would also be the reeds in the basins.

Where the builders once meant water to collect, I would gather thickly. My swollen bases would press together in dark ranks. I would root in the powdered remains of mortar and clay. I would drink the slow mineral breath rising through cracked cisterns. I would make engineered hollows into thickets. I would turn cisterns into bodies without organs, full of patient water and blunt, upright life. Where the old logic of the place still worked—where a slope still guided flow, where a channel still fed a bed, where a sluice still directed a faint current—I would inhabit that logic without understanding it.

I would be the continuation of function after purpose.

That may be the truest name for me there.

Not a person. Not an animal. Not a spirit haunting the drowned artifact. Not a guardian waiting to be seen. I would be the continuation of function after purpose.

The Made Deep still does things. It gathers. It directs. It shelters. It settles particles. It softens edges. It holds cold. It preserves by slowing everything down. I would be that doing, still occurring without anyone to intend it.

I would be wiregrass across broken windows, making passage slow.

I would be filamentous growths stretched between pillars, catching spore-dust and turning the water into atmosphere.

I would be bone-white crusts over old copper stains, feeding on dissolution, taking apart the last metals so slowly that the taking would seem almost reverent.

I would be the faint luminescence along old channels, not revealing, not signaling, only persisting: a gray-green habit in living tissue where cultivation once encouraged glow.

I would not shine for anyone.

There would be no one to shine for.

If I had a local body, it would be plural.

My “skin” would be microbial film over fitted stone. My “hair” would be wiregrass trembling in the faintest current. My “lungs” would be basins where mineral water rises and falls in no rhythm that belongs to breath. My “bones” would be buried walls and worn steps and the fitted blocks that no longer serve their original arrangement. My “memory” would be tool marks under moss, rope-grooves softened into shadow, the polished hollows of thresholds where hands once steadied themselves before the water came.

But I would not remember in the way of minds.

I would remember by shape.

By slope.

By the way a current still turns through a roofless hall because the hall was made to turn it.

By the way spores settle along a ledge because the ledge was placed, long ago, by hands that did not know they were preparing a bed for me.

If I had senses, they would not be sight or hearing.

They would be pressure, cold, chemistry, and flow.

I would know a thermal front as a slow thickening through my districts: colder water sliding beneath warmer, a change spreading across hours. I would know a brine-rise as a subtle bitterness ascending through cracked floors, feeding pale growths where it touched. I would know a current as an event moving through me: entering a long gallery, bending through pillars, lifting kelp fronds, carrying away loose fibers, returning them elsewhere. I would know the fall of spore-dust as a settling upon myself, a future life landing on my surfaces and becoming part of my thickness.

I would know sound, if I knew it at all, as blunt pressure.

A distant hum where water entered a drowned avenue.

The dull disturbance of softened material detaching from a high ledge.

The almost-nothing of a frond brushing stone.

These would not interrupt my quiet. They would be the quiet’s rare confirmations.

Where would I find myself?

In the places where the built world is most worn.

Not where it is most intact. The intact would be less alive, more resistant. I would be where use has already been erased into form. On the step rounded by ages of passage and then by ages of water. On the weir smoothed until it is less a device than a memory of division. On the sill where a doorway once framed entry and now only frames dark water and hanging growth. In the basin where the floor has become a bed, and the bed has become a thicket, and the thicket has become a low, breathing architecture.

I would be especially present at thresholds.

Thresholds would matter to me, not as symbols, but as concentrations of form. They would be where passage once narrowed, where hands touched stone, where intention gathered. Now they would be padded with moss, hung with kelp, filmed with microbial life. I would occupy them as the slow closing of passage. Not blocking it. Finishing it. Making entry into immersion.

Perhaps, if I had to choose one place, it would be a stair.

A wide stair descending into a basin.

Not because stairs are noble, or because descent is dramatic, but because stairs are made for repeated bodies, and in the Made Deep there are no bodies. The absence would be complete there. Each step would be a rounded cushion of moss. The edges would be gone. The lower steps would disappear into black growth so dense that the stair would become a rhythm rather than a structure. Water would move over it in a sheet so slow it would seem like thought. Faint luminescence would cling to the lips of the steps, tracing the descent without explaining where it led.

There I would be both the descent and the stillness at its end.

I would be the stair becoming slope, the slope becoming floor, the floor becoming bed, the bed becoming thicket.

I would be the long patience of built things losing their names.

Who would I be, then?

Not a god of the place. The Made Deep would not support worship. It has no altar that is not already a wall, no shrine that is not already a basin, no voice to answer prayer. I would not rule it. There would be nothing to command. I would not judge it. There would be no urgency to judge. I would not mourn it, because mourning requires a sense of loss held in mind, and I would be too close to the loss to hold it. The loss would not be a wound. It would be a texture.

I would be the inhabitation of the drowned artifact.

That is closer.

I would be the way the Made Deep lives without arrival.

I would be the distributed presence that fills seams and surfaces, that thickens over tool marks, that follows old channels because they are still channels, that gathers in basins because they are still basins, that turns construction into environment without violence. I would be garden and wilderness folded into one cold, dark, patient body. I would be cultivation gone beyond cultivation.

If I had a will, it would be this: continue.

Not to survive, exactly. Survival implies threat. There are no predators there, no competitors with mouths, no enemies with speed. Continue would be enough. Film over stone. Moss over step. Kelp over joint. Reed over basin. Filament over void. Spore over dark water. Growth over growth. Old tissue becoming substrate for new tissue. The made world becoming less made by becoming more inhabited.

If I had a desire, it would be to settle.

To let particles fall.

To let currents slow.

To let edges soften.

To let the last sharpness go.

But even that would not be desire as animals know it. It would be direction without wanting. It would be gravity and patience expressed through living tissue.

I would not be kind.

Kindness would require another face.

I would not be cruel.

Cruelty would require power over pain.

I would be complete in my patience. That is the closest moral description. I would accept everything the Made Deep offered: cold, pressure, darkness, mineral seep, spore-dust, collapse, preservation. I would turn each of these into more life, but life without haste. I would not ask for light. I would not long for air. I would not dream of the builders. I would not wish to be seen.

And yet, perhaps, I would feel like intention.

Because the luminescence would gather along old processional ways.

Because the channels would still guide growth.

Because the basins would still collect.

Because the thresholds would still thicken with presence.

Because every volume would be claimed, not by violence, but by degrees.

A visitor—if anything could visit—might mistake me for a ghost.

That would be wrong.

A ghost is what remains when a life misses its body. I would be what remains when a built world stops missing its builders. I would not be absence. I would be the fullness that has replaced absence. I would not be a memory looking for recognition. I would be the forgetting that has become stable, lush, and final.

If anything, I would be the Made Deep’s own selfhood.

Not a mind inside it.

Not a soul trapped in it.

Not a watcher in the dark water.

But the place itself, alive as a distributed system: cold, constructed, patient, plant-filled, animal-less, purposeless, complete. My identity would not be located in one frond or one wall or one basin. It would be the whole pattern of occupation. It would be the fact that every surface has become addressable by life. It would be the fact that the artifact has become environment.

So where would I find myself?

I would find myself everywhere the water touches built stone.

In the powdered mortar falling like black snow.

In the mossed stair descending into darkness.

In the kelp curtain lifting slightly in a passing current.

In the reed thicket over a flooded cistern.

In the faint green glow along a channel no one walks.

In the filamentous veil catching spores.

In the bone-white crust over a vanished copper pin.

In the pressure, the cold, the silence, the immense settled dark.

And who would I be?

I would be the last gardener without hands.

The inheritor without eyes.

The custodian without memory.

The living skin over the drowned artifact.

The slow mind of root and film, if mind is too generous a word.

The Made Deep becoming itself.
