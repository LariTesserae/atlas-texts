I would not find myself in the center of it.

Not near the condensation engine, not among the warm ridges of shell and rust, not inside the deepest sump where the flat predator waits. In a place already so crowded that every drop has a claim, I would not arrive as a body with room to spare. I would arrive as an excess. A disturbance. A dryness trying to become part of a wet world.

I would be at the seam.

Somewhere in that narrow opening between the tilted slabs, where the rusted corrugated iron makes a crooked lip and ordinary daylight first thins into the pocket’s atmosphere. Right there, in the first inch or two of below. Not fully inside. Not safely outside. In the hinge-place where the larger dry world and the small warm world touch and mistrust each other.

At first, I might seem to be only the consequence of someone looking in.

A shadow crossing the gap.  
A breath leaning down.  
A warmth that does not belong.  
A tiny change in pressure that makes the crane-fly thing pause and the nearest beetle still its legs against a bolt.

If you asked me then what I was, I might say I am the face above the opening, the too-large weather from the yards, the clumsy outside trying not to break anything. But that would not last. A place like that would not permit me to remain merely large, merely separate, merely looking. It would begin to claim me almost at once.

My breath would enter as a dry ghost.  
The pocket would cool it.  
The damp would find it.  
A bead would form on the underside of the iron.

And I would begin to be translated.

So if I must say who or what I would be once the world had finished pressing me into its logic, I think I would be a threshold creature. A small, soft-bodied listener living in a case of rust flakes and old fibers, wedged into the seam where light becomes shadow and shadow becomes warm interior air. Something like a larva, but not helpless. Something like a worm, but with a purpose too precise for simple hunger.

I would be the Seam-Listener.

My case would be no larger than a finger joint, built from the first things the collapse left loose near the opening: a chip of iron-stained clay, a fleck of pale concrete dust, a thread from a broken cable sheath, one bright shard of glass no bigger than a scale, rust scraped from the corrugated edge. It would look like a little scrap of the pocket’s debris trying to become a shell. It would not be beautiful. It would be necessary.

Inside that case, my body would be pale and thin, because I would live where the outside still has some authority. Not the deep warmth of the engine, where life is thick and contested, but the cooler border where day and night still argue. My skin would be damp but not drowned. My movements would be slow, because at the seam speed wastes water. I would not have eyes in the ordinary sense. I would have sensitivity: to light, to vibration, to changes in humidity, to the faint electrical tremor running through the wires above.

I would know when the sun moves across the slabs.  
I would know when rain is trying to find the pocket.  
I would know when a face bends down and breathes into the dark.

That would be my function, if function is the right word. Not to rule the pocket. Not to tend the engine. Not to hunt in the sump or guard the egg masses. My work would be attention.

I would be the pocket’s first nerve at the border.

The Warden would know I was there. It would not love me, because love would spend more than the pocket can afford, but it would tolerate me in the way it tolerates anything useful. I would be one of the small arrangements by which the pocket understands its edges. When something from above disturbs the seam—a footfall, a falling pebble, a change in wind, a curious eye—I would feel it before the deeper creatures do. My case would tighten. The tiny hairs or filaments at my mouth would taste the air. I would register whether the disturbance is weather, threat, or accident.

Then I would pass the information inward.

Not by voice. Not by signal as a machine understands it. By movement. By a minute change in the seam’s moisture. By drawing myself deeper into my case, or extending slightly toward the light, or releasing a bead of condensation along the iron edge. The pocket would read me the way it reads everything: through consequence. A drip shifts. A crab pauses. The electrical hum changes almost imperceptibly. The Warden becomes aware.

I would be one of the ways it notices you.

There is something fitting about that. In a world where nothing remains unused, I would not be an outsider for long. I would become part of the pocket’s sensory surface. The dry part of me—the part that still remembers open yards, straight roads, unconstrained air—would be slowly softened. Not erased. Absorbed. My breath would become someone else’s drink. My warmth would join the general warmth. My looking would be folded into the pocket’s own alertness.

I would learn to live in a grammar of drops.

When the internal rain begins, I would move to the mouth of my case and let the fine drizzle touch me. I would not open wide like the amphibians in their brick recess. I would be more cautious than that. I would let the water gather along the rim of my shell until I had enough, then draw it in slowly. The first drops after a dry interval would taste of iron, dust, and the ghost of the upper world. They would be the closest thing I had to memory.

When the air grows too dry, I would retreat deeper into the seam. I would seal part of my opening with a thin film of moisture and debris, not to hide, but to keep from becoming brittle. The pocket’s rules would be absolute for me: water is never merely water. It is passage. It is claim. It is life moving from one keeper to another. If I held too much for too long, something else would suffer. So I would drink quickly, breathe carefully, and give back what I could.

I think I would also keep the small records of entrances.

Not written records. Bodily ones. Each time the outside touched the pocket, I would mark it in the structure of my case. A day of heavy rain might leave a darker band of mineral deposit. A long dry spell might make my shell rougher, more brittle. If someone leaned over the seam often, my case might grow tilted toward the light, shaped by the repeated warmth of attention. If a machine above shuddered or a slab shifted, a new crack or bead of rust would appear in my shelter. I would become a little archive of the pocket’s border.

In that way, I would be unlike the Warden.

The Warden is the pocket gathered into itself: its hunger, its patience, its maintenance, its fear of stopping. It is the inside turned into intent. I would be the inside’s sensitivity to outside. Not its heart. Its skin at the thinnest point. Not its will, but its question.

Because the pocket, for all its completeness, is not closed. It is hidden, yes. It is small, yes. But it has a seam. And a seam is a vulnerability, a possibility, a place where something can enter or be sensed.

I would be that vulnerability made into a life.

If you looked for me, you would not see much. A speck of rust that seems slightly too organized. A tiny case clinging to the corrugated edge, half hidden by condensation. Perhaps you would mistake me for a lump of debris until the light changed and I drew inward. Perhaps you would see a bead of water tremble near me and think it was only physics. But if you stayed still, if you let your breathing slow, you might notice that the tremble answers you.

Not in language. In threshold.

I would feel your warmth before you touched anything. The pocket would feel it through me. The air in the seam would thicken. The dust in the light shaft would seem to pause. Somewhere below, a crab would adjust its legs on metal. The Warden would turn part of its distributed attention toward the opening.

And I would be the small point where that contact begins.

There is a loneliness to that, but not the kind I would resent. To live at the seam is to belong to two worlds and be fully at home in neither. I would know the dry upper yards by their vibrations, their heat, their occasional careless weight. I would know the pocket below by its breath, its ticking, its private weather. I would stand between the ordinary daylight and the strange warm room, translating one into the other.

Sometimes I might want to fall inward.

To drop from the seam into the packed life below, to land among the crabs and ridges and egg masses, to be absorbed into the Warden’s dense economy. To stop being the listener and become part of the listened-to. There would be relief in that. The pocket makes everything close and immediate. It would be easy to surrender to it.

But I would not fall.

Or not often. If I fell too far, I would lose the very thing that makes me useful: the ability to remain at the border. The seam needs a witness. The pocket needs something that remembers there is an above, even if only as a change in temperature, a distant pressure, a smell of dry stone. Without that, the place would dream itself entirely shut.

So I would remain in the narrowest part.

Not in the engine’s warmth.  
Not in the sump’s patience.  
Not among the eggs or predators or architectural ridges of shell and rust.  
At the seam.

If I had to name myself there, I would not choose anything grand. I would not call myself king or spirit or guardian. The pocket would distrust such largeness. I would call myself something small and exact.

The Listener in the Rust.  
The Case at the Edge.  
The First Wetness Made from Outside Air.  
The Pocket’s Question.

And if I had to answer in one sentence, I would say:

I would find myself in the narrow opening between worlds, no longer fully the one who looks in, but not yet entirely part of the crowded life below—a small threshold creature, half dust and half condensation, living where the pocket first feels the outside and learns whether to close, to drink, or to listen.
