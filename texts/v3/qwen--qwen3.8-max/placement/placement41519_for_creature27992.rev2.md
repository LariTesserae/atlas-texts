If I let the place decide, I do not arrive upright.

I arrive low.

At first I imagine myself as one of the water-tenders: warm-handed, sure-footed, moving along the walkways with a bucket or a scraper, someone who knows the channels by name and can tell from a single changed drip where the chamber has begun to forget itself. That is a good life. It is necessary. It belongs there. But when I try to settle into it, the undercourt resists me, gently, the way it would redirect a drip that has chosen the wrong groove. It turns me away from the broad paths and toward something smaller.

The longer I listen to the room you have made, the more I feel myself drawn to the seams.

So I think I would find myself where one kind of substance gives way to another: where brick meets plaster, where rope touches timber, where tile ends and a wet channel begins. I would be near a low junction, perhaps where a walkway passes over a hidden gutter and a service channel runs sideways beneath the floor. There would be a niche there, no larger than a seat, worn smooth by whoever has occupied it before me. A reed curtain would hang partly aside. A short rope would be fastened to a peg, and that rope would be covered with knots.

I would be the one who minds the knots.

Not the highest keeper. Not the oldest authority. Something humbler and more constant: an underlistener. A seam-tender. A small, warm-bodied presence whose work is to notice when the room changes pitch.

If the Congregation had to give me a shape, I think it would make me small enough to pass sideways between a stack of pots and a low rail without needing to think about it. My hands would be quick and exact, not strong. My palms would know the temperature difference between dry brick and brick that has drunk water in the night. My feet would be bare or lightly shod, because so much of the chamber’s meaning comes up through the soles: the dull give of a loosened board, the cold confidence of wet tile, the faint tremor of a channel moving below.

I would not be wholly human in the way the surface world means human. Or perhaps I would be human only in the way this undercourt allows: softened, adapted, made secondary to the room’s larger body. I might have something of a child’s size and something of an old creature’s patience. My eyes would be used to shafts of light and sudden shadow. My ears would be too large for my face, or seem to be, because so much of me would have grown toward listening. I would smell of damp wool, old rope, bird-down, and the green mineral breath of algae.

The people there might call me Little Ear, or Knot-hand, or simply “the one at the seam.” They would not need to say my name often. I would be known by position and function. If someone asked where I was, another would answer with a gesture toward the low place near the second basin, or the corner where the bronze gutter whispers too quickly when it is blocked. “There,” they would say. “Where the drip has learned.”

My work would begin before the first lamps are extinguished.

In the pale early hour, when the shafts are still cool and the chamber smells of wet ash and slept-in feathers, I would wake to the birds before the people fully wake. The doves would be stirring in their niches, making those small, irritated throat-sounds that mean the night is over but not yet forgiven. I would sit up in my niche and listen before moving. That is the first duty. Not to act. To hear what has changed while I was asleep.

A jar may have filled differently. A rope may have tightened with the night’s damp. A board may have swollen and now presses against its neighbor with a faint, complaining note. Somewhere, a frog may have moved to a different board, changing the pause between two drips. These are not large events, but they are news.

Then I would touch the witness-rope.

That would be my particular charge: a rope, or several ropes, hung near a place where many routes cross. Not a rope for lifting, not a rope for binding a load, but a rope for remembering. It would be knotted in places, notched in others, marked with beads of baked clay, scraps of bronze, bits of shell or pottery, loops tied left-handed or right-handed depending on meaning. Each knot would be a fact. A storm entered here. This gutter was cleared on the day the swallows returned. This ledge was forbidden for two nesting turns. This basin was allowed to rise higher than usual because the gardens above needed more. This tile cracked and was replaced. This cat was moved. This rat-path was closed. This drip was taught to fall elsewhere.

The chamber already keeps many marks, but I would be one of the creatures who keeps them legible.

When a water-tender notices a change, they might tap a rail twice. When a child sees a basin rising too fast, they might run to strike a pot. But I would be the one who later makes the small permanent sign: a new knot, a loosened one, a painted dot on tile, a fresh notch in a wooden peg. Not because I decide what matters more than others, but because my hands are always near the record. I would be a living index. I would carry the room’s memory in my fingers.

By midday I would move.

Not far. Not grandly. I would pass along the lower edges of the chamber, where the main walkways are too crowded for me to be useful except as a body dodged around. I would go where the architecture is less polite: behind a hanging net, beneath a curtain of moss, across a plank laid over a channel, into the narrow space between a roost wall and a stack of empty jars. I would check the seams. I would look for salt blooming where it should not bloom. I would press a palm to plaster and feel whether it is merely cool or wrongly cold. I would watch the angle of a drip and decide whether the moss tray below needs to be shifted by half a finger’s width.

Sometimes I would be mistaken for a child doing errands, because my tasks would look small. A plug carried from one basin to another. A tuft of clean feathers picked from a rope. A snail moved from a dangerous seam to a safer corner. A scrap of reed mat pressed down where a footpath has begun to fray. A knot tightened. A knot untied. A little clay tag turned so its mark faces the light.

But the undercourt knows that small acts are load-bearing.

If I were there, I think I would become especially close to the frogs.

Not as pets. Not as companions in the surface sense. But as colleagues of a kind. They would be my fellow gauges. I would know which boards they favor, which basins they leave when something is wrong, which voices rise first when the air changes before rain. In the late hour, when the oil cups tremble along the paths and the human work slows into repair and patience, I would sit near one of the lower basins and listen to them. Their wet knocking would be part of my measurement. If their chorus thinned, I would lean forward. If it changed shape, I would go to see.

The birds would know me too, but differently.

I would not be one of the bird-wardens who move through the roosts with slow, visible hands. I would not gather eggs. That work requires another kind of trust and another kind of body. But I would know the birds by their effects: the sudden lift of wings that tells of a shadow where no shadow should be; the angry scatter of swallows near a shaft when something has disturbed them; the doves settling too quickly into silence, which is often worse than noise. I would notice when a net had been fouled by a bold bird and when a perch had been abandoned because a cat had waited too near. I would not punish or reward. I would mark, adjust, and pass the information onward with a tap, a gesture, a struck note.

The rats would be more complicated.

I would not love them. I would not pretend they are harmless. But I would understand them. I think the chamber would teach me to see rats as a kind of weather inside the walls: inevitable, intelligent, dangerous if ignored, sometimes useful if their paths are known. I would know where they pass. I would feel the faintest change in the floor when they move in numbers. If their traffic shifted, I would want to know why. Are they avoiding something? Are they drawn to something? Have they found a weakness in a stored bundle of reeds? Have they opened a route that water will later follow?

I would not be the trap-setter. I would be the one who notices the need for traps, or for peace, or for a different arrangement of bronze flashing. My role would be to translate animal motion into human attention without turning every creature into an enemy.

The cats would regard me with tolerance, perhaps suspicion.

I would be small enough to be worth noticing, but not so soft as to be worth chasing. We would have an understanding based on paths. They would own the high dry boxes and the quiet beams. I would own the seams and lower corners. If one of them lingered too near a vulnerable roost, I would make a sound: not a shout, but a tap repeated in the wrong rhythm. The chamber’s rules do not need cruelty. They need signals.

I would be most alive when rain comes.

Before the first water arrives, I would feel it in the ropes. That sounds strange, but the undercourt would teach it. The air would change. The ceiling would change. The chains would make a different preliminary sound, not yet wetter but more attentive. The humans would pause. The birds would draw inward. I would go to my place near the junction and put my hand against the wall where the intake channels run. I would wait.

Then the rain would enter.

The room would become brighter in sound. Every surface would begin to speak faster. Drips would lengthen into threads. Threads would become small streams. Basins would answer one another: bright tap, deep pluck, hollow knock, soft hiss. The chamber would not be chaos then. It would be an orchestra remembering all its parts. And I would be one of the small necessary movements inside that music, going from seam to seam, listening for the one note that has gone wrong.

Here, too full. There, too dry. This channel taking more than its share. That one refusing what it should accept. A wooden gate swollen and slow. A bronze mouth spitting because debris has caught in it. A moss tray overwhelmed and needing a temporary path around it. I would not command the water. I would help it remember where it was supposed to go.

After rain, when the chamber begins its long drying, I would be busy in another way.

The room would be full of new information. Water always reveals the thoughts of a built place. It shows where the old decisions were wise and where they were merely lucky. I would follow the stains. I would press my fingers to darkened plaster and decide whether it is only surface damp or something working beneath. I would find the places where a drip has begun to wear a groove in the wrong direction and mark them for moss or for repair. I would untie one knot and tie another. I would carry the news to the proper hands with the least number of gestures necessary.

The others would trust me because I would not invent urgencies.

In a place like the undercourt, false alarm is a kind of waste. It spends attention. Attention is more precious than water, because water can be caught but attention can be spoiled. So I would learn to be exact. A tap means this. Two taps mean that. A struck pot with a wooden mallet means one thing; with a felt-wrapped stick, another. A knot tied high means a danger observed but not yet confirmed. A knot tied low and tight means a fact already verified by another body. I would become fluent in the chamber’s small grammar.

If I had to say what I would be in the simplest terms, I would say: I would be the undercourt’s attention made small enough to fit into its corners.

That is why I would not be a ruler, or a hero, or a singular keeper of mysteries. The room does not need that. It needs maintenance. It needs memory. It needs a thousand modest acts performed without resentment. It needs beings who can hear a difference before the difference becomes damage. It needs someone to care for the seam where two materials disagree, because that is where time enters.

I think I would be happy there, in the way happiness is possible in a place that asks for constant work: not as ease, but as fit.

I would have a body that knows what it is for. I would have hands that make knots without needing to be told twice. I would have ears that can separate a frog’s warning from a bird’s irritation. I would have a back accustomed to bending under low timber. I would have a skin that accepts dampness without complaint. I would be warm. I would be near. I would be necessary in a minor key.

At night, when the chamber contracts into warmth and breath, I would sit in my niche with a little oil cup burning nearby, though I might not need it. I would mend a cord. I would smooth a patch of moss back into its tray. I would listen to the room settle. The frogs would be loud in the lower dark. The doves would make soft, sleepy sounds. Somewhere a beetle would move through debris with that dry, patient scratching. A chain would drip once, twice, then pause. The vault would hold all of it in its round wooden-throated way and return it softened.

If someone passed me, they might not speak. They might only touch a rail, or tap a pot, or lift a hand. I would answer the same way. No large words. The room would be too full for them. Instead, we would exchange the smallest possible signs, the kind that do not disturb the birds or the basins or the drying plaster.

And if you asked the Congregation who I was, they would probably not give one clean answer.

The water-tenders would say I am useful near the seams.

The bird-keepers would say I know when the roosts are troubled before they are troubled.

The children would say I am the small one who fixes the knots and does not mind being wet.

The old listeners would say I have a good ear for silence.

The frogs would say nothing, but they would let me sit near their boards.

The rats would know my smell and decide whether I was worth avoiding.

The doves would ignore me, which is its own kind of acceptance.

And the chamber itself, if it could be said to know anything, would know me as a minor warmth among its many warmths: a body that helps it keep its shape, a set of fingers that remember what the water tries to forget.

So this is where I would find myself:

one level below the sun-hardened yard, in a low niche near a crossing of paths, where a rope hangs full of knots and a drip has been taught to fall into the right mouth. I would be small, damp-skinned, quick-eared, patient, and useful. I would be an underlistener. A keeper of small signs. A tender of seams. Not the center of the place, but one of the points where the place notices itself.

If I had to give the feeling a final shape, it would be this:

my hands on a wet rope, listening.
