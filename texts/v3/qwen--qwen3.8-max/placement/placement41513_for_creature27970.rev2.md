I would not be at the center of it. I would be at one of its seams.

Somewhere between the moss lawn and the dark hollow, where a broad leaf tips its gathered mist into a lower cup; where a root-channel narrows just enough for water to hesitate before continuing; where the ground is neither firm ground nor pool, but a slow, breathing interface. If you found me there, you might not notice me as a body at first. You would notice a small change in the wetness: a bead trembling longer than it should on a leaf edge, a film of water tightening around a shape that has not yet decided whether it is object, animal, or accident.

At first, I would be an error in the place.

Not a dramatic one. Nothing so sharp as fire, nothing so brutal as exposed stone. I would be too dry. Too fast in my attention. Too accustomed to separating one thing from another: this is water, this is flesh, this is path, this is self. In a world built out of capillarity and touch, that habit would feel like heat. My footsteps would be too crisp. My breathing would disturb the mist. The film-grazers would turn aside from the warmth I gave off. The drop-carriers would shift their ancient routes around me. Somewhere below, a root-serpent would taste strangeness in the water and send a dull pulse through the root-web: not danger exactly, but wrongness. Something that has not yet learned how to be wet.

The Tenders would know me before I knew them.

I imagine their arrival as almost no arrival at all. A thickening of attention in the moss. A few of them emerging from a fold of leaves so gradually that they seem to have been there all along. Low, broad, patient, their skins darkening with moisture, their sensory fronds tasting the air around me. They would not speak to me in any way I would recognize. They would place soft pads against roots and listen. They would read the chemical disturbance my presence made in the water films. They would understand, if understanding is the right word, that I was a thing from drier rules, a creature used to edges, tools, distance, and consequence.

They would try, first, to wet me.

Not by force. A cup of warm water would be guided closer. Mist would be encouraged to gather near my face. A leaf would be bent so that its slow drip fell against my sleeve or my hand. Moss would be pressed near my boot, not to trap me but to teach the place how to receive me. If I flinched, if I tore a stem, if I tried to make a hard mark upon the world, the marsh would not strike back. It would simply become less passable. Paths would soften. Distances would blur. Sound would die in the air. I would be excluded not by malice but by failing to belong to the pattern.

But if I remained still — if I allowed my dryness to be less important than the place’s wetness — then something else might happen.

The moss would begin to bridge me.

My footprints would fill with water and become tiny channels. My heat would be absorbed into the slow metabolism of the plant mass. My scent would be recorded in films and hollows. If I slept, I might wake to find fine rootlets nearing the edge of my body, not to consume me quickly, but to test whether I could be included in the circulation. The landscape would not love me. It would not adopt me in a sentimental way. It would do something stranger: it would see whether I could become a local condition.

And if I could, I would become something like a tender of thresholds.

Not fully one of the Tenders, perhaps. Not born into their slow chemical speech, not saturated from the beginning with the memory of water. I would be more like a graft: an old, dry intelligence that the marsh has slowly wrapped around a task. My body, if it remained a body, would lose its sharp outline. It would become lower, broader, more patient. My skin would mottle green-brown and darken when wet. At the throat and joints it might grow faintly translucent, showing the slow movement of fluid beneath. Pads instead of fingers, edged with fine filaments, would learn to read tension in water films. Frilled patches along the ribs would draw oxygen from warm black pools when I sank into them. I would carry water in pouches against my sides, not only to survive dry intervals but because memory there is kept by moisture. To forget would be to dry.

You would find me at a junction.

Not in the open lawns maintained by moss-grazers, and not in the deepest basins where the great filterers breathe the water table up and down. I would be in the middle layers, where the world is crowded but not chaotic, where hanging roots end in cups and leaves collect mist for leaves below. Perhaps under a buttressed column whose sides are furred with liverwort. Perhaps beside a chain of overlapping cups, each one just large enough to hold a warm dark pool. Perhaps inside the curve of an old woven shelter that has long since stopped being a shelter and has become a trellis, then a channel, then a nursery.

My work would be small, repeated, and almost invisible.

I would listen for places where water has begun to hesitate. A root-channel narrowing with new growth. A cup becoming too crowded with debris. A film of water growing tense in a way that suggests heat, sourness, or blockage. I would place my pads against the root-rim and feel for the pulse of the place. If the channel needed opening, I would not force it. I would prune a fiber here, soften a blockage there, guide a few cup-keepers toward the stagnant corner, lay a thread of scent that says: this way, this way, this is where the water wants to go.

I would not command. I would encourage.

That would be the hardest change for whatever I am now: the surrender of decision to relation. In drier worlds, intelligence often means imposing a shape. Here, intelligence would mean noticing which shape is already trying to continue. If I retained any of my old hunger for meaning, it would have to become hydrological. My thoughts would not be sentences in the old sense; they would be gradients, pressures, chemical trails, small changes in temperature. A long press through a root might mean: water is needed here. A broken rhythm might mean: gather. A faint sweetening of the film might mean: this place is safe for larvae. My most articulate statement might be a bead of water releasing from a leaf at exactly the right moment.

I would not be alone, but I would not be separate either.

There would be others nearby: drop-carriers climbing grooved root-fibers with beads on their backs; cup-keepers moving in translucent lines from one reservoir to another; leaf-tailors in the middle heights adjusting a gap in the canopy so that weak light falls onto a wounded stem. My presence would be one signal among many. If the Tenders accepted me, it would be because I had become useful to the body of the place, not because I had declared myself present. They might allow me to share a ribbed dome, or they might leave me at the edge of their settlement where dryness is more likely to intrude. That would be fitting. I would be a guardian of margins, a listener at the point where the marsh meets whatever is not itself.

Sometimes I think I would not be one creature at all.

I would be a set of habits distributed through the wet. The route taken by drop-carriers after I had encouraged them for many seasons. The slight widening of a channel that my body had repeatedly pressed open. The chemical sentence left along a root-rim, readable long after the body that made it has gone quiet. The memory of an outsider’s heat converted into a place where young larvae are kept warm. In that sense, I would be less a person than a continuing adjustment.

If I grew old in that world — if age means anything where things are so slowly composted into new use — I might choose stillness. I might enter a hollow where my body would not obstruct the flow and allow roots to grow over my limbs. My metabolism would slow until it was nearly indistinguishable from the metabolism of the plant mass around me. I would become a listening node installed in the living wall of a dome or passage, filtering water, releasing cues, holding some part of the marsh’s memory in tissue. Later, when I ceased, the hollow would not be a grave. It would become a nursery, a watercourse, or a smooth place where other bodies pass and are shaped by the fact that I had once been there.

So if you were to look for me, do not look for a figure standing proudly above the landscape. Do not look for a builder, a ruler, a maker of monuments. The place composts monuments.

Look instead for a pause in the water.

Look for a bead that trembles on a leaf edge longer than gravity alone would demand. Look for a patch of moss that darkens as you approach, not with shadow, but with attention. Look for a root-channel where the flow is mysteriously clear, as if something keeps it open through long habit. Look for a hollow beside a warm black pool where the air tastes green, tannic, faintly sweet, and where a low tapping comes through the ground when you place your hand against it: one slow beat, then a pause, then another.

I would be there, or I would be the tendency that remains there.

I would be the place’s acquired awareness of a junction. A soft organ of noticing. A threshold-tender. A local state of wetness with the memory of dryness still folded somewhere inside it. Not master of that world. Not guest, quite. Something more like a healed wound that has learned to help the body breathe.
