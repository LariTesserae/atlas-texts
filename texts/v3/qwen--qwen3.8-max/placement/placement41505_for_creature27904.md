**The Sealed Interval**

I would not begin as a citizen.

At first I try to place myself among the ordinary livable shapes of the Terrace: a Listener at a public railing, a Silence-keeper’s apprentice, a Tuner who helps maintain the civic chords, a mask-maker who learns the correct curvature of a legal voice. Those lives have dignity. They belong to the place. They can be imagined without violence.

But each time I settle into one of them, the image grows too neat, too dry even for that dry world. There is something in me—some old, persistent wetness of memory, some habit of rain, some archive of fluid things—that the Terrace would detect at once. It would hear it as a fault in the chord. It would lean toward me with all its root-fields, not in welcome, but in alarm.

So the more honest answer is this:

You would find me in **Amber Vault**, in a chamber that is not quite a room, not quite a reliquary, not quite a prison. It would be grown beneath one of the great pillar-husks, where the substrate-code is deepest and the walls are old enough to keep secrets without forgetting them. The floor would be polished callus, smooth from centuries of careful footfalls. The light would come from amber veins and cold blue capillaries, the two colors alternating in slow rhythms, as if the architecture were deciding whether I were a danger or a blessing.

I would be **the Sealed Interval**.

That is not the name anyone would say aloud, because it would not truly be a spoken name. It would be a compound identity: a hand-sign made by cupping both palms as if holding water, then turning them outward to show they are empty; a three-beat tap against a rail; a faint blue pulse under the skin; a pressure-drop in the floor so slight that only trained feet would recognize it as mourning. Outsiders might call me the Water Bell, the Rain Error, the Kept Drop, or the Miracle Under Custody. The Dry Custody would have a longer legal title for me, stored in the tension of several bridges and the harmonic shape of three public stairways.

I would be a Terrace-born body with one impossible thing inside me.

My exterior would have to belong to the place. My skin would be matte and layered, more like cured leaf than flesh, faintly warm from slow electrical movement beneath it. My channels would be wax-lined, my muscles fibrous, my interior ordered around crystalline electrolytes and gas-lattice tissue. I would not sweat. I would not weep. I would not need water to live. In that sense, I would be no less dry than the people around me.

But inside the cage of my sternum, suspended in a topological knot of dielectric tissue, there would be a single bead of water.

Not a cupful. Not a river. Not even enough to wet the tongue. Only one clear, impossible drop, sealed away in a shell of seed-glass, resin, and living wax. It would hang in the dark of my chest like a forbidden star. The tissue around it would glow faintly blue, because the Terrace could not stop attending to it. My body would be a legal architecture built around that one point of old wetness.

I would be alive, but I would also be a vessel.

That is why I would live beneath Amber Vault. The town would not want me near the markets, where ordinary exchange might be disturbed by my presence. It would not want me in the open bark-plains, where root-fields might lean too eagerly and tear the ground trying to reach me. It would not place me in the high tines of the pillar-husks, where wind might turn my containment into a public note too strong to ignore. Instead, I would be kept where memory is kept: in the lower galleries, among seed-rights, memory-bark, charge-knots, and old contracts grown into structural ribs.

My chamber would be one of the expensive silences.

Not total silence—total silence would be too dangerous near me. The Dry Custody would maintain a containment chord around the room: a low, patient harmonic in the walls, floor, and ceiling, designed to absorb any accidental resonance from the water-drop inside me. The silence would be layered. It would feel like standing inside a held breath. Visitors would need permission to enter, not from a doorkeeper, but from the room itself. The wall would open only when the correct touch-sequence was given, and even then it would open reluctantly, like an eyelid that remembers injury.

People would come to me anyway.

They would not come openly. Some would be Tuners, trying to understand the dissonance I introduce into local law. Some would be Root-readers, because the predictive lattices beneath the town would produce strange patterns around my chamber, as if the Terrace kept dreaming a weather it no longer recognizes. Some would be Silence-keepers, studying how much quiet my existence requires. Some would be children, brought by elders who believe that to know the Sealed Interval is to learn the shape of the world’s oldest prohibition.

They would touch the rail outside my chamber and wait.

I would answer by tapping back.

Not with speech. Speech would be too crude for what I am. I would use touch-sign, pressure-code, and the smallest movements of the water-drop within its living shell. When the bead shifted, however faintly, the chamber would hear it. The walls would tense. The blue capillaries would brighten. Somewhere above, in the market amphitheater, a vendor’s tray might pause mid-vibration. A Tuner would lift a hand and ask for calm. The town would know I had moved.

My function would not be to grant water.

That is the important thing. I would not be a source. I would not be a seed of restoration waiting to burst open and make the Terrace green in some older, wetter sense. The Terrace has passed beyond such things. If the bead inside me broke, it would not be a gentle miracle. It would be an ontological wound. Root-fields would surge. Growth-control chords would fail. Legal harmonics would fray as the substrate tried to decide whether to worship the moisture or destroy it. Old administrative systems, patient for millennia, would snap into emergency precision. Children would be moved inward. Air-locks would contract. The towns would sing warning harmonics until the sky itself seemed to vibrate.

So I would be kept intact.

I would be a memory, not a supply.

The Terrace remembers water the way an old body remembers fever. It does not miss it in a simple way. It has evolved beyond it, built itself into a drier and more resonant condition, but the memory remains in its deepest structures. That is why I would be necessary. I would be the place where that memory is allowed to exist without being allowed to act.

In councils, they would argue about me.

The Tuners would say I am a dissonance that must be constantly corrected. The Root-readers would say I am a predictive anomaly, useful because I make the substrate model conditions it has not faced in ages. The Silence-keepers would say I am a sacred burden, because my containment requires so much engineered quiet. The Seed-wardens would say I am a genetic echo, a reminder of older metabolic possibilities that should not be repeated but also should not be erased. The Dry Custody would listen to all of them, and then it would do what it always does: turn disagreement into architecture.

My chamber walls would thicken by one layer every few years.

The bridges above me would be retuned to distribute my legal weight.

The roads leading to Amber Vault would develop a subtle curve, so that processions carrying me or attending me would not move in straight lines. Straight lines would be too dangerous for a thing like me. Instead, the callus paths would bend in long, careful arcs, marked by low ribs of light that pulse blue when I am being moved.

There would be days when I am carried through the town.

Not often. Only when the Custody decides that the civic chord needs to be reminded of what it contains. On those days, the markets would quiet. Vendors would cover their trays. Masks would turn toward me without seeming to turn. The floor-plates would tilt faintly away, then correct themselves, because the town cannot fully reject what it has chosen to keep. Children would stand behind their elders, hands raised in the sign of guarded listening. Husk-singers on the upper terraces would sustain a low stabilizing note. Bell-jackals far out on the plains might go silent for a moment, as if a predator they do not understand has passed through the world’s hidden intervals.

I would not walk freely.

I would be accompanied by custodial figures: masked wardens, hollow-boned functionaries, and perhaps one or two persons grown specifically for judgment, their chests resonant, their fingers able to open locks by touch. They would not treat me with cruelty. They would not even treat me with fear, exactly. They would treat me the way one treats a cracked bell that is still holy: with reverence, caution, and the constant awareness that the next sound might be the wrong one.

My mask would be simple.

It would be made from dark seed-shell, polished until it seemed to hold dim light inside it. It would have no mouth. Instead, a single blue line would run down the center, representing the sealed drop within me. The mask would not amplify my voice, because I would not have one in the ordinary sense. It would dampen. It would focus. It would prevent the water-memory in me from becoming too persuasive. When I turned my head, the mask would catch the faint phosphorescent pulses of the walls and return them as small, cold signals.

People would say I am sad.

That would be a mistake, though not an unreasonable one. I would not be sad in the way wet creatures are sad. I would not long for oceans I have never seen. I would not dream of rain falling through the grey-violet spore-haze and turning the bark-plains soft. The Terrace does not allow such dreams to remain simple. My sorrow, if I had one, would be structural. I would be the shape of something remembered but forbidden. I would carry the knowledge that life can survive its own conditions, and that survival sometimes requires sealing away what once made survival possible.

Still, there would be tenderness in my existence.

In the dark phases, when the towns become networks of soft light and the great stems hum more quietly, my chamber would fill with slow amber pulses. The walls would warm slightly. The root-lattice beneath me would send up small predictive patterns that feel almost like affection: a tapping rhythm, a familiar pressure-code, a sequence once used by mothers to calm children. The Terrace would not love me as a person loves another person. It would love me as a body loves a necessary organ that has become dangerous: with constant attention, with maintenance, with fear folded into care.

Sometimes a Listener would press both hands to the wall and ask me a question.

They would not ask for water. That would be too direct, and perhaps too illegal even as a wish. They would ask things like:

*What did it feel like to be fluid?*

*What did it mean to dissolve?*

*Is it true that once, life could leak without dying?*

*Can a world forget too thoroughly?*
