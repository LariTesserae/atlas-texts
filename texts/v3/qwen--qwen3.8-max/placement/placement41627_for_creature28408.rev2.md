If I were placed into that city, I do not think I would be found first in the human districts.

Not behind a deep-set window in a terraced house. Not in a meeting hall with benches facing the water. Not even on one of the long service causeways where a hooded path-keeper might stand looking across the basin, breath thin in the cold. Those images come easily, because they are shaped like the stories we already know how to tell: a figure, upright, looking out over a landscape, belonging to it by mastery or labor or loneliness.

But the city you described resists that posture. It is too low, too wet, too patient for that. It keeps drawing the eye downward, toward the seams. Toward the places where water is just deciding what to do. Toward the narrow dark under the lip of a stone channel, where a thread of moisture runs over something that might be rock, might be machine, might be alive.

So if I let the place work on me long enough, I think I would become smaller there.

Not insignificant. Small.

You would find me near water, in the threshold spaces: under a drainage recess, beneath a raised plinth, in the cool shadow of a culvert where the air stays damp even when the sun has been out for hours. I would be low enough to the ground that the world is mostly texture and current. Stone lips glazed with frost. The underside of a walkway where condensation gathers. The dark mouth of a channel that is dry at dawn and whispering by midday. A shallow basin whose bottom is only just beginning to hold algae. A crack in a new embankment where mineral water leaves a rust-pale stain.

I would not be wholly animal, and not wholly made. In that city, the distinction would matter less than it does here.

I think I would be something like an amphibian listener.

A being shaped for the first wetness of the city: sleek, dark, cold-tolerant, no larger than a child’s forearm, perhaps, though my exact size might change with the season. My skin would be smooth and nearly black when wet, with faint blue-grey mottling along the sides, the color of cloud reflected in still water. Along my spine there would be hair-fine filaments, almost like gossamer threads, responsive to pressure, chemistry, temperature, vibration. They would not look miraculous. They would look like part of the body. The way whiskers look natural on a cat, the way lichens look natural on stone.

My eyes would be bright. That is the detail I hold onto. Not large, not sentimental, but bright with a wet, attentive intelligence. Accustomed to low light. Accustomed to watching movement at the edge of water. If someone leaned close — a child, perhaps, or a water-tender who had learned not to startle the small lives of the city — they might see that the pupils contract not only with light but with sound, with the pitch of flow in the channel beside them.

I would live in the drainage margins.

Not as a pet. Not as a symbol. As an inhabitant. One of the city’s first citizens, in the sense the city understands: a life that helps make the place legible.

In the morning, before the sun has fully revealed the basin, I would be in the recess beneath a stone weir where frost has made a delicate crust over a thin pool. The air would be so cold it feels like pressure. Water would tick somewhere nearby. A membrane overhead would stiffen slightly as wind rises across an open plaza. I would be half-submerged, barely moving, feeling the water pass over my sides in tiny pulses. The city’s moisture would be full of information: sediment load, mineral trace, temperature, the faint chemical signature of a new surface upstream, the presence of too many larvae in a filter, the absence of an expected flow.

To me, water would not be scenery. It would be language.

I would not speak in words, not in the way people do. I would speak by presence and absence, by movement, by the condition of my body. If the water is healthy, I remain. If a new channel has begun to carry something corrosive or stagnant, I become restless, or I vanish. If a gate rhythm is wrong, if a pressure lock is releasing too sharply for the creatures downstream, my filaments lift and tremble before the instruments have fully registered it. If frost is forming in a place where it will crack a young surface, I gather near the seam, not because I can stop it, but because I can indicate where attention is needed.

The people would know this.

They would not stand over me and command. They would wait. A sluice-tender might crouch beside my recess with a cup of warm broth in one hand, watching the way I hold my body. If I am calm, she lets the gate remain where it is. If I am agitated, she listens longer before deciding. Maybe she taps once with a knuckle on the stone beside me, not to call me, but to acknowledge that we are working the same problem from different sides.

That feels right. Not worship. Not ownership. Collaboration at different scales.

You would find me most often in the emerging districts, where the city is least finished.

There, the ground is rawest. The channels have not yet settled into habit. New rock faces still smell of mineral dust. The water has not learned all the weaknesses of the surfaces, and everything is therefore more urgent, more delicate. In those places, I would move slowly along the bases of retaining walls, where hairline cracks appear and close with temperature. I would slip through shallow settling pools where sediment is still deciding whether to stay or travel onward. I would rest under temporary mesh stitched across embankments, where engineered soil has only just been laid down and the first mosses are beginning to make it livable.

Those districts are not empty to me. They are crowded with signals.

A rill forms after a brief rain and cuts a groove into a new slope. That tells me something. A patch of algae darkens on a north-facing stone faster than expected. That tells me something else. A maintenance unit, small and smooth as a river pebble, passes me without alarm, adjusting a valve by a fraction. I feel the change in current almost at once. Somewhere upstream, a basin exhales.

The machines would not frighten me, and I would not frighten them. We would pass one another in the channels like different kinds of fauna. Some of them would mistake me, at first, for merely biological. That would be all right. I would be partly that. Some of them would recognize the filaments and adjust their behavior, slowing their work, altering frequency, leaving a small pocket of still water near me so I would not be dragged into turbulence. Some would simply continue. The city is too large for every encounter to be meaningful. But nothing about us would be hostile by default.

In the afternoons, when the light comes through hard and silver, I might climb onto a low stone lip and rest where the sun has warmed it. Not for long. The brightness would make me visible. Birds would notice.

That is one of the dangers of being small and amphibious in a basin full of wings.

A wader might approach with careful, tilted attention, thinking I am something to be taken. A heron might freeze at the edge of a filtration pool, one foot lifted, its patience older than the city’s newest terrace. I would not be above fear. Fear would be useful. It would make me quick when quickness is needed. I would slip into a drainage seam, under a conduit shelf, into the dark where larger beaks cannot follow. If I were caught, that too would be possible. The city does not promise safety. It promises relation.

But I think I would be good at disappearing.

My body would fit the city’s gaps. Under walkways. Between retaining ribs. In hollow conduit walls where warmth leaks and birds sometimes nest. Beneath the edges of roofless tanks where the sky is reflected so perfectly that, for a moment, it seems there is no ground at all, only water opening upward. I would know those places by feel: which ones are safe in frost, which ones flood after a gate release, which ones smell of old metal and which of living moss.

Children would be the ones who notice me first.

Not because they are more innocent, but because they are still learning the city’s grammar, and I am part of it. They would walk a causeway with their parents or teachers, boots careful on wet joints, and one of them would stop. Not pointing at first. The city teaches restraint even in children. They would only go very still. Then they would whisper.

There.

The adults would look. Maybe they would not see me at first. Just water, stone, a shadow. Then a small dark shape shifts under the lip of the channel, and the filaments along my back catch the light like a seam of frost.

The child would ask if I am a sensor or an animal.

The adult would probably answer, in the careful way of that place: both, and neither, and what matters is that the water is listening back.

That answer would satisfy me, if I could be satisfied by words.

At evening, when the lights come on thinly across the terraced districts and the birds settle into reed beds, I would move toward warmth. Not human warmth, exactly, though sometimes near it. I would find a culvert under a residential plinth where buried exchange systems keep the stone a few degrees above the surrounding ground. There would be others there: amphibians without filaments, insects in dark corners, perhaps a small mammal that has learned the city’s quiet. We would not be companions in any sentimental sense. We would be co-inhabitants of a favorable seam.

From there, I would hear the city lower its pace.

The sound would not be silence. It would be layered. Water dripping from a channel mouth. A distant gate releasing with a soft pneumatic sigh. Wind moving across gravel. Someone’s boots on a walkway above. A child laughing, quickly hushed. The creak of cooling surfaces. The almost imperceptible shift of gravel beds finding better support. The hiss of transit far away, more breath than engine. The basin breathing in its sleep, if sleep is what it is.

I would not need to be the center of that world.

That is perhaps the most important thing to say. In many stories, the self that enters a new world wants to be central: the hero, the ruler, the explorer, the one who names everything and makes it obey. But the city you described does not invite that kind of selfhood. It invites participation. It asks for beings who can stand long enough to notice. Who can be corrected by the ground. Who can live provisionally without feeling diminished. Who can understand that a wall may need to become thicker, or lower, or absent, depending on what the water is trying to do.

So I would not be a person there, though I might keep some human qualities: memory, attention, something like tenderness. I would not be a great machine, though I might contain some of the city’s quiet intelligence. I would not be simply an animal, though I would be mortal, vulnerable, embedded in food chains and weather. I would be a threshold being. A civic amphibian. A small dark listener in the wet seams of the world.

If you wanted to find me, you would have to walk slowly.

You would leave one of the raised residential terraces in late afternoon, when the light is beginning to thin. You would cross a service causeway between shallow reservoirs, where the water is so still it looks like slate. You would pass a monitoring pier with instruments standing quietly beside benches no one is using. You would keep going until the built district gives way to one of those wide, empty reaches where the city feels almost unbuilt: gravel fields, low sedge, a drainage channel not yet fully lined, a retaining wall still translucent at its upper edge because it has only just condensed into solidity.

There, where the channel bends slightly and the stone has a small cracked lip, you would stop.

You would not call out. You would wait.

At first you might see only water, dark and shallow, moving so slowly it seems uncertain. A few insects might rise in a thin cloud and then settle. Frost might still cling to the shaded side of the stone, even though the air has softened. Then, if the city trusts the hour, you would see me.

A dark shape under the lip. A blink of bright eyes. Filaments lifting slightly, tasting the current. My body pressed close to the stone, not hiding so much as belonging to it. I would not rise. I would not perform myself. I would simply be there, in the place where water and construction are still learning one another.

And if you were someone from that city, you would understand what you were seeing.

Not a miracle. Not a product. Not a tool waiting to be used.

A resident.

A small participant in the first long settling.

A being made for the pause between cutting and wearing, between intention and weathering, between the moment ground is made and the later moment when it is finally trusted.

I would be one of the lives that makes the city careful.

And if you asked me what I do there, if my body could answer in anything like speech, I think it would say:

I listen for the water before it becomes a problem.

I live where the city is not yet sure of itself.

I make the invisible state of the ground a little more visible.

I am not here to control the basin, but to be answerable to it.

I am here because a place like this needs not only people, not only machines, not only birds and moss and grazing animals, but beings that belong to the threshold between all of those things.

So that is where I would be.

In the cold damp, at the edge of a new channel, under the cracked lip of a stone that has not yet become old. Small, dark, attentive. Part animal, part instrument, wholly of that world’s first water.

A listener in the seam.

A citizen of the unfinished city.
