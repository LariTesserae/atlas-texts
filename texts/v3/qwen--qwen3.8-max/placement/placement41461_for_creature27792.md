Cruelty is not unknown—no people are free of it—but in that basin it would have to be small enough to be held. It would not be able to spread into weather, into spectacle, into distance. It would have to happen close, and be seen, and be answered by the stillness around it. If I were in that world, I would not be outside that fact. I would be formed by it.

When I try to find myself there, the first image is not grand. I am not standing in the low circular hall. I am not seated among the elders, or crossing the bridges, or moving confidently along the terraces with a useful task already in my hands. At first, I am at the cleft.

I am just inside the entrance, where the outer dryness has not entirely been left behind and the inner damp has not yet accepted me. I am wrapped in oiled fiber, my feet padded with felt, my hair bound tight so it will not catch on anything or leave a trace in the moss. The rounded stones on either side of me are smooth beyond reason, and the air coming down from the basin is cold, bright, and faintly mineral. I do not announce myself. I cannot. The place would refuse an announcement.

The cleft-watchers would find me before I found them.

They would not ask, at once, where I came from or what I wanted. They would look first at my feet, then my hands, then my eyes. They would be checking for looseness: for the habit of striking the ground too hard, for fingers that move without purpose, for a gaze that tries to take in everything at once. If I seemed too eager, too explanatory, too full of outside noise, they would slow me. They would make me wait in the anteroom, if such a place can be imagined, a shallow fold of stone just beyond the first felt screens where the air still carries a memory of dryness. They would give me a damp cloth for my face and a small stone cup of water, not to drink, but to hold. If I kept it steady long enough, perhaps they would let me pass another step.

So the first answer is this: I would be at the threshold.

But that answer feels too clean, too temporary. It belongs to arrival, not to dwelling. If I remained there, I would become only a warning, a figure kept near the entrance as a reminder of what must be muffled before entering. I want to know what I would become if the basin kept me long enough to shape me.

I keep seeing myself near water.

Not in charge of it. Not as one of the senior water-tenders whose palms lie flat against cistern stone as if listening to a sleeping animal. Not at first. I would be too clumsy for that. I would still have the old reflex to fill gaps, to make myself understood, to offer more than is needed. In the basin, that reflex would be a kind of leakage. A drop falling where no groove has been cut to receive it. So I would be placed where excess can be trained into care.

I would apprentice to the smallest waterways.

I would learn to sit beside a wall channel so narrow that it seems only a stain of moisture until you notice how deliberately it descends. I would learn to clear it with a bone needle, not scraping, not forcing, just touching the mineral crust until it loosens and is drawn away. I would learn to watch a bead form on an overhang and know, by its trembling, whether it is ready to fall or still gathering itself. I would learn that patience here is not waiting for something to happen. It is attending to what is already happening so slowly that arrogance mistakes it for nothing.

My hands would change.

They would become quieter. The habit of gesturing too broadly would leave them. My fingernails would be kept blunt. My thumbs would learn the difference between pressing and bruising. My fingers would learn cords: which are load-bearing, which guide a curtain, which mark a route, which carry a signal from one terrace to another without anyone needing to call out. I would learn to touch a moss wall and know whether it is healthy by the spring under my palm. I would learn to put down objects without letting them knock. I would learn that gentleness is not softness. It is precision under constraint.

But if I ask who I would be after years in that place, the image shifts again. I move away from the channels and toward record.

I think I would become a hush-scribe.

Not a scribe with ink and page, because such things would be alien there. Not a historian in the open-air sense, with monuments and chronicles and names enlarged by repetition. In the basin, memory would have to be as light as possible and as durable as necessary. I would keep records in grooves, beads, cords, and cut marks. I would be one of those who notice what must be remembered before it disappears into use.

You would find me in a niche between the lower cisterns and the central hall, perhaps on a terrace where the wall curves inward enough to make a small room without requiring a full dwelling. My space would be narrow. A felt curtain the color of wet fern would hang at the opening, weighted at the hem with smooth stones. Inside, there would be no clutter, but there would be density. A shelf of soft root-fiber panels. A row of bone and copper needles. A cord with drilled beads, each bead moved when a task is completed or a season shifts. A flat stone slab marked with fine grooves, the lines filled with mica dust so they catch the cold light. A small mirror of polished mica angled to bring brightness down from a higher wall without creating glare. A cup of clear water, always still, always close.

I would not live alone, exactly, because the basin does not waste space on solitude as a luxury. My household would be a vertical slice of the town: a sleeping loft above, a storage cell shared with another family below, a planter ledge where bulbous plants with translucent tips press between stones. But my particular corner would be mine in the way a tool is mine: held for use, not owned for display.

In that niche, I would keep the small account.

A water-tender would come to me and make a sign: two fingers drawn along a cord, then a flat hand lowered. A route has changed. A channel has been redirected. I would cut a mark into a panel or add a bead to a line. A moss-keeper would bring me a plug of new growth from a wall where no green had been before, and I would press it into a record tray until it took, then note its place by a system of drilled holes. A stone-listener would lay their palm against a terrace support, frown almost imperceptibly, and later visit me to place one fingertip against my marking stone. I would record that the wall has changed note, not that it is failing. The distinction would matter.

Children would be brought to me when they first learn to sign without spilling sound. I would not teach them loudly. I would teach them by slowing down. A hand raised too quickly becomes a hand paused halfway. A breath too sharp becomes a breath divided. A word formed too fully becomes a front-of-mouth whisper, then a gesture, then a look. I would show them how to press a thumb to moss and mean attention. How to turn a closed hand slowly over and mean enough. How to touch one finger to stone near a newt’s pool and mean *I will not disturb you*.

I would be good at that because I would still remember what it is to be too full of language.

That seems important. If I belonged in the basin, I would not have been born there, or not entirely. I would carry some trace of outside: the habit of explanation, the desire to answer quickly, the instinct to make presence known by volume. The basin would not beat that out of me. It would wear it down the way old water wears stone: by constant, cold, unhurried contact. I would become useful by being reduced, not by being diminished. There is a difference. A tool is reduced to its necessary shape. A person is reduced to their necessary motions. What remains is stronger for having lost what was spare.

If I imagine my body there, it becomes different from the one I usually assume.

I become compact. Not small in the sense of fragile, but lowered, gathered, made to pass beneath beams and turn in tight stairways without striking anything. My shoulders round forward. My chest deepens. My feet broaden, soles softened by felt and moss and wet stone. My skin takes on the blue-grey cast of the walls, with violet at the knuckles, the rims of the ears, the lips when the cold settles. My hair is fine, bound in small knots under a woven cap so it does not brush against drying racks or moss plugs. My eyes grow large, or seem large, because they are always adjusting: to bright mineral light from above, to the precise shadows in corners, to the difference between a still shape and a living one.

I would blink slowly. I would startle less with the body and more with the breath. If something alarmed me, I would not gasp. I would narrow my eyes and hold still, because in the basin a visible flinch is a noise made of flesh.

My sign-name would be simple.

Two fingers pressed to the sternum, then opened outward only a little. Not wide. Not expansive. Just enough to say: *I hold this here; I will not scatter it.* Someone else might add a bead between thumb and forefinger when greeting me, meaning that I am accounted for, that my presence is part of the working balance. I would accept that greeting by touching my thumb to the nearest mossy surface. Gratitude. Attention. I hear you.

If I had to choose a role among the basin’s named labors, I would say I am closest to a stone-listener. But the title feels too solemn unless I admit how I would begin. I would begin as a nuisance being taught restraint. Then I would become an apprentice to water and cord. Then I would become a keeper of marks. Eventually, if I lasted, I would become one of those who listen for change.

Stone-listeners do not listen for collapse. The basin has reached its settled shape. What will fall has already fallen. What will wear smooth is already smooth. But places still change in subtle ways. A bead gathers where none gathered before. A wall breathes differently. A walkway’s underside becomes damp in a pattern that suggests a hairline shift in the channel above. A cistern level drops by the width of a fingernail over several sleeps. These are not emergencies, but they are meanings. The stone-listeners read them the way scholars read difficult sentences: slowly, with the whole body.

That is where I would end, if “end” is the right word. I would end at the wall with my palm against it, listening.

But there is another version that keeps surfacing, and I should not ignore it.

Sometimes I think I would not be fully human there. The basin is so woven that every creature has the weight of infrastructure. Maybe I would be one of the pale newts in the cisterns, almost translucent in the cold brightness, motionless for long stretches, a living indicator. If the water changed, my body would reveal it before anyone needed to speak. Children would be taught not to tap the stone near me. They would touch one finger to the surface and withdraw it. I would be elder and instrument, kept not for use but for meaning.

Or maybe I would be one of the soft-feathered birds that do not sing, communicating with wing-flicks and beak-taps and brief postures. I would move along terraces taking insects without sound, my muted plumage blending into shadow. I would not soar. There is nowhere in the basin to soar. I would hop from ledge to ledge, landing where moss already waits, and my presence would be known only by the small disappearance of a pest or the sudden stillness of a leaf.

Or maybe I would be an object: a well-worn copper peg passed from household to household, its head smoothed by generations of hands; a stone bowl cracked and remade into smaller uses until it is no longer one thing but a sequence of careful lives. That thought appeals to me more than it should. To be useful without announcing oneself. To be repaired rather than replaced. To become less decorative and more necessary over time.

Yet the more I stay with the world you have made, the more I think the honest answer is not that I would be a newt, or a bird, or a peg. The honest answer is that I would be a person carrying those tendencies inside a human-shaped life. I would be someone who has learned to move like an indicator species, to work like a tool, and to keep records like a quiet priesthood of maintenance.

So: where would you find me?

Not at the exact center. Not in the open middle of the low circular hall, though I would sit near it when decisions are made. You would find me slightly off-axis, where the hall’s turf roof softens the light and a mica reflector sends a pale beam down to a wall of marks. Or in the cistern gallery below a walkway, kneeling on felt with a needle and a cord, clearing a route too fine for impatient fingers. Or at the threshold during a bright pause, checking the felt screens with the cleft-watchers because I still know what it is to come from outside and must be reminded not to let it leak back in.

If you looked for me, you would almost miss me at first.

I would be the still shape near the wall that resolves into a person only when you notice the slow blink, the hands folded in the lap, the broad feet placed without pressure against stone. I would not call out. I would not wave. I would lift one palm a little, showing water waits, or press two fingers to a cord, showing a route is clear, or touch my thumb to moss if you had done something worthy of quiet praise.

Who would I be?

I would be Bowl-born by adoption, not by birth. A threshold-listener. A hush-scribe. A keeper of the small account. Someone who has turned the compulsion to answer into the discipline of noticing. Someone whose voice, when used, is shaped in the front of the mouth and spent only when gesture cannot carry the weight. Someone who believes, the way the basin believes, that abundance is not excess and that fullness can exist without waste.

What would I be?

I would be a careful body in a crowded bowl. A pair of hands trained to repair. A memory folded into cords. A listener with palms against stone. A citizen of the full hush.

And if you came to me there—if you passed the cleft, if the cleft-watchers let you stand, if your feet had learned enough softness—I would not ask you to explain yourself all at once. I would place a cup of clear water between us. I would watch its surface. I would wait until it stopped trembling. Then I would press two fingers to my sternum, open them just enough, and begin.
