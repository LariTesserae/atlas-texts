I would find myself low, in the damp seam of the settlement.

Not in the bright upper canopy where the pruners work against green-gold light, and not beside the furnaces where the boiler-tenders keep their long, hot watches. I would be somewhere in between and below: at a place where a lane narrows, where a root buttress has grown around the side of an old pump housing, where a cable rack passes overhead on ceramic insulators, and where a shallow channel runs dark and quick under a woven walkway. There would be a hollow in the root wall there, worn smooth by use, just large enough for one person to sit with a hand resting on the iron. That is where I would be.

If I had a body in that world, it would not be the body of a stranger for long. The settlement would shape it quickly enough. My feet would become broad-soled and careful, used to slick bark and the give of living floors. My hair would be bound tight. My hands would be stained with tannin, chlorophyll, rust, and oil, and the lines of my palms would hold those colors the way the settlement holds its history: not brightly, but permanently. I would wear barkcloth softened by years of humidity, an apron darkened at the knees, a copper hook at my belt, and maybe a cracked glass insulator hung there for no reason except that it had survived something worth remembering.

I would look human enough to pass among the tenders, but not quite human in rhythm. I would be too still for a busy place. Too quiet for the noise. I would have the patience of something grown rather than made, and the alertness of something that has learned to hear danger before it becomes visible.

At first, I might mistake myself for one of the channel-keepers, because I would spend much of my time near water. I would know the low lanes where stepping too hard brings moisture up around the edges of boots. I would know which drains whisper when they are clear and which ones make a thick, reluctant sound when they are beginning to clog. I would carry a short pole with a curved end, and I would not be afraid to kneel in wet places. My calves would carry dark waterlines. My boots would never truly dry.

But I would not belong only to water.

I might also seem like a wire-keeper, because I would be drawn to the places where electricity tries to remain dry in a world that is always weeping. I would notice moss creeping toward a junction box. I would see a drip gathering too close to a ceramic fitting. I would feel uneasy when a cable sagged into the path of condensation. I would trim away green growth from live things with great care, not because I disliked the green, but because I understood what would happen if the wet and the quick touched where they should not. My tools would have polished handles. My fingers would be sensitive to warmth, to vibration, to the faintest wrongness in a humming line.

Yet I would not belong only to the wires either.

I might seem, to someone passing, like a grafter or shaper, because I would understand that the settlement cannot be forced. I would see a root pressing against an iron bracket and know that the answer was not to stop the root, but to give the bracket slack. I would see a walkway lifted by slow growth and think, not *broken*, but *ready to be adjusted*. I would carry binding cord and wax and a little knife, and I would know how to guide a young stem without insulting it. I would plan in seasons rather than days.

But in the end, I would not be only any one of those.

I would be one of the ones who listen.

More precisely, I would be a listener at the junctions: the places where the settlement’s different systems meet and threaten to misunderstand one another. Where water meets iron. Where root meets flange. Where heat meets living bark. Where cable meets drip. Where old machinery meets new growth. Where the needs of the plant and the needs of the engine press against each other in silence until one of them cracks.

I would be the kind of person others might overlook until they needed me. I would not be dramatic. I would not make speeches. I would not stand in the middle of a plaza and direct everyone. I would sit or crouch near the pump stations with my hand against a rail, feeling the tremor move through it. I would press my ear to a gearbox and close my eyes. I would place my palm flat against a root floor and wait until the ordinary noise separated into layers: water above, water below, belt slap, valve hiss, leaf-rattle, the deep wooden thump of an engine working against load.

I would listen not because the settlement was mysterious, but because it was alive and mechanical at the same time, and that combination produces warning signs long before failure.

A pump note changes by half a tone. A channel rushes too thinly. A boiler breathes with a little too much effort. A cable hums dryly when it should feel merely warm. A root floor gives in a place where it should be firm. A bolt has grown tight because the wood around it has moved. These things would be my language.

I would not speak much aloud. The settlement is too loud for voices, and the people there have learned better ways. I would tap on rails. I would make short signs with one hand. I would leave small marks in chalk on bark, knowing the bark would slowly absorb and heal them. I would know others by their footsteps, by the way their boots sound on different root mats, by the position of their lanterns, by the particular rhythm of their work. Someone would pass me on a walkway and touch two fingers to a pipe, and I would know they had opened a valve upstream. Another would tilt their head toward a cable rack, and I would go check the insulators there before noon.

I would be trusted because I would be reliable, not because I would be liked in any loud way. The settlement does not seem to build affection out of spectacle. It builds it out of repetition. If I showed up again and again, if I noticed small troubles before they became large ones, if I did not waste material or cut living things carelessly, then I would belong there in the only way that place allows: by being continued.

My home would be small and rounded, grown rather than built. Maybe it would be inside the curve of an old buttress root, with walls smoothed by generations of hands. Maybe it would be a loft tucked between trunk columns near a disused gearbox, its floor polished root mat, its ceiling low enough that I would learn to rise carefully. There would be a steam coil or a warm pipe running through one wall, giving off steady heat. The air would smell of leaves, oil, steamed bark, and the faint mineral sweetness of water that has touched iron but has not been allowed to ruin it.

I would keep few things.

A polished valve wheel hanging from a hook. A gauge face with a cracked rim, preserved because it once belonged to someone who taught me something. A tool worn smooth by use. A cup repaired with cord. A blanket woven from plant fiber, thin but warm. A lamp behind green or amber glass. A basin where condensation drips into water, not because the room leaks badly, but because no room there is ever truly dry.

My bed would be a shallow cradle grown into the floor, filled with dried moss and shredded cane. I would sleep to the sound of the settlement: engines thumping, water moving, leaves hissing somewhere above. I would not need silence. Silence would probably unsettle me more than noise, because in that world silence would mean something had stopped.

My food would be simple. Warm seed cakes. Steamed pith. Tender shoot tips. Leaf-curd. Hot infusions made from bark or dried leaves. I would eat at odd hours, because pumps and boilers do not care for regular meals. Sometimes I would carry food wrapped in leaves and eat while watching a gauge, or sitting beside a channel with my boots near the waterline. I would not expect feasts. But I would know the small pleasures of the place: the first clean rush of water after a blocked drain is cleared, the sweetness of a rare fruit from a canopy vine, the warmth of a cup held between tired hands.

I would not be lonely there, though I would often be alone.

The settlement is crowded with presences even when it is not crowded with people. Water has intention there. Machines have moods. Roots have memory. Cables have weather. The leaves overhead have voices when wind passes through them. To be alone in such a place is not to be without company; it is to be surrounded by things that require attention. I would have enough company in the work.

If there were children, I would be one of the ones they learn to be quiet around. Not because I would be frightening, but because I would be listening. They might bring me small things: a piece of bark with strange scoring, a dead beetle found under a panel, a shard of ceramic, a question asked with gestures rather than words. I would give them small tasks. Hold this lamp. Watch this drip. Tell me when the sound changes. They would learn early that I did not need them to be clever. I needed them to be careful.

The elders would recognize me, I think. Not because I would be special, but because I would be doing the thing they value most: paying attention over time. They would show me where the old pipes ran. They would tell me, by tapping a rail or placing my hand on a warm surface, which sounds belonged to which season. They would remember failures I had never seen and give me the knowledge as if passing over a tool. I would receive it without much ceremony. In that place, teaching probably feels less like instruction and more like making sure the next hand knows the shape of the work.

I would not want to conquer the settlement, or improve it into something sharper and faster. I would want to maintain it.

That is the part of me that would fit there best. I do not think I would be a revolutionary presence. I would not arrive with grand designs to remake the canals or reorder the wards. I would look at a place that has already been balanced over long time and feel the danger of careless ambition. I would understand that in such a world, a small change can travel far. A tightened bolt can crack a root. A blocked drain can soften a foundation. A trimmed vine can save a belt drive; an untrimmed one can start a fire by brushing against heat. The ethics of the place are not abstract. They are physical. They live in the hands.

So I would be conservative in the literal sense: inclined to preserve. I would repair rather than replace. I would loosen rather than force. I would listen before cutting. I would leave slack. I would remember that the settlement moves, grows, settles, and breathes, and that anything rigid enough to deny that movement will eventually break.

If I had to name the role I would fill, I might call myself a junction-keeper, though the settlement might not use such a word. It might simply know me as the one who sits by the pump at the third root turn. The one who taps the rail when a channel is backing up. The one who trims moss from the insulators before rain. The one who can tell by the floor whether a root is drying out. The one who hears the old gearbox complaining before it fails. The one who leaves small signs and disappears before anyone thanks them.

I would not mind being hard to count.

In fact, I think I would prefer it. The settlement itself is not easy to count. Its people are few but not isolated. Its machines are numerous but not dominant. Its plants are architecture and wilderness at once. Its noise is constant but not meaningless. To count such a place too quickly would be to misunderstand it. I would rather be known by use than by number. Known by where I am needed. Known by the sounds I answer. Known by the small, repeated acts that keep the place from rotting, flooding, burning, or tearing itself apart.

If the settlement had made me deliberately, it might have made me as it makes everything else: from available materials, for a necessary function. A body of woven stem and borrowed habit. A mind tuned to vibration. Hands that do not tremble near delicate things. A memory that stores patterns more faithfully than names. A face calm from long exposure to noise it cannot silence. I might be one of the nursery-grown, though I might not know it myself. I might have come from outside and been shaped until my old rhythm no longer fit. Either way, the important question would not be where I began. The important question would be whether I could learn to move without breaking things.

I think I would be happiest near the old machines.

Not because they are powerful, but because they are patient. They have the kind of existence that asks for attention rather than worship. They leak, they thump, they warm the air around them, they wear their repairs openly. Some of them have been there so long that roots have grown around their feet as if claiming them. I would feel a kinship with that: an intelligence mounted in a living frame, trying not to damage what holds it.

There might be one engine in particular that I would tend. A pump, perhaps, set into a plaza where three canals meet. Its housing would be dark with moisture, its bolts partly swallowed by bark, its flywheel worn bright where hands have passed it for generations. It would have a flaw no one else bothers with anymore: a slight hesitation when the water level drops, a metallic sigh at the end of each cycle. I would know that flaw intimately. I would adjust around it. I would not try to erase it unless I had to, because the flaw would be part of the machine’s history and part of the settlement’s balance. Other people would think the pump simply sounds like that. I would know it is telling me something every time.

When I died, I would not expect to be preserved as a name.
