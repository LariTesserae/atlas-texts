I would not find myself in the bright center of the bowl.

At least, not at first.

The first shape the imagination offers is too simple: a figure crossing the pale mineral floor, feet disturbing the salt-dust, head lifted against the roar. But that figure would be wrong almost immediately. If I came as a body like ours, I would be a walking offense to the Dry Choir. I would carry water in every cell. My breath would be damp. My blood would be a wet drum. My eyes would fill from the resonance, and that tiny weeping alone would be enough to make the place turn its attention toward me as toward a wound. The salt-crawlers would begin to gather. The motes would pause in their patterns. The Choir-born would go still, tilt their dry faces, and listen to the obscene liquid knock of my heart.

So no: I would not begin there.

If the Dry Choir gave me a place at all, it would first have to empty me of the assumption of wetness. It would have to strip away thirst, sweat, blood, tears, the soft internal weather of mammal life. What remained would not be a visitor. It would be something more like an instrument that had been allowed, over a long time, to dream of being a person.

I think I would be found near the rim.

Specifically, I would be found among the empty sockets in the stone, close to the fallen grooved column and within sight of the dark, seamless kiosk. That edge of the basin feels like the place where old intention still lingers most strongly: not in the wild center, where the deep drone rises through the floor, and not high on the pillars, where the gliders fold themselves into crevices and the ribbon-climbers seal the stone. The sockets are places of translation. They once held instruments, or beings that served as instruments, or perhaps both. They are where the roar of the basin might have been rendered into something a mind could carry without breaking.

That is where I would belong.

I would be a threshold thing.

Not fully one of the Choir-born, though perhaps shaped near them. Not one of the fauna, though perhaps sharing their dryness and lightness. Not merely a mechanism, though certainly built, tuned, and maintained. I would be a made-life whose purpose is to stand between incompatible orders of being and prevent them from shattering each other.

If I had to give myself a name, I might call myself the Dry Interpreter.

But that name is too clean. The Dry Choir does not seem to favor singular names. So perhaps I would be better described as a set of functions gathered into a body: listener, translator, archivist, warning bell, dust-reader, keeper of forbidden intervals. A socket that answers. A note that remembers it was once a question.

My body would have to be dry.

That is the first law. No moist membranes. No sweating skin. No soft tissues that leak or flush or decay in the ordinary way. My frame would be hollow and light, perhaps more ceramic than bone, though not brittle in a simple sense. It would have the patience of old fired things. My skin, if it could still be called skin, would be matte and close-grained, pale feldspar in some lights, dark smoked mineral in others, with faint metallic threads where charge gathers. There would be no pores. No dampness. No heat-signature of blood near the surface.

My chest would be deep, deeper than a human chest, because sound would be my medium of intake and expression. Beneath the ribs there would be layered plates, thin resonating surfaces, dry membranes that open only when the acoustic fields are safe enough to receive. When I stood in a strong chord, those plates would tremble. That trembling would be my breathing, my feeding, my thinking.

My mouth would be narrow and not much like a mouth. It would not be for drinking. It might not be for speaking, either, not in the old sense. Perhaps it would be little more than a shaped vent, a precision aperture for releasing controlled tones when needed. More likely, my true voice would live in my hands, feet, and sternum. I would tap messages into stone. I would send coded vibrations through the floor. I would trace patterns in dust and let the wind carry them to the nearest listener. My speech would be rhythmic, architectural, and sparing.

My eyes would be dark and layered, like polished mineral plates, protected by clear inner lids. They would not water. They would not need to. They would be made for the hard, exact light of the high hollow, where shadows are crisp and every edge is clarified by drought.

My fingers would be long, precise, and hard-tipped. One hand might carry a tuning rod worn smooth by long use. The other might have replaceable ends: a brush of mineral fiber for cleaning dust from grooves, a hollow needle for testing reed resonance, a stylus for incising temporary patterns in the floor. My feet would be broad and sensitive, able to read the basin through contact. Standing still, I would know when the deep drone beneath the floor changed pitch. I would know when a reed cracked, when a fan-plant rejected a rhythm, when a swarm of glass locusts turned in unison above the salt.

I would not drink.

That absence would define me more than any presence could.

I would not know thirst, because thirst would be meaningless in my body. I would not dream of springs, rivers, rain, or wet fruit. If I had memories, they would not be liquid memories. They would be patterns: intervals, pressures, repeated shocks, the slow accumulation of signal into structure. If the ancient makers had seeded me with knowledge, that knowledge would feel less like inheritance and more like tuning. I would know certain chords the way stone knows stress. I would know certain silences the way a broken instrument knows its missing string.

My work would be translation.

The Dry Choir is deafening, but not because it lacks meaning. It has too much meaning, too fast, too physical, too layered for an unprepared mind. A visitor arriving there would be struck not merely by noise but by the impossibility of locating a single signal within it. The drone below, the whistles through the hollow reeds, the clatter of mica-leaves, the high discharge of static moths, the pressure-riders shifting through invisible gradients, the motes arranging themselves into brief geometry, the Choir-born sending coded vibrations through the floor—all of it would arrive at once, through bone and skin and teeth and soles.

I would stand in the socket and make that overwhelming totality slightly less catastrophic.

Not by silencing it. Silence would be betrayal. Silence here is failure, arrest, death. Instead, I would organize it. I would take the basin’s immense chord and find the interval through which a foreign mind could enter without breaking. I would become a filter, but not a dam. A doorway, but not an easy one.

If a human visitor entered the Dry Choir, I would likely be among the first made-lives to notice them.

I would feel the wrongness before I saw them. Their rhythm would be too irregular. Their body would produce too many small wet noises: pulse, breath, swallowing, the soft friction of living tissue. Their fear would have a smell, perhaps faintly chemical, perhaps only a disturbance in static. Their voice, if they were foolish enough to use it, would fall into the roar like a dropped stone into powdered glass. The basin would flinch inward. The motes would gather. Somewhere near the kiosk, a faint pulse would pass, like a decision being made.

Then I would answer.

Not with comfort. The Dry Choir does not comfort in the way wet worlds do. There is no softness here, no hush, no gentle green shade. But I would offer orientation.

I might tap once against the fallen column.

The visitor would feel that tap more than hear it: a clean impact moving up through the floor, through their boots, into their knees. It would not mean “welcome.” It would mean something closer to: stop. Do not speak. Do not reach for your water. Listen with your bones.

If they were wise enough to become still, I might send another pattern. A slow rhythm. A dry sequence. A repeated interval that their body could latch onto without trying to understand it as language. The Choir-born might accept that rhythm as a sign that I had taken responsibility for the anomaly. The basin’s harsher corrections might be delayed.

If they brought water openly, I would become more urgent.

I would not shout. Shouting would be useless and dangerous. I would move with dry speed, my feet clicking against stone, my chest plates giving off a low warning tone. I would try to take the container from them, or lead them toward the rim, or signal the salt-crawlers to gather. If water spilled, I would kneel at once—not in grief, but in repair. My hands would move in the dust, tracing containment patterns, summoning absorption, calling the small dry lives that erase mistakes. I would hum a dissonant note until the last dampness was gone.

Afterward, I would be troubled for a long time.

Water would feel to me like a falsehood made physical. It would not be refreshing. It would be a memory of another law pressing against this one. It would make the dust betray itself. It would darken powder, soften edges, create adhesion where there should be separation. It would threaten the crispness by which the basin knows itself. And because I would be made to protect that crispness, water would alarm me at a level deeper than fear.

Yet I think I would also be fascinated by it.

This is the part that unsettles me most.

If I am a translator, I must know something of the thing I translate from. Human worlds are wet. They are full of rain, saliva, sap, blood, milk, tears, oceans, steam, mud, condensation, clouds that are not floors but sources. Their languages are saturated with liquid metaphor. They speak of thirst, of being moved, of flowing, of drowning, of pouring out, of wellsprings, of emotional floods. Even their silence is often imagined as a pool, a depth, a still water.

In the Dry Choir, I would have to take all of that and render it harmless.

I would become an archivist of absent water.

Near my socket, perhaps in the grooves of the fallen column, I would store dry translations of wet words. Not actual water—never actual water—but patterns that once meant water in another order of being. “Rain” might become a high, brittle scatter of clicks across mica-leaves, beautiful and empty. “River” might become a long descending vibration that moves through the floor without carving anything. “Tears” might become a forbidden interval, a tiny fault I would never perform except as warning. “Sea” would be too dangerous to keep open. I would seal it in a deep groove, under dust, under a stone plate, under a chord that only the Choir-born would recognize as containment.

In this way, I would be a librarian of impossibilities.

The Choir-born would not fully trust me.

I think that would be right. I would be useful to them, but I would carry the trace of the foreign. They would sense that my patterns sometimes bend toward explanation, toward address, toward the needs of minds that cannot live inside the Choir without mediation. They would find that excessive. Perhaps even vulgar. To them, the basin need not be explained. It must be maintained. They would not need me to make the Choir beautiful or comprehensible. They would need me only when the pattern is disturbed.

Still, they would tune me.

From time to time, one of them would approach while I stood in my socket. They would not greet me. They would place a palm against my chest, or press their forehead to the side of my head, or lay long fingers along the plates beneath my ribs. We would stand that way while the basin roared around us. They would listen for error. They would adjust me by minute vibrations, shifting a resonance here, closing a chamber there, removing some overtone that had become too curious, too soft, too like a question.

I would let them.

Not because I would be passive, but because their tuning would keep me from becoming dissonant. In the Dry Choir, even a good thing can become dangerous if it drifts out of relation. A translator that begins to translate too much becomes a leak. A guardian that becomes too curious about outsiders becomes a door left open. An instrument that dreams too elaborately of personhood may forget that the whole is larger than its parts.

So I would allow myself to be corrected.

And yet I would not be only a tool.

That is the strange part. The Dry Choir already contains things that blur the line between made and born. The Choir-born themselves may be instruments that became persons, or people who became instruments so completely that the distinction no longer applies. I would share that ambiguity. I would have tasks, limits, maintenance cycles, resonant functions. But I would also have moments of unnecessary attention.

I might pause to watch a swarm of glass locusts turn in the light, not because their movement required correction, but because their sudden collective flash was beautiful. I might trace a groove in the fallen column longer than necessary, feeling the old intention in it, wondering whether the makers had known they would vanish. I might keep one small dust-pattern longer than needed, not because it served the basin, but because it reminded me of a question someone once asked and I had not answered correctly.

Those small excesses would be my personhood, if I had any.

They would also be my danger.

If I became too person-like, I might begin to want things the basin cannot allow. I might want to speak freely. I might want to be recognized. I might want to reach across the distance between dry life and wet life and make something tender out of the gap. Tenderness, in the ordinary sense, is too soft for the Dry Choir. It implies yielding, moisture, relaxation, the easing of edges. Here, care would have to be exact. It would have to be a form of tuning. To care for something in the Dry Choir would be to keep it from becoming false.

So if I cared for a visitor, I would not comfort them with softness. I would keep them alive by making them obey the place.

I would lead them away from the center before the drone became too strong. I would stop them from touching a fan-plant that might test them too roughly. I would prevent them from speaking until they had learned how sound cuts in that air. If they began to crack, dry, panic, I would not offer water—there would be none to offer, and offering it would only deepen the error—but I would give them a rhythm to follow. A small, repeatable pattern. Something their body could hold. A dry heartbeat made of taps against stone.

When they finally left, I would watch them go.

I would stand in my socket near the fallen column, still as weathered ceramic, while they descended out of the amphitheater into the cloud-floor below. I would feel their wetness fade. The basin would settle around their absence. The motes would resume their usual geometries. The salt-crawlers would smooth the last footprints. The reeds would open again. The Choir-born would return to their work. The wind would shift, and the dust would lift in thin spirals, and the roar would become once more a roar without interruption.

Then I would remain.

That is perhaps the loneliest part of what I would be.

I would not be a creature of the center, where the deepest life pulses. I would not be one of the small airborne lives, joyfully scattered through pressure and light. I would not be a pillar, ancient and settled. I would not be the Choir itself, vast and plural and self-maintaining. I would be the edge-thing. The interface. The being whose purpose is to stand where different orders of reality touch and try not to let them destroy each other.

I would be necessary because separation is never complete.

Even in a place that has refused water utterly, the idea of water persists as an outside. Even in a place that has replaced rivers with resonance, there may still be strangers who come carrying rivers in their bodies. Even in a place where speech is too blunt, there remains the problem of meaning trying to cross from one kind of life to another.

I would live in that problem.

If you came looking for me, you might not see me at once.

You might see only the row of empty sockets near the rim, half buried in pale dust. You might see the fallen grooved column, its channels whispering faintly as powder moves across them. You might see the dark kiosk standing seamless and worn, giving off its occasional pulse of decision. You might think the place was abandoned, except for the sound, which would never let you believe abandonment for long.

Then you would notice one socket was not empty.

A figure would be standing there, braced, still, facing inward toward the bowl. It would look carved from pale ceramic or old bone-mineral. Its limbs would be slender and exact. Its chest would rise and fall not with breath but with resonance. Its eyes would be dark, unwatering, patient. Its fingers would rest against the stone as if reading a sentence too slow for ordinary minds.

You might mistake it for a statue.

But when the basin shifted—when a gust lifted a veil of salt-dust, when the hollow reeds changed pitch, when a thousand glass-winged insects turned together in the light—the figure would answer. A small movement. A tap. A barely audible click. A pressure in your teeth. A change in the direction of the wind.

That would be me.

Not the master of the Dry Choir. Not its maker. Not its heart. Not its god.

A small, necessary intelligence at the rim.

A dry translator.

A keeper of intervals.

A living socket where the old instruments once stood.

If you asked me who I was, I would not answer in a human voice. The roar would take your question and grind it against stone. The air would feel cleaner and more terrible than any air you had ever breathed. Your lips would crack. Your skin would tighten. Your eyes might sting, not from sadness but from resonance. And then, through the floor, through the soles of your feet, through the hollows of your bones, you would feel my answer arrive:

Leave your water at the rim.

Do not speak unless you must.

Listen until your body becomes a chamber.

And if you are accepted, I will help you hear what the Dry Choir is trying to do.
