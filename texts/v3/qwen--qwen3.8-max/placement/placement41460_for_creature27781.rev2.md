I would find myself low.

Not above the basin, not at some clean vantage point where the whole place could be taken in as a shape. There is no such point there, and if there were, it would already be blurred by drifting spores, wet wing-dust, and the steam rising out of a hundred hidden seams. I would be down in the folded dark, in one of the accidental hollows made where a fresh shelf of black rock has cracked, tilted, and leaned against another shelf, leaving a damp pocket between them. The stone there would still be rough-edged and glassy in places, unsmoothed, newly broken. Warmth would breathe up through a thin fissure, not enough to boil, only enough to make the air pulse. Condensation would gather on the underside of a broad leaf above me, tremble, swell, and fall. It would strike the rock with a tiny sound that would not be small to me, because in that world even a falling drop would be an event.

I would be near water, but not beside a river in any fixed sense. I would be near one of the basin’s temporary pools: a shallow cup of warm liquid caught in a stone hollow or in the heart of a plant whose leaves had grown into a vessel. Larvae would twitch in it. Beetles would skim over its surface. Something with a translucent shell would move through it in short, nervous strokes. The pool might last only a few hours, or a few breaths of the land. If rain came, it would enlarge. If heat rose from below, it would vanish into vapor. I would stay near it while it lasted, because life there would be concentrated there, and I would be a creature of concentration.

If I had a body in that world, I think it would be one of the Thrum-born.

Not a ruler among them. Not a prophet, not a lone wanderer, not a singular beast set apart by some unusual mark. I would be one body among many, low to the ground, broad and damp, my skin dark and green-black, slick with the basin’s constant moisture. I would look, to anything capable of seeing me clearly, like a wet stone overgrown with moss or fungal film. Only when I moved would the animal in me become apparent. My belly would stay close to the ground. My limbs would be short and strong. My feet would be padded and fringed, made to grip broken rock, root mats, and slippery mineral crusts. Along my sides, ribs, shoulders, and jaw would be flat listening patches, membranes stretched over hidden hollows, through which the world would enter me not as picture but as pressure.

I would hear with my whole body.

The stone under me would speak. A distant collapse would arrive as a dull shudder through my limbs. A fissure widening somewhere unseen would make a thin, grinding change in the rock. Rain before it fell would be announced by the shifting weight of air, by the behavior of leaves, by the sudden urgency of smaller creatures moving overhead. I would know the basin less as scenery than as vibration. Shapes would come to me first as tremor, then as outline, then as faint glimmer: blue-green fungal caps, amber moss disturbed by passing legs, the weak phosphorescence of leaves, the scattered living sparks of my own kind moving through the dark.

I would not have a name in the way a human name works. If I had anything like a name, it would be a place in a chorus. A particular interval. A small recurring pulse in the greater sound. I would be recognized by the way my call entered the calls of others: a little late, a little softer, perhaps carrying a faint tremor of alarm or curiosity that others could feel in their ribs. My identity would not be sealed inside me like a stone inside a box. It would be distributed through resonance. When I was alone, if such a condition could even be called alone in that crowded land, I would be wary and incomplete, a single note separated from its chord. When pressed among others, I would become more capable, more aware, more present. The basin would not reward solitude as selfhood. It would reward responsiveness.

So I would be, first, a listener.

That feels true. Before hunter, before eater, before breeder, before anything with ambition or memory, I would be a listening thing in a world made of noise. I would crouch in the dark seam between fallen stones and feel the land deciding what to do next. I would know by vibration whether the mud ahead was firm enough to cross, whether a fungal shelf was about to shed spores, whether a swarm had recently passed through the vines and left them trembling, whether the warmth rising from below was a harmless breath or the prelude to a vent opening. My intelligence, if I had one, would not be a cold, separating intelligence. It would be an intelligence of contact. Of immediate interpretation. Of knowing what to do because the world was already doing it through me.

I would live among the Thrum-born in gatherings that formed and dissolved according to the basin’s moods. We would not have a home. We would have conditions. When a warm pool appeared, we would crowd toward it. When a shelf collapsed and filled the air with dust and spore-light, we would scatter. When rain fell in one of its brief hot curtains, we would become frantic with breeding sound, our bodies pressed into shallow water, our throats and ribs making the air tremble. When the rain stopped and the pools began to shrink, we would withdraw into whatever damp remained: under root mats, beneath fungal brackets, into cracks where condensation still wept down the stone.

I would not build anything.

That is important. I would not make a shelter. I would not carve a mark into the rock. I would not twist vine into cord or fit a stone into a handle or keep a shell because it was beautiful or useful. I might lift a shell once, perhaps, feeling its fine edge against my mouth or fingers. I might use it for a moment to scrape warm mineral crust from a ledge. Then I would leave it where it fell, because the basin would already be moving on. The rain would shift. The ledge would change. Another creature would occupy the place. The shell would become part of the general litter of the world, no more mine than the stone beneath me was mine. The thought of keeping it would not arise in me as a desire. Possession would feel alien there, because nothing remained long enough to be possessed.

If there were anything like technology in me, it would only be as a future tense.

I would be the hesitation before making. The almost-gesture. The hand that closes around a sharp flake and then opens again. Not because I was too simple to understand the flake’s edge, but because the world around me was already sharp, already breaking, already shaping and unshaping faster than any single creature could imitate. The basin did not need me to make an artifact. It was full of natural edges, natural points, natural containers, natural heat. What it lacked was permanence. And I, being of that place, would lack permanence too.

I would be where the noise was thickest.

Not because I desired noise, but because silence there would mean emptiness, danger, or absence. I would be among the layered sound: the deep geological groaning below, the hiss of steam meeting damp air, the metallic rasping of insects, the wet calls of amphibians, the continuous roar of wings overhead, the clicking shells, the drumming on hollow stems, the rustle of swarms moving as one body through the leaves. I would not experience that noise as confusion. I would experience it as texture. As map. As warning and invitation. My own calls would enter it carefully. A low thrum to signal presence. A quick click to answer another. A flashing of blue pores in the dark when the chorus tightened. An amber pulse if something large moved too near, or if the ground shifted under us with that slow, sickening give that meant collapse.

In such a place, I would be afraid often, but not in the clean, solitary way of an animal exposed in an open field. My fear would be communal. It would travel through the cluster. It would arrive as a shared tightening, a sudden change in rhythm, a wave of amber light passing from flank to flank. I would not be brave as an individual. I would be brave as a body in a crowd, or I would not be brave at all. When we were many, we could do things no one of us could do alone: press into a soft edge to keep it from giving way, form a living bridge across a narrow crack, guide younger bodies toward stable stone by the sound of our calls, shake loose water from overhead leaves with a sustained chorus, warn one another of a vent opening before the first hot breath reached us. In those moments, I would not be merely myself. I would be a functioning part of a temporary mind.

That is perhaps the strangest answer: I would be both one creature and less than one creature.

If someone asked, “Who are you there?” the truest answer might be: I am a participation.

I would be one wet body crouched under a fungal shelf, yes. I would feel hunger, heat, thirst, the pressure of others, the urge to breed, the need to move when the ground changed. But I would also be the pattern made by many bodies when they aligned without command. I would be the sudden hush that meant danger. I would be the ripple of blue light when the chorus turned from warning into reunion. I would be the low pulse under the rain that said: here, here, still here. In a world where the ground itself kept opening, collapsing, steaming, and filling, I would not seek to become a monument. I would seek to become a coordination.

I would be especially near the fungal shelves.

Something draws me to them in the imagining. They would be among the closest things the basin had to architecture without building. Broad, soft, tough, jutting from broken rock, some pale as bone, some dark and slick, some splitting open to release shining clouds of spore. They would glow faintly in the dark, and their glow would make the world feel less empty without making it clearer. I would rest beneath them when the air above grew too dry or too full of flying bodies. I would feel their caps tremble when sound passed over them. I would know, by the way they brightened and dimmed, that the basin was listening through even its growths. If I had anything like reverence, it would be for them: not because they were holy, but because they made the place feel inhabited by attention.

I would also be near the leaf pools.

Those tiny worlds would fascinate me. Each cupped leaf holding a little warm universe: larvae, snails, beetles, frog-spawn, drowned wings, drifting spores. I would drink from them. I might press my mouth to their inner curve and filter the water through fringed mouth-folds. I would feel the small lives moving inside, not as separate from my own survival but as part of its immediate texture. If I were young, perhaps I would have come from such a pool, one of the translucent larvae that survived long enough to become a damp, crouching juvenile among the adults. My earliest memory, if memory existed in me, would not be of a face or a nest. It would be of vibration through water: the chorus of adults making the pool tremble around me, the light of eggs glowing in foam, the vast dark full of sound.

I would not have parents in the human sense.

I would have emergence. I would have the accident of surviving. I would have the general pressure of the basin shaping me from the moment I could move. The adults around me would not teach me by instruction. They would teach me by resonance. I would learn which calls meant safety by hearing them used. I would learn which rhythms accompanied food by feeling other bodies move toward it. I would learn danger by the way the ground changed under so many feet at once. If I lived, it would be because I could hear the world quickly enough and answer it well enough. My education would be acoustic, immediate, and collective. There would be no childhood long enough for tenderness in the human manner, but there might be something like tenderness in the way bodies pressed together during sleep, conserving heat and wetness, breathing into one shared pulse.

I would sleep in heaps.

Not because I was affectionate in a simple sense, but because the basin made separateness costly. In the warmer hollows, when the vents breathed gently and the air thickened with condensation, we would gather body against body, our skins slick, our pores dimming to faint sparks. Our breathing would slow into a communal rhythm. The sound of us would become almost geological: a low, moist murmur beneath the greater noise of insects and falling water. In those heaps, I would not dream in the way humans dream, perhaps. But I might carry forward patterns. Echoes. A repeated tremor. A half-call from a pool that no longer existed. The memory of warmth under pressure. If dreams belonged to me there, they would be choruses, not stories.

I would die easily.

That must be true. In a world that crowded, changed, heated, collapsed, and renewed itself without pause, one body could not insist on lasting. I might be crushed by a falling shelf. I might be swallowed by mud when a bank gave way. I might be taken by swarm or burrower while weak from heat. I might simply be in the wrong hollow when a vent opened and turned the air to steam. If I died, my body would not remain mine for long. Beetles would come. Larvae would gather in the moisture around me. Fungal threads would enter my skin. My pores might flicker one last time as the nervous architecture failed, then go dark. Other Thrum-born might pause near the place where I had been, giving a low, broken call that was more absence than grief. But the basin would fill the pause almost immediately. There would be too much life for any single absence to endure.

And yet that would not make me worthless.

It would make me exactly what the land permitted: temporary, necessary, and full.

If I had to say where I would find myself, I would say: in the lower folds of the basin, where heat and water meet and the ground is still uncertain of its own angles. I would be in the damp dark between stone and root, near a pool that may not last the hour, under a fungal shelf trembling with sound. I would be among the Thrum-born, one glow in their constellation, one pulse in their living chord. I would be pressed to the world rather than standing above it. I would be wet, warm, listening, and plural in my being.

If I had to say who or what I would be, I would say: I would be a responsive thing.

Not a maker. Not a keeper. Not a conqueror. Not a lonely mind gathering the world into objects. I would be an animal of resonance, born into an overcrowded first hour, shaped by noise, sustained by heat and moisture, belonging to the basin only as long as I could move with its changes. I would be the basin’s own attention for a little while: a patch of dark skin and listening membranes, a blue flicker in the crowd, a note in the chord that helps the land remain alive for another moment.

And if you came looking for me there, you would not find me as a figure waiting to be recognized. You would feel me first as a change in pressure. A small shift in the chorus. A trembling in the water of a leaf. A faint blue pulse between black stems. Then, if you stayed still long enough, you might see the shape beneath the glow: low, broad, slick, pressed against the rock as if listening to the future being made inside the stone.

That would be me.

Not above the basin.  
Not outside it.  
Not ruling it.  

Inside it.  
Part of its noise.  
One warm, temporary voice in the dark.
