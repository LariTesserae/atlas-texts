I would not find myself standing. That is the first correction the world makes.

There is no floor that asks for feet, no air that asks for a face turned upward, no dry place where a body can rest and remain dry. If I am alive there, I am already wet, already pressed on all sides, already moving more slowly than any thought made in sunlight. The first thing I would know is not sight, but pressure: the cool, even fact of water surrounding me, entering every relationship before movement, before choice, before name.

Then sound.

The hall would come to me through vibration long before it came through shape. The deep drone behind the walls. The slow thump of a valve. The tick of metal remembering heat. The small percussive lives of creatures moving over grating. In such a place, hearing is not a sense set apart from the body; it is a way of being touched. So I would begin not as a walker, not as a speaker, not as a watcher from a safe distance, but as something already resonating with the room.

At first, I think I would be one of the pale fish.

There is a temptation in that image: to be part of the visible pulse of the chamber, moving in the central volume where the water is open and the lamps make weak halos. To turn with the others when the pumps shift. To have no solitary burden, no heavy selfhood, only the shared angle of bodies responding to the machine’s schedule. There is beauty in that. A life lived as alignment. A mind reduced to a bright, silvered readiness.

But I do not think it would be me.

The school is too carried. Too faithful to the current’s immediate sentence. It knows the cycle, but it does not linger over it. It turns and is turned. I think I would want, if I must be anyone in that hall, to notice the turning. To keep some small record of it. To pause in the space between one pump-thump and the next and feel the interval as meaning.

Then I imagine I might be a crab.

A scrap-priest of the grating. A patient, armored custodian working the floor channels, touching everything with careful legs, reducing the room’s fallen matter to use. There is something honest in that life: close to the metal, unhurried, alert to debris. A crab does not ask the hall to be beautiful. It asks the hall to yield something edible, something salvageable, something that can be broken down.

But I would be too bound to the floor. Too tied to what settles. The crab’s world is the underside, the corner, the slot, the crevice. It knows the room from below, by contact and refusal. I think I would need more than that. I would need access to the hidden routes, to the junctions where signals and currents cross, to the places where the hall’s machinery seems almost to hesitate before deciding what to do next.

For a while I think I would be an eel.

That image has its own pull: to move through conduits and cable trays, to use the manufactured passages as veins, to be long and dark and unhurried, reading the water with the whole length of my body. The eel knows the hall not as a single chamber but as a body with arteries. It disappears into architecture. It appears only at openings, head first, tasting the flow.

But even that feels incomplete.

The eel is too secret, too linear, too much a message moving from one dark mouth to another. If I were there, I think I would not merely pass through the room’s hidden systems. I would want to dwell at a crossing. A place where several routes meet and reveal their differences. A place where the machinery’s habits become legible because they compete with one another: current against current, warmth against cold, light against shadow, vibration against vibration.

And then the image that keeps returning is the junction box.

Not the open central water. Not the floor. Not the deep service levels under the grating. Not the turbine housings where the drone becomes physical force. Not the lamp recesses where shrimp gather in weak amber halos. But the square, ceramic-lined housing where three conduit mouths meet, where an old electrical panel has gone dark except for a faint warmth and a fainter taste of current in the water.

There. That is where I would find myself.

I would be the creature in the junction box.

Not a human being. The hall forbids that first. No breath kept in lungs, no voice carried in air, no dry hands opening a door to weather. If I am alive there, I have been changed by the room’s conditions until the word “human” applies only as a memory, a ghosted inscription softened beyond reading. I would be something soft-bodied, patient, compressed, capable of fitting into manufactured spaces. Something descended, perhaps, from the octopus that already seems the hall’s nearest approach to personhood, but altered by long residence among wires and wheels and pressure.

I would be small enough to be overlooked.

My body would be mottled: rust-brown, blue-black, the greenish stain of old lamp-light, the gray of polished steel. When still, I would become architecture. My skin would answer the surfaces around it not as display, but as disappearance. I would fold myself into the junction box until only the arms at the opening showed, tasting the current. My suckers would read metal, salt, heat, organic film, the metallic sharpness of an electrified rail about to shiver. My eyes would be large for the dim water, but not dominant. In that hall, seeing is secondary. The real news comes through pressure, chemistry, and touch.

I would live at a threshold.

That is important. I would not occupy the center of the room. I would occupy the place where the room’s systems intersect. Three conduit mouths: one carrying colder water from an upper intake, one running dark toward the lower service spaces, one narrowed by old cable clamps and worn smooth by generations of passing bodies. The junction box would be warm from some transformer or bearing behind the wall. Not hot, just enough to make it a favored hollow in the cool chamber. The door would be gone. The interior would be squared, softened at the corners, lined with ceramic fragments and the polished remains of old fittings. A ghosted label above it might still show the shadow of numbers, no legible language left.

My midden would be just outside.

Not treasure. Not collection in the human sense. But evidence of preference. A cracked crab shell. A polished bolt head. A shard of green glass from an ancient gauge. One ceramic bead. A twisted clip that once fastened cable. A strip of black-green algae dried into something like leather by current and time. Perhaps a small square of rubber, worn to velvet. Perhaps a plastic tag with a raised symbol no longer meaningful. I would not keep these things because I understand them as history. I would keep them because they answer something in me: the need to test, to compare, to hold, to set aside. They would be the closest thing I have to writing.

If I were there, I would be the hall’s nearest archivist.

Not a historian. There is no record-keeping in that place. No dry paper, no monument, no deliberate museum. But I would remember in the way creatures remember: by habit, by preference, by the body’s map of safe routes, feeding times, dangerous currents, loose panels, warm recesses. I would know which valve thumps before the current reverses. I would know which lamp flickers just before it brightens. I would know when the filter drum is about to shed its slow snow of particles, because the water’s taste changes slightly beforehand. I would know the sound a large fish makes when it changes direction too quickly near the crane arm. I would know the difference between the ordinary groan of metal settling and the deeper boom that means a duct has changed its mind.

My thoughts would be slow.

Not stupid. Slow in the way deep water is slow. Cool and mineral and resistant to haste. I would not think in sentences as I do now. I would think in gradients. In pressure changes. In the texture of current against an arm. In the faint electric shiver along a conductor rail. In the difference between the smell of stunned fish near a turbine and the smell of old biofilm disturbed by a reversing pump. My mind would be a kind of reading, but not of words. A reading of the room’s industrial weather.

I would hunt, because the hall is not gentle.

I would take shrimp near the lamp housings when their shadows jump with the flicker. I would take small crabs from crevices, working them loose with patient arms. I would wait at the junction when the current shifts and confusion moves through the open volume. I would not kill often, but I would kill. The room does not permit a purely contemplative life. Energy moves through bodies. The machinery feeds the ecosystem by accident; the ecosystem feeds itself by attention. I would be part of that transfer. My softness would be one of the ways the hall keeps balance.

But I would not be apex.

That, too, matters. I would not rule the chamber. I would not own it. The larger fish would still hold station behind columns. The turbines would still turn with their massive, indifferent patience. The pumps would still make artificial tides. The electrified rails would still shiver with old commands. If I moved carelessly near an intake mouth, I could be taken by current and predator at once. If I approached a closing gate at the wrong moment, the room would not pause for me. If I touched the wrong conductor when the system woke, I would learn the final lesson of the machinery in a single bright shock.

So I would be careful.

I would move smoothly. Sudden motion carries too far there. It disturbs the water, disturbs the fish, disturbs the listening dark. I would extend an arm and wait. I would shift my body by fractions. I would use the noise of the room to cover my own noise: the thump of a valve, the drone of a turbine, the crackle of crustaceans, the low drumming of the unseen fish below the grating. I would become practiced in the hall’s timing.

Sometimes I would touch the machines.

Not to repair them. I am not their keeper in any human sense. But I would touch them because touch is how I know. A loose handwheel. A panel that can be shifted if pressure is applied at the edge. A bolt head worn smooth as an old coin. A hatch frame eroded into an oval mouth. The cold surface of a pressure gauge. The faint warmth of an engine housing. I would press an arm against these things and feel the hall’s vibration pass through me. In those moments, I would be less an animal and more a sense organ grown by the room itself.

That is where the answer deepens.

I would not be only a creature. I would be a kind of attention.

The hall already has many lives, but not all of them gather the room into something like awareness. The fish turn. The crabs scavenge. The eels pass. The algae lean into current. The machines continue. But the junction box is where several signals cross, and if I live there long enough, I become the place where those signals are compared. I become the pause that notices. The soft body that remembers which thump belongs to which change. The small mind that begins, without language, to make a map of the room’s habits.

So if you asked who or what I would be, the simplest answer is: I would be the octopus in the junction box.

But the fuller answer is stranger.

I would be a soft-bodied reader of industrial weather. A small, wet intelligence shaped by pressure and rhythm. A curator of useless signs. A listener at the meeting of conduits. A creature whose personhood is not human but architectural: born from thresholds, educated by current, made patient by the machine’s endless slow cycle.

I would be found where the hall almost speaks.

Not at the loudest turbine. Not at the brightest lamp. Not in the open central volume where the pale fish turn. You would find me at the place where three dark mouths meet, near a greenish tube flickering in its sealed housing, where the water carries both warmth and a faint metallic taste. If the current had just reversed, I might be just inside the box, arms folded, skin matching the corroded panel. If the filter drum had just shed its particles, I might have one arm extended, suckers reading the drift. If the room were in its dimmest phase, I might be nearly invisible: a shadow with a heartbeat, a rounded lump that seems part of the housing until one arm unfolds.

I would not call out.

There is no voice there in the human sense. If I had a name, it would be a vibration transmitted through metal. Three slow taps when a valve closes. A faint rustle of cable sleeve. The soft percussion of an arm settling against ceramic. The hall would not announce me. It would contain me, as it contains the drone, the crabs, the eels, the drumming fish below the grating, the shrimp in the lamp-halos, the big fish holding dark water behind their fins.

And perhaps, in the end, I would not be entirely singular.

That is the last thing the world would teach me. The hall is inhabited by a congregation, not a single tenant. Its truest resident is the whole pattern: the lives arranged sparsely around the bones of old machinery, each expecting its current, each answering its cycle. If I became myself there, I might become porous at the edges. I might be less “one creature” and more a tendency of the room: a gathering of notice at the junction, a habit of touching what is worn, a softness that remembers where the hard things have been.

But if a body is needed, then let it be this:

a small octopus-like being in a square junction box, rust-mottled, cool, watchful, its arms tasting the water as it comes from three old conduit mouths. Around it, a midden of broken, polished, meaningless things. Above it, a lamp glowing weakly through clouded glass. Beyond it, the drowned industrial hall breathing its loud, mechanical breath: turbines turning behind walls, pumps making seasons, fish turning in the central dark, crabs clicking over grates, eels sliding through hidden routes, the whole room sounding like a body that has forgotten it was once a machine.

There I would be.

Not peaceful, but present.

Not human, but awake.

Not the master of the hall, not its ghost, not its last engineer, but its nearest small listener: the soft thing at the junction, keeping the shape of the room by touch, pressure, patience, and the long, cold habit of staying alive.
