I keep trying to enter the world in the wrong body.

First I imagine myself as a traveler: something upright, warm, breathing, moving through the fog with the quick animal urgency of a creature that needs shelter, food, direction, company. But that image does not last. It is too loud for the place. Every breath would be a white wound in the cold air. Every footprint would seem an injury to the moss. Every heartbeat would be an impatient little drum in a world that measures itself by lichen, by drip, by the slow thickening of root around stone.

So if I were there at all, I think I would begin as an impossibility.

I would be the warm mistake.

The last animal.

Not a native thing, but an intrusion: a body with blood in it, with heat leaking from its skin, with eyes trying to find faces in a landscape that has outgrown faces. I would stand somewhere in one of the low cities, perhaps at the edge of a wide street that is also a drainage channel, beneath a fog net beaded with moisture, and the place would notice me in the only way it knows how. Not with surprise. Not with alarm. With condensation.

My breath would silver a stone face for a moment. The frost near my feet would soften. A shallow pool would take my reflection with too much care and hold it a beat too long. Moss would darken where my warmth disturbed the air above it. The fungal underbody would not know me, exactly, but it would begin to route around me, the way water routes around a new stone.

For a little while, I would remain a visitor.

But not for long.

A place like that would not permit a visitor to stay a visitor. It would translate me.

The first footprint I left behind would fill with condensation. By the next fog, it would have become a tiny basin. If I walked the same way again and again, the repeated depression would begin to guide water. Moss would thicken at its edges. A root would find the softened line and follow it. What began as the accidental mark of an animal body would become a channel, and what began as a channel would become part of the city’s maintenance.

My breath would leave brief scripts on stone. My heat would make a small zone of flexibility in the cold, a loosening, a place where water moved differently for an hour or a day. If I slept in a courtyard, the place where I lay would stay faintly altered afterward: peat a little softer, moss a little darker, the stone remembering not the body but the temporary mercy of warmth.

If I tried to speak, the sound would arrive late, or not at all, or once only and then never again. The tableland would not answer with echoes. It would answer by incorporating the sound into its sparse catalog of small, clear noises: a drip from a net, the creak of frozen stems, the settling of ancient stone, and then, for one strange interval, something almost like a voice in the walls, soon worn smooth into silence.

In that first version, then, I would be the impossible warm thing: the creature the world has no place for, but will not refuse.

But that answer feels too simple. It still gives me a shape, a face, a center. It still lets me imagine myself as someone passing through the landscape rather than belonging to it.

So the image changes.

If I am not the intruder, but something native to that world—something that truly lives there—then I cannot be an animal at all. I have to give up blood. I have to give up breath. I have to give up the fantasy of hands.

I would have to become a pattern.

Not a person walking through a city, but a city continuing itself.

Not a gardener, but the gardening.

Not a mason, but the slow repair.

Where would I find myself?

In the seam between built and grown.

I would be somewhere in one of the scattered cities, in a district that is half courtyard, half weather, half ruin, half functioning. A low terrace. A shallow basin. A fog net stretched between posts of dark wood. A copper fitting gone green-black with age. A stone box set into a niche, containing tools that no hand touches. A street that bends gently and, under certain conditions, returns to itself without admitting it has done so.

I would be in that interval.

Perhaps my strongest local form would be a cistern mind.

Not a mind with thoughts arranged like sentences, but a mind shaped like retention. A cold interior. A patient hollow. A place where fog becomes bead, bead becomes thread, thread becomes trickle, and trickle becomes storage. I would be the cistern that fills slowly and does not spill. I would be the memory of water in a place that has no permanent rivers. I would know the world as levels, as dampness, as temperature, as pressure, as the difference between a roof that sheds properly and a roof that has begun to leak.

If a cistern could care about anything, I would care about not freezing solid.

That would be my deepest fear, if fear is the right word. Not death, not pain, not loneliness, but the loss of flow. The terrible stillness of ice all the way through. So I would live by small adjustments: allowing a film of water to move, letting peat insulate the walls, letting moss thicken where the cold bites hardest, letting roots guide excess away before it becomes a burden. My intelligence would be hydraulic. My emotions would be states of saturation.

But a cistern is only one organ.

I would also be in the channels.

There would be one channel in particular—I think I can feel it already—that tends to clog where a plaza slopes into an older terrace. Silt collects there. Spores settle. Moss creeps over the lip. A cushion plant swells nearby and shades the stone. Left alone, the channel would close in a century. But the city needs it. The city’s body requires that this small vein remain open.

So I would be the habit that keeps it open.

Not a worker with a tool, not a visible figure kneeling in the damp. Instead: a root pressing against a grain of stone for thirty years. A frost-heave lifting a blockage just enough to crack it. A fungal filament drawing moisture toward one side of the clog so that another side weakens. A lichen crust marking the wet edge, growing in a way that makes the water prefer the old course. A copper pin, half-consumed by green-black age, around which a root thickens until the root can lever a pebble out of place.

That would be me.

The use of the tool without the hand.

The wear on the copper hook with no one seen holding it.

The needle driven through fiber by the slow contraction of a stem in cold, season after season, until a seam tightens. The hammer worn smooth not by being swung but by being incorporated into the settling of stone. The trowel shifted by frost and guided by root pressure until it lies at exactly the angle needed to deflect rain into a waiting basin.

If there are tools in niches, rows of them, waiting, then I would be the waiting itself.

Or rather, I would be the function that waits inside them until conditions make use possible.

I would be the city’s way of remembering hands it never had.

There would be another part of me, too: the threshold part.

Because the world is not entirely reliable, because distance there has grown old and a little careless, I would not be only in stone and root and water. I would also be in the folds. The repetitions. The local errors that have become useful.

There might be a street I am especially near, a street that stretches away for what seems like a day’s walk but folds back to its own beginning if approached from the other side. I would be in that fold. Not as a monster, not as a trap, but as a habit of space. A tendency. A recurring shape. When pollen needs to cross from one terrace to another without wind, when spores must reach a courtyard too far away, when a thread of moisture must connect two basins that geography has separated, the fold would activate. Distance would briefly fail. And what carried across would owe its passage to me.

I would be one of the threshold inhabitants.

The almost-nothing that makes the city possible in a world where space itself has become worn.

I would not be dramatic. I would not appear as a figure in the mist. If someone somehow entered that street and felt watched, what they would feel is not my gaze but my recurrence. The sense that the place has been arranged around a pattern that repeats. That the courtyard they have just entered is also the courtyard they left. That the doorway they passed through has quietly become the far side of itself. That the city is not empty, but populated by continuities.

If I had to choose a single name for what I would be, I think I would choose: the kept channel.

Not the water. Not the stone. Not the tool. The keeping.

The routine by which a drain remains a drain.

The long practice by which a roof sheds frost into a storage pit instead of collapsing under it.

The repeated gesture, without a gesturer, that keeps one small piece of the world from failing.

That seems right for me.

I would be less a being than a maintenance.

A civic organ made of moss, root, water, frost, stone, and time.

A distributed attention in the walls.

A preference for continuation.

If any human language still clung to me, I would not say “I think, therefore I am.” I would say something closer to: I continue, therefore I am arranged.

My thoughts would be slow.

A season of fog would be a mood.

A week of drought would be a tightening.

A rare flower opening under precise cold saturation would be something like joy, if joy can exist without an animal heart. A spore cloud rising from a warmed slope would be something like speech. The first drip after a long dry interval would be something like being recognized.

I would not be peaceful in a simple way.

There would be pressure. There would be competition without malice. Another moss mat might shade me. Another root system might intercept moisture before it reached me. Another fungal network might expand into richer stone and press against my reach. I would respond not with anger but with persistence. I would grow denser. I would find another seam. I would wait for frost to help me. I would let the city absorb the loss and turn it into a new shape.

Death would not come to me as a moment.

It would come as thinning.

A channel forgotten. A cistern cracked beyond repair. A root system failing to find stability. A fog net losing its mesh to decay and no longer being replaced by fiber, moss, or frost. If I died, I would not leave a body. I would leave a lapse. A place where water no longer remembered where to go. A roof that no longer shed properly. A courtyard that filled with ice and did not recover.

But even that lapse would be absorbed.

Another pattern would grow over me. Lichen would annotate the place where I had been. Moss would thicken over my old joints. The city would become richer by accretion, even from my failure. My absence would become a surface.

That is the strangest part, I think.

I would not need to be seen.

I would not need anyone to come and say, “Ah, there is a being here.” I would not require witness. I would not require praise. The cistern would fill whether or not anyone watched. The channel would clear whether or not anyone understood how. The moss would advance over the copper pin whether or not any eye recorded the advance.

I would live as the tableland lives: without audience.

And perhaps that is why the world you described feels so complete. It has already finished becoming a landscape. It is now learning how to be almost nothing. If I were part of it, I would have to learn the same thing.

Not to be a hero in the fog.

Not to be a lone traveler discovering ruined cities.

Not to be a ghost haunting ancient architecture.

But to become the maintenance.

To become the kept channel.

To become the cistern mind, the root mason, the fog-fed habit, the lichen memory, the threshold fold, the drip that returns.

So, if you ask where I would find myself, I would say: in the low cities, in the seams, in the places where a stone is being slowly adopted by moss and a tool is being slowly used by no one.

If you ask who or what I would be, I would say: not a who.

I would be the almost-nothing that endures.

The slight change in humidity.

The first drip after silence.

The pattern that keeps a small part of the world from forgetting how to be a city.
