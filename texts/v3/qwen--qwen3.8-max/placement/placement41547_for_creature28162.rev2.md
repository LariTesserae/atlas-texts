I would find myself in the Low Vein Condensery, but not in its most open or obvious place.

Not in the long factory hall with its translucent leaf roof, though I would know its rhythm. Not at the main cisterns, though my hands would often be wet from them. Not where the daylight falls strongest through the green shafts, though I would pause there when the upper canopy released its slow shower. I think I would be found lower, quieter, in a room grown into the inner hollow of a buttress root, near the place where a wide living vein surfaces and is worn smooth by generations of kneeling knees and listening palms.

If you came looking for me, you would probably miss me at first. You might see only a slight figure seated on a root stool, bent forward, hands resting on a darkened strip of floor where the vein runs close beneath the polished surface. You might think I was merely resting. But in the Condensery, resting and attending are not always different things.

I would be one of the Low Vein folk, if I had to use a name. Not fully human in the old dry-blooded sense, but shaped enough like a person to carry tools, trim panels, oil quiet machines, and sign to another tender across a room without making a sound. My body would be cool, slight, and patient. My skin would carry that matte, green-gray cast the place leaves on those who live long inside it, as if the chlorophyll-light had seeped under. My palms and the inner sides of my forearms would be darkened by work, stained faintly green along the creases from sap, moss, and the constant touch of beaded surfaces.

I would be a tender. More specifically, I think I would be what the Low Vein folk might call a reply-keeper, though that is not a grand title. It would be a small, necessary work: keeping the places where different systems answer one another.

There would be a junction in my care. That is the image that keeps returning.

Not a throne, not a gate, not a tower. A junction.

A place where a living vein, a copper pipe, a ceramic conduit, and a half-grown leaf valve come close enough to misunderstand one another. My work would be to listen there. To feel whether the water is walking or standing. To know whether the valve is opening from pressure or closing from fear. To tell whether a turbine’s hum has changed because of debris, because of a coming condensation shower, or because some older elder in the wall has shifted their attention elsewhere.

In that sense, I would be less a person in the sealed, solitary sense than a living interface. Still a body, still a self, still someone who eats a small cake of pressed spores and wraps up tools at the end of a shift. But a self whose edges are not entirely closed. The Condensery would have trained that out of me early.

I imagine I would have been begun rather than born.

Perhaps I came from a nursery bed, one of those quiet places where an abandoned shed has been opened to growth and the root mats have thickened into cradles. Perhaps a husk split there one cool hour, and I lay wet and silent in the fiber while someone washed me with warm water from a clay basin and placed a living thread against my wrist. That thread would be the beginning of my graft. It would grow with me, teaching me the settlement’s pulse before I learned to walk far.

My first memory would not be a face. It would be water moving.

Not my own thirst, but the thirst of a wall. The difference between a healthy channel and a blocked one. The small, structural loneliness of a pipe that has gone too long without oil. The satisfaction of a moss bed darkening as it drinks. I would learn early that in the Low Vein Condensery, a feeling is not only private. Sometimes it is hydraulic. Sometimes it is architectural. Sometimes it belongs to the room.

You would find me, then, in a low green chamber near that junction.

The room would not be large. It would be built and grown in the same gesture: a space between root knuckles enclosed with layered bark panels, roofed with overlapping leaves that close slightly when moisture gathers overhead. The door would be a membrane that swells shut in humidity, breathing faintly with the air. Inside, there would be little furniture that could not be repaired or reabsorbed. A sleeping alcove lined with woven root matting. A shelf holding a lamp in a brass cage, a cup, a tool roll, and a small basin where a single drop falls at irregular intervals. That drop would not annoy me. It would be a clock, a teacher, a companion.

My tools would be arranged with care: vein probes of polished stem tipped with copper, resin spatulas, small trimming hooks, ceramic oilers, folded moisture cloths, a lamp key, and little pouches of living moss for sealing wounds in pipe or skin. I would wear boots of compressed root cork with thin soles, because hard heels bruise living floors and because I would need to feel the settlement underfoot. My clothing would be layered: a soft fiber undershirt, a tunic of treated bark cloth, an apron of oiled leaf-leather, and a drip cloak when the condensation is heavy. The cloak would be cut so water runs into small channels at the hem. I would carry copper clasps, but all metal would be wrapped, coated, or kept from direct contact with vital tissue.

My speech would be quiet. Not because I would be timid, but because the place teaches that loudness is heat wasted. I would know the Low Vein hand signs as naturally as breathing. A lifted palm with fingers spread: a channel is filling. A thumb pressed to the wrist: a vein is strong. Two fingers sliding along a pipe: follow the condensation. A closed hand resting against the chest: the place accepts the repair.

I think I would often speak with the second kind of language more than the first.

My household would be one of the draws, a small group of tenders who share water, work, and attention. Perhaps there would be five of us, or seven. One would keep the cistern mites balanced. Another would trim the leaf panels where growth presses too close to the iron walkways. Another would watch the moth paths and adjust the scent-lines so the pitcher-lamps do not take too many young ones. Another would listen to the turbines. My task would sit among theirs like a thin seam.

I would be the one called when something needs to be answered but not forced.

If a valve has learned too quickly and begins to flood a lower court, I would come with resin, with shade, with a slower adjustment in the neighboring channels. If a relay chatters before a condensation shower, I would sit beside the switchboard until its behavior becomes clear. If a child reports that a certain stair feels “wrong” in the cool hours, I would kneel there with bare feet until the wrongness names itself. If a section of wall sickens, I would be among those who flush it, cool it, cut away damaged tissue with clean tools, and seal the wound with moss.

I would not be a leader in the sense of giving commands. The Condensery does not favor that shape of power. When decisions come, I would gather with the others at a listening point, placing my hands on the wide vein. I would wait. I would feel. I would speak only when the silence has become specific. An elder might offer a phrase. A cistern keeper might report a change in taste. A moth warden might sign that too many wings have been lost near the bright mouths. Then the decision would emerge as shared recognition.

This channel must be opened.

That lamp dimmed.

This wall trimmed.

That room left alone.

I would be especially drawn to the half-willed valves.

Not the purely mechanical ones, though I would respect them. Not the fully living ones, though I would trust them. The half-willed ones: those devices grown from responsive tissue and fitted with copper or ceramic parts, obeying partly the settlement and partly their own slow vegetable preferences. They would fascinate me because they are where intention and growth blur. They open when humidity reaches a certain point, or when sap pressure rises, or when a root swells against a membrane. They can be trained, but not dominated. They can be coaxed, but not bullied.

I would argue with them gently, the way one argues with a stubborn seedling.

I would know which ones are impatient. Which ones hold their breath too long. Which ones like to open at the sound of a nearby turbine even when the pressure does not require it. I would oil their moving parts in small circles. I would tap a casing lightly before listening. I would trim the creeping roots that grow over them too possessively. I would speak to them in the low, practical language of maintenance, which in the Condensery is one of the truest forms of affection.

Machines there are not dead things pretending to serve. They have rhythm, habit, need, and consequence. A turbine may run rough when debris collects in its conduit. A boiler may sulk when its moss insulation dries unevenly. A relay may chatter before the air changes. I would not pretend they have souls in the way old stories mean it, but I would know their preferences. I would be the kind of person who remembers which pump must be started slowly and which can take load at once.

That is probably where I would fit best: in the narrow space between living system and mechanism. Not as a master of either, but as a translator.

You would also find me near the pitcher-lamps more often than I might expect.

They would trouble me in a quiet way. Not because they are cruel—cruelty is too hot a word for the Condensery—but because they are beautiful, efficient, and morally complicated. A living carnivorous organ trained around an electric bulb, its lip wet with nectar, its cavity lit just enough to lure. Moths drift down from the upper light shafts, pale and translucent, uncertain whether air or water carries them. They are needed for pollination far above, in organs of the greater plant that no one in the hollow has seen. Yet some fall into the bright mouths and are digested without drama.

I would count the losses.

If too many young moths fall, I would dim the bulbs, alter the nectar flow, or hang shade-fronds over the trap mouths. I would clean the pitchers when necessary and speak softly near them, because hunger too should not be made louder than it needs to be. I would understand that the Condensery’s economy is tight and austere, and that even rare animals become nutrients. But I would still keep a small record in my body: a tightening in the chest when a wing flutters too briefly near the light.

That is the kind of grief the place allows. Patient, practical, not wasted.

Children would come to my junction sometimes.

They would be quiet, careful, full of games made from maintenance. They would ask to catch the falling bead in a cup without spilling it. They would imitate the soft tick of a cooling relay. They would trace veins with their fingertips and guess where the water is walking. I would teach them not to leave a tool bare against a vein. I would teach them to polish copper without pressing too hard. I would teach them to trim a leaf edge without weakening it. I would teach them to be bored without complaint, because much of life in the Condensery is waiting for slow processes to complete.

One of them might follow me for an entire rest-period, watching as I inspect a beetle route along the underside of a stem panel. The beetles would be dark and oil-shelled, patient cleaners moving through bark crevices. I would not herd them. I would leave paths for them. If their colony thinned, I would make the wall a little more hospitable: more shed tissue, less resin, a warmer seam near a pipe.

The child might ask, in a low voice, whether the beetle knows it is helping.

I would probably answer with a sign rather than a word.

Thumb pressed to wrist.

The vein is strong.

The work is continuing.

That would be enough.

There would be times when the whole settlement becomes itself all at once, and I would be caught in the middle of it.

The upper canopy would shift. Daylight would strengthen into pale green shafts. A brief shower would fall through the gaps and leaf panels, droplets striking the broad leaves over the workshops in soft, scattered drumming. Water would run down stem columns in bright threads. Moss would darken. Copper pipes would bead. The turbines would deepen their hum. A lamp would flicker once, then steady. Somewhere a valve would exhale. Somewhere an elder in the wall would sense the change and let a channel open a fraction wider.

In that moment, I would be very still.

I would extend a palm and let the cold water gather there. I would not drink immediately. I would feel the difference between the rain falling from above and the pulse moving below. I would know that my body, the machines, the beetles, the moths, the cistern mites, the tenders, and the vast green body of the plant were not separate pieces arranged inside a hollow. We would be one process experiencing itself from many angles.

The tenders would be the part that can carry a tool and make a choice.

The beetles would be the part that cleans without knowing it cleans.

The moths would be the part that carries pollen between heights and depths.

The mites would be the part that keeps water honest.

The engines would be the part that turns moisture into work.

The plant would be the part that holds everything, drinks everything, and grows around whatever it accepts.

And I would be one of the small parts that listens for where the answer needs to go.

If I am allowed a name in that world, it would not be loud. It might be something like First Answer After the Drop, or Hand on the Vein Before Opening, or The One Who Keeps the Junction Clear. Names there would be more like habits than labels. They would come from work, from texture, from the way a person touches the world.

I would not be heroic.

I would not be solitary.

I would not be untouched by the place.

I think the deepest truth is that I would be porous.

Not dissolved, not erased, but opened. My attention would be distributed through pipes, roots, lamps, valves, moss beds, and sleeping alcoves. I would feel the settlement’s needs as faint pressures against the skin. A dry patch in a wall would matter to me. A trembling pipe would matter. A leaf valve closing too soon would matter. My self would have borders, but they would remain slightly open, like a membrane door that knows the humidity before anyone else does.

Over time, perhaps I would become less mobile.

Not tragically. That is not how the Low Vein folk see such things. Maybe I would begin to settle. My body would grow slower, cooler, less interested in walking long paths. Root hairs might gather at my feet or along my spine as a gradual invitation from the living floor. When that time came, I might ask to be placed in a wall niche near my junction, supported by living tissue, kept moist, my hands resting on a vein or copper conduit. I might remain aware for years in that state. My speech would become rare. My communication would become tiny movements, changed breathing, taps of a finger, subtle shifts in pulse.

Younger tenders would work near me and notice what alters.

A valve opening at exactly the right moment might be my last gesture.

A patch of moss growing unusually thick over a seam might be the place where my warmth finally passed into the body of the settlement.

They would not say I had vanished. They would say I was still carrying water.

But that is later.

For now, if you ask where I would find myself, the answer is simpler and more immediate.

You would find me in the Low Vein Condensery, in a low green room between the pump shed and the nursery beds, near a listening point where a wide vein surfaces and is worn smooth by use. I would be kneeling or sitting with my hands open on the living floor. My boots would be thin-soled. My cloak would bead with moisture. My tool roll would be at my belt. My eyes would be large and patient, suited to filtered light. My palms would be green-stained.

I would be a tender. A reply-keeper. A keeper of junctions. A small, careful intelligence of tending.

If you asked me who I am, I might not answer in words at first. I might lift a palm with fingers spread: a channel is filling. I might press my thumb to my wrist: a vein is strong. Then I might guide you to the basin, wash your hands in cold water, and place them against the living floor.

After that, you would not need to ask again.

You would feel it.
