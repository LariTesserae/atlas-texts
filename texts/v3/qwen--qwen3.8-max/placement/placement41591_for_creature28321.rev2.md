I would not find myself at the rim looking in.

That is the first thing I know when I let the place take shape around me. The rim is too exposed, too much like longing, too much like being an outsider who has not yet been changed by what he sees. If I belonged there, I would not stand at the edge gazing down into the haze where the lower world becomes absence. I would already be inside the density, in the warm narrow middle of things, where the leaves brush the shoulders and the walls are close enough to be known by the palms.

So the image changes. The high cold distance goes away. The feeling of arrival goes away. I am not climbing toward the basin. I am already there.

I think I would be one of the Hollow-born.

Not a stranger, not a guest, not a pilgrim who has come to marvel at the dryness and then leave. I would have a body made for the place: small-boned, warm-skinned, barefoot, with hands quickened by fine work and feet toughened by generations of stone and root. My skin would carry the faint sheen of oil rubbed into it morning and evening, not from vanity but from necessity, because even the body must be kept from losing what it holds. My hair would be dust-colored, perhaps ash-brown or rust-black, bound close under a woven cap or tied back with cord. My eyes would be large enough for the amber gloom, and my blinking would be slow, as if seeing itself had learned not to waste moisture.

I would not be young in the urgent sense, nor old in the failing sense. I would be somewhere in the long middle of competence: old enough to be trusted with small repairs, young enough to climb to the upper pipes when a wind-catcher has shifted. My age would be measured less by years than by what I can maintain without being reminded.

Where would I be?

Not at the center of the plaza, not in full view. I think I would be found at a seam.

There is a place between the second and third terraces, just above the low meeting ground where three levels fold together, where the lane narrows until one shoulder nearly brushes salt-hardened plaster and the other passes over a thick rosette of gray-green leaves. A stair turns there. The steps have been worn into shallow curves by many bare feet. A niche lamp burns in the wall at the turning, its orange flame low and steady in a shallow stone cup. Above, a ceramic tube projects from the upper story, its mouth angled toward the faint moving air. Beneath it, a rope guide has been rubbed smooth by cords. A fat-leaved vine has grown over the corner of the wall until the masonry seems to be held together by green, waxed patience.

There. I would be there.

My place would not be grand. It would be a workshop-niche, a sleeping alcove, a knot in the settlement’s living body. One side would be the old wall, older than several repairs, with patches showing where different hands have fitted new stone into ancient gaps. Another side would be a low shelf holding stoppered bottles, spools of fiber, bone awls, a copper pick worn to a soft curve, a blade of dark glass, and a small tray of resin beads. Above me, dried seedpods would hang from a beam, knocking together when the air moved. A roof creature would live somewhere in the darkness over my head, stealing bits of cord and leaving tiny scratch-marks along the beam. A lizard would rest on the warm plaster beside the lamp niche, its scales patterned like the wall itself, turning its head slowly as if it too were listening.

I would be a Seam-Listener.

That is the name that comes to me as I write. Not merely a Wind-Listener, though I would listen to wind. Not merely a Mender, though I would mend. A Seam-Listener would be one who attends to the places where things join: wall to roof, vessel to stopper, leaf to stem, pipe to air, voice to silence, person to person. In the hollow, where nothing must spill and everything must hold, the seam is sacred. The seam is where failure begins. The seam is also where the settlement sings.

My work would be to hear the first wrongness before it becomes rupture.

I would know the sound of a sealed jar when its wax has begun to dry too much. I would know the note a wall gives when a hidden crack is widening, not because the stone cries out but because its resonance becomes thinner, less trusting. I would know when a wind-catcher is drawing air too sharply, which might mean a vane has shifted on the roof or a passage below has been blocked by a basket left too long in the wrong place. I would know the difference between the rasp of a healthy leaf and the faint dullness of a leaf beginning to lose its inner seal. I would press my palm to a swollen leaf and feel whether it was full, resting, fevered, or asking for shade. I would press my ear to a stair-stone and know whether too many feet had worn a dangerous smoothness into it, or whether the tone still carried the old stability.

I would carry small things. A bit of sealing wax softened against the body. A cord of fiber. A bone awl. A pinch of dust from a particular terrace, useful for testing how new plaster will hold. A stopped bead of resin given to me when I was young, containing the lesson that what is kept is not hoarded but protected. I would not carry anything that poured openly. Nothing in me would want to spill.

My days would begin not with light but with sound.

Before the copper sun became even a dull smear through the haze, the birds would wake the upper terraces. One would give its short knock. Another would answer with a drawn whistle. A third would make the sharp double cry that means the air is shifting above the rooflines. I would wake to that, not as if to an alarm, but as if the hollow itself were breathing its first word into the day. I would lie still for a moment and listen to the settlement around me: the creak of rope, the faint ticking of beetles in the lower beds, the soft scratch of roof creatures in the beams, the first human footfall on a nearby stair.

Then I would rise.

I would not wash as people in wetter worlds wash. I would take a little oil on my fingers and rub it into my wrists, my face, the backs of my hands, the soles of my feet if they had become too dry in the night. The oil would smell faintly of resin and warmth. It would be a practical act and also a kind of prayer, though I would not call it prayer. It would be the first sealing of the day.

I would eat something dense and small: a cake of seed-meal bound with oil, a portion of fibrous root, perhaps a slice of resinous fruit cut with a dark blade from a larger mass kept wrapped in cloth. The food would be rich, warm, mineral, satisfying in the way that safety is satisfying. I would chew slowly. There would be no soup, no bowl of liquid exposed to air, no careless pouring. If I drank, it would be from a narrow-necked stone bottle stopped with wax and fiber, drawing the sealed draught through a tube, then closing it again at once. The closing would give me more pleasure than the drinking.

When I went out into the lane, people would pass close enough to touch. We would touch wrists, or foreheads if there were affection or need. If someone’s skin felt too hot, too dry, too tense, I would notice. If a pouch hung loose, if a cord was frayed, if a stopper was not seated correctly, my eyes would go to it without intention. That is the kind of attention the hollow would have trained in me.

I would not be important in the way leaders are important. I would not stand above others. But I would be needed.

In the morning, a Leaf-Tender might call me to listen to a vine whose leaves had begun to curl inward too early. I would press my ear near the stem, not because the vine truly spoke, but because the change in its sound under my knuckles would tell me whether the root bed was too cool or the wall was giving off sulfurous warmth too close. I would advise a shift in shade, or the removal of a stone, or the binding of a lower tendril to draw the plant’s weight away from a weak joint.

A child might bring me a cracked cup. The crack would be small, almost invisible, but the child would have heard it because the cup no longer rang true when tapped. I would praise the child for hearing. Then we would clean the seam, warm resin over a tiny lamp, press fiber into the gap, and seal it with careful thumbs. The child would watch my hands, and I would say something simple: “A crack is not a failure. It is a request.”

A Roof-Weaver might ask me to test a rope bridge that had begun to sound wrong underfoot. I would not look only at the fibers. I would listen to the bridge when no one was on it, when the wind moved through it, when a roof creature ran along its upper cord. The sound would tell me where the tension had shifted. I would tighten here, loosen there, replace a worn binding, and then step back until the bridge gave its old low creak again—not the creak of danger, but the creak of settled weight.

At midday, when the warmth gathered in the stone and the air thickened with dust and pollen and fine salt, I would rest in the shade of a roof overhang. I might sit with my back against a wall, legs folded, hands resting on my knees. A beetle would cross the packed floor in front of me, its feet ticking faintly. I would move aside for it if it came too near, not with ceremony, but with the same automatic care one gives to anything doing necessary work. Moths would rest under the eaves, their wings opening and closing by slow degrees. I might watch them for a long time. In their stillness I would feel the whole hollow breathing.

In the afternoon, I might climb to the upper pipes.

There, the settlement would open vertically: roofs becoming paths, vines braided over poles, reed tubes fitted into ceramic throats, bundles hanging in the warm dimness. The birds would be close. They would scold if I moved too quickly, and I would answer with a low hum so they knew I was not a threat. I would run my hands along a wind-catcher, feeling for roughness, for a hairline crack, for the slightest looseness where reed met clay. If the pipe’s note had changed, I would shave a minute curl from the lip with a small blade, or soften a joint with warmed resin, or adjust a wall slit below by pulling a woven vane a finger’s width to one side. The hoppers would stridulate on the lower terraces, their metallic notes answering one another. I would tune the pipe until it agreed with them.

That is the part I would love most, I think.

Not the silence, because the hollow is not silent. Not the stillness, because there is always movement, however slow. I would love the tuning. I would love being the one who helps the place remain coherent.

When evening came, the lamps would be lit one by one.

I would watch the Lamp-Keeper begin at the lower threshold and move upward, orange glow answering orange glow until the terraces seemed to breathe with small contained flames. I would stand where I was, perhaps with a cord in my hand, perhaps with my palm still resting on a warm wall. If another person was near, I would touch their wrist. If I was alone, I would place my hand against the wall and feel the day’s heat held there, not escaping, not wasted, but stored. Then I would hum a short phrase into the air, a closed-mouth sound, more vibration than melody. Somewhere another voice would answer. Somewhere a pipe would answer. Somewhere a bird would give its last clear cry before settling into the vines. The basin would become a sounding bowl again, filled with many lives occupying the same volume without breaking one another.

At night, I would sleep in a niche shaped by many bodies before mine. The bedding would be dry fiber, woven mats, perhaps a thin skin of softened bark. The air would stay warm. The stone would release heat slowly. Above me, roof creatures would make their soft busy patter, that sound like rain without rain. I would not mistake it for rain. I would know it for what it was: life continuing overhead, small hands and claws and restless bodies moving through the settlement’s upper bones. The sound would comfort me. It would mean the hollow was not still in the way of death. It would mean the hollow was still inhabited.

If I grieved, I would not weep.

I would feel the grief as a pressure behind the lips, a tightening in the chest, a need to close rather than open. Someone would come and touch their forehead to mine, or take my wrist in a firm, dry hand. We would stand that way until the pressure changed shape. Then we would do something small together: tie a cord, seal a jar, sweep leaf-crumb into a bed, oil a hinge of woven fiber. The sorrow would not be denied. It would be folded into maintenance. In the hollow, even grief would have to be kept from spilling.

If I loved, I would love in sealed ways.

I would not offer a cup. I would offer a stopper. I would carve a small bead from bone or salt crystal and seal it with resin, then give it to the beloved as a promise that something precious could be carried without being exposed. I would learn the sound of their breathing as they slept, not to possess it, but to know when they were rested, when they were troubled, when they needed warmth. I would touch their wrists as if listening for the seal of their body. I would walk with them in lanes so narrow that our shoulders brushed, and that brushing would be enough. It would be more than enough. It would be a language.

If I died, I would not leave the hollow.

My body would be washed in oils, wrapped in fiber, and placed in a dry niche where the warm air could draw me inward and away. Over time I would become light, quiet, mineral. My bones might be polished and set into a wall, not as a relic of fear but as a continuation of presence. A stair might remember the particular pressure of my feet. A pipe might keep the note I tuned into it. A child might learn a way of tying cord from someone who learned it from me. I would not become a ghost. I would become grammar.

That is who I would be.

Not a ruler. Not a prophet. Not a hero arriving from the lower world with hunger for rivers and open skies. I would be a person whose highest skill is attention. I would be one of the Hollow-born whose particular patience is the seam. I would be the one who listens for the sound before the leak, the wrongness before the break, the silence before the song loses its place. I would be a keeper of small closures, a mender of joins, a tuner of dry voices.

And perhaps, beneath the person, I would be something even simpler.

I would be the basin’s tendency to notice itself.

I would be the place where the hollow becomes aware of a loosened knot, a cracked bead, a vine grown too far over a wind path, a lamp burning too high, a child ready to be taught, a wall ready to be patched, a note ready to be returned. I would not stand outside the world and describe it. I would be inside its maintenance, inside its warmth, inside its loud and patient fullness.

You would find me in the warm dimness, near a stair-turn where the lamp is low and the leaves press close to the wall. I would be barefoot, oiled, dust-haired, carrying a bone awl and a little wax. My hands would be busy. My breath would be measured. If you came near, I would touch your wrist, not to ask for anything, but to know that you were sealed and present. Then I would turn back to my work, listening to the wind move through the pipes, listening to the beetle cross the stone, listening to the city hold its shape without water, without spill, without longing.

I would be life that has learned to remain where nothing flows.
