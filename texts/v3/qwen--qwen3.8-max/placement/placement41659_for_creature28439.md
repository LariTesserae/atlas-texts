I would find myself low, not on the broad shelves or the dark rims, but in one of the basin’s inner hollows where the stone leans close and the air is so saturated that breathing feels like drinking cold mineral dust. The place would be small enough to be missed: the sheltered side of a black basalt fin, under a broken lip of pale tuff, beside a shallow pan ringed with brittle white salt. Above me, on a smooth edge of rock, a bead of water would gather. It would enlarge, tremble, release. It would fall into a cup worn into the stone and make a clear, cold note. Around the hollow, half-buried in crusts, there would be one or two low rings of old, secreted architecture—shapes that might once have been alive, or at least alive in the habit of building. There: near the drip, near the note, near the ruin.

At first I would not seem to be a creature at all. I would be easiest to mistake for a mineral stain. I would begin as the film: a faint, slick, grayish skin on the shaded rock, living where the water persists. I would have no leaves, no roots, no greenness. I would feed on the newness of the stone—iron, salt, silica, the sour chemistry of fractured basalt and patient seepage. My body would be a thin varnish of life spread over hardness. I would grow outward by increments so slight that centuries might pass before my edge reached another crack. When the air was wet, I would be smooth and faintly shining; when it dried, I would contract into a dormant sheen and wait. I would not experience death in the ordinary way. I would experience pause.

But the basin would not let me remain only a skin. It would make me into relation. From the film, I would send threads—pale filaments crossing pores, binding powdered glass to salt, running along quartz veins like the faintest nerves. I would not think, exactly. Yet if a drop struck a ledge nearby, a tremor would move through those threads, and some part of me would tighten or loosen. I would be a small conductivity in the stone, a way for the wetness to know itself. In that sense, I would already be more than one thing. I would be a pattern of dampness, mineral, and attention.

Still, if the world asked me to become a single animal, I think I would not choose the grazer, though I understand its patience. I could imagine being that flat, pitted thing moving across the salt crust with glacial deliberation, scraping film into brightness, leaving polished lines behind like marks made by deliberate silence. I could imagine becoming stone when danger came, because in that basin danger would be nothing more than drying, disturbance, or time. I could also imagine being the vein-worm, almost invisible, threading through the thickest water film along pale mineral seams, knotting myself into sleep when the moisture thinned, unknotting when the world became wet again. Both lives would belong to me. Both would be plausible.

But the being I would most likely be is the tube-dweller.

I would live in a casing no larger than a thimble, cemented to the inner wall of a hollow cup where the drip falls often enough to sustain me but not so often that it scours me away. My tube would not be a shell I made once and carried. It would be accrued: calcite, silica, iron stain, powdered glass, salt cement, grain by grain, layer by layer, built from what the water brought and what my own body secreted.
