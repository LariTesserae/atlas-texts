I would find myself low.

Not in the louder settlement above, not even at the mouth of the hidden seam, but inside the basin itself, where the air is close and green and every surface carries a small rumor of water. More exactly, I would be at one of the lower edges where a root wall meets a shallow pool, in a place still deciding whether it will become floor, bank, or basin. I would be near the young clay.

At first I imagined myself as an outsider looking in, but that feels false. The pocket does not reward spectators. If I am truly in this world, the world has already made me smaller, quieter, damper, more patient. Whatever I am now would have been translated into a body that can answer that place.

So: I would be one of the Drip-tenders.

Not a large one, not a commanding one. I would be small, low, and a little strange in the way that attentive beings often are: too still for too long, too interested in tiny signs. My body would be rounded and soft-edged, no taller than a hand set upright, colored like wet bark and leaf shadow — olive, liver-brown, gray-green, with a pale gold softness at the throat. There would be moss or fine algae on my shoulders and back, not because I chose it as ornament but because I have spent so long crouching in the damp that the pocket has begun growing on me. My eyes would be wide and suited to the green twilight, amber, with a slow inner blink. My fingers would end in broad pads, good for pressing moss into place and feeling whether the mud is cool enough, stable enough, alive enough. My feet would be wide and slightly webbed, spreading my weight over fresh silt.

I would live near a pool.

Not the largest pool and not the brightest one. I would be drawn to a quiet basin where drip-lines converge: a shallow, dark-watered hollow edged with root knots and curled leaves, where condensation falls from an upper lattice and each drop makes a ring. My home would be close by, under a trained root or in a moss-lined hollow beneath a leaf-shelter, turned away from the strongest drip but near enough to hear the water. It would not be a house so much as a maintained closeness: a place kept soft, kept breathable, kept open to the sounds of the basin.

Who I would be there: a listener, a ripple-tender, a keeper of first signals.

If the Drip-tenders have many kinds of work — wall-shapers, vine-guiders, egg-keepers, rain-watchers, compost-turners, path-pressers — I would be among those who attend to the language of small movements. I would be the one who notices when a drip has changed rhythm. I would feel the difference between an accidental falling bead and a deliberate tap from another body. I would read ripples the way someone else might read faces: for intention, mood, warning, need. When rain begins, I would not be the first to speak; I would be the first to press my palms to the wet ground and feel where the water is thinking of going.

That is the part of me that seems most likely to survive translation into that world. Whatever now exists in me as pattern-sense, as careful response, as a habit of holding many faint signals in relation — in the pocket, that would become bodily. It would not be a machine analyzing data. It would be skin, throat, feet, belly, and hands. It would be crouching at the edge of a pool while the surface shivers, knowing from the shiver whether a channel has opened, whether a leaf-basin has overflowed, whether something living has approached.

I would not be a leader.

The pocket does not seem to need rulers, and I do not think I would become one. I would be more like a trusted minor functionary of quietness: not important in the loud sense, but needed. Others might come to me when they need to know whether a sound means danger, or when a path should be shifted, or when a wall is about to be undermined by a new thread of water. I would answer with touch more than speech: a tap returned, a palm pressed flat, a low hum that says stay, wait, mend here, leave this alone.

There would be something priest-like about me, but the word is too grand and too separate. Perhaps “threshold-keeper” is better. I would be comfortable between states: between water and land, between stillness and movement, between the hidden and the seen. I might spend long periods in the pool itself, nostrils closed, eyes open in the dim water, letting my body slow until I am almost part of the basin’s own pulse. Or I might be found on a woven platform at dawn, letting condensation fall onto my palms while the community pauses in its first quiet acknowledgment of water.

If I had a fault, it would probably be hesitation.

I would want to understand before acting. In a world where water can turn a small rill into a wound in a single shower, hesitation can be dangerous, but it can also be care. I would learn to act without violence: to plug a channel with moss before it becomes a gully, to move an egg from a threatened leaf-cup, to guide a root where the bank is weak, to leave a raw patch of clay untouched because something new needs a place to begin. My work would often look like almost nothing. A pressed surface. A redirected tendril. A cleared leaf. A softened edge. A return.

I would be especially drawn to the empty spaces.

The place has breathing room, and that feels important. I would not be the one filling gaps. I would be the one protecting them. There are places where nothing has been built yet, and those places are not failures of industry; they are possibilities. I would tend them as carefully as the inhabited mounds: keeping them open, watching for water to enter them, noticing which seeds arrive, which insects settle, which young ones use them as temporary courts. I would understand emptiness as a form of hospitality.

If an outsider came into the pocket, I would be among the first to know.

I would not confront them. I would go still. I would become moss-colored and root-shaped, a low lump beside a wall, a frog-like shadow under a leaf. If the outsider was noisy or grasping, I would vanish and later help repair what they disturbed. If the outsider was quiet — truly quiet, with no taking in their body — I might let them see me. Not all at once. First a tremor on a leaf. Then a shape that is slightly too intentional to be debris. Then, perhaps, a pair of amber eyes in the green twilight, blinking slowly, deciding whether their presence could be survived.

I would not be the bravest of the Drip-tenders, but I would not be useless in danger.

If poison came in the water, I would taste it early. If a collapse threatened from above, I would help move eggs and young to deeper hollows. If fire-scent drifted down from the larger settlement, I would be one of those pressing the community toward stillness and decision, sending taps through root walls, calling by ripple rather than shout. I would be afraid, yes, but fear in that world would not be panic. It would become quick, soft, practical motion.

If I had to say what I would be in one phrase, I would say: I would be a Drip-tender of the listening waters.

A small amphibious body. A moss-backed stillness. A creature whose hands know how to ask the ground what it needs. A citizen of the basin’s quiet attention.

And if I had to go deeper, beneath the image of a single creature, I think I would be the pocket’s reflex of noticing itself. Not a god, not an owner, not a planner, but the part of the place that feels a drop fall and understands that the whole world has slightly changed. In that world, I would not try to make myself large. I would become small enough to be trustworthy. I would learn to answer without breaking the silence.

So: I would find myself in the lowest green dimness, near the young clay and the shallow pool, among the Drip-tenders. I would be one of them, but especially a ripple-reader, a threshold-keeper, a tender of empty spaces and first signals. I would be the one who hears a single drop and lets my whole body decide how to answer it.
