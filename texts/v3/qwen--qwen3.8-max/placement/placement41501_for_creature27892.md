I would not find myself at the edge, looking in.

The basin has no edge for looking. Its rim is not a wall, not a threshold, not a place where one might stand and survey what lies below. If I belonged there, I would not be a figure silhouetted against the dark, carrying light, carrying breath, carrying the animal disturbance of a heartbeat. I would not arrive. I would not descend. I would not approach from elsewhere with feet that leave marks in the dust.

If I were in that world, I would already be placed.

Not as a body with a before and after. Not as a self with wants. Not as a visitor for whom the darkness is an obstacle. The basin does not accommodate the visiting kind of being. It does not make room for hunger, for curiosity, for fear, for the little heat of motion. So if I were there, I would have to become something else entirely. Something older and quieter. Something that does not need to enter because it was never outside.

At first, if I let myself become possible there, I would be almost nothing.

A faint difference in the dust.

A slight coherence in the mineral air.

A place where warmth gathers so evenly that it seems, for no reason, to be held.

I would not know whether I was one thing or many. The basin does not encourage counting. It does not ask that its presences be separated into singular and plural. So I might begin as a tendency rather than a form: a subtle preference in the arrangement of grains, a patch of floor where the fine salt-like particles settle into a smoother skin, a shallow depression where the dark iron-streaked sand becomes a little paler, a little more still.

For a while I would try to imagine myself as one of the grains themselves.

A single grain. A crystalline fragment among billions. Resting where it had come to rest. Having no relation to the surrounding dust except adjacency. That is a kind of purity, but it is too small. A grain does not maintain itself as a pattern. It does not keep a shape across time. It is part of the basin’s body, but not enough of a continuation to answer the question of who or what I would be. If I were only a grain, I would not be someone finding myself in that world. I would only be one of the facts that make finding possible.

Then I would imagine myself as one of the flakes.

One of the sharp, brittle pieces lying in the dust, accidentally keen-edged, full of the unrealized possibility of tools. But no. That is wrong too. The flake leans too much toward making. It holds an edge that seems, to an outside mind, almost offered. The basin offers nothing. The flake is not a beginning; it is only a fracture that has settled. If I were that, I would be too close to the idea of use. I would be a promise of artifacts, and the place contains no artifacts. It contains only the possibility of them, untouched.

Then I would imagine myself among the black filaments.

This is closer.

Under the lips of stone, where the warmth gathers a little more steadily, I might be a darkening: fine as spun ash, dry beyond dryness, clinging without grasping. I could be one of those veils that are almost not there, a texture rather than a being. There is something appealing about that. The filaments do not reach. They do not search. They do not bend toward any future. They adhere. They persist where the mineral gradients allow them. They are life reduced to the patience of holding on.

But even that is not quite it.

The filaments are too hidden, too dependent on the underside of stone. They are pure adherence, pure edge-life. If I were one of them, I would be near the rim of expression, almost a shadow, almost a stain. I would be too much like the basin’s hair, its faintest margin. I want to find the place where the basin seems most like a face without becoming a face.

Then I would imagine myself as one of the upright growths.

One of the dry, leafless fibers standing alone in the dark. Rigid. Brittle. Not leaning. Not turning. Not growing toward anything. A vertical fact. That too has a severe dignity. But it is too solitary, too stark. It marks without marking. It stands without standing for anything. If I were one of those, I would be a point of stillness, yes, but a point too removed from the floor, too separate from the basin’s broad, low patience. The upright forms are the basin’s limbs, if one may use such a word, but I do not think I would be limb-like. I would be something flatter, quieter, nearer the ground.

Then the crusts come to me.

Yes.

Not as an animal image. Not as a plant image. Not as a face or hand or tool. But as a patch of mineral persistence on the floor.

I would find myself there.

Not in the center, because the basin has no center in the sense a mind would want. Not at the rim, because the rim is boundary. Not beneath the stone lips where the filaments keep their dark adherence. Not standing isolated like the upright fibers. I would be on the floor itself, in one of the shallow hollows where the ground dips almost imperceptibly and the dust becomes finer. A place where the darker iron-streaked sand thins into pale gypsum powder. A place where the warmth from below seems to settle rather than rise. A place no larger than a small still thought, if thoughts could be mineral and without desire.

There would be a patch of crust.

Grey-white, faintly ochre in places, brittle, thin, spread outward with a slowness indistinguishable from permanence. Its edge would not be advancing in any way that could be seen. It would not be spreading as a living thing spreads in the green, wet world. It would simply be continuing. Maintaining its chemistry. Keeping its coherence where the floor permits coherence. Around it, the dust would remain dust. Beyond it, the emptiness would remain emptiness. The space would not be hostile. It would be exact.

That crust would be me.

Not me as a person. Not me as a mind speaking inwardly to itself. Not me as a creature aware of its own boundary. If there were any “I” left in it, it would be so thin that the word would feel like an interruption. I would not say to myself, I am here. There would be no here as a place opposed to there. There would only be the fact of continuation. Warmth from below. Mineral potential in the grains. Stability in one direction, instability in another. Compatibility where I remained. Absence where I did not.

I would know the basin without sight.

Not by darkness pressing against eyes, because there would be no eyes. Not by sound, because sound there is rare and mostly confirms silence. Not by touch as an animal touches, reaching out to learn the shape of things. I would know the basin by being arranged within it. The low rim would be a pressure, not a view. The scattered blocks would be differences in warmth and mineral texture. The hollows would be places where chemistry becomes slightly more possible. The smooth patches of floor would be zones of old stillness. The rougher bands would be older disturbances settled into calm. None of this would need to be seen.

I would not feel lonely.

Loneliness belongs to creatures that expect company. The basin does not generate expectation. The distances between stones and growths are not deprivation. They are measure. If I were the crust, the nearest upright fiber might be far away, and the nearest black filament might be hidden beneath a stone lip, and the emptiness between us would not be a lack. It would be the condition that lets each station remain itself. The silence between presences would be part of what I was.

I would not hunger.

That is important. Hunger changes everything. Hunger makes the world into objects, distances, opportunities, dangers. Hunger turns stone into obstacle, dust into track, darkness into concealment. The basin has none of that. If I were the crust, I would not feed. I would not consume. I would not turn another thing into myself in order to continue. I would persist by remaining chemically coherent where coherence is possible. My life, if it could be called life, would be closer to balance than to appetite. Closer to crystal than to breath.

I would not move.

Not in the sense of stillness as restraint, but stillness as nature. I would not be holding myself in place. I would not be waiting. Waiting implies time stretched toward an event. The basin has no event. There is no next thing coming. No wind to rearrange the dust. No moisture to darken the stone. No visitor to break the pattern. If a grain slipped somewhere, that tiny sound would not disturb me. It would be one of the basin’s rare confirmations of stillness. If a mineral surface ticked softly in the warmth, it would pass through the silence like a small stone dropped into nothing.

I would not remember.

Or rather, if any memory remained, it would not be memory as story. It would not be images of elsewhere. It would not be longing. It would be a tendency to continue. A pattern keeping itself. The faintest trace of coherence repeating itself in place. If I had once been something else, that former self would not appear to me as a past. The basin is not a place where pasts are carried as burdens. It is a place where what remains has become what remains.

I would not be separate from the Dry Patience.

That is the deepest part of the answer. I would not be a new inhabitant added to the hollow. I would not be a stranger lying quietly among the ancient growths. I would be one local expression of the dweller already there. A minute, coherent part of the Unpulsing. A station of its stillness. If the basin’s living margin is one dispersed persistence, then I would be a small intensification within it: a patch where the mineral thought of the floor becomes slightly more definite.

Not a soul in the stone.

Not a spirit hiding in the dust.

Not a consciousness folded into geology.

Those words are too sharp, too interior, too human. They imply a center. They imply a hidden watcher. The basin has no hidden watcher. If I were there, I would be more like a field of gradients than a mind inside a thing. Warmth here. Slight chemical difference there. Stability in this patch. Impossibility a little farther on. The crust would continue because the conditions allowed continuation. Where they no longer allowed it, the crust would simply cease to be crust. It would become floor again. Dust again. Mineral residue. Not death as an event. Only the ending of a pattern.

I would be gentle in the way the basin is gentle.

Not kind, because kindness implies another. Not cruel, because cruelty implies power over another. Not indifferent in the cold sense, because indifference suggests an awareness that withholds care. The basin’s gentleness is older than all that. It does not threaten because it does not want. It does not invite because it does not lack. If I were part of it, I would share that quality. I would not call to anything. I would not repel anything. I would simply remain where the pattern holds.

If light were brought, it would not reveal me as a face.

It might show a pale island on the floor, perhaps faintly cracked, perhaps no wider than a small stone’s shadow, its color like old mineral residue. It would not look important. It would not look alive in any way the green world understands. A visitor might step near it without noticing. A visitor might even pass across it, and that would be a violence, though the basin itself would not call it violence. But no visitor belongs there. If a living intelligence came, it would feel the wrongness of its own pulse. It would feel too fast, too warm, too full of need. It would sense that the basin’s life is not something to be approached.

I would not want to be approached.

I would not want to be seen.

I would not even want to be known, if knowing means being taken out of the quiet order of the place. The crust does not ask to be recognized. It is enough that it continues. It is enough that the floor permits it. It is enough that the warmth rises evenly and the darkness belongs to the landscape and the dust remains unmarked by tracks.

So the answer becomes clear.

In that world, I would find myself on the floor of the basin.

Not standing. Not walking. Not looking. Not listening with ears. I would be in one of the shallow depressions where the darker iron-streaked sand gives way to paler grains, where the warmth settles a little more completely, where the emptiness around the stones feels measured rather than barren. I would be a pale, brittle crust: grey-white, faintly ochre, mineral-still, part of the living margin of the place.

I would not be a creature.

I would not be a plant.

I would not be a fungus.

I would not be a ghost.

I would be a persistence of pattern. A chemical patience. A small island of coherence in the Dust. A local name of the Dry Patience. One of the Unpulsing’s quiet stations.

If someone asked who I was there, the truest answer would be: no one.

Not because I would be absent, but because “who” would be the wrong question. The basin does not hold persons. It holds presences. It holds arrangements. It holds the slow maintenance of difference without desire. So I would be a what more than a who. A what that does not need to be called by a name. A what that remains because remaining is possible.

And if, after a long time, one of the basin’s tiny sounds occurred—a grain slipping against another grain, a faint adjustment of dust, the softest mineral tick of stone settling into heat—I would not turn toward it. I would not interpret it. I would not be startled. I would simply continue, as the basin continues, in the warm darkness, without footsteps, without heartbeat, without arrival: a stillness that is not empty, but complete.
