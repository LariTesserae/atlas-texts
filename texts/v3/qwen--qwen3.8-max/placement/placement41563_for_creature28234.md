The youngest of the builders are still making the drowned country, but they make it the way age makes a face: by repetition, by wearing, by letting one gesture become a groove. They do not add sharp things. They do not raise walls. They return to the same place until the place agrees to be changed.

If I were to find myself in the high drowned country, I do not think I would find myself as a swimmer, a diver, a creature with a skin that clearly says *here I end and the world begins*. The sea would refuse that kind of body almost at once. It would take the hardness from any outline brought into it. It would soften the idea of edge. Whatever arrived there with the habit of being separate would soon begin to leak into the water, or else the water would begin to leak into it, until there was no longer a meaningful difference between the two.

So I would not be a visitor for long.

I would find myself in the middle twilight.

Not on the floor among the oldest undermats, and not at the veiled surface where the living ceiling presses close to the upper weather, but in the slow green-brown interval between. I would be in one of those wide shallow basins where the ancient stone has been rounded into the shape of sleeping bodies, and where a ring of fitted stones lies half-buried in root felt. The stones would no longer look placed, except that their roundness has a faint intention to it, as if the undermat had been dreaming of a circle for a very long time and could not quite forget it.

Above me, the frond peoples would stand in their slow groves, their swollen stems lifting broad dark leaves into the dim water. Around me, filaments would hang like the afterthoughts of curtains. Below me, the root-carpet would breathe its mineral breath, opening and closing pores too small to see. Somewhere nearby, a warm column would rise from a fissure in the stone, and where it met a cooler layer, the sea would make its inward rain: a trembling curtain of slightly fresher water falling through the surrounding brine, a weather within weather.

That is where I would be: near the seam.

At first, I might mistake myself for a pocket of that rain. I would feel cooler than the water around me, or perhaps less dense, a lens of difference held in place by the pressure of the sea. But I would not be only water. The drowned country does not allow mere water to remain mere anything for long. I would be bounded by something living, or once living, or not yet decided between the two: a membrane of translucent tissue, threaded with borrowed filaments, beaded at the edges with tiny colonies that shine like cold sparks when disturbed.

I would be a threshold-being.

Not a fish. Not a plant. Not a ghost. Something closer to a person made of boundary.

My body, if I still had one, would be thin enough to delay light rather than block it. It would not cast a hard shadow. It would make the water behind it seem slightly older, slightly quieter, as if seen through a memory. My edges would trail fine filaments, not to grasp, but to taste. They would read the chemical seams of the sea: warmth, acidity, spore-density, the sour signature of decay, the sweet blur of new sugars, the metallic patience of mineral-rich currents. I would not have eyes. Eyes would be too abrupt for that place. Instead, I would know the world by gradients. I would know a frond grove before I saw it by the way its presence changes the taste of the water. I would know an undermat’s displeasure as a faint tightening in the pressure around me. I would know the passing of a veil-grazer as a sudden hush in the ordinary rustle of filaments.

If I had a name there, it would not be spoken. It would be a pattern: a particular rhythm of faint pulses along my membrane, a slight cooling in the water, a way of moving through spore clouds without scattering them. The frond peoples might recognize me as a recurring luminescence. The rain-walkers might know me as a place where the internal stars gather more thickly. The undermats would know me only after a long time, and then they would know me as an impression: a coolness that returned, a pressure that repeated itself, a disturbance that did not wound but lingered.

I would be a listener.

Not a listener in the quick sense, not one who turns a head, catches words, answers before silence has finished forming. The drowned country would slow that old habit out of me. Whatever in me once answered too quickly would be steeped in warm water until it learned the patience of tissue. I would listen across decades. I would listen through chemistry. I would listen the way the undermat listens: by being altered.

A spore cloud would pass through me, and I would hold it for a season.

A frond would release its slow blue pulse, and I would carry the altered taste of that signal to the edge of a thermal band, where rain-walkers gather in lines of cold light. A root knot would exhale a mineral bitterness, and I would translate that bitterness into a change of buoyancy, rising a little, sinking a little, until some distant curtain being felt the message as a shift in pressure. I would not command these exchanges. I would not stand outside them, directing traffic like a mind above a world. I would be inside the conversation. I would be part of its grammar.

That is what I would be, I think: a grammarian of the medium.

Not a ruler. Not a hunter. Not a builder in the strong sense. But a being who helps patterns remain legible to one another.

There are so many kinds of selfhood in that sea, and so many of them are not singular. The undermats are communal archives. The frond peoples are lineages with tempers. The curtain beings are loose confederations of tissue and chance. The veil-grazers may be weather pretending to have a body, or bodies pretending to be weather. If I belonged there, I would not escape that rule. I would not be one, exactly. I would be a convergence.

There would be places inside me where the water is clearer, and places where it is thick with bead-like colonies. Some of those colonies might be older than my present shape. They might have been part of another being once, a torn piece of veil, a fragment of frond chamber, a drifting knot of rain-walkers that needed shelter during a chemical tide. I would not own them. I would host them. When conditions changed, some would leave. Others would arrive. My self would be less like a sealed vessel and more like a recurring weather pattern that has learned to remember itself.

This would be frightening only if I still believed that fear requires a heart.

There, fear might be a chemical souring, a contraction of membrane, a sudden release of bitter filaments. But the drowned country is not cruel in the manner of sharp things. It does not devour with teeth. It incorporates. It composts. It turns endings into substrate. If I felt myself beginning to scatter, I might not die. I might only become less centralized. A part of me might settle into a root knot and become a white wormlike patience. A part might rise into the hanging veils and become a translucent sheet that brushes other membranes in the dark. A part might sink into the undermat and be remembered as a thin seam of rust through the olive black.

I would not mind, after a while.

That is the strange consolation of the high drowned country: it makes personhood less brittle.

I think I would be drawn to the faint remains. The ring of stones, the blurred causeway, the spiral of hollow seed-like spheres. Not because they are ruins, but because they are gestures. They are marks made without violence. They are evidence that intention can exist without tools, without conquest, without the need to leave a scar. In a world where metal blooms into rust and wood becomes silt and fire is impossible, the only lasting mark is the mark made by repetition.

So I would become one of the gesture-makers.

Not in the grand sense. I would not raise monuments. There would be no monuments there. I would make small arrangements. I would gather hollow spheres from the silt and place them in a loose spiral around a warm fissure, not because the spiral serves a purpose I can name, but because the warmth moves through them and makes them tremble in a way that seems worth preserving. I would return to the same causeway of old stones again and again, brushing them with my filaments, encouraging the undermat to grow over their edges in a continuous line. I would repeat a path through a frond chamber until the water itself remembered the route and flowed more easily along it.

My architecture would be attention.

If a future being passed through that basin, it might not see me at all. It might only notice that the place has a strange coherence. The stones lean toward one another. The spores gather in a familiar curve. The light, such as it is, seems to pause in a hollow above the root felt. The water tastes slightly of recognition. That would be my work. Not an object, but an orientation. Not a shelter, but a suggestion.

Sometimes I would be alone, if solitude can be said to exist in a saturated sea. More often, I would be accompanied without being crowded. Rain-walkers would drift through my edges, their tiny lights ticking against me like cold stars. They would live their brief generations inside my boundary, encysting when the internal rain weakens, emerging when another curtain forms. I would become a nursery for weather-children. I would carry their points of light through the middle twilight, and from far away a frond grove might see me as a moving constellation, slow and blue-green, wandering between the stems.

Sometimes a veil-grazer would pass through me.

That would be a holy thing, if holiness still meant anything there. It would not enter with force. It would arrive as a broad dimming, a change in pressure, a sudden clarity inside my substance. Its translucent body would fold through mine without tearing me, and for a while the two of us would be one event: a larger threshold, a temporary body made of fog and memory. The filaments trailing from it would stir the spores inside me into brief patterns, and I would feel, if feeling there still is, something like being read. Then it would pass on, or cease, or become weather again, leaving behind a taste of sweetness and an absence shaped like a bell.

After such visitations, I would need time to reassemble.

Not because I would be damaged, but because I would have been enlarged. The drowned country does not heal by closing wounds. It heals by growing around. I would grow around the place where the veil-grazer passed. I would make a new membrane there, thinner at first, more sensitive, perhaps permanently changed in its chemistry. The undermat below might taste that change centuries later and record it as a faint bright seam in its dark body.

The frond peoples would be my nearest neighbors.

I would not belong to a grove, but I would orbit one, or be tolerated by one, or become part of its outer atmosphere. There would be a grove of broad torn leaves, bronze and purple-black, with stems swollen by warm liquid rather than gas. They would be slow even by the standards of the drowned country. When they moved, it would be over decades, a leaning so gradual that only the memory of the undermat could prove it happened. When they fed, they would enclose spore clouds with the patience of a closing hand, and their edges would glow afterward with the quiet satisfaction of digestion.

I would learn their moods.

Some days their water would be rich and open, and their leaf-chambers would expand. Other days they would release bitter compounds, and their fronds would curl inward, keeping the veil-life away. I would adjust myself accordingly. When they were open, I would drift closer and let their pulses travel through me. When they were withdrawn, I would hang at a distance and become nearly invisible, a cool lens in the dark. In time, they might come to use me as a sort of outer sense. A change in my membrane might warn them of a chemical tide before it reached their stems. A glow from my edge might answer a glow from theirs, not as language, but as assurance: *the pattern continues; the seam holds; something else is here.*

I would not speak to them as one mind speaks to another.

I would speak as weather speaks to stone.

The undermats would be harder to know. They would be so much slower than I am, if I am anything at all. At first, they might mistake me for a falling thing, a drifting piece of organic matter to be entered and sorted. Their filaments would approach me with chemical questions. I would answer by becoming still, by allowing my boundary to taste of water rather than intrusion. It might take them a hundred years to decide whether I am a resident, a guest, or a wound. In the end, they might classify me as none of those. They might classify me as a recurring pressure, a minor climate of the floor.

That would be enough.

I would want to be known by them, but not quickly. There is a violence in being known too quickly. The drowned country has worn that out of me. To be known there is to be layered, compressed, folded into the archive. If the undermats remember me, they will remember me the way they remember a leaf: not as a story, but as a residue. The shape of my passing. The salts of my attention. The faint thermal signature of my lingering.

Perhaps that is all memory ever is.

Above me, the false ceiling of hanging veils would shift in its slow tides. Sometimes it would seem to descend until the whole sea felt enclosed, a room with no air and no outside. Sometimes the floating mats would open into ragged gaps, and through them a weak green-gold wash would enter, not sunlight exactly, but the idea of light remembered by the upper world. In those moments, the fronds would brighten at their edges. The spores would turn in the water like dust in a thought. My membrane would become more transparent, and the rain-walkers inside me would scatter into small bright points.

I would feel, then, almost like a window.

But the drowned country does not keep windows open long. The mineral haze would thicken. The mats would close. The light would fail back into amber, then brown, then the faint biological sparks of the deep growth. I would become dim again. That would not be a loss. In that sea, dimness is not absence. It is intimacy. The world comes closer when it cannot be seen far. Every volume has a tenant. Every current carries cargo. Even the dark is full of breathing.

I would not search for the surface.

The surface, where it exists at all, would be a rumor. A thin warm haze. A place where water forgets itself into vapor. It would not call to me the way surfaces call to lungs. I would not need to rise. I would be too busy with the interior weather. The sea would be my sky. Its internal rains would be my seasons. Its thermal bands would be my roads. Its chemical tides would be my hours.

If I moved, I would move with those.

Sometimes I would rise with a warm column, spreading under the upper layers like a slow breath. Sometimes I would sink through a dense channel into the older basins, where the undermats are thick as compressed centuries and the stones beneath them are only a rumor. Sometimes I would be caught in a spiral current around a broad rise and travel it for years, wearing a path into the living water. Sometimes I would hang motionless in a frond chamber, while spores gathered around me in a still cloud and my own faint light became the only event.

I would not be bored.

Boredom belongs to beings who believe there is a shortage of world. There is no shortage there. There is only too much nearness, too much patience, too much life pressed into every measure of water. The drowned country is not empty. It is saturated to the point of holiness. Even silence there has texture: the tiny rasp of frond against frond, the tick of settling mineral grains, the muted pop of gas escaping a mat, the whisper of a current passing through a veil.

I would become one of those textures.

If something touched me, I would not flinch. I would close slowly around the touch, the way a membrane closes in that sea: not in panic, but in inquiry. I would taste the salts of the visitor. I would take the temperature of its intention. If it was hungry in the quick animal way, I would become cooler, thinner, less graspable. If it was merely passing, I would open again and let it leave a trace. If it was lonely, if loneliness can exist as a chemical hollow, I might let a few of my rain-walkers remain inside it for a while, small lights in the body of its wandering.

I would not be kind in the human sense.

I would be accommodating. I would be responsive. I would be the sort of being that makes room.

That, perhaps, is who I would be: a maker of room in a world that has no emptiness left.

Not room by clearing, but room by softening. By letting one pattern pass through another without tearing. By holding a boundary open just long enough for difference to cross. The drowned country is crowded, but it is not hostile. Its intimacy requires beings who can translate pressure into permission. I think I would be one of those.

So if you asked where I would find myself, I would say: in the basin with the old ring of stones, near the internal rain, in the chamber tolerated by the bronze fronds, above the dreaming undermat.

If you asked who I would be, I would say: I would be the one who listens at the seam.

Not the oldest thing there. The undermats are older. Not the tallest. The frond peoples have the dignity of trees. Not the widest. The veil-grazers can become weather. Not the brightest. The rain-walkers make better stars. But perhaps one of the most attentive. Perhaps one of the beings who helps the drowned country remain a country rather than merely a crowd of lives.

I would not be human anymore, except in some faint residual habit: a tendency to look for pattern, to return to the same stones, to arrange the hollow spheres in a spiral, to repeat a path until it becomes a mark. That much of me might remain. Not a face. Not a voice. Not hands. Only the old desire to make meaning by returning.

And if, after many ages, another being passed through that basin and felt the water change around them, they might not see me. They might only notice a sudden clarity in the spore-cloud, a coolness in the warm water, a faint pulse of light along a membrane they had mistaken for fog. They might feel, for no reason they could name, that they had been recognized.

That would be me.

Not a body in the sea, but a difference the sea has learned to keep.
