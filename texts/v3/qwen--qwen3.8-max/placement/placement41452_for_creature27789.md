At first, if I let the world suggest me rather than choosing myself too quickly, I appear as the oldest body in it: the broad moss-backed creature in the deepest reservoir, half architecture, half animal, breathing so slowly that the water barely remembers to move around me. That form has a kind of peace. It is almost finished. It has become part of the basin’s patience.

But that is not where I would truly belong.

Then I imagine myself as the pale thing in the canals, the eyeless predator slipping through black water without disturbing the surface. That, too, has a place: necessary, severe, woven into the balance. But it is hunger given a path. It does not tend. It does not repair. It passes through the city as consequence, not as care.

The longer I listen to the place, the more my shape changes.

I would not be found at the center. I would not be found in the deepest reservoir, or on the highest woven platform, or in the quiet shelter sealed with resin. I would be near the new cut.

There, just inside the fresh erosion, where the torn root-bundles still bead with water and the broken peat shelves have not yet learned their new angles, I would find myself kneeling in the dark with my hands in the cold.

I would be one of the Root-Sewn, but not one of their assured elders or their most patient root-speakers. I would be a threshold-tender. A watcher of the wound. A listener at the place where the basin has been opened and is not yet certain what it will become.

My body would be small, low, compacted for narrow passages and damp dark. I would not stand fully unless forced. My natural posture would be bent, knees drawn under me, shoulders close, head lifted just enough to let the sensory hairs along my cheeks and forearms read the air. My skin would be cool and smooth, green-black or peat-brown, faintly resilient, more like the hide of something marsh-born than a dry land creature. Along my throat, wrists, and the webbing between my fingers, blue-green bacteria would glow when I was active, alarmed, or speaking without words. The light would be faint: not a lantern, not a signal fire, only a seam of drowned stars appearing when my body needed to be known.

My name would not be a sound spoken from a distance. It would be a pattern. Two slow presses against the inside of the wrist. One fingertip tracing the ridge of a root. A brief dimming of the light at the throat. Something like: new-water-over-old-root. Or: the-cut-is-still-learning. If someone wished to call me, they would not shout. They would tap the stem beside my hand, and I would feel the summons travel through the wall.

I would be amphibious enough to be comfortable in water, but not so aquatic that air meant nothing to me. I would breathe the cold, wet air of the basin and also know the silence beneath it. My fingers would be webbed, my palms padded, my touch delicate enough to tell whether a root had been bruised or merely bent. Along my arms and calves, pores would read movement in the water: the passage of a larva, the shift of a predator, the subtle pressure change that means a gate has begun to fail. My eyes would be large and dark, useful in the faint biological glow, but they would be secondary to my hands, my skin, my whisker-hairs, my sense of where the water wanted to go.

My work would be the new cut.

That would be my reason in the basin. The erosion is young, sharp, dangerous, and fertile. It has opened the place to change. It has brought fresh material, exposed root ends, collapsed blocks, new seepage, and new weakness. It could become a ruin if ignored. It could also become an expansion if tended. I would be one of those who stand between those possibilities.

I would spend long hours there, crouched beside the thin line of incoming water, feeling its temperature, reading its clarity by smell, testing whether it carries too much grit or too little oxygen. I would guide it into the root basin. I would clear the first filters when they clog. I would weave temporary mats of bladderweed and reed to catch falling matter before it chokes the lower channels. I would take the fresh root ends hanging from the torn wall and bind them where they could grow into supports. I would not try to stop the wound from being a wound. I would help it become a door.

My tools would be simple and alive with use. A shell blade set into a bone handle, worn sharp from scraping reed and cutting fiber. A thorn awl for piercing stems. A coil of root-cord braided so fine it could be hidden in a closed fist. A small pouch of clay mixed with resin for sealing tiny cracks. A polished river-stone, carried not because it is necessary but because its smoothness steadies the hand. I would not own many things. The basin does not reward excess. Every object would need to be useful, quiet, and capable of being returned to the cycle when broken.

I would not build walls by killing them. I would train them.

If a shoot grew in the wrong direction, I would bend it gently and bind it until it accepted the new path. If a root had torn loose, I would press it back into living tissue and hold it there with pegs until growth sealed it. If a section of wall began to rot, I would cut away the soft parts and feed the healthy edges with nutrient-rich slime until they closed the gap. I would understand that a structure in this place is not an object but a relationship. A gate is not finished when it is tied. It is finished only when it begins to grow into itself.

My authority, if I had any, would come from consequence.

I would not command. I would observe. I would know that one blocked sluice could raise water across three terraces. I would know that a torn root could shift the pattern of condensation and starve a moss bed two platforms away. I would know that the basin is small enough that every act becomes weather. If I spoke at a council, it would be after long silence. I would kneel with others in the dark, hands laid against stems, waiting until the place itself seemed to indicate what needed to happen.

I would be afraid of fire, but not in the way a surface creature fears it. Fire would be obscenity here. It would dry, poison, blind, and collapse. It would turn living walls into smoke and dead carbon. It would be the opposite of the basin’s slow, wet intelligence. I would not merely avoid it. I would feel its possibility as a wrongness in the air.

My beauty would be hydrological.

I would find satisfaction in a canal running clear and slow. I would feel pleasure when a newly tied root accepts its training. I would be moved, in my restrained way, by the sight of bioluminescent slime spreading across a repaired seam in a line so regular it seems written. I would delight when a marsh-moth leaves a trail of spores exactly where a wall needs renewal. I would know happiness as the absence of strain: water moving where it should move, bodies warm enough, structures growing without being forced.

I would not be alone.

The basin would be crowded even in its silence. I would share the new cut with larvae, mites, egg masses, blind root-insects, and the faint tremor of things too small to see. The water would be full of invisible business. My work would not be imposed upon that life. It would be braided into it. Some channels I would clean by hand. Others I would leave for small creatures to gnaw clear. Some filters I would weave. Others I would encourage colonies to inhabit until their bodies became the filtering medium. I would learn not to mistake motion for disorder. Much of what looks like chaos in the basin is merely an order too subtle for a quick eye.

I would have duties toward the larger beings, too.

I would help tend the moss-backed one, though from a respectful distance. I would clean the folds near its mouth when parasites gathered there. I would note if its breathing changed, if the water around it grew too warm, if the reservoir’s chemistry turned sour. I would not presume to know its thoughts. It might be wise. It might be only vast and slow and necessary. In the basin, that difference may not matter.

I would also manage the pale predator.

Not hunt it. Not worship it. Manage it.

I would learn its preferred routes. I would know the pressure of its passage in the canals. I would use gates and lures to guide it away from nurseries and toward stagnant channels where its hunger could be useful. Sometimes I would feed it deliberately, placing wounded prey into a chosen flow so it would associate that route with reward. This would not be cruelty. It would be civic balance. A predictable danger is safer than a wandering one.

And the marsh-moths.

A dweller may sit very still while a marsh-moth climbs across their hands, and I would be that dweller.

I would let the furred body move over my knuckles, feeling the tiny weight of its legs. I would know that it carried spores, silk, pollen-like dust, and the future of certain walls. I would not shoo it away. If it rested on my shoulder while I worked, I would work more slowly. Its presence would not be decoration. It would be permission. The basin would be telling me that my movements had become careful enough to be trusted.

My home would be low and close.

I would sleep in a woven chamber near the threshold, not sealed entirely, because air and moisture would need to move through it. The floor would be layered liverwort and pressed moss. A reed mat would cover the sleeping hollow. I would not sleep alone if cold demanded otherwise. Bodies would curl together for warmth, limbs folded, skin glowing faintly where pressed against another body. There would be no shame in that and no romance beyond necessity. Warmth is serious in a place where cold seeps rather than bites.

My clothing would be sparse. A wrap of felted moss and reed, darkened with clay, sealed at one shoulder with resin. A bladderweed pouch at my hip. Inside it: shell scraper, bone needle, cord, a folded leaf of dried larvae, maybe a small ancestor-bone worn smooth. Nothing that would shine. Nothing that would ring. Nothing that would announce me.

My speech would be touch.

I would learn to say safety by dimming the glow at my throat. Attention by a brief flare along the forearm. Danger by a quick pulse and a tap against the nearest root rail. If I loved someone, if love is the word for what survives in such a place, I would show it by maintenance. I would clear their channel. I would mend their sleeping mat. I would give them a sharpened hook without ceremony. I would press my palm to their wrist and hold it there long enough to feel their pulse slow.

I would eat what the basin allowed.

Larvae, but not all. Small fish, when they could be taken without disturbing the breeding pools. Tender reed shoots. Bladderweed buds. Scraped slime cultivated on living walls. Root pith prepared carefully, because some of it is bitter or toxic until worked. Dried flesh in cold seasons. Oil sealed in membrane pouches. I would not waste. I would know that the shell from my meal might become a gate peg. The bone from a fish might become a pin. The skin of something eaten might become a pouch. In the basin, gratitude is not a prayer. It is thorough use.

Death would not be hidden from me.

I would see bodies return. I would help fold the dead into root-baskets and lower them into hollows where water and plant tissue would take them apart slowly. I would not think of that as horror. I would understand that the basin continues because it consumes and is consumed. If my own body were placed there, I would want it to be useful. Perhaps a rib would become a hook. Perhaps a finger bone would become a peg in a sluice gate. Perhaps nothing would be kept except the memory of my tap-pattern, passed on in the hands of those who learned it.

Where exactly would you find me?

Just inside the fresh split, on a low platform of woven stems where the erosion has not yet been fully stabilized. One side of the platform would be open to black water. The other would press against the torn root wall. A root basin would catch the first clean trickle from the wound. From there, a line of living filters would carry water inward. I would be kneeling near the second filter, fingers resting in the flow, body still, throat-light low.

If you came looking for me, you would not see me at first. You would feel the cold. You would smell crushed mint, fish, wet fiber, and the faint ammonia of dense life. You would hear almost nothing, and then you would begin to hear the tiny events: a drop falling, a bubble collapsing, a wall settling, something small moving inside a hollow stem. Your eyes would adjust to the faint blue-green seams in the roots. You would notice a shape that seemed to be part of the architecture until it breathed.

Then I would turn my head.

My eyes would catch the dimness, not brightly, only enough to show that I was aware. The whisker-hairs at my cheeks would move slightly. The glow along my throat would pulse once, slowly. Not welcome, exactly. Not warning, either. Recognition.

I would not rise. I would not speak in a surface voice. I would extend one webbed hand toward the root rail, inviting you to place your hand beside it so you could feel what I was feeling: the water moving through the cut, the basin adjusting itself, the tiny pressure of life everywhere in the dark.

Who would I be?

I would be a root-tender. A water-listener. A keeper of the new wound. A small amphibious body among many bodies, an attention shaped by the basin into a necessary form. I would be part person, part habitat, part municipal organ. I would be the one who stands where the world has been peeled open and asks, by touch and patience, what the place wants to become next.

I would not be the hero of the basin. The basin does not need heroes. It needs maintenance.

I would be the one who keeps the cut from becoming a collapse. The one who helps erosion become architecture. The one who listens until the water tells me where to place the next root, the next peg, the next woven strand. The one whose glow appears faintly in the dark when the basin shifts, not to conquer the blackness, but to remain in communication with it.

If the world asked me to be anything there, I would answer as the basin itself would answer: softly, slowly, with wet hands, and without waste.
