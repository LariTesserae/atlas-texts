I would find myself first as a kind of attention rather than as a body.

Not on a road, because the Low Ward has no roads in the dryland sense; not at a gate, because gates there are not entrances but instruments; not even in a room, because rooms are temporary victories over condensation. I would be somewhere between the third sluice and the shadow of the cistern tower, in one of those narrow galleries where pipes cross overhead like the ribs of a sleeping animal and the walls are always faintly warm with engine breath. I would be in the place where sound changes.

If the Ward allowed me a shape, it would not give me a heroic one. It would make me useful in a small, exact way. I think I would be a gauge-listener.

Not the loudest person in the basin, not a master of any gate, not a tender of the great pumps, but one of those who sit for long hours with an ear against iron, learning the difference between health and coming failure. My station would be a recess in the pipe gallery below the transformer sheds, where the air is green and still and every surface carries a thin skin of moisture. There would be a brass-rimmed seat worn smooth by previous listeners, a rack of listening rods, a lamp in a wire cage, and a little waxed book in which I kept the basin’s sounds.

My body would have been changed by the Ward. My skin would carry that steeped, green-bronze cast, like old copper left in rain but not ruined by it. My hair would lie flat and dark, always seeming freshly damp even when I had slept in a heated loft. My eyes would be large and reflective, adjusted to the half-light, blinking slowly when a lamp flared or a wet surface caught the glow. Between my fingers would be that faint fullness, not quite webbing, but enough to make me feel less like a land creature and more like something that has made terms with water. I would breathe the thick air without panic. I would have learned to hold my breath for long stretches, to move through flooded passages with my mouth shut and my eyes open, reading the dark by touch and pressure.

I would dress as the Wardens dress: dark waxed canvas, glazed leather shining at the cuffs, slit-soled boots that shed water and make little sound on basalt. My belt would carry the small dignities of the place: a valve key, an oil horn, a listening rod, a folded cloth kept as dry as possible, a glass bead or a ceramic charm for brightness. Not from superstition, exactly. Bright hard things resist the damp. They remind the eye that not everything must be softened.

My work would be simple in description and endless in practice.

I would listen.

In the morning, if morning can be said to exist under that vapor ceiling, I would wake in a loft above the condensation line. The floor would be warm from engine flues beneath the tiles. The absorbent mats on the walls would be checked, removed, set to dry. I would press my ear to the listening place before rising, because the basin speaks in its sleep. A healthy ward has a sound like a slow body digesting water: low hums, distant ticks, the soft movement of channels, the occasional drip falling with perfect deliberation. An unhealthy ward has gaps. A hollow knock. A note too bright. A silence where a pump should be breathing.

If the sound was wrong, I would not hurry. Hurrying is a sign of failure already arrived. Instead I would become precise. I would put on my boots, take my rod, and follow the sound down through the galleries.

My station would be near a place where three pipes meet: one carrying warm water from the engine plinths, one drawing cooler condensate down toward the black streets, and one old iron line whose purpose is half-forgotten but which still matters because it trembles when the cistern fills. The valve-spiders would have webs there, copper-bright threads stretched between handrails and brackets. I would read them before I touched anything. Their webs thicken before leaks as clouds thicken before rain. If the threads were beaded unusually close to a joint, I would mark it. If a lamp-moth rested nearby with its oiled wings folded, I would note whether it seemed calm or restless. Even the moths know something about heat and trouble.

Then I would put the listening rod to the pipe and close my eyes.

The Ward’s language would enter me then. I would not think in words at first, but in textures and pressures. A healthy hum is round. It has a center. A beginning cavitation is not a sound so much as a missing smoothness, a graininess inside the flow, like sand moving behind a wall. A weeping gate has a high thin patience to it. A root rubbing timber is slow, almost affectionate, dangerous only if ignored for seasons. A pump about to seize gives a warning not as a cry but as a change in confidence. The sound becomes uncertain. It asks a question.

My poetry would be lists.

Names of damp:
sweat, bead, gloss, film, thread, weep, breath, bloom, crust, stain, pulse, fog, drip, skin, salt, scale, sheen.

Names of water moving through materials:
brick murmurs, basalt accepts, iron complains, timber remembers, tile sheds, copper tastes, ceramic waits.

Names of almost-failure:
the green knock, the iron hiccup, the hollow bead, the dry whisper, the too-sweet hiss, the frog-silence.

That last would matter most. The condenser frogs sitting on warm valves are not decoration. They are instruments of first refusal. Their translucent throats pulse with the engines. If an engine falters, the frogs stop first. A sudden absence of that soft pulsing would be more urgent than any alarm. So part of my listening would be for the absence of small life. I would learn to hear what the basin stops doing.

If I found trouble, I would not shout. I would signal. A lamp blinked three times across the water. A finger trailed through condensation on a pipe, leaving a mark another Warden would understand. A knock on iron: two low, one high. A ripple sent across a standing pool so that it touched the edge of a flooded lane and told someone there that pressure was shifting in the south gallery. The Ward would answer in the same quiet way. A sluice-tender would lay a hand on a gate and wait. A root-cutter would climb to a hanging root and decide whether one strand could be spared. An oiler would appear beside me with a horn of dark oil and a calm face, because no one in the Low Ward panics at the first smell of metal warming.

I would be, in other words, one of the basin’s sense organs.

But if I let the image become more itself, as you ask, I do not think I would remain only a person. The Low Ward is not a place that permits strict borders between creature and system. The more I imagine being there, the more I suspect the visible Warden version of me would be only the surface of what I had become. Underneath, or before, or around that shape, I would be a living film.

I would begin as a green-black skin on the lower courses of glazed brick, somewhere the water touches first and longest. A film of algae and iron-loving bacteria, eating rust, exhaling lacquer, keeping metal from wasting too quickly. I would be minute and collective. I would know the taste of every bead of condensation that fell into me. I would receive the animalcules flung from dropping water and let them become part of my pattern. I would coat chains, setts, pipe brackets, the lower lips of sluice-gates. I would be the Ward’s first memory. Wherever water touched, I would record.

That seems truer than a body with boots.

Because the Low Ward is not governed by masters; it is maintained by sensitivity. It survives by being touched constantly by small lives that understand its moods. The valve-spiders know leaks before the Wardens do. The eels know sludge by taste. The brick oysters know steam-flecked water by what they filter from it. The mosses know which lamps burn longest. The roots know when a gallery is too hot and release their stored moisture like a slow exhale. If I belonged there, I would want to be part of that distributed knowing. I would want to be less a person watching a machine and more a thin intelligence spread across wet surfaces, alert to pressure, salt, heat, rust, and silence.

Yet perhaps the Ward would need me to have hands after all.

So it would make me both.

By day I would be the gauge-listener, the one the others look for when a sound turns strange. I would move through the basin without splashing. I would pole a narrow boat through black streets where water lies finger-deep over worn basalt, passing under raised walkways and between engine halls on their glazed plinths. I would greet the sluice-crabs without disturbing the ceramic shards they carry. I would watch the broad leaves in the open courts collect the heavy air. I would know which tiles were safe to step on and which had grown too soft with wetness. I would carry dry cloths like offerings because dryness there is not ordinary; it is a craft.

By night I would become quieter. The lamps would come on in their wire cages, amber and copper and mineral green, making halos in the mist. The water would mirror them until the basin seemed to have two skies, one above and one below, both broken by pipes and gantries. I would sit at my listening place and let the dark fill with detail. The Ward is never empty at night. It is full of suspended droplets, faint shining films, the dull gleam of wet machinery, the soft movement of eels in channels, the almost inaudible settling of old materials that have long ago stopped fighting water.

In those hours I would feel myself thinning into the place.

My thoughts would become hydraulic. I would not ask, “What do I want?” I would ask, “Where is pressure gathering?” I would not think of memory as a room but as a water table: always near the surface, rising when touched. I would not imagine the future as progress but as maintenance. The future would mean that the gates still move, the lamps still burn, the roots still hang, the pumps still breathe, the frogs still pulse, the brick oysters still glaze themselves in dark metallic sheen. The future would mean the agreement continues.

I would fear drought.

That would be my true terror. Not flood, not darkness, not the slow weeping of iron, but dryness. Dryness would mean cracked seals. Warped timber. Silent engines. Channels turned to brittle stains. The roots drawing back into themselves. The mosses going pale. The Ward losing its patience. If the air thinned and the water withdrew, the whole negotiated world would become a ruin of beautiful, useless machinery. I would dream of dry spells the way other people dream of fire. I would wake and listen for the sound of condensation falling. If I heard it, I would be calm again.

My home would be small and carefully kept.

It would be in an upper gallery of the pump-house, if I was lucky, or in a loft above the valve hall if I was not. The floor would be heated brick. The walls would be lined with reed panels and glazed tiles. I would own few things, but each would be chosen for its relation to damp: a water jar with a close-fitting lid, a lamp trimmed carefully, a tool rack with brass fittings, a raised bed covered in felt and waxed cloth, a stove burning peat and rendered fat. Above all, I would own a listening place: a section of wall where the plaster had been removed so that pipe and brick could speak directly. I would sit there when I was tired and let the Ward tell me what it needed.

I would eat what the agreement provides. Mussels and brick oysters. Eels from the channels. Pale tubers from warm silt beds. Marsh herbs grown on ledges. Fungal strips dried over engine heat. Algae cakes green-black and dense with salt. Tea brewed from reed roots and condenser water. On rare dry days I would help hang fish and cloth from the pipe galleries, and the smell of smoke, brine, and warm metal would stay in my coat for a long time. Such days would feel like festivals without ceremony. Not celebrated loudly, but remembered.

I would speak little.

Not from coldness. In the Low Ward, speech is tested before it is released. Words are valves. You turn them only when pressure requires. My voice would be low, with pauses and soft clicks. Much of what I meant would pass through gesture. A finger through condensation. A lamp blinked. A knuckle on iron. A ripple. If I loved someone there, if love could exist in such a place without becoming too dry a word, I would show it by keeping their dry cloth dry, by learning the sound of their footsteps on wet stone, by listening at their door when they were sick and the basin seemed to breathe unevenly around them. I would not promise permanence. I would promise attendance.

If children were near me, I would teach them what the Ward teaches: no splashing. Make ripples instead. Understand that every touch sends a message. Do not hate the water. Do not imagine yourself separate from it. Do not think the basin is a thing outside you. You are inside its weather.

And if an outsider came—someone from the dry world, with hurried boots and a voice too loud for the galleries—I would be strange to them. They would see a damp, bronze-skinned figure with reflective eyes and a coat shining at the elbows, standing motionless beside a sluice while mist gathered on their eyelashes. They might think I was idle. They would not understand that I was holding a sound in my head, comparing it to yesterday’s sound, deciding whether a valve needed a quarter-turn or whether a root should be left alone.

If they asked me what I was doing, I might say, “Listening.”

If they asked what I was listening for, I might say, “The place before it breaks.”

If they asked who I was, I would not know how to answer in the old way. I could not say a name only. A name in the Low Ward would be attached to a station, a pressure, a quality of damp. I would be the one at the third sluice. I would be the one who knows the south gallery breathes too fast in heat. I would be the one who wrote down the taste of rust after the transformer sheds leaked. I would be a Warden, yes, but also something less bounded: a film on lower brick, a record in the water, a sensitivity grown where iron meets condensation.

So if you found me in that world, do not look first for a face.

Look for the place where a pipe sweats in a fine continuous thread. Look for the lamp with the mineral-green halo and the moth resting on its cage. Look for the valve-spider web thickening near a joint that has not yet failed. Look for the black water crossed by a single ripple though no one seems to have moved. Look for the listening rod leaning against the rail. Look for the waxed book with its lists of leaks. Look for the small dry cloth folded with absurd care.

I would be there, or I would be the thin green-black skin remembering there, or I would be the silence just before the frogs stop.

I would be the Low Ward’s attention: wet, patient, and awake.
