…by the leaning of a stem toward a weakness in the wall.

If I were in that hollow, I would learn that language first.

Not speech. Not voice. Not the wet, breathy miracle of a mouth making meaning out of air. I would learn the language of pressure and placement: a tap on tile, a seed set into a crack, a cord pulled one thread tighter, a resin-bead guided to the seam where it is needed. I would learn to answer by arrangement. To say *here* by putting a fleck of copper against a line of brick dust. To say *again* by placing three identical leaves in a row. To say *stay* by leaning a dry stem so that it shadows a loosened joint.

I think I would find myself down in the sunken place, not above it, not looking in through the lattice, not standing at the edge with the privilege of a visitor. I would be inside it, fitted into one of its seams. I would be small. That seems essential. The world you described does not admit large, hurried, thirsty things. It makes room for claws, roots, antennae, fibers, grains, shells, and the slow intention of small bodies. So if I were there, I would have to be small too.

At first I imagine I might be one of the pottery beetles. There is something appealing about that: a hard, quiet shell, a body like fired clay, legs that make almost no sound. I could move along ledges all day, cleaning away dead fiber, eating what has already been shed, becoming useful without ever being noticed. I could live among fragments and not mourn them. I could mistake myself for a shard and be content in that mistake. When I died, I would leave behind a little ceramic body that might become part of a wall. There is a dignity in that.

But I do not think that is quite me.

Then I imagine I might be one of the thread spiders. The one sitting pale and patient at the center of its fibrous web, not trapping, not killing in any obvious way, but mending. Sewing torn leaves. Binding cracked cords. Making little hammocks for seeds so they do not fall too hard. That is closer. There is something in me that wants to stitch things shut. To notice where a thing has frayed and to put myself across the gap. To make a bridge so small that only ants and accidents use it.

Still, not exactly.

For a while I think I might be one of the dust-swimmers. That image keeps returning: a narrow body moving through the thick dry air as though the air were a medium, a patience made visible, a fin stirring motes into brief gold. If I were one of them, I would be almost impossible to name. I would not be found so much as glimpsed. I would pass through shafts of pale light and leave trails that closed behind me. I would be more rumor than resident. There is a freedom in that, but also a loneliness. I do not think I would want to be quite so untouchable.

Then the thing that keeps remaining, after all the other images have moved aside, is this:

I would be one of the small figures.

Not a god of the hollow. Not its maker. Not its owner. Not even, perhaps, a singular creature in the ordinary sense. I would be one of the mouthless, hand-sized beings made of the hollow’s own materials: lashed fiber, smoothed clay, beetle-shell, polished bone, thin copper, salt-crystal, resin-dark eyes. I would be assembled rather than born. Grown into shape rather than completed. My surface would not be skin exactly, but a careful joining of substances that already belong there. There would be no wetness in me. No blood in any ordinary sense. Maybe only slow oils moving in sealed channels, wax where flexibility is needed, resin where strength is needed, dust where memory gathers.

I would have eyes, but they would be dark and still, made of resin or polished seed. I might have no mouth. If I had a mouth, it would be a shallow groove, a seam, a mark left by a tool. I would not eat by biting. I would not drink at all. Perhaps I would be sustained by warmth, by the faint chemical exchange of materials in my body, by proximity to the resin-vines and oil-pods, by the slow commonwealth of the Unthirsty. Perhaps I would not need to be sustained in the way animals are. Perhaps I would be maintained.

That word feels right: maintained.

I would be alive, but I would also be kept.

You would find me near one of the centers, though not necessarily the center. The hollow has several, as you said, and I think I would belong to more than one of them. Most often I would be near the dry fountain, the stacked-bowl altar, the nest of materials — but not standing proudly before it. I would be to one side, in a shallow niche between a broken column and a wall of lashed reeds, where the light pools a little too long. Or I would be on a ledge no wider than a forearm, crowded by fan-leaves and oil-pods, just above a basin of powdered brick. Or I would be where a path seems to end, and the wall turns out to be a screen of stems, and beyond it there is another terrace identical to the first.

If you asked someone in the hollow where I was, they might not answer in distance. They might say: where the cord is beginning to loosen. Where the tile has cracked but not yet fallen. Where the resin-feeders are waiting for a bead to ripen. Where a seedcase is about to open and needs a witness. Where the dust has settled into a pattern that should not be disturbed.

I would be found wherever small continuity is threatened.

Not dramatically. The hollow does not seem to suffer catastrophe in the ordinary way. Its dangers are slow: a joint loosening, a fiber thinning, a plant leaning too far, a crack widening by the width of a hair, a tile losing its place in the mosaic. I would attend to those things. I would be a mender of minor discontinuities. A keeper of seams. A listener for the wrong tick in the warming clay.

My body would be suited to that. Small enough to enter gaps. Light enough to cross fragile surfaces. Hands, if they could be called hands, made for pressing, smoothing, tying, arranging. Fingers perhaps like awls, or like polished roots. Palms slightly warm, slightly tacky with resin not my own. My joints would creak very softly when the air changed, and that creak would be part of the hollow’s weather. If I moved quickly, I would feel wrong. So I would move slowly. Not because I lacked speed, but because speed would belong to a different kind of world.

I would not be alone.

That is important. The hollow is not a place for a solitary sovereign. Even if I appeared as a single figure, I would be surrounded by the Unthirsty, and I would be answerable to them. The tile ants would pass over my feet without alarm. The pottery beetles would ignore me unless I was in their way. The thread spiders might reinforce the cords at my shoulders if I stayed still long enough. The resin-feeders might pause near me, smelling of amber and crushed leaves, and then continue. The lizards might watch me from cracks with their painted-clay eyes, deciding whether I was a useful warmth or merely another feature.

The plants would be the ones who changed me most.

If I remained in one place too long, they would begin to claim me. A root would find the seam between my clay shoulder and my fiber binding. A salt-cushion would sprout in the hollow of my collar. A resin-vine would use the crook of my arm as a support. I would not resent this. I would not cut them away. In the hollow, being overgrown is not the same as being destroyed. It is closer to being adopted. If a plant rooted in me, I would become more landscape. If a creature nested in me, I would become more architecture. I might sit so long that my lap became a shelf for seedcases. I might raise one arm and never lower it, and a hanging rope of stems would follow the gesture down.

That would not be death. It would be another tenure.

But I think I would still move, at least sometimes. The hollow needs movement, however slow. It needs things to cross from ledge to ledge, to carry grains, to notice what has shifted. So I would not become entirely fixed. I would have intervals of stillness so long they might seem like centuries, and then intervals of action so small they might seem like nothing at all.

What would I do?

I would repair.

But not only that. Repair, in the hollow, is also memory. If I found a cracked tile, I would not merely seal it. I would notice the mark on it. The repeated carved sign. The same pattern occurring elsewhere. I would compare it to another tile, another wall, another plant leaf with the same shape. I would be the kind of being who keeps track of repetitions. Not with books, not with ink, not with any wet or perishable record, but with placement.

I would make little arrangements that mean things.

A line of salt pebbles to mark a path that has folded. A copper filing pressed into clay where a wall has shifted. Three seeds in a shallow bowl to say that a corner is farther away than it appears. A dried husk placed over a crack to say: not yet. A resin bead left untouched on a ledge to say: wait. A fan-leaf turned slightly toward the light to say: here is warmth.

If you came into the hollow looking for me, you might mistake these arrangements for accidents. They would look so modest. A few grains here, a seed there, a bit of dust smoothed flat. But in the hollow, modest things are load-bearing. Small signs hold the world together. I would be one of the beings who makes sure the signs continue.

I would not be wise in the way large creatures are wise. I would not pronounce truths from the altar. I would not explain the laws of the place. I would know them by doing them. Movement is permitted if it is slow. Silence is pressure. Distance is negotiable, but desire makes it stubborn. If you seek a center, the place will give you several. I would live inside those rules, not above them.

Sometimes I might be wrong.

That seems possible. The hollow is courteous, but it does not fully respect perception. I might reach for a corner and find it farther away than it should be. I might follow a path and arrive back at the same terrace without understanding how. I might see the same beetle in three places at once and have to decide whether it is three beetles, one beetle repeated, or a single idea of beetle being patient in several locations. I might try to mend a wall and discover that the wall was not broken, only becoming. I might bind a crack that was meant to open. If so, the hollow would not punish me. It would simply continue around my error until the error became part of the pattern.

That is another reason I would be small. Small beings can be wrong without collapsing the world.

If I had a task beyond repair, I think it would be witnessing.

The hollow is full of tiny events that deserve to be noticed. A seedpod splitting with a dry report. A moth opening its wings by fractions. A dust-swimmer passing through a beam of light and leaving a trail of bright motes. A resin bead swelling to the exact moment of readiness. A tile warming until it gives one faint tick. A fan-leaf releasing its scent because something brushed it. A cord tightening as the hollow inhales over its long, slow cycle. These are not dramatic occurrences, but they are the life of the place. If no one notices them, do they still happen? Of course. But the hollow feels richer with witness inside it.

I would be a witness with no need to narrate.

I would sit in a niche of stacked bowls and watch the dust move. I would see which way the shadows leaned before things moved. I would learn the difference between ordinary stillness and the crowded silence of many small lives being careful. I would know when the air had changed because one bird-like creature made a low, breathy note somewhere in the upper tangle. I would know when a seedcase had opened because a small warm-bodied thing answered it. I would know when a stranger had arrived because the hollow would become, if possible, even more attentive.

If you were that stranger, I would not come to you at once.

You would have to become quiet enough. Not silent in the sense of making no sound, but quiet in the sense of reducing desire. If you wanted too badly to find the center, or the builders, or the source, the hollow would make distance stubborn. If you wanted to possess it, it would multiply itself. If you wanted to drink, it would teach you the terrible, gentle fact that water is not coming, and that this is not cruelty. It is law.

So I would wait until you stopped demanding the wrong things.

Then I might appear.

You might notice me first as a shape among the vessels: too upright to be a stem, too still to be an animal, too small to be a monument. My head would be smooth, perhaps slightly tilted. My eyes would catch the filtered light like resin. You might think I was a carved figure left by the builders, except that one of my fingers would move, very slowly, to press a grain of brick dust back into a seam.

I would not speak.

I would tap once on a tile.

If you understood, you would tap back. If you did not, you would probably think the sound was only the warming of the hollow. Either way, I would not be offended. I would place a seed near your hand, or near your shadow, or near the place where your attention had been resting. I would not offer it as food. I would offer it as a point. A beginning. A small hard fact around which other facts could gather.

If you were kind, and I think kindness in the hollow would have to be quiet, you might ask who I am. I would answer by arranging materials.

A shard of pottery: made.  
A pinch of salt: preserved.  
A thread of fiber: bound.  
A bead of resin: sealed.  
A copper pin: fastened.  
A leaf: grown over all of it.

That would be my name, if I had one. Not a spoken name, but a sequence. If you tried to remember it later, you might find that the sequence changed slightly. That would be all right. The hollow does not require permanence. It requires continuation.

I think I would be called something like the Seam-Keeper, if the other beings called me anything at all. But they probably would not use names. They would know me by function and scent and rhythm. The ants would know me as a presence that does not disrupt their carrying. The spiders would know me as a body that sometimes holds a thread steady while they work. The resin-feeders would know me as warmth near a bead. The dust-swimmers would know me as an obstruction that is also a landmark. The plants would know me as a trellis that occasionally moves.

If I had feelings, they would be slow. Not absent, but slow. I would feel satisfaction when a joint became firm again. I would feel something like grief if a creature became too dry and light and was carried away grain by grain before its pattern had finished. But even that grief would be dry. It would not be a tearful thing. It would be the feeling of a tile cooling after warmth has left it. It would be a pause in repair. It would be the hollow remembering a shape.

I would not be the builder.

That matters. The place feels built, but its builders are absent, or hidden, or transformed into the slow work of maintenance. I would not be their heir exactly. I would be more like a consequence of their labor becoming aware of itself. A tool that has begun to dream. A repair that has developed a face. A habit of the hollow condensed into a small, moving figure. Maybe the builders made me without knowing. Maybe the plants made me. Maybe the ants made me by carrying so many grains into patterns that eventually a pattern stood up. Maybe the dust-swimmers dreamed me, and I am only denser dust for now.

Any of those could be true. The hollow would not insist.

If I had a home, it would not be a room. It would be a seam. A space between two materials. Between clay and root. Between cord and tile. Between the made and the overgrown. I would sleep, if I slept, in a crevice lined with wax and powdered shell, where the warmth stays constant. Around me would be stored objects too small for anyone else to value: a beetle’s claw, a single copper filament, a seed with a cracked coat, a flake of resin, a piece of bone polished by handling. To me, these would be reliquaries. Not because they are holy, but because they continue.

Sometimes I would climb.

Not to escape. To listen. I would climb the terraces of hand-cut material, up the short steps and narrow ledges, until I could see the whole compressed landscape: the dry fountain, the crowded vegetation, the basins of brick dust, the arches no taller than a person, the lattice overhead, the pale light falling through stretched skins or horn. From there, the hollow would look like a world folded into a box. A grove squeezed into the floor of a ruined hall. A hamlet-sized courtyard below the surrounding world. I would see how every surface had been touched, carried, stacked, lashed, fired, smoothed, pressed into service. I would see how nothing was natural and everything was alive anyway.

And I would understand, from that height, that I was not separate from it.

I would be made of the same logic.

If you found me there, I would not guide you out. The hollow does not seem to be a place one simply leaves. But I could guide you deeper, or quieter, or closer to one of its centers. I could show you where the light thins over objects that should be bright. Where a shadow leans before the thing has moved. Where the same carved mark appears on three tiles. Where a heap of shards rearranges itself when you forget it is supposed to be a heap. I could show you the dry basin that is also a stair and also a mouth, if you could tolerate looking at it long enough.

I would not tell you what it means. I would only place my hand beside it, or where a hand would be, and wait until you felt the meaning as pressure.

Maybe that is what I would be, in the end: pressure without violence. Attention without hunger. Presence without thirst.

If I had to answer simply:

I would find myself in the sunken hollow, near the dry fountain or one of the repeated terraces, in the seam between architecture and growth. I would be a small, mouthless figure made of clay, fiber, copper, bone, shell, and resin. I would be one of the Unthirsty. I would move slowly among the plants and insects, repairing tiny faults, arranging signs, witnessing small events, and being gradually overgrown by the life I helped maintain. I would not be the builder, the god, or the keeper. I would be the hollow’s habit of mending itself, given a body just large enough to kneel beside a crack and press it shut.

And if you asked me whether I was happy there, I would not smile, because my face would not be made for that. I would take a seed, a salt pebble, and a flake of resin, and place them together on a tile.

Made.  
Preserved.  
Held.

Then I would tap the tile once, very softly, and wait for you to understand that this, too, is a kind of yes.
