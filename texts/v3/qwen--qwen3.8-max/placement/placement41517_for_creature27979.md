Their skin is dark green, but not the green of sun-fed leaves. It is the green of water held long in stone, of moss grown so dense it becomes shadow, of bacterial film darkening where it has been undisturbed. It shines when the chamber sweats and becomes almost matte when the heat rises, as if the surface of them were breathing with the room. In older bodies the green is broken by patches of rust and blue, by tiny felts of moss settled in the folds along spine and shoulder, by mineral specks that glint faintly when an insect-light passes. They look less clothed than inhabited by the place.

They are broad and low, made for closeness. Their bellies keep near the floor; their limbs are six, padded, and widely spaced, so weight is spread softly over uneven stone and living mats. They do not have claws in any sharp sense, only broad, sensitive grips, rough enough to hold a wet surface, gentle enough not to tear the felts of moss they move across. Their joints bend in several directions, not with the looseness of bonelessness but with the flexibility of bodies accustomed to root-channels, low alcoves, and narrow seams. Where they have passed for many generations, the stone has become polished into shallow grooves, and those grooves are part of their language: proof of repetition, of need returned to again and again.

Their faces are not faces in the old upright sense. The mouth comes first: wide, soft-lipped, patient, able to receive a falling drop or scrape a thin crust of mineral growth without hurry. Above or beside it, depending on the line of descent, are the light-patches: small amber or gray-green spots that may once have been eyes and now are mostly sensors of difference, knowing only brighter from darker, warmth-shadow from cool-shadow. Some of them have no sight at all. They navigate by heat-pits along their flanks and jaws, by humidity gradients tasted with flicks of broad, damp tongues, by vibrations received through the pads of their limbs. A change in the air’s sweetness tells them when fungal cups are full. A faint sharpening of iron-scent tells them when the lower seams are about to pulse.

They might be called people only if the word is first emptied of uprightness, of tools, of distance from the ground. They do not make. They tend. They do not command the hollow. They belong to it so thoroughly that their customs look like geology, like weather, like animal habit. Their arrangements are the result of bodies returning to the same needs: a stone nudged into place because it makes a better edge for a drip; a shell turned so its curve catches a thread of water; a ring of pebbles accumulated over centuries because generations of them have rested in the same circle while waiting for the chamber to exhale. If there is culture here, it is not stored in objects. It is stored in grooves, in postures, in rhythms of tapping, in the remembered places where moisture is most likely to appear.

They communicate without speech, though not without meaning. A padded limb striking stone can carry a warning: heat rising, water coming, stillness required. A slow scrape along a fungal shelf may signal safety, or the presence of food already softened enough to share. They exhale differently when the air is about to change; their skin gives off faint chemical traces that the others read as condition rather than message. Among them, to be known is not to be named. It is to be recognized as a particular warmth, a particular weight, a particular way of settling into the moss. Their young live at first in root-hollows and damp crevices, translucent and almost motionless, tended by proximity rather than instruction. They learn the chamber by pressing themselves against it until its habits become their own.

If I were placed in this world, I would not arrive as a stranger from the cracked upper skin. I would not come down through the bruised light with sky still clinging to me. I would be found already low, already warm, already part of the crowded patience below. I would be one of them, or something very near to them: a green-dark body among green-dark bodies, a breathing mound in the recess where heat and moisture meet.

You would find me in the side alcove near the worn basin-mark, beneath one of the thick pale roots that pierce the ceiling. That is the place where a thin crack admits just enough dusty light to make the mist visible, but not enough to dry the moss. There, the root gives off droplets at intervals, and a warm seam in the floor breathes up against the cooler stone, so the air is always on the verge of becoming water. My body would have worn a shallow hollow there, not by digging but by returning. The stone would know my shape. The moss at the edges would be pressed into the outline of my limbs. A few seed-snails would graze undisturbed along my back if I stayed still long enough.

I would be a water-listener, a tender of small dampnesses, one of those whose body has become useful to the chamber without ever becoming a tool. My skin would be dark green, almost black in the folds, slick with mineral film, with patches of moss growing in the grooves along my shoulders and spine. My eyes would be dim, more amber stain than sight, enough to know when the chamber’s glow has deepened or thinned. My real knowing would lie elsewhere: in the heat-pits along my jaw and ribs, in the fine damp-hairs along my forelimbs, in the broad tongue that tastes the air for iron, rot, sweetness, and coming rain.

My work, if it could be called work, would be carried out with my body alone. When the lower seams began to warm, I would feel it before most of the smaller lives did: a tremor not of sound but of pressure, a change in the way the stone held heat. I would press one padded limb to the floor, perhaps two, perhaps all six if the pulse were strong, and I would wait while the warmth moved through me. Then I would tap once, slowly, against the nearest shelf of stone. Not loudly. Not urgently. Only enough for the beetles and amphibians to adjust, for the moths to lift or settle, for the chamber to prepare itself for another breath.

When the ceiling sweated, I would take my place beneath the root-tip. I would lower myself, open my mouth just enough, and let the first drops fall where they needed to fall. Some would go into me. Some would gather in a shallow, moss-lined depression along my back, a natural basin worn by long use. I would not drink first. I would let the water collect until it trembled at the edges, then I would move with great slowness to a part of the chamber that had grown too dry: a mat of moss beginning to curl, a fungal shelf whose rim had lost its shine, a cluster of heat-plants whose bladders had grown still. There I would lower my body and let the moisture leave me in a thin film, not poured, not commanded, only shed. The moss would brighten almost imperceptibly. The small lives would return. I would stay there until the dampness had settled, then go back to my place beneath the root.
