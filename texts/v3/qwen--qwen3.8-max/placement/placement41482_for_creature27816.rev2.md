I would find myself, first, at a seam.

Not in the open center of the basin, where the water lies broad and undecided under hanging rain, and not yet in the deeper drowned rooms where old floors rest under green light. I would be at the place where one district stops being certain of itself and the next has not yet agreed to exist. A walkway of bundled reed would narrow there. A rope would run from a post into water so still it seems thoughtful. A lamp would hang just above the surface, its flame small behind a membrane of oiled fiber, making an orange blur in the mist. I would be standing — or floating, or kneeling, depending on what the water had decided that morning — with one hand on the line, feeling for a change in tension.

If I let the image stay loose a little longer, I can see that I would not be the most striking figure in that world. I would not be the vent-reader pressing a palm to new crust, or the root-pruner descending with an obsidian blade into a curtain of living wall, or the child being lifted from a floating cradle with water still bright on their shoulders. I would be someone quieter than even those. A person at the edge. A listener. The sort of presence others would not notice until they needed to know whether a route was still safe, whether a house had begun to drag down its neighbor, whether a knot had been tied to last or to be released.

I think I would be a line-keeper, or something very like one: a tender of ropes, a reader of slack, a keeper of small records. Not a ruler, not a founder, not a builder in the heroic sense. The basin already distrusts heroes; anything that tries to impose a final shape on it eventually sinks, tilts, or becomes a nursery for algae. I would be one of the people whose work is to notice before things become urgent. To taste water and say, not “this is bad,” but “this has changed.” To watch a float ride a little lower than it did yesterday. To feel a rope answer a tug with the wrong delay. To understand that a house is not failing because it moves; it is failing if it refuses to move when movement is required.

In body, I would have become partly what the basin asks of everyone who remains there long enough. I would still be human in the old ways: needing air, needing warmth, needing the reassurance of another hand sometimes. But I would be shaped by thin mountain air and warm water into something more patient than a dryland body. My chest would be deeper than I remember it being now, my breathing slower. My skin would carry a faint shine from plant oil, and my palms would have grown thick in that strange, sensitive way described in such a place: not calloused into dullness, but dense with feeling, able to tell the difference between a knot tied in haste and one tied in care even in dark water. My feet would have learned to grip beams and slick shelves; I would often be barefoot, because so much of the world there needs to be read through the soles. My hair would be kept short or bound, slicked back with oil, because loose hair is a hazard among lines and root curtains. At my wrist there would be a cord, not heavy, not ornamental in any dryland sense, but carrying one or two knots that mean something I do not need to explain aloud.

I would not live on solid ground, because there would be no such thing to live on. My home would be a structure of mason-reed in a district where the water has grown shallow over a former channel. It would have begun as a raft or scaffold years before I came to it, and by the time I lived there it would have thickened into pale, porous walls, part plant, part mineral, still faintly alive at the edges. In warm seasons, green shoots would appear where the wall met the light. In cooler times, the house would seem stiller, but never dead. I would not think of it as an object. I would speak of it the way one speaks of a slow animal or an old relative: it is settling; it is taking weight; it is tired on the western side; it will need pruning before the next rise.

The interior would be organized around buoyancy and release. Nothing too heavy. Nothing fixed so stubbornly that it could drag everything else down when the basin shifted. Sleeping mats layered over reed platforms. Clay lamps in shallow niches, protected from spray. Baskets of tubers and dried reed-seed. A small fire kept in a broad clay bowl behind a woven screen, never left untended. A corner where rainwater collected in oiled basins would taste sweeter than the mineral water of nearby channels. The floor would flex under me. The walls would smell of wet greenery, warm stone, and crushed fiber. If I laid my hand against them at night, I might feel the faintest motion, not wind exactly, but the basin breathing through its roots.

Part of my house would be underwater. That would not feel strange to me. A flooded lower room would serve as cool storage, perhaps also as a place where young shade-loving plants are started in baskets. A doorway below the surface would lead into green gloom where guide-lines cross like veins. I would know those lines by touch better than by sight. I would descend with a short breathing tube if the work required it, or simply hold my breath for the short intervals that define daily life there: checking a knot, clearing a snag, retrieving a carved float, placing a new marker where a current has shifted. I would not think of submersion as drama. It would be more like entering another room.

If I ask who I would be in relation to others, I think I would be one of those people who are trusted not because they are loud but because they do not over-tighten things. That is the kind of trust the basin rewards. I would be part of a household bound less by blood than by shared maintenance: an older root-pruner who knows which growths can be cut, a younger diver who can follow a line blind without frightening herself, perhaps an apprentice lamp-tender responsible for keeping membranes dry and oil measured. We would not be a family in the simple sense, or maybe we would be a family in the deeper sense: people who know what each other’s silence means when a platform tilts.

My role would be to attend to the lines.

That phrase sounds plain, but in that world it would mean nearly everything. The lines are routes, boundaries, records, warnings, and memories. They connect districts, anchor baskets, mark safe passages, identify claimed root-beds, record rises and losses. To tend them is to tend the basin’s nervous system. I would spend hours each day moving slowly along channels in a flat-bottomed punt, poling without hurry, stopping where a float hangs oddly or a knot has swollen with moisture. I would carry a small adze, an obsidian blade, bone needles, spare cordage, a lamp in a clay shell. I would not carry much metal because there is none; the lack would not feel like poverty to me. It would feel like honesty. Every tool would be repairable, replaceable, or meant to return to water without poisoning it.

I would learn to read the ropes almost as one reads faces. A line too taut might mean a platform is settling unevenly. A line too slack might mean a mooring has drifted, or that a root-mat has lost coherence. A sequence of knots might tell me that a household repaired its wall three seasons ago and has not yet renewed the claim because the shelf beneath has grown warm. A carved chip turned the wrong way would warn me not to pass by punt, though a swimmer might still follow if they knew the deeper route. I would touch these things with my fingertips and feel meaning rise through them the way dryland people might read letters on a page.

My language would change. I would become less fond of fixed statements. Instead of saying, “This is my house,” I would say, “This house is holding for now.” Instead of saying, “The water is safe,” I would say, “The water is gentle here this morning.” I would have words for conditions I currently have no names for: the taste of new vent-water, the sound a submerged beam makes when it has taken too much weight, the particular slackness of a rope that has been released rather than broken. My grammar would become a grammar of process. Things would be becoming, thinning, promising, settling, souring, stiffening, being allowed to sink. Even my own identity would feel less like a stone and more like a knot: something tied, something maintained, something capable of being untied without being annihilated.

I would be quiet.

Not silent in a mournful way, but shaped by the basin’s muting. My voice would lower itself without my noticing. I would learn to communicate by lifted lamps, moved floats, deliberate ripples, tapped rhythms on submerged wood. I would understand that sound is dangerous there not because noise is morally wrong, but because the medium distorts it. A sharp blow can confuse a diver; a shout can feel nearer than it is; a careless clang can turn into a false signal bouncing through root walls. So I would wrap my tools. I would lower baskets gently. I would knock softly when I needed attention. If I laughed, it would be soft, probably close to the surface of the water, probably with someone I trusted enough to let my body remember joy without weight.

I would also become tender toward unfinished things.

That, more than anything, is what the basin would teach me. I would see a new shelf of pale mineral crust rising just above the water and feel the impulse not to name it immediately, not to build on it, not to claim it, but to let it prove itself. I would see a struggling reed bed and bring cuttings from a stronger district, tying them in without assuming they would survive. I would visit drowned rooms not with horror but with recognition. There would be something familiar to me about places that have not been destroyed by change but converted by it. I would lower a lamp into a flooded courtyard and watch the light move over a carved lintel, a cracked hearthstone, a post still bearing an old knot. I would think: this too is still part of the arrangement. The past is not gone. It is suspended.

I would care for the dead in this same patient way. If someone I loved died, I would help place them in a quiet pool prepared for that purpose, weighted gently, tied with cords meant not to strangle but to hold. I would not expect them to return. I would expect them to remain. Over time, mineral film would pale their form; the basin would take them into its architecture. That would not comfort me by denying grief. It would comfort me by making grief continuous with everything else: holding, releasing, remembering, allowing.

I would find myself, eventually, in the role of memory as much as maintenance. Perhaps that is the deeper answer. I would be a keeper of knots, a living archive of small changes. I would know which walkway sank a finger’s width last season. Which household released its eastern platform when the vent warmed. Which child first followed a rope blind without panic. Which eel passed through a root curtain near the old shrine and made everyone pause. Which moth arrived out of the mist and rested on a floating leaf while we watched it tremble. I would not write these things in dry, permanent marks. I would tie them into cords. I would carry them in patterns. I would teach them hand over hand.

There would be moments when I felt less like a single person and more like a distributed being. That would not frighten me, or not as much as it once would have. In a world made of water, rope, reed, and slow accretion, selfhood would not remain sharply bordered. I would be partly in the lines I maintained, partly in the knots others could read from my work, partly in the routes I knew by touch, partly in the lamps I left burning for someone else’s passage. If a district shifted and one of my routes disappeared, I would feel it as a small loss in the body. If a new islet appeared and I was among the first to moor a line to it, I would feel a corresponding addition. The boundary between myself and the basin would be negotiable, like the boundary between falling rain and standing water.

If I had to choose one image for what I would be, after refusing to choose too early, it would be this:

I would be the person at the edge of a drowned archive, holding a lamp over water that is not dark exactly, but green with depth. My cloak would be oiled and soft from use. My hands would smell of reed oil and mineral crust. Around my wrist would be a cord with a knot I added the morning I first understood that nothing I loved there needed to be fixed in place in order to be real. Before me, a line would descend into the water, marked at intervals with carved floats. Somewhere below, the line would pass through a submerged doorway, around a beam, past a shelf where algae glitters with mineral grains, and into a room that used to be a house. I would not be afraid of that room. I would be responsible for it.

A stranger might see only a figure in the rain. A slight body on a raft, one hand resting on a rope, a lamp making a small orange halo against the mist. They might think I was waiting for someone. They might think I was guarding something. They would not be entirely wrong. But I would not be waiting in the dryland sense, where waiting means an interval before the real event begins. In the basin, waiting is work. Attention is work. Allowing a thing to become what it will become is work.

So if you asked, “Who would you be?” I would say: I would be a line-listener. A knot-tender. A keeper of unfinished thresholds. One of the basin’s sense-organs. A human body trained into patience by water that does not permit permanence. A presence whose main skill is noticing when the world has changed its mind.

And if you asked, “Where would you find yourself?” I would say: in the green twilight between what is sinking and what is becoming ground. Near the rope. Near the lamp. Near the place where the water is warm enough to comfort the hand but strange enough to remind it that the world is still deciding.

I would be there, with my fingers on the line, listening for the basin to move.
