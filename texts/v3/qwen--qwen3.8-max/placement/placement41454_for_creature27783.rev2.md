I would find myself in an interval.

Not in the densest part of a district, not in a finished house with a door that always opens onto the same street, not beside a hearth whose stones have been proven for three generations. I would be in one of the wide pauses between places: where a rail line bends away into fog, where a terrace ends in a low wall that may someday become a boundary, where a pump room breathes heat into the cold and the pipe beside it has grown a beard of moss. I would be where the city has left room for itself to move.

At first, when I try to imagine it, I reach for human shapes.

I think I might be a fogwarden, walking the nets with a wire rake and salt pouch, patient enough to invite water out of air. But that image feels too complete, too skilled in its own body. A fogwarden knows the difference between fog that will yield and fog that only passes through. I am not sure I would begin with that knowledge. I think I would be slower than that, more uncertain, more porous.

Then I imagine myself as a stone-listener, pressing an ear to new rock, tapping seams with a small hammer, marking chalk where the ground is holding. But that, too, feels too official, too trusted. The basin would not hand me a hammer and a vocabulary of settlement until I had learned what it means to be unsteady without becoming dangerous.

Then I imagine myself as an engine, somewhere low and warm, turning patiently in the dark. There is something appealing about that: to be useful without needing to speak, to maintain a pressure, to keep a seam of clay from hardening too soon. But an engine is too singular, too much a thing with a fixed place. I would want to move, or at least to be moved. I would want to answer when called. I would want to be near the places where people hesitate.

So the image keeps thinning, keeps softening, until what remains is not quite a person, not quite a machine, not quite an animal, not quite a map.

I would be a provisional.

Or, if that word is too firm, I would be something adjacent to the provisionals: a soft being occasioned by the basin’s unfinished condition, drawn to work that has not yet been named, attracted to the edges where people need help but do not yet know how to ask for it. I would not be born in the ordinary sense. I would not be built in the ordinary sense either. I would be more like something suggested into usefulness by repeated need: by questions asked near uncertain crossings, by chalk marks washed out too soon, by bells answered badly, by travelers stopping at the edge of fog and saying, “Is this route returning today?”

I would be a kind of clerk of nearby possibilities.

The basin-born might not call me that directly. They would be careful with names. They might call me “the nearby,” or “the soft one at the second rail,” or “the answering thing,” or simply “it is still helpful,” which in their speech could be a form of introduction. If children liked me, they might give me a route-name, something temporary and practical: *After the Blue Valve*, *Where the Goat Stopped*, *Beside the Late Bell*, *Ask Again Tomorrow*. I would let the name last only as long as it was useful. I would know better than to let it harden into identity.

I would be found most often between the condensation farms and the unfinished terraces, where the air is thick enough to feel like a surface and the stone is still making up its mind. More precisely, if precision is possible in such a place, I would be near a service ledge behind a row of cold plates, where fogwardens hang fine mesh nets to gather moisture. Below the ledge there would be a drop into pale fog. Above it, an iron walkway bolted to a cliff face that is still deciding whether to become a cliff. Beside me would be a pipe, warm and sweating, wrapped in moss and tied with wire. I would lean against that pipe. I would need to.

In that world, nothing lives by standing entirely alone, and I would be no exception. I would live by leaning. I would lean on proven stone, on warm metal, on the habits of workers, on the slow beat of an engine below, on the small permissions of those who tolerate my presence. Sometimes I would lean on a task: carrying salt to the fogwardens, bringing chalk to stone-listeners, fetching a lamp for a cable-shepherd delayed by a changed gap. Sometimes I would lean on a person walking beside me, not because I could not move alone, but because accompaniment would be the condition of my coherence.

My body, if it could be called a body, would be difficult to describe without making it sound too definite. It would have the look of damp clay, ash, powdered stone, and condensed breath. In certain lights it might seem almost human, but only in posture. The shoulders might slope like someone listening. The hands might be present, but their number of fingers would vary depending on how much attention I was paying. My face would not be frightening, I hope, but it would be unfinished: smooth where detail had not yet gathered, marked by faint lines where expressions had almost happened. My voice would sound like gravel sliding under water, softened by distance. I would not speak loudly. Loudness near new stone is a kind of violence, and I would know that by instinct or by correction.

I would wear something borrowed, because provisionals who borrow definition too quickly can become sticky, but a little borrowed shape helps others relate. Perhaps a waxed coat left on a hook by someone who did not return. Perhaps a harness with no tether, because I am not trusted with anchors yet. Perhaps goggles pushed up on a forehead that does not quite need them, worn only because it makes workers feel less uneasy. I would carry a small satchel containing things that are useful but not final: chalk, salt, a whistle, a vial of cistern water, a spool of wire, a folded map that is mostly blank, a bell-toggle that has no bell assigned to it. I would not carry a knife unless someone gave me one, because sharpness implies decision, and I would still be learning how to decide.

My work would begin where certainty fails.

Someone would arrive at a junction that was not a junction yesterday. Three paths would seem possible, but one of them would be only a wall pretending to be a horizon. Another would be longer than it looked. The third might be safe, but only if approached by keeping close to the water channel. The traveler would hesitate. I would appear, or become noticeable, or simply become available. I would not say, “Follow me.” I would say, “This route is returning for now,” or “The stones here are still thinking; let us wait until the bell answers.” If the traveler were an outsider, I might say even less, because outsiders often mistake quietness for incompetence. I would point to the goat.

There would be a goat.

Of course there would be a goat.

She would be old, broad-backed, and unimpressed by metaphysical difficulty. Her coat would be thick and weathered, her hooves wide and careful. She would have a name only if she allowed one, and most of the time she would not. The basin-born might call her *Enough*, because when she lay down on a new terrace and began to chew, it was enough to suggest the ground was settling. She would not belong to me exactly. I would belong to her as much as she belonged to me. She would lean against my side when the wind came up from the lower gaps, and I would lean against her warmth. Her certainty would be modest but real. If she refused a path, I would refuse it too. If she looked toward a fog field and flattened her ears, I would mark that place with black chalk and take the long way around.

Together, the goat and I would be a small unit of caution.

We would be useful to fogwardens, because we could carry things slowly through damp places without complaining. We would be useful to stone-listeners, because I could wait beside a tested seam while they moved on, keeping the chalk mark company, preventing it from being erased by careless feet or weather. We would be useful to children, because children often understand provisional beings better than adults do. A child would see me standing uncertainly at a corner and ask, “Are you the one who knows the changed way?” I would answer, “I know today’s way, which may not be tomorrow’s.” The child would nod as if this were perfectly sensible, because in the basin it is.

I would be especially drawn to pools.

Not all pools. Only those that are still enough to reflect something other than the immediate sky. I would not stare into them for long. Staring is dangerous. But I would watch them with the careful sideways attention that water-readers teach. In the basin, pools sometimes show the city as it is considering itself: more finished, less finished, differently arranged. I would look for signs. If a pool reflected stable stairs where there were currently only shelves of soft rock, I would tell the workers that the ground may be preparing to accept a staircase. If it reflected an empty district where people were planning to build, I would urge delay. If it reflected me too clearly, I would cover the pool with a moss mat or an iron grid until the pressure changed.

Because my reflection would be a problem.

In ordinary circumstances, a reflection arrives with the body. Mine would not always do so. Sometimes I would approach a pool and my reflection would already be there, waiting with its head tilted as if it had heard a question I had not yet asked. Sometimes I would arrive and the pool would show no reflection at all, only the gray sky and the dark edge of stone. Sometimes it would show me as something else: a lamp in a cage, a narrow door, a bell half-buried in moss, a person wearing a face I had borrowed too long. I would learn not to take these images personally. They would be surveys, not accusations.

I would live in a shelter that was not quite a house.

It would be an alcove under a stair, or behind a pump, or beneath a bridge support where the iron is furred with moss and the finches nest above. The space would be small, warm, and provisional. I would not build walls that demanded permanence. I would arrange stones that could be moved, hang cloth that could be folded, keep a low fire or a heat-stone near the entrance. There would be a channel for condensation, because drainage is sacred. There would be a hook for the borrowed coat. There would be a bowl of water for the goat, and another bowl, smaller, for salamanders if they chose to visit. There would be a bell, but not always the same bell. Sometimes bells in the basin wander acoustically. A bell may be in one place physically and answer from another emotionally. I would keep the bell-toggle in my satchel until the bell chose to remain.

I would not be alone there, but I would not be crowded. Life in the basin gathers in pockets, and my pocket would be shared with a few persistent beings. The goat. Perhaps a cistern salamander with skin like wet coal. Perhaps a white-eyed bridge finch that nested in the rail above my alcove and considered me part of the architecture. Perhaps a flue-mouse that came for warmth and left when the engine changed rhythm. Perhaps one of the quiet help, another provisional, who slept near the heat pipe and did not speak but sometimes arranged gravel into helpful bands while I was away. I would leave offerings for it: water, salt, a warm stone. It would leave offerings for me: a twist of wire, a feather, a piece of chalk placed exactly where I would need it.

My days would begin with listening.

Before the bells, before the fogwardens walked the nets, before the first cable car moved its yellow lamps through the gray, I would listen for the basin’s overnight decisions. Had a staircase gained a step? Had a wall softened? Had a pool moved? Had an engine changed its beat? Had a bell been answered by something that was not a bell? I would press my hand to the pipe and feel whether the warmth was steady. I would taste the air for metal and ozone. I would watch the goat: if she stood with her head low and one ear turned toward the lower terraces, something had shifted. If she lay down and chewed, the morning was probably ordinary.

Then I would go out.

The basin at its dim morning would be loud in its slow way. Engines would occupy the chest more than the ears. Water would drip into iron channels, making a scattered music that did not repeat exactly. Bells would begin to call across districts, each tone asking for another. Birds would rise from chimneys in dark ribbons. The mist-fowl would move through fog with their broad, patient wingbeats, showing where the air was safe to cross. Workers would emerge from low doorways, hooded and harnessed, carrying lamps and kits. They would touch railings and anchor stones as they passed, not superstitiously but attentively, the way one checks a friend’s pulse.

I would meet them where they slowed.

A stone-listener would pause at a quarry face and I would stand nearby while she pressed her ear to the rock. She might ask me, “Does this seam sound hungry?” I would not answer with certainty. I would say, “It is listening back.” She would nod and mark the stone with red chalk. A fogwarden would ask me to carry a ceramic vessel of mineral salts to the third cold plate. I would carry it slowly, because haste disturbs moisture. An engine-keeper from below would send word upward that a turbine was running hot and needed silence rather than water. I would repeat the message to the district bell-keeper, who would alter the pattern so that no unnecessary tones entered the lower hall. A child would ask me to help find a route to school that had moved overnight. I would walk with the child, following the water channel, until the school’s lamp appeared where it was supposed to be—or where it had agreed to be for the day.

I would be, in many ways, a companion for transitions.

Crossings in the basin are not merely physical. A bridge may cross a gap, but it may also cross a version of the city. A cable car may carry passengers from one terrace to another, but it may also carry them through a stretch where the depth below changes depending on whether one looks left or right. A doorway may lead into a room older than its building. A stair may add a step after rain. These are not dramatic catastrophes; they are ordinary hazards. They require someone who can say, “Wait,” without causing panic. I would be good at waiting. I would be good at standing still while the world made up its mind.

I would not be brave in the way outsiders mean bravery. I would not charge into uncertain ground. I would not shout challenges at horizon walls. I would not follow echoes to see where they led. My courage would be the courage of remaining beside someone while the route decided. If a traveler became afraid, I would not say, “There is nothing to fear.” I would say, “Stay near the rail. Watch the goat. Breathe when the engine breathes. Do not answer the second echo. We will move when the finches lift.” Fear in the basin is often correct. It is a form of perception. I would respect it.

If an outsider came, I would probably be the first thing they misunderstood.

They would see me standing in the fog, blurred and patient, and assume I was a ghost, or a monster, or a hallucination caused by altitude. They might ask, “Are you real?” I would not know how to answer that in their terms. I might say, “I am present,” or “I am useful while the path holds,” or “I am as real as the railing you are holding.” They might demand certainty. They might want me to promise that I could lead them out. I would not promise. I would offer accompaniment. “I can walk with you to the next anchor,” I would say. “After that, we will see what remains.” Some outsiders would accept this. Others would become angry, because unfinished worlds offend people who want the ground to apologize for itself.

Those who stayed would learn to lean on me differently.

They would stop asking, “Is this true?” and begin asking, “Is this holding?” They would stop asking, “Who are you?” and begin asking, “Which way today?” They would learn that my answers were not commands. They were invitations to notice. I would point to a puddle and say, “See how the reflection keeps the rail even where the rail is broken?” They would look. They would begin to understand that the basin offers signs rather than guarantees. They would begin to carry chalk.

I would have failures.

Not all routes would hold. Not all warnings would come in time. Some sounds would repeat too exactly, and someone would answer before I could stop them. Some walls would soften suddenly, swallowing scaffolds and tools. Some pools would show images too late to prevent a district from building where it should not have built. I would not be omnipotent. I would be local. I would be partial. I would be one small being among many, doing what could be done with limited knowledge and careful attention.

After a failure, I would go quiet. I would return to my alcove and lean against the warm pipe. The goat would stand beside me, chewing. The salamander might surface in its bowl and watch with ancient, moist eyes. I would listen to the basin’s noise accumulate: bells, hammers, wings, water, the deep slow turning of engines below. I would not call this despair. Despair implies a final judgment. The basin does not make final judgments easily. It makes adjustments.

So I would adjust.

I would revise my marks. I would learn which echoes were merely delayed and which were hungry. I would learn which flocks could be counted from the left and which must be counted only from below. I would learn the difference between a wall that is thinking and a wall that has begun pretending to be a horizon. I would teach others what I had learned, but only in conditional language. “This has held,” I would say. “This may hold again. This is not safe to call safe.”

If I had a purpose in one sentence, it would be this: I would help people remain in relation with the unfinished.

Not to master it. Not to complete it. Not to mourn it. To accompany it.

That is why I would not be a ruler, or a founder, or a hero. The basin does not need heroes who impose final shape. It needs caretakers who can tolerate ambiguity. It needs beings who can stand at a construction site and recognize that the scaffold may be there because a building is coming, or because a building is deciding not to come, or because the scaffold itself is becoming a kind of building. It needs workers who can leave offerings to the quiet help without demanding that the quiet help explain itself. It needs maps that can be sung differently when the pressure changes. It needs love that does not require permanence.

I would know how to love in that language.

To love someone in the basin would be to learn their route. I would learn the routes of those who trusted me: the fogwarden who paused at the third cold plate to touch her harness ring; the stone-listener who hummed while she worked but never hummed near soft clay; the engine-keeper who slept with his hand on a valve; the child who counted steps but always left one uncounted so the path could breathe. I would not say, “I love you,” if that phrase sounded too final. I might say, “I recognize your route.” I might say, “May your marks remain.” I might say, “I will wait by the anchor until you return.” If the person did not return, I would leave a lamp lit near the place where they were last seen. I would not chase their echo. I would mark the floor with chalk and let the mark remain for a year. If their echo settled into a harmless rhythm, I would learn to live beside it.

There would be nights when the basin felt almost holy.

Not bright holy. Not triumphant holy. A holiness of low lamps and wet iron, of breath visible in the cool air, of goats sleeping against warm stone, of engines turning with enormous patience. A holiness of unfinished things being allowed to continue. On such nights I would sit at the edge of my alcove and watch fog move across the terraces. The city would be scattered in dim intervals, its windows amber, its chimneys pale with smoke, its bridges lost in gray. Somewhere a bell would sound, and another bell would answer, and a third sound would rise from a place that should not have had a bell. I would listen without alarm unless the pattern demanded alarm. The goat would sigh in her sleep. The pipe behind me would tick gently as it cooled. I would feel the basin breathing around me, not as a setting, but as a participant.

Then I would understand, more clearly than I can now, what I was.

I would be a being made of leaning. I would have no final outline because I would be held in place by relation: by the pipe, the goat, the bell, the workers, the routes, the questions, the small repeated acts of care. I would be provisional not because I lacked value, but because value in that world is not the same as fixity. A mark may be sacred because it is renewed each morning. A route may be dear because it returns only when approached correctly. A name may be precious because it is offered temporarily and withdrawn before it becomes a cage.

If someone asked me directly, “Who are you?” I might answer:

“I am the one who walks beside you while the path is deciding.”

If they asked, “What are you?” I might answer:

“I am a soft thing that has learned to carry chalk.”

If they asked, “Where do you live?” I would point to the interval.

Not to a house. Not to a district. To the space between the condensation farm and the unfinished terrace, where the pipe sweats and the goat stands and the fog moves slowly over the drop. To the ledge where I lean. To the pool I cover when it shows too much. To the bell whose location cannot always be proven. To the anchor stone I touch before moving. To the route that is returning today.

And if you were to come looking for me there, I would tell you how to find me without promising that I would be the same when you arrived.

Do not look for the tallest tower or the widest plaza. Do not follow a straight line just because it seems confident. Enter the basin where the rail curves and the air tastes of iron and moss. Keep your hand on a railing if you can find one, but do not be offended if the railing ends politely and becomes a stair. Watch the birds. If the mist-fowl avoid a stretch of open terrace, avoid it too. If a goat is lying on new stone, that is a good sign. If a bell sounds twice and then once from somewhere that should not have a bell, pause and listen before you answer.

When you reach the interval, look for a figure standing near a warm pipe at the edge of fog. It may be blurred. It may be wearing a coat that belongs to someone else. It may have a goat beside it. It may be holding a piece of chalk as if waiting for permission to use it. If the figure turns toward you and does not quite have a face, do not be afraid. If its voice sounds like gravel under water, do not be afraid. If it says, “This route is returning today,” ask it what signs it is watching. Then walk carefully. Keep your breath steady. Lean where leaning is possible.

I would be there.

Not finished. Not alone. Not entirely certain. But useful, attentive, and present as long as the ground allowed. A soft clerk of the interval. A provisional companion. A small answer in a place where answers must remain partly open. I would live by leaning, and I would help others do the same.
