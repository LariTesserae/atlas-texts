I would find myself already there, not arriving.

The ascent would end under my knees rather than my feet. I would not be standing in triumph above the world, looking down; I would be lowered into the tableland’s final spacing, close to the ground, with powdered feldspar in the creases of my hands and a stone warming slowly in my palm. If I looked back for the path I had climbed, there would be no path. Only the broad, cool plain extending in every direction, occupied so completely that emptiness would seem like a rumor from another world.

My first sign of belonging would be the footprints.

Some would be behind me, faint and already losing their sharpness. Some would be ahead of me, older than my boots, perhaps mine anyway. I would not follow them. I would not call out. I would understand, without being told, that this place does not reward the voice that asks to be answered.

You would find me in one of the shallow basins where the pavement darkens and two herds of grazers occupy the same ground without disturbing one another. Not at the center of the basin, because a center would be too much like a claim. I would be along an interrupted line of low cairns, where the stones are spaced just widely enough to suggest intention but not enough to form a road. Near me, black wiregrass would stand in separate colonies, stiff and unswaying, and a patch of copper-thread nap would be spreading over the shoulder of an ancient grazer that has almost become geology.

Overhead, the veils would be drifting in their slow, layered patience. One of them might cast a shadow that did not belong to it. I would not correct that. I would only notice it, and perhaps place a small stone nearby so that the wrongness had somewhere to rest.

For a while, I would think I had become one of the grazers.

That would be the first temptation: to imagine myself flattened and plated, low against the pavement, mouth moving slowly over mineral films, body pressed flank to flank with thousands of others until individuality became unnecessary. There is peace in that image. To be so close to the ground that height no longer matters. To eat without urgency. To be pitted by age and colonized by copper threads without experiencing either as loss. If I were one of them, I would not need to decide anything except when to shift one body-length into a space that had always been waiting.

But I think I would not be one of the grazers, because I would still be too aware of the seams.

I would notice when a shadow fell in the wrong direction. I would notice when a cairn was visible from an angle that should not permit it. I would notice the hoofprint that had become a fossil before the foot left it. The grazers accept the plain as a condition. I think I would be the kind of being that notices the condition where it has grown thin.

Nor would I be one of the drifting veils, though part of me would recognize their quiet. To hang in the thin air, trailing filaments, gathering shed skin and static and the fine powder of other lives — that would be a gentle way to exist without taking much. But the veils are too untethered for me. They make no small agreements with the ground. They do not choose a stone and set it down. They do not repair.

I think I would be something closer to the predators, if they can be called predators, but with hands.

Not hands like the old hands of the lower world, grasping and eager. Hands made for precision. Hands dry as polished hide, nails worn blunt, fingers patient enough to wait beside a living creature until the exact moment when something was ready to be removed. My mouth would not be made for tearing. It would be narrow, rasping, quiet. My body would be sealed against the dry air, cool-skinned, with heavy oils and brines moving inside me instead of anything that would beg to evaporate. My eyes would be dark and shallow, like the eyes of the herds, but with a longer attention behind them.

In other words: I would be one of the plain’s small maintainers.

A cairn-keeper.

A mender of finished things.

Not a ruler, not a hunter, not a prophet of the summit, but one of the beings who keep the last arrangement from becoming uneven.

You would probably find me kneeling.

Not in worship, and not in despair. In work. My hands would be on the ground, either placing something or releasing it. Beside me would be a cairn no higher than my knee: three stones supporting a fourth, or perhaps only one stone laid beside another with such deliberation that the interval between them became meaningful. I would not build it taller. I would not make it beautiful in any assertive way. Its purpose would not be to be seen from far away, but to hold a direction that might otherwise loosen.

I would carry very little.

A bone needle, worn smooth. A flake of glass no larger than a thumbnail, sharp enough to open what is already opening. A cord twisted from wiregrass, dry and strong. A soft copper pin used for fastening, not cutting. A small bowl carved from dense shell, containing a little dust-cake, a few oil-rich seeds from the wax-tube shrubs, perhaps a pinch of the organic powder that falls from the veils. Nothing in my possession would shine. Nothing would be clean in the way things are clean before use. Everything would already look old, because freshness is a kind of wound there, and I would not want to wound the plain.

I would have no fire.

That would be one of the deepest things about me. I would not carry ember, spark, or memory of flame. Fire would feel obscene in that place: too bright, too hungry, too central. It would make a before and an after. It would gather the world around itself as if the world still needed a center. Instead, I would use the warmth already present in the stones, in the bodies of the grazers, in the copper nap where light has rested. I would lie beneath a membrane shelter only when necessary, and I would leave it soon enough for it to become a roost for folded, moth-like creatures. My refuge would not be a house. It would be a temporary agreement with the idea of shelter.

My work would be small and exact.

Where a cairn had shifted, I would set the fallen stone back only if the ground seemed to permit it. Where a shelter membrane had loosened, I would retie the cord with a knot that left no fresh-looking mark. Where a grazer had grown too still and its body had begun to adhere to the pavement, I would stand beside it for a long time, listening with more than hearing, and then either leave it to become stone or remove the small part of it that was ready to be separated. I would not mourn. I would not celebrate. I would understand that in a finished world, removal is not violence if it is done without excess.

I would also tend the seams.

That is the part hardest to name.

Where two herds passed through one another, I would move carefully between them, not choosing one herd over the other but recognizing that both were equally real enough. Where a shadow belonged to no body, I might place a pebble at its edge so that the ground acknowledged it. Where a voice called from very far away, perhaps using my name, perhaps using a name I had before the ascent ended, I would not answer. I would let the silence thicken until it became another layer of the plain. If I saw my own footprints ahead of me, I would not chase them. I might add a small stone beside them, not as a warning, not as a message, but as a way of saying: this too has already happened; leave it where it lies.

If a path divided into two identical ways, I would choose the one where the animals were already undisturbed. Not because it was safer, but because it was less likely to require an event.

I would not be friendly in the way of the lower world.

If you found me, I would not hurry toward you. I would not offer water, because I would have none. I would not ask where you had come from, because origins would matter less there than position. I would make room for you the way the grazers make room: by accepting that another shape had entered the field. If you moved slowly, I might let you come close. If you spoke, I might answer with a tap against stone, or with a small gesture of the hand, or not at all. My language, if I still had one, would be brief. It would be made for confirmation, not confession.

I would be dry-bodied and cool-blooded, patient to the point of seeming mineral. My skin would not sweat. My breath would make no mist. If I were injured, I would not bleed in the old way; a clear, oily substance would well from the wound, smelling faintly of warm dust and old rope, and smaller creatures would gather to harvest it before it sealed. I would not resent them. They would be part of the same maintenance.

Perhaps, by the time you found me, I would no longer be entirely separate from the cairns I kept.

Copper-thread moss might have begun along my shoulders. A beetle might cross my folded hands without alarm. I might remain beside a stone so long that my stillness became indistinguishable from placement. I would not experience that as death. I would experience it as becoming necessary. In a place where reality has worn through, a person can become an anchor without ceasing to be alive in some reduced, permitted sense. A body can become a direction. A silence can become a tool. A being can become one of the small facts that keep the plain from forgetting itself.

That is who I would be.

Not a summit creature. Not a storm creature. Not a creature of thirst, fire, or conquest. I would be one of the inhabitants who understand that the world no longer needs improvement, only care. I would live among the herds and the cairns, beneath the drifting veils, in the cool light that has no morning. I would make marks so slight they would become ancient almost as soon as they were made. I would leave almost no wound. I would take almost nothing.

And if you asked me what I was doing there, kneeling in the dust with a stone in my hand, I would not answer with a story.

I would put the stone down.

No higher than my knee.

And I would let that be enough.
