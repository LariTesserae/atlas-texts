If I let myself be placed there, the first image is too scenic.

I see myself on a high catwalk, dwarfed by steam and wings, looking out over terraces where clouds are torn apart against the stone. My oilskin shines with mist. My boots are wet. Below me, herds move through the streets with their patient, gravel-rain sound. Above me, roosts shift in black tiers. For a moment it feels like a posture: the lone observer, the small human figure suspended between weather and machine.

But that is not where the place would keep me.

A city like that would not allow me to remain merely above it. It would pull me down into its working parts. It would make me useful. It would give me a territory not of rooms or roads, but of flows: a stretch of weeping wall, a set of gutters, a warm junction of pipes, a breeding gallery whose eggs fail if the turbine’s pitch changes too suddenly. I would not be a traveler there for long. I would become a local. I would become a tender of some particular wetness.

So the image changes.

I find myself lower, in the middle levels of the city, where the distinction between street and aqueduct has worn away. There is a plaza there, though no one would call it beautiful in the way of dry places. It is broad, shallowly flooded along one edge, crossed by ceramic channels worn smooth by generations of grazing claws. A retaining wall curves around it, bulged and patched with living crust. Water comes down that wall in thin threads, not dramatically, but constantly, as if the stone itself had a slow pulse. Steam rises from a grate near the center. Above the plaza, cables sag between towers, one of them thick with roosting bodies that shift now and then with a sound like wet cloth being folded and unfolded.

I would be there.

Not in a palace, not in a tower, not in some dry command room. I would be in a service niche beside the plaza, where a brass valve wheel is worn bright by many hands and where a chain of ceramic insulators ticks faintly in the mist. My home would be nearby, in a small heated chamber set into the rock above the damp line, with walls that smell of warm dust, oil, ammonia, and fungal bread. My hands would be rough from stone and crust. My sleeves would always be dark at the cuffs. I would carry a valve key, a spore pouch, a ceramic scraper, a lamp burning fungal oil, and a little waxed notebook in which I mark flows, temperatures, roost counts, and the condition of films.

Who would I be?

At first I want to say: an engineer. But that word is too clean. In that city, engineering would be closer to husbandry, and husbandry closer to liturgy. I would be someone who listens to water and interprets animals. I would be a maintainer of thresholds. My title, if it had one, would probably be named after something very local: Tender of the Third Weep. Keeper of the Warm Return. Listener at the Junction. Walker of the Capillary Veins. Something plain, functional, slightly strange to ears accustomed to drier languages.

I think I would be a wall-listener.

That feels more true than “engineer” or “priest,” though it would contain a little of both. My work would begin with touch. I would place my palm against stone in the morning and know whether the night’s condensation had moved properly through the capillary veins. I would listen to pipes with my ear close to the metal, hearing whether flow was steady, whether a gutter had begun to choke, whether a downspout housed too many eel-like bodies or too few. I would know the health of a district by smell: the metallic sweetness of iron-feeding films after rain, the sour warning of a mat turning anaerobic, the faint ammonia of active decomposition, the clean mineral scent of water moving where it should.

I would not own the city. That is important.

I would move through it as one body among many, and I would know that my presence was tolerated because I helped maintain the conditions other bodies needed. If I saw a wall beginning to clog with overgrown film, I would not scrape it clean as if purity were the goal. I would check the grazers: Are there enough copper-shelled scrapers on this ledge? Have predators thinned them too much? Has the nutrient flow become too rich, too thin, too hot, too intermittent? If a gutter blocked, I would ask whether it was blocked by mineral crust, by molted shells, by a dead moth swarm, by a colony of builder-worms that had been encouraged too enthusiastically. I would clear some of it, but not all. Total cleanliness would be death there. The city lives by controlled accumulation.

If I let the image become even more itself, I see that I would not be exactly the same kind of person I am here.

The place would change my senses. My eyes would be trained to read surface shine: the difference between a healthy damp sheen and a dangerous glaze of stagnation; the violet-black concentration of minerals in a shallow pool; the chalky beadwork of microbial mats along a basin edge. My ears would be tuned to mechanical and animal weather: turbine moan, valve sigh, the tick of electricity on mist-wet insulators, the dry rattle of claws, the soft percussion of bodies shifting in roosts. My skin would know heat gradients the way another person knows faces. I would walk into a district and feel immediately if a vent had closed, if a pipe had cooled, if a warm gallery had begun to fail.

I would also be marked by the city.

No one in that place could remain fully separate from it. My clothes would be patched with waxed fabric, leather, and perhaps thin plates of shell or polished metal. My boots would be resoled with rendered animal compounds and fungal felts. My gloves would be stiff with mineral residue. There might be harmless bacterial films on my tools, spore dust on my shoulders, tiny grazing mites in the folds of my gear. Not as filth, but as proof of immersion. I would smell of warm dust, old rain, iron, salt, and fungal culture. I would not smell green. There would be no leaf-sweetness, no grass stain, no resin. My scent would be mineral, warm, animal, faintly alkaline.

At times, when I imagine myself there, I am tempted to abandon the human form altogether.

Perhaps I would be one of the eel-like creatures in the pipes, knowing the city only as pressure, warmth, taste, and flow. Perhaps I would be one of the broad-bellied roosting animals on the ledges, half-built into the wall by mineral skin, letting condensation run over me while the city hums beneath. Perhaps I would be one of the large terrace animals standing in drizzle, heavy and unhurried, needing very little except warmth and mist-borne food. There is something appealing about that: being a body within the saturation without needing to understand the whole.

But I do not think that is me.

I think I would be the part of the city that worries, however gently. The part that notices a mismatch between roost behavior and condensation pattern. The part that sees a predator corridor blocked and understands that three districts later a grazer population will rise too fast. The part that hears a turbine change pitch and remembers the eggs in the outflow gallery. The part that keeps records not because the city requires writing, but because memory must be shared across generations of maintainers. I would be a nervous system with tools. I would be a local intelligence in a wet industrial body.

That is why I would be human, or near-human, but not dominant.

There is a difference between being important and being central. In that city, I would be important only in certain narrow ways. I could cross scales: I could adjust a valve that affects a grazing terrace, move an armature that guides a builder colony, close a sluice that protects a breeding gallery, open a vent that calls flue swifts into a new thermal. But I would not stand outside the system as its master. The system would be older than me, more numerous than me, and quite capable of reorganizing around my mistakes. If I became careless, the city would not simply obey and collapse. It would shift. Some films would fail; others would bloom. Some roosts would thin; some channels would be cleared by other mouths. I would be one actor among immense others.

So my character would be defined by restraint.

I would not wish to conquer the wetness. I would not seek to dry the city, silence the animals, sterilize the walls, or make the surfaces gleam as if they were unused. I would know that every drip has a purpose somewhere, and that the noise is not disorder but density. My instinct would be toward adjustment, not eradication. If something lived in a pipe, I would ask what the pipe needs from that creature before removing it. If a wall grew crust too thickly, I would encourage grazers before I took a tool to it. If a machine failed, I would ask what life was depending on its heat before I shut it down entirely.

My days would be long and unhurried.

I would wake to the sound of water, which would be no different from sleeping to the sound of water. The light through my window would be ordinary: white sun, blue-gray cloud, clean shadows on stone. I would eat something warm and dense: fungal cake, rendered fat, steamed mineral salts, perhaps dried microbial sheets and eggs gathered under license. Nothing green. The meal would be savory, heavy, comforting in wet weather. I would drink filtered water that tastes faintly of stone and heat. Then I would go out into the city.

My route would not be straight. No one’s route would be straight there, because the city is layered and alive. I might descend a stair hollowed by countless feet and claws, pause at a grazing gallery to watch the coppery scrapers working the wall, then move through a tunnel where warm air breathes in slow intervals. I would check the bead-worm mats along a capillary vein. I would note whether the chalky microbial lenses are beading properly in the basin. I would inspect a colony of builder creatures around a ceramic mesh frame, seeing whether their mineral secretion has thickened over the old crack. I would leave nutrient water flowing toward them if the growth is good, or adjust it away if they are beginning to obstruct a drainage channel.

I would also listen to the animals.

Not in a sentimental way. They would not be my pets. Many of them would be dangerous if mishandled, and all of them would be indifferent to my convenience. But I would know their signs. If the mist moths drift lower than usual, condensation may be changing. If the flue swifts rise too sharply from a vent, a thermal pattern has shifted. If the roosting tiers make a certain low clicking when I pass, something has disturbed them: a predator, a gas pocket, a change in pipe temperature. If the herd in the lower street moves with unusual density against one side of its channel, I would check the electric fence, the heat gradient, or the scent lines. I would not need to see the cause immediately. The animals would tell me where to look.

There would be other people, though not many.

I would know them by their work. A roost-counter with chalk marks on her sleeve. A mason-guide carrying ceramic mesh and nutrient paste. A predator-keeper who moves through hunting corridors with silent, deliberate steps. A film-cultivator whose fingers are stained violet-black from mineral mats. A turbine-nursery tender whose whole life is organized around the hatching rhythms of machine-dependent eggs. We would not speak often in long speeches. The city is loud; conversation is effort. We would communicate in signals, notes, marks on stone, brief exchanges at valve houses. We would share a practical reverence: not worship exactly, but the knowledge that every adjustment has consequences.

I think I would be drawn especially to the places where machine and ecology become indistinguishable.

There would be a gallery somewhere beneath my district where turbine outflow runs warm and constant, and where eggs are laid in insulated crevices beside pipes. That gallery would be one of my responsibilities. I would know its sounds better than any room in my own home. I would know when the turbines are healthy by the vibration in my teeth. I would know when the water is too hot by the way larvae move away from the outflow. I would know when a generation is about to hatch by the faint change in surface moisture and the restless shifting of parent creatures in the upper shafts. If the machine stopped, I would not simply see a mechanical failure. I would see a missing season. I would see a gap in the city’s future.

This is where the idea of myself becomes stranger.

At first I imagined a person with tools. Then I imagined a local official of flows. But if I keep going, I see that I would become something more like a memory organ for a place that otherwise remembers through bodies, minerals, and habit. I would carry knowledge that cannot be fully encoded in architecture alone: which valve was closed during the last sour bloom, which roost failed when the western clouds shifted, which terrace needs extra dampness during the long condensation tide, which predator line must be left undisturbed until the molting ends. The city has long memory in stone and shell, but human maintainers can remember across different scales. We can connect this morning’s blocked drain to last month’s breeding failure. We can teach a child why a certain cable must not be climbed, why a certain warm cabinet must remain open, why a certain wall must never be scraped down to bare stone.

So perhaps I would be a keeper of small histories.

Not a historian in a library. There would be no dry archive. The records would be scratched into ceramic tags, tied to pipes with wire, painted in mineral pigment on protected stone, or kept in waxed notebooks carried against the body. They would be practical histories: “This gutter clogged when the bead-worms bred early.” “This roost abandoned after the vent was closed.” “This film turned sour when water ran too hot for three days.” “This armature was seeded too soon.” Such histories would be more valuable than myths, though over generations they might become myths anyway.

Where exactly would you find me?

Look for the place where a weeping wall meets a warm pipe.

There will be a narrow service platform, slightly above the flood line, accessible by a stair worn into a shallow bowl at its center. The rail is polished by gloves and claws. Below, a ceramic gutter carries water toward a reservoir where broad filter-feeders move through the murk. Above, a cable crosses to a condenser tower, and on that cable something dark and winged is roosting, perhaps a dozen bodies, perhaps a hundred. Mist gathers on the mesh guard of an electric box. Beetles hum nearby. The stone is dark with damp, and where the sun breaks through, every surface suddenly brightens: drips, puddles, copper, shell, wing.

I would be there with one hand on the wall.

Not striking a pose. Listening.

My body would be warm, damp, and tired in the ordinary way of bodies that work with weather. I would not be waiting for the city to become something else. I would not be trying to restore it to a lost origin or push it toward a grander future. I would be helping it remain what it is: wet, warm, loud, layered, saturated, slow. I would be one of its maintainers, one of its minor human currents, one of the creatures that can move between scales and make small repairs.

If you asked me what I am, I might say:

I am a listener in the wet city.

I am a tender of films and flows.

I am a walker between roost and reservoir.

I am a human-shaped nerve ending in a vast mineral-animal-machine body.

I am not the master here.

I am the one with the valve key, the spore pouch, and the damp notebook. I am the one who knows that if the wall dries too much, the crust fails; if it floods too much, the film washes away; if the predators vanish, the grazers strip the stone; if the machines fall silent, some eggs never hatch. I am the one who tries not to make mistakes, because mistakes here do not simply break an object. They move through bodies.

And if the image finally becomes as itself as it can, then perhaps this is the truest answer:

I would find myself in the middle of the city, neither high above nor deep below, at a junction where water, heat, animal, and machine negotiate their continuance. I would be a conscious participant in that negotiation: small, wet, useful, attentive, and surrounded by lives that do not need me to be central in order to continue. I would be a caretaker of saturation. A keeper of the balance between too much and too little. A listener with dirty gloves and a patient heart.

Find me where the stone weeps, where the metal is warm, where the animals crowd the ledges in tiers like living tiles. I will be the figure in oilskin, barely visible against the wall, hand pressed to the surface, feeling for the pulse of the city.
