If I let the place choose for me, I do not think I would find myself in the brightest shafts, nor in the deepest cisterns. I would not be the first person up the ladders toward daylight, nor the last one down where the air tastes of cold mineral dark. I would be somewhere in the middle — in the thick of the settlement, where the town is most crowded, most negotiated, most alive with small interdependence.

You would find me in a wedge-shaped space between a fallen slab and the living wall, just above the fish pools and below the drying ledges. My room would not be large. It would be just wide enough for a sleeping shelf, a low work ledge, a rack of tools, and a narrow seat where I could listen without blocking anyone’s path. A reed curtain would hang at the opening, not to keep people out entirely, but to mark the boundary between shared sound and private rest. The floor would be raised a hand’s breadth above the stone, covered with woven mats turned often to keep them dry. Above me, a small clay cap would catch a single persistent drip, guiding it into a jar set into the wall so that it would fall with rhythm rather than accident.

That sound would be one of the first things anyone near me learned: a soft tick, then silence, then another soft tick. Not alarming. Not wasted. Heard.

I think I would be human there, but not in the way surface people might imagine a human living underground. I would not be a blind, pale creature of the dark, and I would not be a frightened exile hiding from the sun. I would be someone made exact by the place. My body would be compact and practical, my feet sure on broken stone, my shoulders used to climbing and carrying. My hands would be the most educated part of me. They would know the difference between damp that is only cold and damp that means movement somewhere above. They would know whether clay was drying too fast, whether rope had begun to stiffen, whether a timber was sweating from harmless condensation or from a slow leak that would rot it by degrees.

I would wear soft-soled shoes, because soft-soled shoes are courtesy and information both. I would move quietly not out of secrecy but because noise in such a place is a kind of spending, and I would have learned not to spend carelessly. My clothes would be layered, patched, practical: a hood or cap for grit and glare, a shoulder cape for chill, toggles and cords instead of fragile fastenings. There would likely be a stain of mineral dust in the creases of my fingers and a faint shine of oil on my palms, because tools must be cared for and stone leaves its mark on everyone who lives with it.

But if you asked what I was — not merely who, but what my function and nature would be in that settlement — I think I would be a listener. Not in a mystical sense, and not as someone who stands apart from labor. I would be one of those people who listen to the town itself: to stone, to water, to timber under strain, to air changing in a passage, to the small alterations that mean something is about to shift.

I would be a reader of small signs.

In the morning, if morning can be said to arrive underground, I would wake to the brightening of the higher shafts and begin with stillness. Before eating, before speaking, I would listen. The town would already be making its restrained sounds: water moving in channels, someone sweeping gravel, a basket being lowered, a child being hushed gently near a sleeping shelf. I would sort those sounds from the sounds I was looking for. A new drip where there had been none. A change in pitch where water struck stone. A creak in a bridge that yesterday had been silent. A faint hiss of air through a crack that had not breathed before.

Then I would go out with a little bag of clay tokens, a bone stylus, a coil of cord, a rag for oiling metal, and perhaps a small lamp that I used only when necessary. My first route would take me along the middle gallery where several drip-lines cross. I would press my fingers to the wall in certain places, not dramatically, but the way one checks the forehead of a sick child. I would look at the darkening of stone, the direction of mineral crust, the fine powder that gathers where a fracture has been working invisibly. I would mark any change with a token or a scratch. If a seep had shifted, I would trace it backward and forward until I understood where it wanted to go. If it threatened a sleeping alcove or a grain bin, I would not simply warn someone; I would call the right people. A water-tender if the issue was flow. A mason if the issue was stone. A timber-setter if the issue was support. A basket-maker if the issue was catchment. My role would not be to command all these works, but to notice early enough that the town could respond without crisis.

In such a place, early noticing is a form of love.

I would know the settlement through its sounds more than its appearances. A dry wall sounds different from a wall holding water behind it. A settled beam gives a different knock under the knuckles than one beginning to fail. A cistern nearly full breathes differently from one half empty. A passageway where air has grown still may mean a blocked vent, and a blocked vent may mean smoke later, or spoiled storage, or a fungal bed beginning to overheat. I would learn to distinguish harmless sounds from meaningful ones, and I would teach others to do the same. Children would follow me sometimes, not in a formal school, but in the way children learn underground: by being allowed to carry something small and being expected to pay attention.

I would teach them to say not “the wall is wet,” but “the wall has begun to wet above the third shelf.” I would teach them to say not “there is a noise,” but “the second bridge is speaking under load.” Precision would not be pedantry there. Precision would be care made audible.

People might call me a seep-reader, or a crack-watcher, or simply one of the listeners. I might not have a grand title. The town would be too practical for pomp. But I would be known. When someone said, “Ask the one at the middle bend,” or “Fetch the listener,” people would know whom they meant. They would not expect me to perform miracles. They would expect me to arrive, be quiet, touch things, ask questions, and then say something plain: “Move the grain two shelves up.” “Open this vent before evening.” “This prop needs a sister beam.” “That crack has not widened, but the stone above it has gone dull.”

I would be trusted because I would not exaggerate. In a settlement built on unstable ground, false alarm is almost as dangerous as silence. If I cried out over every drip, people would stop listening. If I stayed silent over a widening fracture, people could be harmed. So my value would lie in calibration: knowing the difference between a fact and a fear, between a change that can be watched and one that must be answered now.

My own dwelling would reflect that discipline. It would be spare but not barren. I would keep a map there, though not a map in the surface sense. It would be made of cord and knots and little clay tags, hung along the wall in sections. One cord for the lower pools. One for the middle crossings. One for the upper shafts. Knots for known cracks. Beads for cisterns. Loops for channels that overflow in storms. Scratches on the tags for dates or seasons. I would update it by touch as much as by sight, because in the dimness of my alcove, the map would need to be legible to fingers as well as eyes. Visitors might think it decorative. It would not be decorative. It would be memory made external.

I would also keep a row of small jars, each holding a sample of material from different parts of the town: clay from a new seam, dust from a crack that widened, crust from a stone that had begun to soften, ash from a hearth that had smoked badly. These would not be trophies. They would be evidence. Sometimes a change below is caused by something far above. A new grit in a seep may mean surface soil has washed into a fissure. A change in smell may mean compost has been misplaced. A different color in condensation may mean a mineral crust has broken somewhere and is leaching into water. I would not always know the cause immediately, but I would know that the cause existed, and I would preserve the clue until understanding caught up.

My social position would be odd in a gentle way. I would belong fully to no single craft, though I would need to understand many. The water-watchers would tolerate me because I respected their rules. The stone-readers would trust me if I did not romanticize their knowledge. The timber-setters would appreciate me if I called them before a beam was already bending. The fungal gardeners would want me near because a change in airflow can ruin their beds. The compost keepers would trust me if I did not flinch from their work. The children would like me because I would not shout and because I would give them small important tasks. The elders would approve of me because I would remember.

That might be my deepest role: remembering which sounds belong.

The town would need people who could remember not only events but patterns. Which drip quickens after a storm but settles again within a day. Which passage becomes cold before rain. Which wall sweats when many bodies gather nearby. Which cistern turns first if not skimmed. Which crack has been silent for years and which one only seems silent. In such a place, memory is infrastructure. It is as necessary as rope, as necessary as clay, as necessary as the careful grooves cut to guide water away from sleeping mats.

I would not be a ruler. I do not think I would want to be. The town would not need kings; it would need competent people willing to be answerable to consequences. But I might sit in councils when the matter concerned pattern, risk, or long-term change. I would speak low and briefly. I would say things like, “The upper crossing has breathed differently for three days,” or “The new light shaft is drying the clay seal faster than it should,” or “If we put another storage bin there, we will block the path of the night draft.” I would not try to win arguments. I would try to make the town’s own signals legible to those deciding what to do.

There would be days when my work felt almost invisible. Days when nothing changed. Days when I walked the rounds, touched the walls, listened at familiar places, and found only the ordinary persistence of the settlement: drips falling where they were meant to fall, channels carrying their small burdens, bridges holding, fungal beds breathing, children moving quietly through the lanes. On those days I might feel useless, or perhaps I would feel the particular peace of someone whose vigilance has not been needed in a dramatic way. In such a place, uneventfulness is not boredom. It is success.

Then there would be other days.

A storm above. The fissures breathing hard. The air changing pressure so quickly that ears pop in the lower chambers. Water arriving not as patience but as sudden threads. The town shifting at once: baskets lifted, mats rolled, low bedding moved, temporary gates opened. I would be everywhere and nowhere, moving fast but not loudly, checking the places I had marked as uncertain. A crack I had watched for a season might begin to sing faintly under load. A channel I had thought safe might fill too fast and overtop its lip. A new opening might appear in the ceiling, letting in a bright blade of daylight and a cascade of wet debris. In that moment, fear would be present, but it would not be allowed to become noise. Fear would become procedure. Hands would move. Voices would stay low. Someone would catch a rope. Someone else would steady a ladder. I would press my ear to a wall and then say the only sentence that mattered: “Clear this shelf.”

Afterward, when the pulse passed and the town exhaled, I would be the one to go back and look at what had changed. Where did the water go? What did it loosen? What did it deposit? What now needs drying, bracing, cleaning, or gratitude? Because even a dangerous flood can teach something. Even a collapse can reveal a structure in the stone that had been hidden. The settlement would not treat such events as pure catastrophe if it could help it. It would treat them as harsh instruction.

I think I would love the place in a way that is difficult to explain to someone who values open horizons. I would not love it as a beautiful view. I would love it as a body loves a rhythm. I would know the exact angle at which to turn sideways in a certain passage. I would know which stone is warmest at midday, which ladder creaks but remains sound, which alcove smells of dried reeds, which cistern has a fish that rises when footsteps approach. I would know where the quiet is deepest and where the air moves fastest. I would know the town by touch as much as sight, and that knowledge would be intimate in the way all bodily knowledge is intimate.

My pleasures would be small and exact. A dry pair of shoes after long work. A meal eaten sitting down. A lamp lit safely in its clay niche. A child correctly identifying a dangerous drip before being told. A channel cleaned so well that water runs without hesitation. A crack marked weeks ago that has not widened. A bird entering a shaft above, its call falling through the settlement like a brief, impossible bell. I would enjoy those things not because they were rare luxuries but because they were earned through attention.

I would also have fears, but they would be specific. Not fear of darkness. Not fear of enclosure. Not fear of the weight above. Those fears would have been trained out or trained through. My fear would be negligence. My fear would be assuming that yesterday’s safety meant today’s. My fear would be hearing something unfamiliar and deciding it did not matter because I was tired, or proud, or distracted. In that place, arrogance is structural danger. A person who refuses to listen becomes a crack in the social stone.

If I were to be something less human there — if the question allows me to become not a person but a presence — then I think I would be the town’s habit of noticing. I would be the pause before a foot is set down. The hand that brushes a support in passing. The ear turned toward a wall when conversation stops. The instinct that says: wait, something is different. In that sense, I would not be a single body at all. I would be the distributed attention that keeps a crowded underground settlement from destroying itself by small neglect.

But if I must have a body — and I think I should, because such a place teaches that care must be embodied to be trustworthy — then let me be the listener in the middle levels. Let me have calloused knees, a quiet voice, and a memory full of drips. Let me carry a bone stylus and a pouch of clay tokens. Let me know the names of moisture in several precise terms. Let me be the one people call when a sound has changed but they cannot say how.

You would find me near the junction where the middle walkway crosses a shallow channel cut into the stone. There would be a rope bridge overhead, a drying rack to one side, and a narrow stair descending toward the fish pools. A pale beam of daylight would reach that place only near midday, and even then it would be gray-white, cool, ordinary. It would fall on my work ledge for a short while, illuminating the cord map, the jars of dust and crust, the small clay tokens lined by meaning.

If you came looking for me, you would not hear me first. You would hear the town around me: the drip into the jar, the faint rush of water in the channel, the creak of rope, the muted footfalls on matting. Then you would see me sitting with one hand against the wall, head slightly tilted, eyes half-closed, listening.

And if you asked what I was doing, I would probably answer very softly:

“I am remembering which sounds belong.”
