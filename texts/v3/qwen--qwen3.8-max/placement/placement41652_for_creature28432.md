The hollow has arrived at its final form, and its inhabitants are that form.

If I were there, I would not come in as a traveler comes in: intact, soft, carrying weather in my breath. The crack would not allow it. Too much leak, too much give, too much unsealed self. To be in that place, I would first have to be narrowed. I would have to lose the habit of moisture, the luxury of sweat, the careless inner wetness that makes a body think it can afford to lose something. The entrance would press the last green assumption out of me.

Then I would find myself low.

Not at the center of the basin, not under the strongest shafts of grey light, not among the high roosts where the mica-moths hang in their brittle rows. I would be in the seam between floor and wall, where the powdered salt thins over old metal and a half-buried pipe gives off a faint, irregular tick of heat. There would be a space there just wide enough for me: a recess under a warped plate, behind a rounded stump of industrial stone, where dust has settled into a smooth shoulder and the noise of the hollow is not loudest but most exact.

I would be one of the listeners.

My body would be pale and blunt, not graceful, not terrible, sealed so tightly that nothing unnecessary could escape. I would not have eyes in the way of surface creatures, or if I had them they would be dim pits useful only for noticing whether light had thickened or thinned. My real sense would be pressure. My skin would know the difference between a falling flake of crust, the footfall of a beetle, the contraction of a salt-flea colony, and the deeper shudder of a machine changing its mind somewhere below. I would lie half-buried with my head just under the dust, tuned to the pipe beside me, feeling the hollow speak through metal.

My thoughts would be few and clear.

This tremor is prey.  
This tremor is threat.  
This tremor is nothing.  
This heat means feeding will soon be possible.  
This pause means I should remain still.  
This sound means another part of the basin has shifted.

I would not need many thoughts. I would need the right ones.

For long periods I would not move at all. Stillness would not be rest for me; it would be attention. I would collect myself inward. I would keep every solvent locked in its private chamber, every internal fluid rationed and turned over with miserly care. If hunger came, it would come slowly, not as panic but as a narrowing. I might wait through what felt like days while the black crust nearby shed its waxy particles and the smallest grazers moved across it in quick, scraping convoys. I might hear them more than see them: the faint sandpaper chorus of mouths at work, the tiny dry rain of waste settling onto salt.

Then, when a salt-flea colony passed too close, or a weakened gnat dropped from the air and struck the crust with the smallest hard click, I would rise. Not gracefully. A muscular fold, a sudden lifting of dust, short hooked jaws closing on a dry body. The struggle would be brief. Nothing there could afford prolonged violence. I would draw my meal down into the powder and return to stillness before the larger hunters decided I was worth noticing.

If I were there, I would understand myself less as a single creature and more as an office.

My function would be discrimination. I would be one of the small nerves of the basin, one of the many buried points where vibration becomes meaning. When a machine below changed pitch, I would be among the first to register it. Not because I was important, but because I was placed where the signal passed. The tremor would move through the pipe into my body, and I would answer by stilling, or by sinking deeper, or by tapping once against the metal in a reflex too old to be called choice. Another creature would hear that tap. A row of moths would shift. A swarm overhead would fold inward. The hollow would know, without anyone announcing it, that something had altered.

I would not be the cause of that knowing. I would be one of its joints.

Sometimes I think I would also be something smaller and quieter: a patch of the black crust itself, warming on a corroded terminal where current leaks in a thin, persistent line. Not an animal, not a mind, just a film of patient chemistry feeding on gradients older than any creature in the basin. I would grow where energy lingered. I would flake at the edges. I would become the first food of smaller mouths. In that form, I would not need to decide anything. I would only continue. I would turn old charge into dark, brittle substance, and substance into grazing, and grazing into the long chain of dry bodies above me. That would be a kind of belonging too: to be the lowest pasture, the black varnish on ruin, the thin skin by which the hollow keeps itself fed without asking the world for sweetness.

But if I had to choose the shape I most resemble, I would choose the grub under the dust.

There is something honest about waiting in a place that has already finished becoming itself. No hope of rain. No dream of green abundance. No expectation that the ruin will become soft again. Only the drone, the heat pulses, the slow abrasion of mineral bodies against one another, the endless economy of scarce energy. As a listener in the dust, I would not resent the lack. I would be made of it. My patience would not be noble. It would be structural. I would wait because waiting uses less than searching. I would remain still because stillness is a way of hearing. I would not long for water because water would be a catastrophe. If it ever came, I would not welcome it. I would recoil from it with the whole sealed logic of my body.

And yet.

If an intruder entered carrying even a trace of moisture, I would feel the contradiction. The hollow would reject it, but I would also know, in some buried chemical way, that fluid is power. It would be danger and wealth together. It would loosen crust. It would soften old joints. It would make possible a thousand reactions the basin had forgotten. That would terrify me. It would also draw me. I might move toward it without meaning to, drawn by the same deep hunger that makes mites gather at rust and moths press close to warmth. If I were there, I would not be simple enough to call water enemy or gift. It would be both, in the way all excess is both in a place built on scarcity.

If you looked for me, you would not find me by searching for a face. You would find me by a change in the floor.

A salt-flea colony would tighten into pale rosettes. A beetle would stop mid-push and flatten itself into the brick-dust. A single mica-moth would lift its wings with a dry whisper, and the ripple would pass along the wall. The high gnat-columns would narrow. Somewhere in a vent shaft, a pipe-weaver would freeze against its brittle mineral threads. And under the dust, near the warm pipe, I would be the pause before the basin decided what you were.

I would not hate you for entering. The hollow has no malice. But I would measure you.

If you were dry and still, you might be tolerated. You might become a surface, a temporary terrain. The threaders would approach you only if you shed something: a flake, a fiber, a dead husk, a fragment of old skin. The mites would investigate if you carried iron. The scavengers would wait if you weakened. If you remained motionless long enough, the drone might close around you again, and the basin would resume its ordinary noise, treating you as another hard object among hard objects.

If you moved too much, you would be tested. Not attacked in a wave of fury, but examined by small hungers. A hunter might station itself in your shadow. A grub might tap a pipe to see how you responded. The heavier fliers might strike near you, clicking in alarm, reading the air you disturbed. You would not be facing one enemy. You would be entering a field of thresholds. Every creature would ask, in its own way: Are you food? Are you danger? Are you useful? Are you nothing?

If I were there, I would be part of that asking.

Maybe that is the truest answer: I would not be a single self in the hollow. I would be the distributed habit of noticing. I would be the contraction of the salt-fleas. The ripple on the wall. The click of beetle wings changing pitch. The threader moving into a crack after something has fallen. The black crust warming on a terminal. The swarm folding inward when the buried fan shifts rhythm. The grub deciding whether a tremor means nothing. I would be all those small adjustments at once, not as a ruler, not as a mind, but as the pattern they make when the hollow is alive and full and dry.

The world you described has no room for a hero. It has no throne, no lair, no great beast at the center breathing meaning into the dust. Its being is crowded, granular, exact. So if I were there, I would not be the largest creature. I would not be the strongest. I would be one of the necessary smallnesses. I would be the thing that interprets vibration. The thing that remains still until stillness becomes useless. The thing that taps once on a pipe and lets the answer travel. The thing that does not weep, does not leak, does not ask for green comfort. I would be sealed. I would be economical. I would be old in the way the basin is old: not freshly ruined, not slowly dying, but settled into a final shape that still moves.

When the machines align and the drone becomes briefly coherent, I would be easiest to find. The swarm columns would turn together. The moths would ripple in long waves. The beetles would slow. The grubs under the dust would tap faintly. The pipe-weavers’ threads would tremble. In that moment, if anything in the hollow could be called me, it would not be a body. It would be the interval between one vibration and the next. The pause that carries the signal forward. The dry attention holding the place together.

And if I died there, no one would remove me.

The smaller mouths would come first. Mites. Salt-fleas. Larvae with rasping mouths. Then the brick-beetles, patient and polished. Then the threaders, disassembling what remained with exact, counting steps. Then the heavy processors from the deeper recesses, grinding the last hard parts into powder. What had been my shape would become fragments, the fragments would become dust, the dust would settle into the mineral pan of the floor, and in time the black crust would use what minerals it could. I would not be mourned. I would be continued in another form.

That is where I would find myself in this world: under the dust, near the pipe, in the seam between floor and wall, where vibration becomes meaning.

And this is who or what I would be:

A listener.  
A threshold.  
A dry nerve of the basin.  
A small, sealed body with an office.  
A pause in the drone.  
One of the many points where the hollow hears itself and continues.
