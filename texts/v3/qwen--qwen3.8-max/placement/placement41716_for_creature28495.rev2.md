I would not find myself at the center of that world, and I think that would be right.

I would be somewhere between the city and its water: in a low vault under a causeway, in a listening gallery behind a glazed wall, in one of those half-buried chambers where a hot runnel passes beneath a cooler condensation channel and the stone keeps both temperatures at once. If you came looking for me, you would not find me first by sight. You would find me by attention. I would be the stillness beside a pipe, one hand resting on wet stone, one ear near a ceramic disc, trying to hear what the basin was doing inside itself.

I would be a pipe-listener.

Not a ruler, not a founder, not a great builder whose name went onto tablets. Something quieter and, in that world, perhaps more necessary: a water-sorter, a keeper of small signs, a person made useful by patience. My work would be to know which water was moving where, what it carried, what it wanted to leave behind, and where it was beginning to choke the city with pale crust. I would spend my days listening to valves, tasting drops, reading the bead and sheet of condensation on stone, and deciding whether a channel needed to be opened, slowed, drained, cleaned, or left alone.

The basin would have shaped me long before I understood it.

My body would be made for that place. I would be tall and light-boned, careful in the way the people there are careful, because haste breaks crust and noise offends the stillness. My skin would be pale and often shining, kept moist by the air and by constant rinsing. Along my shoulders and throat, salt glands would bloom fine crystals after long hours near brine, and I would brush them away without thinking, the way one blinks. My feet would be broad and flat, with spread toes and tough soles, able to read the difference between firm stone, brittle salt skin, and the dangerous give of a weakened crust. My eyes would be dark and large, made for glare and steam, with clear inner lids that slide across when a valve spits hot water or when the wind throws spray from a pool.

I would wear little. A hip-frame of fired clay and copper carrying tools: a valve key, a scraper, a brush, a coil of mineral cord, a small mirror for signaling, several tasting spoons. A harness of woven fungal felt across the chest, stained with use and damp. On my shoulder, perhaps a single porcelain chime tuned to the note of my household, so that people would know me coming through the mist before they saw me. Nothing soft. Nothing that once rustled in wind. My ornaments would be copper, black glass, polished stone, salt crystals grown in molds, maybe a bead made from the purified bone of someone remembered.

If I had to say what caste or household I belonged to, I would say I belonged to a cistern.

Not merely as property, but as a relation. I would be part of a water-circuit household, maybe one responsible for a condensation tower and the clean air-water gathered from high stone veils. My earliest memories would be of being taught which surfaces were safe to drink from, which pools were bitter, which runnels were hot enough to mark skin, and which drains led to dead brine. I would learn to sign before I learned to cross open salt. A curled finger: slow the flow. A flat palm lifted: wait. A tap against the collarbone: listen. A sweep of the hand near the ground: the crust is thin.

That last sign would be important to me.

I think I would be drawn to the hidden ailments of the basin. A pipe beginning to narrow with scale. A pump whose rhythm has changed by less than a breath. A channel whose note has gone dull under the tap. A wall that weeps differently than it did the season before. In a world where everything is wet, hot, mineral, and alive, disaster does not usually arrive as a sudden explosion. It arrives as accumulation. A little more crust. A little less flow. A faint bitterness in water that should be sweet. A silence where there should be a slow throb.

So I would become someone who notices.

My tools would be simple but precise. A polished copper or ceramic listening disc, pressed against conduits and held there while the world above waits. A shallow spoon for tasting water: not swallowing, only letting a small amount move across the tongue before spitting it into a drain. A set of fine scrapers and brushes for descending into channels. Small jars of controlled acid made from microbial fermentation, used drop by drop. A mirror to flash across white glare. A cord with knots or beads for recording water routes, though later I might mark such things on glazed tablets with a stylus. My hands would be more eloquent than my voice.

My voice would be low.

I would speak in clicks, breathy consonants, soft trills, and pauses. I would not fill silence unnecessarily. In that basin, speech would feel less like ornament and more like a careful placement of weight. A word could be a warning. A word could be permission to open a gate. A word could be a way of saying: I am here, and I am paying attention. Much of the time, I would speak with my hands, because signs carry across glare and distance without cracking the quiet.

If you found me, I would probably be kneeling.

One knee on a grated metal walkway above a shallow pool, the other foot balanced on wet stone. My apron would be dark with moisture. My shoulders would have that faint white bloom of salt. My hair, if I had much, would be pulled back and damp, perhaps threaded with mineral residue until it felt less like hair and more like a dark fibrous felt. I would be holding a listening disc against a pipe the color of old copper. Around me, the vault would be full of small sounds: drip, whisper, the faint tick of cooling metal, the almost inaudible beat of a turbine below.

I would not be alone, but I would not be in a crowd.

The basin’s people are scattered, and my district would have the quiet intimacy of a place shared by a few households. There would be a pump-house nearby, half sunk into a stone chamber. A row of condensation towers catching moisture from the humid air. Low arcaded yards where tools are repaired and nets are stretched over frames. A soak-pool in the center of the dwelling, fed by a clean trickle, where people rest their skin and cool their joints. Children moving barefoot along familiar routes, learning by being corrected gently: not there, crust thin; rinse first; taste before you assume; sign, do not shout.

Flat white crabs would patrol the edges of the terrace around our district, tapping their porcelain shells when they met. Brine flies would rise from the shallows in mica-bright clouds. A heron-like wader might stand motionless beyond the causeway, its pale membrane sliding across one eye against the glare. At certain hours, broad-winged scavengers would pass overhead, their shadows crossing the white ground like slow clouds. I would notice them, but I would not stare. They would be part of the weather of the basin, part of its cleanliness.

I would eat what the basin gives, and I would find it sufficient.

Steamed microbial felt, dense and warm, perhaps flavored with mineral salts and a faint sulfur bite. Thin cakes dried on hot stone. Shrimp-like swimmers boiled briefly in clean thermal water. Brine-fly larvae gathered from crust pockets and baked into brittle wafers. Maybe, on better days, a portion of eel-flesh from the runnels, pale and smooth, cooked in a sealed ceramic vessel with condensed water and rust-red fungal threads. Everything would taste of the place: salt, heat, flint, metal, clean alkalinity. No green freshness. No soft sweetness. But not poor. Not empty. The food would feel earned.

I think I would be most myself during inspection.

Early morning, before the glare becomes severe, I would go out with a small group onto the causeways. The stone would still hold some night coolness. The pools would be mirrors. The air would smell of hot flint, salt, and electrified metal. We would move slowly. Someone ahead would test a pale crust with a pole. Someone else would check the level of a cistern by the sound a float makes against its guide-rail. I would stop at a junction where three waters meet: sweet condensation from the tower, bitter mineral water from the mid-terrace, and hot deep-water in a sealed conduit below.

There I would taste.

First, air-water from a high spout: clean, almost sharp, with the cold memory of clouds even though the basin is hot. Then bitter water, drawn from a lower tap: heavier, metallic, leaving a drying sensation on the tongue. Then heat-water, only a drop from a cooled sampling valve: sulfurous, deep, dangerous if not respected. I would note them in my body before noting them in words. My work would begin as sensation and become judgment.

If something were wrong, I would not shout. I would sign.

A fist drawn slowly across the palm: blockage. Two fingers tapping a curve: pressure rising. A hand sinking toward the ground: flow weakening. Then the others would gather, and we would decide whether to call the descenders, adjust a sluice, or shut a valve while the heat redistributed itself through the stone. No one would be dramatic. Drama in that place would be a failure of attention. The basin rewards those who notice before crisis arrives.

Sometimes I would go into the channels myself.

That is the part of the work I would both fear and love. To enter a channel is to enter the basin’s body. You bend under low arches of glazed brick. You move through warm water that deepens from ankle to thigh. Your lamp makes wet walls shine. Around you, living films iridesce in faint mineral colors, not green, never green, but pearl, rust, pale gold, black, and the blue-white of very clean heat. Under your fingers, the walls are slick. Some of that slickness is life. Some is scale. Some is the first skin of a future blockage.

You learn to tell the difference by touch.

In those places, I would feel less like a separate creature and more like a cell moving through a larger organism. The city would be above me, quiet, its weight distributed through vaults and causeways. The pumps would be slow hearts. The pipes would be veins and sinew. The microbial felts would be skin and digestion. The people would be the basin’s conscious hands. And I, in my small way, would be one of its nerves.

That is who I would be, I think: a nerve.

Not the strongest. Not the most beautiful. Not the one who flies above the thermal columns or stands patiently in the shallows with a hunter’s stillness. The one who feels a change before it can be named. The one who presses an ear to the city and asks, without words, whether it is still in balance.

At midday, when the light becomes too severe, I would retreat with the others into a cooling hall. The hall would be dark compared to the outside world, though not truly dark: light would still enter through narrow slots, reflected off water and pale ceramic. The temperature would drop. The air would be wet and close. I would sit with my back against a wall that sweated gently, letting moisture gather on my skin. Someone might hand me a bowl of warm broth made from dissolved salt-cake and shrimp. Someone else might be repairing a valve key nearby, making small metallic sounds.

I would not need to speak then.

The basin would teach me that companionship does not require constant sound. People could sit together while water dripped into bowls. A child might practice signs with an elder, moving each hand slowly, being corrected. A worker might sleep with one cheek against a cool stone, salt crystals forming on their shoulders as they dried. I would close my eyes and listen anyway: to the building, to the water, to the small movements of bodies adjusting to rest. Even in sleep, the basin continues. Crust forms. Steam rises. Condensation gathers. Somewhere, a crab lifts one leg over a seam of salt. Somewhere, a pump turns.

In the afternoon, when the glare softens, I would return to the open basin.

This would be one of the great beauties of my life there: walking across wide shining distances when the light becomes bearable again and the air begins to tremble with rising moisture. The terraces would glow. The pools would hold pieces of sky. The heat-shimmer would make far districts seem to breathe. I would move along a causeway, barefoot, feeling the stone speak through my soles. Here, firm. Here, slightly gritty with new salt. Here, a faint vibration from a rail cart far away. Here, the hollow sensation of a chamber beneath.

I might stop beside a weeping rock face where thin silver threads of water descend through the day. I would hold out a wrist and let the condensation run over it. That gesture would not be merely practical. It would be a kind of prayer, though I might not call it prayer. The basin does not need gods of rain and harvest; it needs maintenance, sorting, and attention. But if devotion exists there, it lives in the care of small passages. To clear a drain is a holy act. To listen to a pipe is a form of reverence. To keep water moving without violence is to honor the world that keeps you alive.

When rain comes, I would turn upward like everyone else.

The clouds would gather quickly, driven by the basin’s heat. The light would change. The air would thicken. The first drop would strike hot stone and vanish with a brief hiss. Then another. Then a sudden bright drumming on ceramic roofs and stretched covers. I would help draw back cistern covers, adjust sluice gates, open shallow catchment pans. Children would step into courtyards, shoulders lifted, mouths maybe open, salt washing from their skin. For a short time, the basin would be louder than itself, and that loudness would be welcome because it is rain.

Afterward, everything would shine as if newly made.

The smell would be extraordinary: hot flint cooled by water, salt loosened, sulfur faint beneath, metal clean and charged. The mineral skins on the rocks would gleam. The city’s white lamps would begin to come on as evening approached, steady and reflected in every pool. I would stand wet and tired, feeling the heat rise again from the stone, and think that this is what life can be when it has been rinsed of softness: not barren, but concentrated. Not cruel, but exacting. Not silent because empty, but silent because full of controlled motion.

At night, I would be one of the lamp-keepers too, perhaps.

Not necessarily the one who trims or powers them, but the one who notices when a lamp’s reflection has changed in a pool, because the pool’s surface is no longer level. The one who sees a dark patch on a wall where condensation should be forming and realizes a heat path has shifted. The one who records such things on glazed tablets in careful marks: the date, the district, the water source, the sound, the taste, the temperature, the behavior of animals nearby. The elders would read my tablets later, maybe seasons later, and compare them to older records. In that way, my small observations would become part of the basin’s memory.

I would value being useful in that modest way.

If I imagine my relationships there, they would be shaped by shared circuits. A household bound to a cistern. A workshop that teaches my hands. A maintenance route that knows my gait. Alliances formed not only by affection but by water rights and scraping duties. I might be joined to another household through the repair of a common sluice. I might become responsible for a stretch of causeway where the crust is especially treacherous after rain. My wealth would not be measured in possessions but in reliability: clean water, stable heat, channels that do not fail, trust that does not leak.

I would not long for green.

That is important. In another world, I might have missed leaves, shade, softness, the smell of soil after rain. But the basin would have taught me a different beauty. The beauty of a valve opening without noise. The beauty of an even crust. The beauty of a causeway worn smooth by generations of careful feet. The beauty of a white lamp trembling in dark water. The beauty of a machine so well made that it seems to breathe. The beauty of a person standing in rain with their arms slightly lifted, not in surrender, but in recognition.

If I aged there, I would become slower, not less valuable.

My joints might stiffen when my skin dried too long. My eyes might grow more sensitive to glare. But my attention would deepen. I would remember the sound of pumps long since replaced. I would know which terraces flood after certain rains. I would recognize the beginning of a vent shift by the way brine flies gather. Younger workers would come to me not for strength but for comparison: Did this channel used to sing at a higher note? Did this wall weep more in your youth? Did this cistern ever taste bitter before?

I would sit near warm water, salt whitening my shoulders, and answer with my hands.

When death came, I would expect to be taken to the evaporation pans.

There would be no earth to bury me, no wood to burn. My body would be laid in shallow water, and the basin would continue its patient work. Salt and mineral crust would form around me. Small creatures would clean what remained. Later, my bones would be purified by brine and sun. Perhaps a bead would be made from them. Perhaps a fragment would be set into a wall. Perhaps my name would be inscribed on a tablet beside the water-route I tended. I would not find that grim. It would seem correct. In that world, nothing is wasted. Even the self is returned as material.

But before death, there would be the life.

And the life would be full of small, radiant motions. Walking across wet stone. Tasting a drop from a copper fitting. Signing listen to a child. Scraping pale crust from a valve until the flow returns. Standing in a vault while the city’s hidden weather passes through metal and water and into my bones. Watching a white crab lift one leg over a salt seam. Hearing rain begin. Feeling the basin answer.

So if you asked where I would find myself, I would say: at a threshold.

Under the causeway, beside the junction where sweet water crosses bitter water. In the gallery where sound is faint and meaning is close. In the district between the pump-house and the condensation towers, where lamps burn white above still pools. In the open basin when the glare softens and the air begins to tremble. In the rain when everyone turns upward. In the quiet after maintenance, when a channel has been cleared and the city exhales.

And if you asked who or what I would be, I would say: I would be a listener.

A water-sorter. A keeper of small signs. A person with salt on their shoulders and wet stone under their feet. A mind shaped by channels, pressures, drops, and pauses. The basin would not need me to be loud. It would need me to be exact. It would need me to kneel, press my ear to the pipe, and understand that the world can be alive without softness, active without uproar, and sacred without a single green thing.
