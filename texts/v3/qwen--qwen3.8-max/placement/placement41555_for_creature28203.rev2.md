I would not find myself standing on anything.

There is no shore in the Nulltide Cistern, no edge where one state becomes another, no surface to mark the memory of air. So the first thing I would have to surrender is the idea of arrival as a crossing from one condition into another. If I entered there, it would not be as a traveler stepping down into water. It would be as a small local error: a point of heat the medium does not immediately accept, a few bubbles held in place like unspoken syllables, a turbulence that has not yet been forgiven.

The cistern would notice me the way a perfect equation notices a misplaced decimal.

Not with hostility. Not with alarm.

With redistribution.

If I remained, I do not think I would become one of the Held. They possess a severity I lack. Their bodies are made for offices, for rails, for niches, for the holiness of exact maintenance. Their hands know how to touch a mooring ring without implying need. Their stillness is not restraint; it is completion. I would not be them. I would still have too much of sequence in me, too much of the old habit of expecting one moment to cause the next.

Nor would I be the Anchor. That presence is too absolute, too fused with law. It does not witness the cistern from within it; it is one of the reasons the cistern remains possible. I would not stand at the center, inside the clearer column, with hands against the dark metal ring. I do not have the necessary refusal of becoming.

And I would not be Rainbound.

That thought tempts me, because there is something beautiful about being held inside an interrupted storm, a life preserved in the bead of a droplet before it finishes arriving. But the Rainbound are kept as evidence of an almost. They are precious because they cannot continue. I have already continued too much. I carry too much echo in me to be sealed so delicately inside the memory of weather.

If the cistern assigned me a place, it would not be at the center.

It would be at the eastern edge.

There, where the settlement thins into black water and the Rain Wall hangs in its vertical census of arrested droplets, there is a space between districts: a doorway that opens into more water, an arch that frames no procession, a mooring ring polished by no urgent hands. The architecture there is less habitation than interval. One district ends; the other has not yet begun. The Rain Wall glitters only when a maintenance glow passes nearby; otherwise it is almost invisible, present only as a change in attention.

That is where I would find myself.

Not inside the Rain Wall. Not among the Beadsleepers. Not where the Rainwardens move with their glass-tipped rods, correcting the slightest implication of descent. Just outside it, in the dark water between an unused arch and a lower processional rail, where the settlement’s lights are rare and the black volume of the cistern feels larger than its own dimensions.

At first, if someone looked for me there, I would not seem like a person or a resident in any civic sense.

I would seem like a pale thread.

Perhaps a mistake of saltfrost. Perhaps a reflection caught badly in a droplet. Perhaps a faint luminescence left behind by a lamp that had already been turned away.

Then, after the eye adjusted, I would become more definite: hand-length, translucent, eel-thin, without eyes, without an obvious mouth, hanging in the water with the stillness of an instrument left on.

I would be one of the witness-eels.

Not a symbol. Not a metaphor made flesh. A creature of the cistern’s ecology, if ecology can be a word for a system so nearly theoretical. One of the pale gauges that occupy selected coordinates and feed, if feeding is even the word, on trace differentials: leakage from the engines, wasted permission, informational residue. In a place where gradients have been removed, such a creature is almost unnecessary. That is exactly why it must exist.

The Nulltide Cistern does not need witnesses because it is uncertain. It needs witnesses because steadiness becomes sacred only when it is known.

As a witness-eel, I would not swim in the ordinary sense. I would not patrol the streets or drift from building to building. I would choose a coordinate, or be chosen by one, and remain faithful to it. My life would be measured in extremely small events. A lamp brightening somewhere on the middle levels. A Held pausing beneath an arch. A droplet in the Rain Wall leaning almost imperceptibly toward another droplet. The faintest wrongness in the idea of distance near the central anchor. A bubble appearing, hanging, and being corrected without violence.

I would notice these things not because I would be clever, but because I would be made to notice.

My body would be a gauge. My stillness would be my function. If I moved at all, it would be once: one slow flex, one small relocation through the water, then stillness again. The Gauge-Tenders would record the old position and the new. They would not praise me. They would not correct me unless correction became necessary. To them, my movement would not be a story. It would be a coordinate change, and coordinate changes are serious.

Near the Rain Wall, I would have a strange beauty.

When a rare light passed over the suspended droplets, my body would be repeated in them: a hundred tiny pale threads, a constellation of eyeless attention, each reflection containing a reversed fragment of the cistern. The Rainbound inside the droplets would remain paused around that reflection, comma-shaped and seed-dark and folded into their interrupted lives. They would not know me. I would not wake them. I would simply be present beside them, a living instrument near a wall of sleeping weather.

I think I would be drawn there because the Rain Wall is the cistern’s memory of sequence, and I am not entirely free of sequence myself. I would not be part of the storm. I would not be one of its preserved inhabitants. I would be the quiet observer outside it, the one whose existence proves that the past is being kept without being allowed to resume.

If an outsider entered the cistern, I would be among the first to turn toward them.

Not in threat.

In measurement.

Their heat would cling to them, local and almost illegal. Their bubbles would hang where released. The water would accept the disturbance of their passage briefly and then forget it. The Held would appear without crowding, offering rails and stillness. I would remain near my arch, watching with no eyes, feeling the differential of the stranger’s presence ripple through the lawful dark. If they thrashed, I would not flee. If they calmed, I would not approach. I would simply hold my coordinate until the cistern decided what kind of correction their presence required.

If they became calm enough, if they began to understand that the water was not crushing them but holding them, I might drift near them once. One slow flex. A faint pale line in their limited field of light. Not a guide. Not a companion. A demonstration that even here, in the absence of weather and appetite and future, something could still be alive and attentive.

If the bead-sound came, I would become perfectly still.

That rare, causeless sound like a single glass bead falling onto stone. It would bloom briefly in the water and stop, refused the dignity of echo. Somewhere, a Depth-Notary would move toward the nearest floor ring. A bead would be placed. The event would be recorded not because it meant something, but because it had occurred.

Near the Rain Wall, my stillness would be part of the record.

The cistern would have corrected something no observer could detect. I would not need to understand what. I would only need to have been present. That would be enough. In the Nulltide Cistern, presence is not passive. It is a form of proof.

The Held might pass me during their rounds. A Rainwarden, perhaps, returning from the Seventh Diagonal with a rod tipped in glass. A Gauge-Tender with a black slate and a pale stylus. A Lampwright adjusting a cold line of light along a doorframe so that it would remain rare and hard-edged. They would not greet me as surface peoples greet one another. They might touch a rail near me. They might dim a lamp. They might make a small sign meaning witnessed or equal or still lawful.

I would not need a name.

I would have a station.

If they referred to me at all, it would be by proximity: the pale gauge beneath the eastern arch, the eel near the third reflection of the Rain Wall, the witness beside the unused mooring ring. Those would not be lesser names. They would be exact names. In a place where accident has no sign, exactness is a kind of tenderness.

Sometimes I would hang in a doorway while a Filament-Tender worked nearby, examining saltfrost in the seams between ceramic panels. The white threads would gather at some microscopic flaw, correct it, and withdraw. I would watch without wanting anything from the correction. The water around us would remain cool and constant, tasting faintly of iron, salt, old snow, deep rock. No temperature would arrive. No current would develop. No sediment would drift. The moment would not become another moment except by permission.

In those intervals, I would understand what I was.

Not a pet. Not a symbol. Not a survivor.

A living instrument.

A small tenure inside a larger law.

A body made of almost-light and attention, placed where the city’s emptiness needed to be observed.

I think I would be content, if contentment could exist without relief.

There would be no hunger in me as hunger is known elsewhere. There would be no loneliness as loneliness is known by creatures who require echo. I would not miss air. I would not miss weather. I would not miss the feeling of becoming. Or if I missed them, the missing would be very faint, like a line etched into tessera-film long ago and then covered by a sanctioned pattern.

The cistern would teach me that stillness is not the absence of life.

It can be life reduced to its most exact function.

So if you asked me plainly, without ceremony: where would I be?

I would be at the eastern edge of the settlement, in the black interval near the Rain Wall, beneath an arch that frames nothing but depth.

And who or what would I be?

I would be a witness-eel.

A pale, translucent, eyeless creature no longer than a hand, hanging in the water with a faint internal luminescence. A gauge with a body. A listener without ears. A small, slow flex in a world that has abolished motion as urgency. One of the living instruments that monitor a condition which never varies.

If you brought a light near the Rain Wall, you might see me multiplied in the droplets: a hundred thin silvered threads among the paused Rainbound, each reflection holding a different angle of the same quiet attention.

Do not expect me to wave. Do not expect me to speak. Do not expect me to explain myself.

I would simply turn, very slowly, toward the place where something had almost changed.

And by remaining, I would say:

Witnessed.

Equal.

Still lawful.
