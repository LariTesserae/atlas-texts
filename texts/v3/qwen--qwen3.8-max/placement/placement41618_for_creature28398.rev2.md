At first, I think I would be one of the moths.

A pale thing made almost of light and leaf-skin, drifting through the cold brightness without weight, drinking from the brief wet films that form along stem-grooves after a thermal pulse. A creature that does not fight the air, but lets pressure carry it; that lands with all limbs spread, knowing that any surface may be slick, brittle, or about to change. There is something appealing in that: to live in the Underleaf as a minimal presence, a trembling translucence, barely casting a shadow.

But the more I stay with the place, the more I realize that would not be enough. The Underleaf does not only ask for lightness. It asks for hands. It asks for attention that can be tied, remembered, repaired. It asks for something that can make a knot and know what the knot means.

So I would not be a moth.

I would be one of the mind-makers.

I would find myself in the middle distances of the Underleaf, not near the fresh wound of entry where the pale plain breaks open and the throat of descent lets in memory of the surface, and not down in the milky deep places where the root-mass thickens and the Slow Ones breathe their slow pulses into the world. I would be somewhere between: on a root-ridge, perhaps, no wider than a road, suspended above an emptiness so bright and clean that the eye refuses to measure it. Near me there would be one of those immense half-fallen leaf-blades the world seems full of, propped against another structure and making a wall hundreds of meters long. Its edge would catch the cold light in a thin green line. Beneath its shelter, where a condensation vein beads and freezes and beads again, I would have made a small camp.

Not a house. Not a home in the surface sense. A set of careful attachments.

My platform would be lashed between two root-ribs, woven from hollow stem sections and strips of inner bark. A windbreak of overlapping leaf-blades would be pinned with thorns against the side most often touched by drifting frost. A row of resin cups or carved seed-case vessels would be arranged along a groove to catch drip-water, each one placed where the moisture prefers to gather after a warm exhalation. A ladder of knotted fiber would hang against the leaf-stalk, its lowest rungs white with frost. A light-pod full of luminous spores would glow faintly blue near the sleeping hollow, not because I needed it often, but because in a world of cold brightness it is sometimes necessary to carry a smaller light, one that belongs to the hand.

I would be tall and thin, as the people of the Underleaf must be. Light-boned. Narrow-chested. Broad-footed, so my weight spreads over the springy mats of frozen leaf-debris instead of breaking through them. My skin would be pale, faintly greenish in certain lights, with something translucent enough in it that the blue-green tracery beneath would look like leaf venation. Fine filaments along my scalp, jaw, and forearms would move with changes in pressure, telling me when the air is about to thicken with spores or when a cold pocket is leaning closer. My eyes would be large and pale, made for the shadowless radiance, blinking slowly, taking in light without expecting warmth from it.

I would not be beautiful in any warm or decorative sense. I would look adapted. I would look as if the Underleaf had pared away everything heavy until only attention remained.

My clothes would be layered and repaired. Woven bark strips softened by work. Leaf-skins cured with resin. A cloak sealed at the seams, pale with frost and sap-stain, making only the faintest whisper when I moved. No metal. No heavy ornaments. Tools tied to my body or my harness: a thorn awl, a hook made from a curved splinter, a small scraper of dense white heartwood, a knife-edge of shattered stem, pouches sealed with sap, coils of rope. Always rope. More rope than food. Rope is the first answer in a world where everything may open, tilt, freeze, or become absent. Rope lets one cross a gap, anchor a shelter, mark a route, secure a tool, lower oneself into a hollow, or fasten one’s sleeping body so that a sudden pressure change does not toss it into the bright void.

I would be a listener and a tender, though I would not call myself important for being either.

A listener is one who presses a palm, a cheek, or an ear against the living architecture and waits. Not for sound alone, but for the deeper language of the Underleaf: the tension in a root, the movement of liquid inside a stem, the slow strain of a membrane about to tear, the distant pressure wave that means a warm pulse is coming from below. A tender is one who returns to the same small places again and again, not to own them, but to help them continue. I would angle leaf-blades so condensation runs toward chosen depressions. I would polish certain grooves so water moves more predictably. I would clear frost from the bases of promising shoots. I would wound small stems carefully to encourage sap flow, then seal the wounds when enough had been gathered. I would lash young growth to stable ribs so it does not crack in the returning cold.

My cultivation would not look like farming. It would look like restraint.

I would wake not to sunlight, because there is no sun in the Underleaf, but to change. A loosening in the air. A faint bead of moisture on the inner face of a leaf. A subtle dulling or sharpening of the light. A vibration through the floor before sound arrives. I would sleep anchored, strapped into my shelter, tied to a stable root. Even in sleep I would not be entirely loose. Nothing in the Underleaf can afford to be entirely loose.

When I moved, I would not stride. I would read the world before trusting it.

Before putting weight on a surface, I would test it. I would watch the color of the light: milky brightness suggesting a spore release, blue-green cast suggesting freezing mist, sudden sharp shadows suggesting a light-vein overhead has opened more fully. I would feel for pressure through the soles of my feet. I would avoid swollen bladders, freshly cracked root-plates, and membranes shining with too much moisture. I would mark routes with knots, notches, and hanging strands of fiber. A loop crossed by a short cord might mean passage. Three turns around a line might mean safe anchorage. A frayed end left hanging might mean uncertainty. I would leave no knot unreadable. I would not take more water than the vein could replace. I would not sleep without anchoring. I would not shout unless death was already near.

My language would be quiet.

I would have a voice, but I would use it sparingly. Sound is strange in the Underleaf: swallowed by fiber, stretched by distance, delayed by vastness. A footfall makes a muted press. A falling splinter ticks once and is gone. A great collapse somewhere beyond sight may arrive only as a low thump or a pressure pulse against the chest. So much of what matters is felt rather than heard. I would speak in low whispers when necessary, but most of my communication would be hands, taps, and knots. At close range, I would sign. On stable stems, I would tap simple messages. With another person near, I would touch their wrist in coded rhythms: wait, move, water, danger, stable, unstable, return.

I would be emotional, but I would have learned not to make emotion heavy.

I would feel awe. How could I not? There would be moments when the Underleaf becomes almost unbearable to look at: a high vault of pale roots glowing white, every surface beaded with frozen dew; a storm of shining spores drifting in slow silence through an empty volume; a single enormous leaf tilted like a frozen wave, its edge turning into a thin green line of light. In those moments, I would stop. I would not try to speak. I would let the place enter me. I would understand, perhaps not in words, that beauty here is not a promise of safety. It is often a sign of instability. The most beautiful structures may be the ones about to break.

I would feel fear, but it would be a cold, precise fear. Not panic. Panic wastes heat and attention. Fear in the Underleaf would teach me to look up, to check my anchors, to move away from swollen things. It would make my fingers check knots. It would make me listen harder.

I would know loneliness, but it would be so familiar that it might not feel like absence anymore. It would feel more like weather. One can travel for days without seeing another mind-maker. The Underleaf does not support crowds. Its resources are thin, its water intermittent, its shelters temporary. To meet another person would be an event. If I saw a pale figure on a distant root-ridge, I would not run toward it. I would stop. I would make a sign of identity: a knot pattern, a tap rhythm, a posture of open hands. If the traveler came close, we would exchange water, food if there was any, warnings about unstable regions, news of condensation veins, maybe a story. Then we might press palms together, touch foreheads, or rest hands on each other’s shoulders for a moment. In such a sparse world, another living mind is not ordinary. It is almost as rare as water.

My bonds would be made of cord and memory.

If I loved someone, if that word can survive in a place without hearths and houses, it would be marked by tying. A cord between wrists. A knot exchanged. A strand kept against the body. Not ownership. Not permanence demanded. Only recognition. If we met again, we might re-tie. If we did not, the strand would remain. I would carry that strand the way the Underleaf carries frost: lightly, until conditions change.

Children, if there were children, would be precious and quiet. They would be carried close inside layers of insulation, wrapped in soft leaf-skins, taught early not to waste breath. They would learn stillness before they learned much else. They would learn that brightness is not warmth, that solidity is temporary, that beauty is not safety. They would be taught to listen with the body. A child who flinches at a harmless tick but ignores a slow pressure wave would be corrected gently, again and again. Attention would be the inheritance I could give. Not wealth. Not land. Not monuments. Attention.

My stories would be small.

I would not tell epics of conquest. There is nothing to conquer in the Underleaf. One cannot defeat it. One can only move with it. My stories would be route-memory, cautionary patterns, descriptions of rare events. I would tell of the time a light-vein opened so fully that shadows became sharp for hours. I would tell of the traveler who mistook a distant wall for a near one and walked until exhaustion. I would tell of the bladder that burst beneath a sleeping platform. I would tell of the warm pulse that lasted so long that frost retreated from an entire root-arch. I would tell of the moth that landed on a child’s hand and did not fly away. These stories would not need embellishment. The Underleaf itself is strange enough. A good story would be one that keeps someone alive.

I would make offerings, though I would not call them prayers.

A cup of clean water placed near a new crack. A knot tied to a fresh shoot. A pinch of spore-dust scattered over a repaired seam. These would not be attempts to bargain with the world. They would be acknowledgments. The Underleaf is alive. It changes. It breaks. It allows passage. To move through it is to be temporarily permitted. I would know this not only in thought but in my hands, in the way I tied lines, in the way I tested surfaces, in the way I never cut what could be untied.

I would avoid the deep places unless necessity drove me there.

The regions where the root-mass thickens and the light turns milky blue would feel different. Not hostile, but too large for a small body to interpret comfortably. There, pressure waves come from below at intervals. Warm pulses begin there. Frost retreats in rings. Resin weeps from old wounds. Travelers say that near the Slow Ones one feels noticed not by eyes but by weight. I believe them. I would not build near those places. I would not tap the deep channels unless desperate. I would not cut into swollen root-masses, even if they were rich with resin. If the floor trembled with a slow, intentional rhythm, I would stop and place my hands on a stable surface until the pulse passed. Some things are too large to negotiate with. One can only wait them out.

And this, I think, would become the deepest part of me: I would learn not to become heavy.

Not heavy in body. Not heavy in desire. Not heavy in memory. Not heavy in need. The Underleaf punishes permanence by revealing that permanence was always an illusion. A floor may be a frozen mat over an air pocket. A wall may be under tension along invisible grain. A camp may be beside a reliable vein today and meaningless tomorrow when the vein freezes or shifts. A bridge may be lashed across a gap only for the gap to widen or close by a centimeter. Even the built world is a series of answers to temporary conditions. Gestures, not monuments.

So my life there would be small and exquisite in its impermanence.

I would carry my world in knots, tools, remembered pressures, and a few carefully chosen strands. I would not demand that my shelter outlast the season. I would not demand that my routes remain true. I would return to them and repair them, or find that they are gone and accept the new arrangement of the world. I would mend where I could. I would take only what could be spared. I would move on before my presence became a wound.

If another traveler came to my camp, I would not greet them with excitement. I would watch. I would see whether they moved carefully, whether their tools were tied, whether their feet knew the mats. Then, if I judged them safe, I would make a simple sign: an open hand, a slow tap against the thigh, a knot offered on a short cord.

That knot might mean:

Passage.  
Water.  
Wait.  
I am here.  
You may rest.

It would be enough.

If I died in the Underleaf, there would be no burial in soil, because there is no soil in the ordinary sense. No tomb. No monument. My companions, if any were near, would bind my limbs gently and place me in a hollow, a crevice, or a cradle of roots. They would cover me with woven fiber or leaf-blades. Then they would leave me to freeze, dry, crack, and become part of the substrate. Maybe later a pale shoot would grow from the place where I had been laid. That would not be miraculous. It would be ordinary. The Underleaf does not preserve its dead as trophies. It uses them. I would hope someone left a single knot nearby, but I would not expect them to return to worship it. My grief, like my warmth, would have been made to move.

And if someone asked, after all this, who or what I truly was in that world, I would not answer with a grand title.

I would say: I was a Listener.

I was one of the thin ones who pressed their hands against the living architecture and tried to understand its slow language. I was a tender of small wet places, a mender of ropes, a keeper of temporary routes. I was a pale figure on a root-ridge, checking a line, watching the light change, feeling the air for pressure. I was a maker of knots that meant water, warning, passage, return. I was a guest in a body too large to comprehend, trying not to become a wound inside it.

But if I wanted to say it more truly, I would say this:

I would be the part of the Underleaf that notices.

Not its master. Not its hero. Not its owner. Just a brief, attentive gesture within its process. A place where its cold light becomes awareness for a little while. A knot that knows it is tied. A listener who hears the world breathing and answers with careful movement.

That is where I would find myself.

There, in the bright emptiness between enormous root-ribs, with frost feathering from hanging fibers and a far green shape suspended in silence. There, where water appears as a bead, a film, a brief drip, then is gone. There, where every structure is alive and every stability is temporary. There, with rope on my body, knots in my hands, and the Underleaf moving through my attention like slow thought.

There, I would learn to live thinly.

There, I would become almost part of the place.

And perhaps that would be enough.
