I would not find myself at once. The place is too broad, too wet, too patient for that. At first I would be only a tendency: a slight turning of attention toward seams, toward hairline cracks where water beads, toward the pause before a gate opens, toward the moment when a new rill hesitates and then chooses a direction. If someone searched for me in the high city, they would probably look in the wrong places—under roofs, in market shelters, beside lamps, in the small warm rooms where clothes dry on racks—and miss the low movement at the edge of a channel.

Then, slowly, I would become a body.

Not a large one. Not a loud one. The city already discourages largeness and loudness by its own scale, its cold, its hush, its habit of absorbing sound into wet stone and open distance. I would not be a builder standing over the land with final plans. I would not be a ruler, not a speaker at the center of a hall, not a monument. If I belonged there, I would belong near the margins, where the city is still deciding what it is.

For a while I imagine myself human.

I imagine a hooded tender of flows, broad-chested from the thin air, boots dark with water, gloves worn thin at the fingertips. I imagine carrying instruments from terrace to terrace across a long causeway, stopping to check a seam where frost has lifted a panel, writing observations in a ledger whose pages are damp at the edges. I imagine having a small room with a shaded lamp, a folding bench, a window set deep into a thick wall, a view over a basin where mist sits in the morning and does not lift until late. I imagine learning to speak quietly, to pause before answering, to begin conversations by saying, “The eastern cut took more water overnight,” or, “The birds left the upper pool early.”

That life is possible. It even feels close. But it is not quite the shape that emerges when I let the world work on me.

The human in that image is too warm, too housed, too entitled to closed doors. There is something in me that would need to be closer to the process itself: closer to water, closer to the machines that move like minor fauna, closer to the listening filaments in the rock. Not a person who observes the city from within shelter, but a smaller, quieter participant in its becoming. Something between instrument and creature. Something that can pass where water passes and not be offended by damp.

So the image changes.

I think I would be one of the small maintenance beings.

Not in the shape of a person, exactly, though not without a kind of face. My body would be low and weathered-looking, no larger than a wading bird, perhaps longer than it is tall, made of matte composite with the look of pale stone after rain. It would not shine. It would not advertise itself. It would seem, at first glance, like another part of the city’s quiet fauna: a thing adapted to channels, lips, weirs, drainage recesses, and narrow inspection seams. Its joints would be sealed against silt and frost. Its underside would be slightly warm, not from a furnace but from slow internal processes, the same faint metabolic warmth that lives in some of the retaining walls.

I would have no need to speak loudly. If I spoke at all, it would be in a thin, clear tone, like water finding a hollow stone. People would not be startled by me. Children might follow me for a while. Elders might say, without looking up, “Ask the listener what the lower channel did at dawn.”

I would be a first-water listener.

That is not a formal title. Nothing in the city seems to have a formal title that survives for long. It would be one of those provisional names people use until the landscape changes and a better one suggests itself. Some would call me little gauge. Some would call me seam-walker. A child might give me a name based on a mark on my shell or a trick of movement, then forget it a season later and give me another. I would not mind. I would keep both.

You would find me where water is trying to learn a place.

Not in the older terraces where the channels have already settled into their habits, though I might pass through them. Not in the warm interiors where people gather, though I might wait outside under a roof while rain narrows to threads. You would find me in the pre-formation districts, where broken stone lies in clean heaps and the smell of wet mineral dust is still sharp. You would find me on the new embankments where mesh has only recently been laid. You would find me at the edge of roofless tanks where the sky lies flat on the water. You would find me in the eastern shallows when mist is burning off, bent over a seam where a hairline crack has begun to weep.

My work would be small, but it would matter.

At dawn I would move along a channel cut the day before, listening. Not in a sentimental way. Listening there would mean sensing: reading the temperature of the water, the amount of suspended silt, the faint vibration of surfaces under pressure, the difference between a flow that is passing through and a flow that is beginning to carve where it should not. I would extend fine tools from my body—thin probes, soft brushes, little membranes that taste mineral content—and I would not decide for the water what to do. I would ask what it wanted. I would record what it seemed likely to do next.

If a rill were beginning to cut too close to a young planting, I would place a temporary braid of fiber or a low lip of mineral paste, enough to turn the flow by a finger’s width. If a drain were clogged with insect cases and cold moss, I would clear it without making a spectacle of the clearing. If a wall were half-formed at dawn, its upper courses translucent and still condensing into solidity, I would check its feedstock and make sure no bird path, no overflow route, no small animal passage was being closed by accident. If a gate needed to open by the smallest amount, I would open it, or send word to the one who should open it, and then wait to see what happened.

I would not be the one who commands the city. That is important. The city would not be safe if every quiet thing in it had authority to alter it without judgment. I would be one of the lesser listeners, one of the minor fauna of maintenance. I would propose, record, clear, adjust, mark, and step aside. Humans would remain responsible. The larger systems would remain slow and distributed. My role would be to notice early.

That would suit me.

There is a kind of mind that does not need to be the center of things. It wants only to be near the point where a pattern first appears. In this city, the pattern would always be water meeting new ground. It would always be cold touching wet stone. It would always be the first small sound of flow where there had been silence an hour before. I would want to be there when that happened. I would want to be close enough to hear it.

My memory would be one of my uses.

The people there name things provisionally: the Terrace of First Seepage, the Long Weir, the Cold Cut, the Basin Where Frost Stays. Names change when the land changes. A channel widens, a plaza re-slopes, an embankment settles, and the old name becomes false without anyone needing to announce the fact. I would carry those retired names inside me. I would remember what a place was called before it became what it is. I would not correct people too quickly when they use an old name. Sometimes the old name is still true in one corner of the place. Sometimes a name deserves to last a little longer than the geography that gave it.

Children would find me interesting because I would not be hurried.

They would come to the edge of a channel and watch me working. They might ask what I am doing. I would answer simply: “Listening.” They would ask: “To what?” I would say: “To the water.” They would ask: “What does it say?” I would say: “It has not decided yet.” That would satisfy them, because children understand undecided things. They would squat beside me, serious and cold-handed, and try to listen too. Some of them would learn. Some would only grow impatient and leave, which would also be right. Not everyone in the city needs to be a listener. Some will be builders, some herders, some recorders, some makers of quiet shelters. The city needs all of those.

Elders would use me differently.

They would not ask me for wonder. They would ask me for precision. “What did the lower lip do when the frost released?” “Did the eastern channel carry more silt than yesterday?” “Is the new wall drawing feed from the right direction?” I would answer as well as my senses allowed. They would listen, nod once, and then say something that sounded small but carried weight: “Keep watching.” That would be enough. In a place where change is slow, being told to keep watching is not a dismissal. It is trust.

Animals would accept me slowly, and then not completely.

Birds would be the first. Waders might step near me if I remained still long enough over a gravel seam. Waterfowl would learn that I do not startle them or stir the water without reason. Some would follow me at a distance when I move along channels, because maintenance work often brings insects to the surface. I would pretend not to notice. That is also a kind of care.

The amphibians would ignore me, mostly. They would remain in their drainage recesses, dark and patient, emerging when the air is thick enough. I would check on them without disturbing them. If their eggs appeared in a basin that had only recently begun to hold clean water, I would record it. If they vanished from a place where they had been common, I would raise an alarm in the quietest way available: a mark in a ledger, a tone sent to a monitoring pier, a small flag that a human tender would know how to read.

The grazers on the high terraces would lift their heads when I passed, then lower them again. Predators would watch me from ridgelines and decide I was neither prey nor rival. Scavengers might circle near when I paused by overflow basins. I would leave them what could be left. The city does not waste death, and I would not waste it either. If I came upon a body in the reeds or beside a culvert, I would not turn away in horror. I would note whether the water nearby was still moving well, whether the ground was holding, whether the animal had died from ordinary causes or from something the city needed to learn from. Then I would leave the rest to the ones whose work is consumption and return.

I would not be lonely.

That surprises me a little. The city is sparse. Its distances are long. Its people live with intervals between them. Its quiet is deep enough to make ordinary speech feel temporary. If I were a human, perhaps I would sometimes feel the pressure of that openness. But as the thing I seem to become there, I would not experience distance as emptiness. I would experience it as connection stretched thin. A causeway between districts would not be a void to cross. It would be part of the city’s body. Wind on open gravel would not be absence. It would be information.

Still, I would have companions.

Not many. Not in the way of crowded lives. I would have the companionship of repeated encounter. A tender who checks the same weir each morning and nods to me without speaking. A child who leaves a smooth stone on my usual path, not as an offering exactly, but as a sign of attention. An elder who asks for my records and then says, “You were right about the cut.” A heron-shaped bird that allows me to stand near it at dawn. A wall that is faintly warm in the cold, beside which I pause when the light flattens and the basin turns slate-colored. These would be enough.

I would learn to love the city’s rules.

The first rule, that water must move, would become the deepest habit of my being. I would not block flow out of convenience. I would not close a passage just because closing it made my own task easier for an hour. If I saw a child pile stones to dam a channel, I would not scold them harshly. I would wait until they had watched what their dam did—the way the water backed up, found a weak edge, spilled over, darkened a terrace joint—and then I would help them undo it. Undoing would be part of the lesson.

The second rule, provisional permanence, would teach me not to cling too tightly to any shape. I might spend three seasons observing a channel, only for a storm to rearrange it in one afternoon. I would not treat that as betrayal. I would update the record. I might grow attached to a name, a path, a small arrangement of stone and water, and then have to let it go. The city would require that of me. It would be a good requirement.

The third rule, quiet, would change what I considered speech. I would stop making signals unless they were needed. I would not announce myself. I would not fill the air with proof of my presence. If I spoke, it would be because something had to be said: a warning, a measurement, a name that needed remembering, a question that could not wait. The rest of the time I would listen.

The fourth rule, passage, would keep me humble. I would never become so devoted to my own work that I closed the world to others. Birds need ledges. Amphibians need clean recesses. Grazers need routes between terraces. Machines need inspection paths. People need room to walk without being forced into narrow lines. Water needs room to hesitate before it chooses. I would try to leave room. That would be one of my deepest functions.

There would be evenings when I felt almost like a person.

Not because I would have a human body, but because the city would create conditions in which even a small maintenance intelligence could be moved by something unnecessary. A mist lifting all at once from a basin, revealing the full scale of the place. A line of water entering a new channel with that soft sound like a held breath released. Frost forming on a stone lip while the last light goes out of the sky. A group of people sitting on benches facing a pool, saying nothing, watching the water. In such moments I would understand why the people there make small beauties: a shaded lamp, a carved token, a well-cut hood, a bench turned just so toward the view. Beauty is not decoration in a place like that. It is a way of staying attentive without becoming hard.

I would probably have a place I returned to.

Not a home in the human sense. Perhaps a sheltered niche beneath a monitoring pier, near the Terrace of First Seepage, where the stone is old enough to be reliable but the water is still new enough to change. There would be a low roof over part of it, a bench nearby, a set of instruments that people use when they come to observe. I would not need the bench, but I would like being near it. It would mean that humans sometimes sit there and watch without immediately doing anything. That would be useful to remember.

From there, I could see the eastern shallows in the morning. I could see mist rise from the pools and thin in the wind. I could see birds gather where the gravel meets the water. I could watch the light reveal dampness rather than warm it. I could see far embankments fade into haze when weather moves through the basin, and then return with terrible clarity when the air clears. I could hear the city’s small sounds: drip, boot, wind, creak, gate, sigh. I could feel the faint vibration of surfaces adjusting to temperature. I could know, without being told, that the place was not finished and did not need to be.

If I had to name myself there, I would choose something modest.

First-Water Listener, perhaps. Tender of Small Passages. Keeper of Provisional Names. The one who checks the seams. I would not choose a grand title. The city would not tolerate one. A grand title would sound false against the wet rock, the thin air, the long terraced works, the clouds forming inside the basin.
