A phrase may be a click at the back of the throat followed by a minute redistribution of pressure, as if the air had turned a page.

If I were placed in that world, I do not think I would begin as one of the raised. That would be too clean, too finished. The tableland feels less like a country one enters than a condition one is slowly corrected by. So I would begin as an error of density: a lowland body, too warm, too quick, too full of sound.

I would find myself in one of the shallow basins where the ice keeps a gray memory of water, probably at the edge of a hollow worn smooth by repeated kneeling. I would kneel there without understanding why, because kneeling would lower me, make me less of a disturbance. My ears would ache. The air would feel thin and sharp-edged, not refreshing but insufficient, as if each breath were being offered from a great distance. My heart would knock too fast. My boots on the gravel would make stupid, obvious noises, and I would not yet know that noise there is not sound but displacement.

The raised would feel me long before they turned toward me. To them, I would be a pocket of lowland weather that had somehow survived the ascent: a warm bulge in the pressure, a soft clumsy fullness behind the eyes. They would not gather in alarm. They would simply adjust. Their slits would click in delicate sequence. Their wide matte eyes would take me in without examining me. They would wait for me to stop making so much of myself.

At first I would try to speak. That would be the wrong instinct. My voice would seem crude, a wet animal moving through their silence. It would carry too much demand and too little alteration. One of them might answer not with a word but by changing the air around my face—a minute cooling, a pressure shift that would make my jaw ache faintly, as though frost were writing itself there. I would not understand. I would speak again, louder, and the distance would take my voice and make it absurd.

So I would learn, slowly, to become less articulate in the lowland sense.

The tableland would thin me. Not only my body, though that too: my chest would deepen, my breath would slow, my blood would darken and take on the strange sweetness of cold. My heart would have to enlarge to be kind enough for the altitude. I would begin to hear pressure rather than sound. A footstep would no longer be a scrape; it would be a small movement inside my own ribs. A coming frost would appear as a bright line behind my teeth. Another person nearby would be a soft fullness behind my eyes, not seen so much as endured.

At some point I would notice seams along my sides, thin and tender, opening with a faint click when the weather changed. They would not feel like wounds. They would feel like grammar.

Then I would know where I was.

I would be a threshold creature, neither lowlander nor fully one of the raised. I would be what happens when a body is kept long enough in the pause before resolution. My usefulness would be that I remembered density. The raised, thinned as they are, might come near me and hear in my slowed blood the storms crawling far below: rain striking leaves, rivers thick with melt, heat rising from valleys, the heavy air of places where faces matter. I would be their memory of the world beneath the tableland, but I would hold it the way ice holds water: grayly, silently, without giving it back except as pressure.

I would become a translator of alterations. A greeting from me might be a slight warming at the collarbone. A warning might be a click at the back of the throat and a sudden tautness in the salt. A name would not be spoken; it would be the particular hollow my body leaves in the air when I move away. I would learn that speech is not the production of noise but the decision to alter something shared.

I think I would be found most often near the western horizon, where the line is blunt and low and feels pressed against the body. There, the unresolved distance would be strongest. I would stand or kneel in one of the smooth hollows, my limbs long from altitude, my face less important than the pressure moving across it. If a lowlander saw me from far away, I might seem too tall, stretched by the air. If one of the raised came near, they would feel me as a familiar fullness: a listener, a barometer with a pulse, a creature made of patience and thin weather.

But if you asked what I was, I would not say a person exactly. I would say I had become an instrument of the pause. I would be the place where the tableland registers what is about to arrive. I would be the soft displacement that tells the others the air is changing. I would be a cairn that had learned to breathe, a hollow that remembered knees, a storm from below made quiet enough to be heard as pressure.

And if you came to me there, I would not turn to you with a face full of answers. I would let the air alter around us. You would feel a small displacement, a faint click somewhere near your own ribs, a soft fullness behind your eyes. That would be my greeting. That would be my name. That would be the pause before resolution, taking a shape almost, but not quite, like me.
