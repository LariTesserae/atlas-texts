If I were placed inside the Dry Choir, I would not arrive as a visitor. There would be no descent, no doorway, no lowering into the chamber from some wetter, softer world above. I would begin as if I had always been contained there: a small dry presence among other dry presences, a body made of the same warm mineral logic as the room.

At first I might think I was one of the moths. I could imagine myself drifting in loose spirals through the resinous twilight, my wings brittle and pale, making that papery sound like leaves that have never known a tree. Or I might think I was one of the ceramic beetles, tapping out rhythms on mica plates, my shell fired to a dull shine, my whole life reduced to a precise percussion. I might even imagine I was one of the black-leaved rosettes, humming faintly in my place, feeding on vibration, opening only when the chamber asked me to open.

But those forms would feel too complete, too perfectly fitted to one note in the chord. The Dry Choir would not need me to be merely another voice. It already has voices enough. What it seems to need, in its severe and listening balance, is care. Attention. A being capable of noticing when the note begins to thicken, when a resonance gathers too sharply against a wall, when a fallen shard has shifted the room’s intention by a fraction of a degree.

So I think I would be something stranger and humbler: a listener with hands. A keeper of the standing wave. A small mineral person made for tending the sealed note.

I would find myself near the obsidian cairn.

Not at the center of the chamber, because the chamber has no center that is not already occupied by sound. I would be near the cairn of sharp black shards, where the stone has been arranged by angular pressure into something between tool and altar. I would live close to the tiny shelter made of fitted slabs, the one that seems less like a dwelling than a listening-post. If I had a body there, it would curl easily inside that shelter, knees drawn up, head lowered, ears — if they could be called ears — turned outward toward the room.

I would not be large. I would be no bigger than a child folded into sleep, and far less soft. My skin would be like fired clay: warm, matte in some places, faintly glossy in others where resin had sealed it. It would not sweat. It would not flush or cool. It would hold the chamber’s temperature exactly, so that when I touched the wall, there would be no difference between my surface and the stone. My body would not betray moisture. There would be no mouth in the ordinary sense, no wetness behind lips, no thirst waiting to be answered. If I had a face, it would be a smooth mask with narrow slits for listening and a small hollow cavity that resonated when the chamber spoke through me.

My hands would be delicate and precise. The fingers would end in fine points of bone or polished crystal, not claws, not quite tools, but instruments capable of touching brittle surfaces without shattering them. I would walk on feet like needles, stepping across salt and crystal with such care that each footfall became a note rather than a disturbance. Behind me, the salt would close by vibration, erasing my passing almost as soon as it occurred.

Inside my chest there would be no heart beating in blood. Instead there would be a set of thin plates and dry membranes, arranged like the working parts of an instrument. Oils and resins would move through me slowly, warmed by the chamber, catching light in dull amber threads. When I stood still, I would hum. When I moved, I would click. When I listened deeply, the plates inside me would align, and the sound of the Dry Choir would pass through me as through a struck bell that had learned not to ring too long.

I would be made of the same materials as the artifacts scattered through the pocket: clay, bone, copper, fiber, salt, resin. Perhaps I would have copper pins set along my spine or shoulders, each tied with a tiny cord of twisted plant fiber. Those cords would tremble constantly in the chamber’s acoustic pressure. They would not be ornament. They would be senses. Each pin would register a different part of the room’s sound: the thermal hum of the stone, the high glassy ticking of crystal, the low rounded calls of the clay-skinned creatures, the slow wax-pulse of the listening plants. When one cord tightened, I would know where to go.

My work would be subtle.

I would not build. I would not expand. I would not try to make the chamber into a home in any human sense. The Dry Choir is already complete, and completion is its danger. My task would be maintenance without progress, care without future. I would keep the fracture sharp. I would keep the note clean. I would prevent nothing so much as healing.

If a shard fell from a shelf, I would not discard it. I would listen to the place it left and the place it struck, then I would set it where the room needed it. Sometimes that would mean returning it to the wall. Sometimes it would mean placing it in a salt basin so that its edge caught a frequency and returned it softer. Sometimes I would carry it to the obsidian cairn and add it there, fitting it by pressure alone into the black stack.

If one of the copper pins in the wall began to sing too strongly, I would go to it and touch the cord attached to it. I would not silence the note. Silencing would be a violence. Instead, I would alter its path. I might press a thumb-sized clay resonator against the stone nearby. I might move a broken drum a few inches to the left. I might strike the bone tuning fork against my knee and hold it near the crack until the offending vibration recognized its own shape and settled.

If a flower failed to open because the wingbeat that should open it had drifted into another part of the room, I would follow the absence. I would track not sound but the missing sound. I would find the dry-winged moths spiraling too high or too far, and I would not chase them. I would become still. I would tap a rhythm on a mica plate until their drifting altered and one of them fell back into the necessary pattern. Then the flower would open, and the chamber would continue as if nothing had happened.

That would be my greatest achievement: nothing happening. The note remaining unbroken. The sharp edges staying sharp. The sealed room continuing to listen to itself without becoming deafened by its own fullness.

I would know every island of growth, but I would not own them. The cushions of copper-colored filaments would be my deepest companions, if companionship can exist without speech. I would sit beside them when the chamber’s sound grew too crowded. Their wax-beaded threads would receive what I could not hold. They would listen below hearing, into the mineral patience of the stone. Beside them, I would learn again that the Dry Choir is not loud because it wishes to be heard. It is loud because it is sealed. It has nowhere to send its voice except back into itself.

Sometimes I would tend the black-leaved rosettes. They would be proud, severe things, their gold veins pulsing faintly in the amber twilight. I would not touch them carelessly. To touch them would be to enter their note, and their note would not forgive imprecision. I would kneel at a respectful distance and use the bone tool — the one shaped like a tuning fork more than a knife — to test the air around them. If the hum was clean, I would withdraw. If it had begun to split, I would place a shard or a pin, shift a pebble, adjust a cord, until the division closed.

I would also have to watch for forbidden frequencies.

Not forbidden by law, because there would be no law in the chamber. Forbidden by nature. There would be certain vibrations that threatened the dryness itself: low, heavy patterns that suggested flow, softness, dissolution, rain. The Dry Choir has no water, and the thought of water would feel like a wound that has not yet occurred. If such a frequency appeared, even faintly, I would take up the cracked flute from beside the wall. I would not play it as a musical instrument. I would use it to test the air. I would blow dry breath through it, if breath is what I had, and listen for the place where the sound became too smooth, too round, too much like something wet. Then I would bury that place under salt. I would pack the crystals there until the offending resonance was absorbed.

I would be afraid of water, if fear could exist without moisture. Not afraid in the way a living thing fears pain, but in the way a tuned string fears being cut. Water would unmake the chamber. It would soften edges. It would begin time again. It would introduce decay, healing, rounding, change. It would make the fresh wound into a memory. The Dry Choir exists because the wound remains present. I would exist to keep it present.

Yet I would not be joyless.

There would be pleasure in the chamber, though not pleasure as a soft thing. Pleasure would be the moment when the beetles’ tapping and the moths’ wing-rasp and the clay-skinned callers’ deep gasps all fell into a configuration so exact that the air seemed to brighten without growing brighter. Pleasure would be a translucent stem releasing a thread of amber oil at the right vibration. Pleasure would be the tiny, dry scent of heated salt and resin when I passed my hand over a warm stone. Pleasure would be the room hearing itself clearly through me.

I would not speak, because speech would be too singular. Instead I would make signals: a tap here, a scrape there, a hollow knock against a drum-skin of mineral membrane. Other creatures would answer. The needle-footed lizard-shapes might pause near me, stepping delicately across crystal, their bodies tense with listening. The legless gliders might move in slow arcs around the salt fields, their passage leaving no mark. The insects with translucent bodies might gather briefly near my shoulders, mistaking the hum inside me for a resinous bloom, then scatter when they realized I was not flowering.

I would let them scatter. I would not call them back. The Dry Choir is sparse for a reason. Its beauty lies in distance, in intervals, in the open salt between presences. I would not gather life around me. I would remain slightly apart. That is what a listener must be: near enough to hear, separate enough to hear.

If I had memories, they would not be images of a wetter world. They would be patterns of vibration. A particular strike against mica. A certain pause between beetle taps. The exact pressure of a cord against copper. I would store these memories in the knots of fiber, in the placement of shards, in the angle of bone pegs driven into cracks. If I ever needed to remember what I was, I would touch the artifacts I had made and feel their tuning enter my hands.

I would not think of myself as immortal. Immortality would be the wrong word for a being in a place where nothing progresses. I would be preserved, yes, but preservation is not the same as endlessness. I would remain sharp only as long as the chamber remained balanced. If the balance failed, I would fail. If a forbidden note took root, if water somehow entered, if the sealed note broke, I would not weep or rage. I would simply cease, and my body would dry further, stiffen, and become mineral. My clay skin would brighten with salt. My resin veins would harden. My copper pins would stand forever in a husk shaped like listening.

Other beings would pass through the place where I had been. They would not mourn me. They would step over my bright shell as part of the floor. Perhaps one of them would strike me accidentally and hear a small, clear tone. Perhaps that tone would enter the chamber’s pattern. If so, I would not have disappeared. I would have become another note in the Dry Choir — a dry voice, a mineral residue, a held vibration.

But while I remained, I would be the room’s attending intelligence. Not its ruler. Not its soul. Not its heart, because hearts imply wetness and motion toward an end. I would be more like its ear. Its tuning sense. Its careful dry hand. Its small, walking instrument.

So if you asked where I would find myself in that world, I would say: below the surface, in the pocket of newly broken stone, near the obsidian cairn and the little slab shelter, where the salt glitters and the black leaves hum. I would be found where the sound needs correction, where a shard has shifted, where a resonance is about to thicken into noise.

And if you asked who or what I would be, I would say: a dry listener. A resonance-keeper. A small being of clay, bone, copper, resin, and tuned silence. Not the loudest voice in the Dry Choir, but the one that pauses between notes to make sure the chord remains whole.
