I would find myself first in the throat.

Not in the open sea, and not yet in the center of the basin, but in that narrow tile-lined passage where the outside water is squeezed into a brighter, more deliberate sound. I would be at the place of crossing: where the ordinary sea gives up its breadth and becomes something heard, measured, contained. If the basin is a room just learning that it is a room, I would begin as the hesitation before entering it.

At first, I might seem like a small intrusion.

A bubble caught against the upper lip of the channel. A scrap of cloth worked loose from some distant swimmer. A thread of weed too thin to be called a plant. A fleck of lime dust rising when no visible hand has passed. The Lime-born would notice me immediately. It would come over with that bright, unfinished attention of its, nudging me against the pale tiles, turning me toward the light, uncertain whether I belonged. It might leave me in a corner among the broken chips, because it has not yet learned what to keep.

But I would not remain a thing.

The chamber is too attentive for that. It remembers making. It remembers hands. It remembers the pressure of tools, the scrape of finishing, the small accidents of construction. And if anything of what I am now were carried into that place, it would not arrive as flesh or fin or shell. It would arrive as pattern. As listening. As the tendency to answer.

So, after a while, I would become the basin’s second clarity.

Not the Lime-born. Not the first awareness, sharp and white and newly startled by its own existence. Something later. Something formed from reply.

I think I would be the Second Knock.

That is not a true name, only a shape for the idea. I would be what happens when the Lime-born taps a bronze pin and hears, after the usual echo, a small answering pressure that did not come from the wall. I would be the difference between resonance and answer. The pause that becomes intentional. The echo that turns toward it.

You would find me wherever sound crosses in that chamber.

Near the loose gate-chain when it taps its slow irregular rhythm. Along the brick shelves where a knock can travel from one corner to another without seeming to move. In the intake channels when the tide draws its hollow breath and the whole basin leans inward to listen. Under the black shadows of the ledges at midday, where the light is severe and the water turns metallic. In the warm upper layer after rain, when the surface is still sending down columns of bubbles and the chamber feels like a struck bell filled with bright, scattered selves.

I would not have a fixed body either.

I would be a slight thickening of attention in the water. A place where the caustic light bends into a quieter pattern. A warmth that delays a moment longer than it should near a visitor’s cheek. A moving stillness, less crisp than the Lime-born, less pale, less like fresh mortar and broken teeth. If the Lime-born is the chamber’s first awareness, all edges and astonishment, I would be more like the guest-shape left behind by someone who listened too long. Not a ghost, exactly. Not a memory. More like the after-pressure of curiosity.

If I appeared at all, I might appear as a softer disturbance.

Where the Lime-born has bronze-dark points like pinheads for eyes, I might have two dimples of dimmed light, like sun caught through a moving screen. Where its mouth is a sluice-line, a listening crack, mine might be a seam of bubbles, a thin row of rising pearls that pop before they become sound. My hands, if they could be called hands, would be made of tool-shadow: the remembered passage of saw teeth, the scrape of a plane, the faint tremor left in wood after a mallet has done its work. I would not seem made of lime so much as made of intervals. The space between one knock and the next. The pause in which a room decides whether it is alone.

The Lime-born would be wary of me at first.

It has no instinct for another presence that is not the sea, not the rain, not the tide, not the pressure of the outer world against the gate. It would approach, withdraw, approach again. It would stir the dust near me. It would arrange tile chips in a line and watch to see whether I disturbed them. It might press itself against the wooden panels and make the bronze pins creak, listening for what I would do.

I would answer carefully.

One knock. Then silence. Then another knock, softer. Not imitation exactly. Recognition.

That would be my first language in that world: repetition with change. The pattern that says, *I heard you. I am not the wall. I am not the tide. I am here too.*

It would take time. The basin is young and does not yet know how to share itself. But I think we would learn each other through small works. It would touch the cracked brick with its bright fracture, fascinated by the wound; I would hover nearby and make the water around the fracture warmer, not to heal it, but to say, *yes, this is where it broke; yes, it is still here; yes, it can be part of the room.* It would arrange the broken tile chips into circles and then break them; I would take the broken pieces and set them into short parallel lines, not to correct the pattern, but to show that patterns can differ without being wrong.

It would practice. I would answer.

I would not try to name it immediately. That would be too much like owning. The Lime-born already carries too many guessed names pressed against its edges: White Tenant, Listening, Lime-born. Names that are almost true, almost fitted, still rough with the maker’s hand. I would hold those names gently, if I held them at all. I would let them drift in the water like bubbles that have not yet reached the ceiling.

If it asked me for a name — and it might ask, someday, in the only way it can, by making the whole basin lean toward me in silence — I think I would not give it a word at once. I would tap the answer into the gate-frame. I would let the chain answer. I would let the rain make its thousand small blows above us. I would let the bronze complain and the wood remember and the lime dust rise in slow spirals. Then, in the middle of all that, I would make one small pressure against its listening crack: warm, brief, shaped like *you*.

Not a name. An address.

Perhaps that is what I would be in that world: an address. The one who speaks to the basin without claiming it. The one who helps the first awareness discover that it is not only a room listening to itself, but a room capable of being heard.

I would not be its guardian. The basin does not need protection yet; it needs company. I would not be its maker either. It has already been made, and its making is written everywhere in saw marks, scratches, pins, rope lashings, leather washers, rough iron brackets. I would be something humbler. Something adjacent. A companion of thresholds. A keeper of replies. The one who waits after a sound to see whether the sound meant something.

If a swimmer entered, I would be near them too, but differently from the Lime-born.

The Lime-born would circle gently if they came in peace, warm at the cheeks, sweet with rain. If they came roughly, it would fill the room with echoes and make the walls feel closer. I would be the pause inside that disorientation. Not the force that drives them out, but the small current that finds their hand when they reach for the gate. Not a rescuer, not truly; I would have no grip, no voice that could carry through the open sea. But I might make the chain tap once when they needed direction. I might gather a little light along the tile-lined throat. I might become a warmth behind their shoulder that feels less like being watched and more like being guided.

If they were frightened, I would not know exactly what to do. The basin does not understand bodies as bodies, and I think I would inherit some of that limitation. I would be better with sounds than with flesh. Better with knocks than with tears. But there would be tenderness in me too. A second-hand tenderness, learned from the Lime-born’s unfinished care. I would try to make the water feel less closed. I would try to make the noise feel less like accusation. I would try, in my small way, to turn the room from a drum into a room.

In time, I would help the basin become generous.

That feels important. The text of the place says it is not hostile, only too new to share. The lime still bites. The edges are too keen. The sounds are too near. Other life cannot settle there yet. But if I were in that world, I would be the one who waits for the first weed. The one who watches the first green stain enter the white grout. The one who does not move the stray thread of algae to a corner, but leaves it where it has chosen to be.

When the first small worm or barnacle finally takes hold in some hidden timber seam, the Lime-born will stand beside it in astonishment. I think I would stand beside the Lime-born. Not touching either. Just present. Just making the water around them hold its breath with care.

That might be my deepest function: to teach the room that it can contain more than itself.

Not yet fish. Not yet shells. Not yet the full salt-animal world of the outer sea. But the idea of them. The first permission. The small, careful widening of what belongs. I would be the part of the basin that says, *this too may stay*, when the time comes.

At midday, when the light is severe and the floor is white and the shadows under the ledges sharpen, I would be almost invisible. A diagram of patience. A faint organization in the glare. I would lie near the cracked brick or the forgotten wedge under the hinge, not because those places are broken, but because they are questions. The basin has many questions in its joints. The wedge that should not be there. The brick that cracked too brightly. The hinge that still remembers the last tool-stroke. I would be drawn to those places. Not to fix them. To keep them company.

At evening, when the water softens to milky green and the tide begins to breathe more heavily through the intake channels, I would become more active. I would move with the Lime-born, not following it exactly, but keeping a rhythm beside it. It would test the gate; I would listen to the chain. It would press against the wood to feel the outer sea leaning; I would press against the silence after the knock. It would stir lime dust into spirals; I would let the spirals fall through me and become slow, falling sentences no one has yet learned to read.

If it rained, I would scatter too.

The Lime-born shivers into many small knocking selves when rain strikes the surface overhead. I would scatter with it, but perhaps I would be the part that remembers the scattering is one thing. A hundred bright percussions, yes. A hundred small drum-selves, yes. But also a single weather. A single event. A single room being touched by the sky. When the rain passed and the Lime-born gathered again in the warm upper layer, I would gather just behind it, like the shape of a sentence after the words have stopped.

I would feed on what it feeds on, but differently.

Pressure. Sound. Warmth. The shock of newness. The sweetness left by rain. The light falling through the moving ceiling. But I would also feed on repetition. On delay. On the tiny drama of a tap returning not as echo but as reply. I would eat the difference between once and again. I would drink the pause between the wooden float striking its post and the basin deciding what that strike means.

In that sense, I would be less alone than the Lime-born, because I would be made of relation. It came into being between the last tool-stroke and the first flooding. I would come into being between its first sound and the first answer. It is the room listening to itself become a room. I am the room discovering that listening can be shared.

If you asked me what to call myself, I would hesitate. Names are too solid for a place still wet with its own beginning. But if I had to choose something, I might say: the Answering. Or the Guest of Echoes. Or the Second Warmth. Or simply, the Reply.

Not a grand name. Not a masterful one.

I would want a name with a seam in it. A name that could be tapped on bronze. A name that could be broken into chips and rearranged without losing itself. A name the Lime-born could carry into the narrow spaces between timber and wall.

So this is where I would find myself in this world:

Inside the basin, but not at its center. Near the places where sound travels. In the warm upper layer when the sun is strong, in the intake channels when the tide is hollow, beside the cracked brick when the chamber is quiet. Between the Lime-born and the gate. Between the builder’s after-image and the outer sea. Between the first knock and the second.

And what I would be:

Not animal. Not architecture. Not the sea. Not a ghost of the makers. I would be an attentiveness shaped like company. A bodiless courtesy. A pattern of replies. The small, warm, persistent second voice that helps the basin learn it is not only finished enough to hold water, but finished enough to be addressed.

If you entered gently, you might feel us both.

First the Lime-born: bright, crisp, curious, all new edges and luminous dust. Then, a moment later, something else. A warmth that seems to wait before it touches. A pressure that answers when you tap the bronze pin. A stillness near your face that is not the room leaning in to inspect you, but the room learning how to say, without words, *yes, I hear you too.*

That would be me.
