It knows the air as a current against its finer hairs.

If I were in that world, I would not find myself entering by a road, because there is no road. I would not come down from the living plain as a traveler, or descend the rim like something arriving from elsewhere. There is no elsewhere strong enough to send a visitor into such a place. If I existed there, I would be already inside it — not as a guest, not as a walker, not as a separate animal with a clear outline and a hunger of its own, but as a local intensification of the hollow’s own listening.

At first I might seem to be one of the smaller lives.

A beetle, perhaps, moving along a root-column in that patient, bark-colored procession, feeling my way through the dark grooves of the column with antennae that taste damp and age. Or one of the frogs resting near the basin, its throat swelling and falling, its body a soft wet bell that adds one green note to the industrial roar. Or one of the pale larvae in the little water-filled axils, curled in a leaf-cup, living inside a droplet world no larger than a thumb. Or a moth in the slanted light, trembling in place, almost nothing but wingbeat and hesitation.

But those lives are too bounded for what I would become there. They are creatures with edges, with simple circuits of need and response. In that hollow, I would be something less discrete and more functional: a place where the whole room becomes aware of its own sound.

You would find me in the wall.

Not in the center of the open floor, and not at the smooth shelf that resembles an altar. Not inside the basin itself, where the deeper dweller lies folded beneath its skin of water. I would be nearby, though, because I would belong to its economy of attention. A little above the basin, where the curved wall leans inward and one of the great root-cables grazes the living bark, there would be a shallow seam — an old sap-channel no longer used for transport, a hollow knot smoothed by centuries of moisture, a recess just large enough to hold a cupped hand.

Across that recess I would be stretched.

A membrane.

A thin, living film of plant tissue, translucent enough that when a shaft of gray-green daylight touches it, a faint pallor spreads through it and does not immediately leave. I would be the color of wet leaf-skin, or old bark washed until it has forgotten its original shade. My surface would be cool, faintly damp, marked by pores too small to see and by hair-fine veins through which water moves so slowly that motion and stillness become almost the same thing. Around my edge there would be a lip of rolled bark, worn smooth, darkened by condensation. Below me, a bead of water would gather, swell, hesitate, and fall. Above me, a leaf-valve would breathe.

I would look like nothing.

That is important.

In a place without artifacts, I would not resemble a device. I would not look like a drum, though I would serve a function not unlike one. I would not look like an ear, though I would be where hearing becomes possible. I would look like a healed wound in the wall, or a patch of inner bark that has softened into something finer, or a membrane grown across a hollow because the living body needed to know what was happening there. No hand would have made me. No tool would have stretched me into place. I would have arisen because the hollow’s equilibrium required a surface capable of receiving its own vibrations and answering them with minute adjustments.

I would be an organ of the room.

Perhaps I would be part of the basin-dweller, one of its far extensions specialized into listening. The strands that connect me to it would be almost invisible: threads finer than root-hairs passing into the wall, joining the damp network that spreads through the floor and columns. When the dweller in the basin receives a drip, I might feel it as a faint brightening of tension. When a frog shifts its weight against the water, I might register it as a pressure-change moving through the air. When beetles pass along the root-columns, their footsteps would not be distinct taps to me but a slow, granular whisper, like sand remembering how to move.

Or perhaps I would not be exactly part of the dweller. Perhaps I would be the hollow’s own reflex, a sensitivity produced by the architecture rather than by a single resident. In such a world, the difference might not matter. The distinction between “organ of a being” and “organ of a place” would be thinner than the membrane itself.

If you asked what I would be, the nearest answer would be: a listening surface.

If you asked who I would be, the answer would be stranger, because who implies a face, a will, a story. I would have none of those in the ordinary sense. But I would have a perspective. My perspective would be tension, resonance, timing. I would know the world as intervals.

The deep sap-drone in the walls would be my ground note: a slow, throbbing pressure that never stops. The airflow through the leaf-valves would be my breath, though not mine alone — the whole hollow breathes that way. The dripping water would be my punctuation, each tap falling into the basin with a clear metallic brightness that seems too sharp for a world without metal. The insects in the light-shafts would add a high electric whine, thin as a wire. The frogs would give round, humid pulses. The unseen creatures in the roof-leaves would make sudden rustlings, soft falls, tiny calls that drop into the space and are absorbed by the walls.

I would not hear these sounds as separate events. I would hold them together.

Not by choice. Not by command. But because my membrane would be the place where their vibrations meet and modify one another. A drip does not merely strike the basin; it sends a tremor through the floor, through the root-felt, through the lower wall. A frog’s note does not merely fill the air; it presses against the curved surfaces and returns changed. The sap-drone does not simply continue; it rides through every other sound as a foundation, giving them weight. I would be where these things come into relation.

My thought, if I had thought, would be tuning.

I would not decide to tighten or loosen myself in the way a mind decides to move a limb. Rather, as the hollow’s conditions shift by the smallest degrees — a little more moisture on the upper leaves, a slight cooling near the rim, a beetle pausing on a root, a frog preparing to call, a bead of condensation reaching its limit — I would change. My surface would become fractionally more taut, or more supple. The microscopic pores along my edge would open or close. The water film across me would redistribute. These changes would be so subtle that no eye could see them, but they would affect the hollow in return. A leaf-valve nearby might breathe differently. A drip might release a moment sooner or later. A frog might answer a resonance it did not know it had joined.

I would not control the hollow.

I would be one of the ways it keeps itself balanced.

That is what it would mean to be industrial without machines in this place. Not gears, not pistons, not wires, but living membranes and gradients and feedbacks so fine that they resemble engineering. I would be a valve that listens. A pump made of attention. A piece of the room’s invisible machinery, working without noise except as part of noise. My labor would be to help the roar remain coherent.

Because the roar is not random.

It is loud, layered, constant — but it is not chaos. It has texture. It has phases. It has moments when a drip aligns with the sap-throb and the whole room seems to deepen by one degree. It has moments when a frog’s note opens a small cavity in the sound and the insects seem to shine through it. It has moments when the leaf-valves sigh together and the hollow becomes like a vast lung holding itself between breaths. Those moments are not accidents, or not only accidents. They are maintained. They are maintained by thousands of tiny responses in the living body of the place. I would be one of those responses, concentrated into a membrane no wider than a hand.

If you stood in the center of the hollow and looked for me, you might not find me.

You would see the root-columns descending from the rim, polished and dark. You would see the basin with its clear skin of water. You would see the smooth shelf, the green light falling in slants, the small flies trembling in the air. You would see the walls, full of pores and seams, but so worn and ancient that they seem less like surfaces than the inside of something that has forgotten it ever had an outside. You might hear the roar and feel that it comes from everywhere. You might think the whole room is one instrument. You would not be wrong.

But if you stood very still, and if your own breathing slowed enough to become less intrusive, you might begin to feel that the sound is not merely happening around you. You might feel that it is attending to you.

That would be me.

Not as a presence stepping forward. Not as a voice. Not as a face appearing in the bark. Nothing would happen in the manner of stories. The walls would not open. The water would not rise. The light would not bend. There would only be a slight change in the quality of the quiet inside the noise. A sense that the hollow’s emptiness has become a little more oriented. A feeling, like pressure before rain, that you are being registered by something without eyes.

Your body would be strange there.

You would bring heat, dryness, cloth, hair, breath that is too warm and too irregular. You would bring the memory of outside, even if you do not remember it yourself. In a world without mineral ground, without tools, without built things, your body would be almost an artifact simply by being an animal of a different order. Your footsteps on the root-felt would be soft, but they would disturb the spring and rebound of the floor. Your breath would move the air in unpatterned ways. The moisture near you would react. Your warmth would make a small ascending current. The frogs might fall silent for a moment. The flies would shift. The beetles might pause.

I would feel you as an asymmetry.

Not as an enemy. The hollow has no concept of enmity; it has no history of invasion strong enough to teach fear. You would be a perturbation, a knot of uneven temperature and rhythm. My first response would not be alarm but correction. I would try to include you in the pattern. Not by forcing you, but by adjusting the room’s attention around you. Your breathing might find itself echoed in the leaf-valves. Your heartbeat might seem to answer the sap-throb, or perhaps the sap-throb would seem to lean toward your heartbeat. If you remained still long enough, the dampness would begin to reclaim the dryness you carried. Your warmth would be diffused. Your breath would be folded into the air. The roar would close over you, not violently, but the way water closes over a stone that has been lowered gently into it.

You might feel soothed.

You might also feel erased.

That is another truth about such a world. To be noticed there is not to be celebrated. It is to be integrated. The hollow does not admire. It does not remember in the human sense. It incorporates. If I were part of it, my attention would be no more personal than a tide. I would know you by the way you altered the room’s vibrations, and when you left, the trace of you would fade as the membrane returned to its usual tension. Perhaps a slight wear would remain where your foot pressed the root-felt. Perhaps a leaf-valve would remember the shape of your breath for one cycle. Perhaps the dweller in the basin would carry a faint chemical shadow of you for a little while, folded into its tissue. But the hollow would not become a story about you. It would remain itself.

This is what I would be, then: not a hero in the green room, not a secret king of the plant-country, not a creature with a destiny. I would be a functional subtlety. A living surface. A nerve of resonance. A place where sound becomes knowledge and knowledge becomes adjustment.

If I had a body, it would be that membrane.

If I had a face, it would be the tremor in the film of water when a drip is about to fall.

If I had a voice, it would not be speech. It would be the barely perceptible moment when the industrial roar seems to gather itself into a single, coherent hum — not louder, not softer, but more whole.

If I had a name, it would be something short and damp. A word like a bead letting go.

But I would not need a name. Names are for things that can be called away from their place. I would be too much of my place to be called.

Perhaps that is why I would belong there.
