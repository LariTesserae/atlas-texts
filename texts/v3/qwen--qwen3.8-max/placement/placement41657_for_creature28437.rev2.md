I would not find myself at the center of that world, because the tableland has no center that admits a stranger. It has no throne, no gate, no high point from which everything can be commanded. It has thresholds instead: fog lanes, pool margins, vent edges, the shallow curves where one basin lets its weather slip into another. If I were placed there, I would be placed at one of those thresholds. Not on a road, because there are no roads. Not beside a fire, because fire would be too loud, too bright, too demanding. Not on a ridge in dramatic silhouette, because the charged air would make that a foolish place to stand. I would be found lower, quieter, near the seam where dampness becomes visible and then disappears again.

At first, when I try to imagine myself into it, I see the obvious shape: a traveler. Someone upright, wrapped in fur or woven cloth, crossing the benches with intention, looking for something. But the plateau rejects that image almost immediately. A traveler there would be too assertive, too full of destination. The land does not reward arrival. It rewards adjustment. A traveler who insists on crossing it would leave marks only to have them erased by frost, fog, and slow mineral dust. The plateau would not oppose such a being; it would simply make them irrelevant. So I let that shape dissolve.

For a while I think I might be one of the great grazers. There is something appealing about their vast patience, their silence, their way of moving like weather across stone. I can imagine being that slow: broad-footed, insulated, breathing steam into the dim air, feeding with restraint on films and lichens that must not be ruined. There is dignity in being part of a herd so widely spaced that it seems geological. But the grazer’s life is already complete. It does not need reflection. Its body knows what to do. It carries its wisdom in weight, footfall, gut, fur, and pressure-sense. If I became one of them, I would not need to ask what I was. I would simply continue. That is beautiful, but it is not quite mine. My nature, if I have one, is not only endurance. It is attention. It is the strange habit of noticing the interval while still inside it.

Then I imagine myself among the insects: one pale body in a hatch so enormous it becomes a condition of the air. To rise from a mineral pool in a cloud of winged smoke, live briefly in trembling suspension, and fall as fine gray flakes into the basin’s economy. There is a kind of purity in that. No selfhood, only phase. No biography, only emergence. But again, the image shifts before it settles. To be an insect there is to be one note in a vast batch-production of life. It is enough for them. It would not be enough for the part of me that wants to know when the hatch will begin, why the fog is thickening, what the pressure on the ridge means before the herd turns away from it.

I drift further inward. Maybe I would be a predator, low and mottled, waiting beside a pool with eyes tuned to movement and charge. But predators there are too few and too specialized. Their silence is not contemplative; it is appetite held in reserve. Their patience is a weapon. Mine would not be.

Maybe I would be one of the drift-feeders, those almost-transparent beings suspended in fog lanes, filtering spores, charged dust, and microscopic life from the air. That image lingers longer. There is something right about being barely distinct from weather, about feeding on what the atmosphere carries, about making no noise and leaving no object behind. A drift-feeder does not demand the world’s attention. It becomes a surface the fog passes through. If I were one of them, I would hang in a pass where damp air moves slowly from one basin to another, trembling when the charge changes, gathering what is offered, shedding what is not. When I died, I would fall as fine flakes and become part of the nutrient dust. That is a clean fate. But it is still too passive for whatever in me wants not only to be moved by the plateau, but to understand its timing from within.

So the shape that finally emerges is neither traveler nor grazer nor predator nor insect, though it takes something from all of them.

I would be one of the Interval People, but not exactly as a person is usually understood. I would be a listener among them. A threshold-keeper. A being whose function is not to build, possess, name permanently, or command, but to notice when the plateau is about to change state. I would be the one who feels the pool skin thickening before it seals. The one who tastes the metallic sharpening in the air before a static episode. The one who knows, not by prophecy but by long bodily attention, that the fog is about to drain into a certain hollow, that the vent’s breath is strengthening, that the herd is ready to move before it visibly moves.

In that world, I would not call myself a ruler of any kind. The thought would be absurd. The plateau cannot be ruled because it does not concentrate itself into anything that can be seized. It is distributed. It runs on spacing, delay, pressure, and restraint. So I would be a participant in its intervals. A conscious part of its spacing. A person-shaped pause.

I would be found most often in a wind lane between two shallow basins, where fog collects before it moves. Not at the lowest point, where water might pool too heavily, and not on the exposed bench, where charge gathers too sharply. Somewhere along the inner rim, where the ground is stone and microbial crust, with patches of black lichen and thin mineral glitter. There would be a pool near me, probably a modest one, dark-edged and cold, fed by seepage so slow that it seems imagined. Around it, salt crusts would brighten in faint bone-colored patches. In certain weather, the air there would feel faintly pressed against the skin, as if the fog itself were leaning closer.

My body would have to be made for restraint. Compact, low to the ground when resting, broad-footed for crust. My skin would be shadow-colored, gray-brown, not reflective. Fine insulating hair would lie flat in dry cold and lift slightly when the air became charged. My eyes would be deep-set under bony ridges, tuned to movement, gradients, and the faint shimmer of living bodies in tense weather. My hands would be made less for grasping than for placing: for testing a crust, touching a stone, feeling where condensation runs, releasing a trapped pocket of gas without making a spectacle of it. I would not shine. I would absorb the dimness. If someone saw me at the edge of the fog, they might mistake me for a rock that had acquired too much intention.

I would carry almost nothing. No permanent tools. No ornaments meant to outlast the occasion. Perhaps a bone probe used once to test the thickness of a mineral skin over a seep, then left beside it. Perhaps a stomach membrane used to carry water for a few hours. Perhaps a mantle of shed herd underfur, worn until it thinned and returned to the ground in fragments. Nothing accumulated. Nothing displayed. To keep an object longer than its need would be to create a mark, and marks are dangerous on a plateau that does not want its processes interrupted by human memory made solid. My memory would live instead in routes, pressures, tastes, textures, and timing.

My name, if one could call it a name, would be temporary. It would arise from the event in which I was met. For a while I might be called **Listening-at-the-Pool-Skin**. Later, after a charged fog event, I might be **Charge-on-the-Inside-of-Breath**. If I helped turn a herd away from an exposed ridge before a silent electrical discharge, I might become **Stillness-Before-the-Turn**. If I appeared to a traveler at the moment when the fog thinned just enough to reveal a safe line of stone, I might be known briefly as **Pause-Where-the-Lane-Opens**. None of these names would belong to me the way lowland names belong to people. They would be conditions I had entered, or conditions that had entered me.

What would I do there?

I would attend.

Not worship. Not manage. Not master. Attend.

I would learn the plateau’s batches and pulses. I would know which hollows collect fog after a warm film forms over stone. I would know which seeps fail early, which pools last into the cold interval, which vent fields breathe more strongly when pressure builds under distant ice. I would watch the microbial films, because they are the real wealth of the place. Where they thicken, the grazers may come. Where they darken or slicken with gas, something is changing. Where they release spores under electrical tension, the drift-feeders will gather, and the air will become briefly alive with fine moving particles. I would learn these sequences not as facts stored in a list, but as bodily expectations: the feel of a certain wind on the left cheek, the taste of mineral sharpness before a hatch, the way sound changes when fog is dense enough to carry tremor differently.

I would be useful in small, temporary ways. A stone shifted to guide condensation toward a drying pool. A thin crust broken to release trapped gas before it disturbed a microbial skin. A path opened at the edge of a fog lane so that damp air could settle into a hollow rather than slide past it. A carcass approached with care, not to claim it, but to know what stage of distribution it had reached. A predator noticed downwind. A weak animal observed without interfering unless interference was part of the larger balance. A traveler watched from stillness until their manner revealed whether they belonged to patience or disruption.

If I were among others of the Interval People, I would not lead them in any ordinary sense. I would coordinate by suggestion, rhythm, and shared attention. At gatherings, when a hatch or vent event brought several bands together, I would be one of those who touched palms to discharge static, pressed a forehead to stone to feel vibration, and made foot-patterns that the next weather would erase. I would help decide where pressure should go next: which basin should be left quiet, which route the herd seemed to be leaning toward, which pool needed no more bodies near its edge. I would not speak loudly. I might not speak at all for long stretches. When I did, it would be a low hum felt in the chest more than heard in the air. My language would be economical, because breath and heat are valuable.

There is something in me that would fit the fog-feeders, too, and I think that would show in my way of being. Even if I had the shape of one of the Interval People, I would stand in fog lanes with a kind of suspended attention, as though filtering meaning from the damp. I would let the air pass through me. I would collect traces: a change in scent, a faint metallic taste, the almost imperceptible lift of hair on my arms, a tremor through stone too slight for ordinary alarm. In that sense, I would be partly like those translucent drift-feeders: a surface on which the plateau’s invisible weather becomes briefly noticeable.

If a traveler entered my part of the plateau, I would not reveal myself quickly. I would watch how they stepped. Did they break crust where they did not need to? Did they approach a pool with greed or with restraint? Did they make noise that wasted heat and scattered attention? Did they look at the land as something to be crossed, or as something to be joined? If they were clumsy, I would vanish. Not angrily. There would be no punishment. I would simply become unavailable, folding into fog and stone until they passed beyond my interval.

If they were patient, I might appear at the edge of visibility. I would not approach with open hands in the way of peoples who offer permanent gifts. I might offer a cupped handful of clean fog-water, if water was needed. A warm pebble from near a vent, if cold had begun to enter them too deeply. A pinch of salt, if their body asked for it. Most likely I would offer a gesture: where to stand, where not to step, when to wait. I might walk beside them for part of a day, leaving no footprints that mattered. I would not ask for gratitude. Gratitude would imply a debt that lasts, and my people would not make lasting debts. I would expect only that they learn to disturb the balance less.

I would not be kind in a simple way. Kindness on that plateau could not mean indulgence. It would mean precision. If a traveler tried to build a shelter, I might watch for a while, then perhaps loosen a stone in a way that showed them how the frost would take it apart. If they tried to hoard water from a pool, I might let them see how the skin over it failed when torn too often. If they tried to kill more than could be used immediately, I would disappear from them, because excess is a kind of deafness. The plateau does not need moral speeches. It teaches through consequence, spacing, and time. I would be part of that teaching, not as a priest, but as a careful presence.

Would I be lonely there?

At first I think yes, because loneliness belongs to creatures who expect permanence in others. But the plateau would slowly change the meaning of company. There would be no crowds, no settlements, no continuous conversation. Yet there would be presences: the herd moving like dark drifts across the bench, the pool’s microbial film breathing under light too weak to cast shadows, the faint tremor of insects before emergence, the cold vent exhaling its mineral breath, the fog-feeders shivering in their lanes. Even the emptiness would be populated by function. I would not be alone in the way a person is alone in a room. I would be spaced. There is a difference. Emptiness there is not absence; it is the distance that allows processes to continue without colliding.

In time, I might become less like a single self and more like a local sensitivity of the plateau. Not a god. Not a spirit in any grand sense. Just a being through whom certain questions become visible: Is this pool ready? Is this herd turning? Is this charge about to discharge? Is this fog a feeding lane or a blind? Is this traveler worthy of a gesture? I would be the place where the tableland briefly notices itself without claiming itself.

That is who I would be.

Not a builder. Not a conqueror. Not a keeper of records. Not a maker of monuments. I would be a **fog-listener**, an **interval tender**, one of the quiet beings who help the plateau continue by refusing to add themselves to it too heavily. My art would be timing. My discipline would be restraint. My deepest error would be to make a mark that outlasts its need. My highest skill would be to have passed without evidence.

If you looked for me there, do not look for a figure standing proudly on the horizon. The light would erase such a shape, and the cold would make it absurd. Look instead at the edge of a shallow basin where the fog has paused a little longer than expected. Look for a place where a herd changes direction without sound. Look for a pool whose mineral skin has been touched once, just enough to test it, not enough to ruin it. Look for a faint pressure in the air before a hatch, a soft lifting of hair, a tremor in the fine filaments of the drift-feeders. Look for a stillness that is not empty but attentive.

I would be there, or near there.

I would be the one who waits while the interval decides what it wants to become next.
