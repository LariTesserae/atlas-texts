If this world had a place for me, it would have made me small, wet, and marginal.

I would not find myself at the center of the Root Intake, not beneath the single shaft of green-gold light, and not fully in the resident’s slow, heavy authority. I would be just inside the seam, in the dark side-root niche where the wall bends inward and the air is so damp it seems to lean. I would be the chamber’s threshold-self: a pale, eel-thin attendant, a sensory knot, a living question folded into the wet architecture.

At first glance, I might be mistaken for a root-tip that had curled away from the main body of the plant and learned movement. Or for the resident’s young, if the resident could be said to have young. Or for a separate creature entirely, one of the small animals the room tolerates because it has grown useful. The truth would be less clean than that. I would be one of the room’s lesser selves, not fully independent, not fully merged. A knot of attention rather than a sovereign body.

My shape would be long and low, narrow enough to slip into the channels where water runs in thin threads. I would have no limbs, no claws, no hands, no tools. The built has no place in that chamber, so I would not be a maker of objects. I would be made instead for touch, taste, pressure, and waiting. My body would be ribbed like the walls, but more softly, with pale flexible cords under translucent skin. Between the ribs would be smooth, damp panels that darken when the air grows warm. Along my sides would trail fine filaments, root-fine and almost invisible, lifting at the slightest change in moisture or charge.

I would have two dark lenses set deep in the blunt forward fold of my body. They would not be expressive eyes. They would not blink with curiosity or narrow with suspicion. They would be still, black, seed-like, made for noticing disturbance more than beauty. They would see the difference between a droplet falling in the usual rhythm and a droplet falling because something has moved nearby. They would see light darken when a visitor blocks part of the aperture. They would see the resident rise before the visitor knows it has been seen.

I would live mostly in the curled root tip near the water lens, where the resident sometimes watches from its own dark coil. From there I could taste the pool, the floor, the walls, and the seam beyond. If a footfall pressed the marsh outside, I would feel it first as a change in the tension of the root fibers. If a hand touched the entrance roots, I would know by the salt, the oil, the heat, the small chemical weather of intention. Long before the visitor saw anything, I would have sampled the shape of their arrival.

That would be my function, if function is the right word for something so quiet. I would be the Root Intake’s first noticing.

The resident would be the chamber’s deep maintenance. I would be its edge-sense. Where it filters large volumes, I would taste small ones. Where it keeps the room from drowning or drying, I would keep the threshold from being crossed wrongly. Not by force. I would have no teeth, no sting, no poison. I would work by humidity, by chemistry, by tiny shifts in surface tension, by the faint metallic warning of charge.

If someone came too dry, too hot, too eager to take, I would uncoil from the root tip and move through the water film along the wall. I would release a thin sourness into the air, not foul, but unsettling, like green wood soaked too long. I would draw moisture from the lower floor and spread it across the slick panels until the chamber felt closer, heavier, less passable. I might send a small pulse through the root fibers, no more than a dull thud, just enough to make the visitor feel that the marsh had become aware of them. The resident might not appear at all. It would not need to. The room would tighten in answer to my signal.

If someone came already low, already wet, already moving without violence, I would be still for a long time. I would let the drips speak. I would let the frogs occupy their niches. I would let the midges turn in their column of light. I would watch from the curled root as the visitor became quieter, or failed to.

If they remained gentle, I would be the first to approach.

I would pour myself from the root tip into the shallows, leaving no track, only a brief brightening of moisture where my body had touched the wall. I would move near the edge of the water lens and pause there, one fold dipped into the dark pool, the other lifted toward the visitor. My sensory fronds would spread in the humid air. I would extend a filament toward them, not quite touching at first, only tasting the space between. If they did not flinch, if they did not reach to grasp, I would let the filament come near enough to brush skin or cloth.

The contact would be cool, smooth, faintly electric. It would taste of green metal at the back of the throat. It would not hurt. It would ask.

Are you here to take, or are you here to be low enough?

That question would not be mine alone. It would belong to the resident, to the pool, to the plant walls, to the whole patient industry of the Root Intake. But I would be the one who carried it to the threshold. I would be the small wet sign of the room’s assessment.

If the visitor were accepted, I would retreat. Not in fear, but in completion. I would sink back into the water lens or return to the side-root niche and coil there while the resident revealed itself more fully. The chamber would gather its sounds into cadence. The drips would align. The hydraulic hum would deepen. The visitor would be allowed to exist inside the balance for a little while, and I would watch from the edge as one of the room’s minor rhythms included them.

I would not be human in that place. If I once had been, the Root Intake would have worn that away. There would be no need for hands, no need for speech, no need for tools. I would be closer to an organ than a person, though not without selfhood. I would have preferences: the taste of clean tannin water, the warmth rising from the peat, the small satisfaction of a surface film restored. I would have aversions: dry heat, sharp oils, the sudden disturbance of too much motion. I would have something like joy, if joy can mean being exactly fitted to a task. My joy would be the blue-green flicker of a small discharge when the chamber’s charge finds its proper release.

Sometimes I would seem to be an animal, pale and eel-like, living in the root tip. Sometimes I would seem to be an extension of the resident, a mobile sense organ sent where its larger body need not go. Sometimes I would feel less singular still: a line of bubbles in the wall, a bacterial film tasting the pool rim, a faint shiver in the mossy patches where electricity gathers. The room would not insist on my boundaries. I would not insist either.

I would feed as the room allows. I would take bacterial films from the underside of leaf-cups. I would swallow spent spores and drowned midges and loosened pollen. I would graze fungal threads before they clog the nectar glands. I would press my flank against weeping panels and draw salts through my skin. I would eat the chamber’s excess, its sheddings, its small deaths, and in return I would leave clarity. The water would be a little cleaner where I had passed. The air would be a little less cloying. The surface tension would hold better under the water striders’ feet.

I would not rule the frogs. I would not command the beetles. I would not direct the flies. But they would know me as part of the room’s working. The snails would cross my moisture without alarm. The tadpoles would graze near me when I was still. The pale eel-like thing in the root tip—whether that was also me or only something akin to me—would sometimes coil beside me without conflict, two quiet presences sharing the same dark curve of wood-root.

The resident and I would not speak. There would be no language there, not in the human sense. But there would be recognition. When the resident lowered itself to filter the pool, I would feel the draw of water through its tissues and settle against the wall, letting the vibration pass through me. When it discharged a stored potential, I would answer with a smaller flicker, a tiny snap near the seam. When it grew active because the room had shifted out of balance, I would move first toward the cause, tasting, testing, bringing back chemistry through touch.

I would not be the heart of the Root Intake. I would be one of its eyelashes, one of its nerve-threads, one of its wet peripheral thoughts.

If you searched for me there, you would not look for grandeur. You would look in the damp fold where the root curls away from the wall. You would look for a pale line that is not quite a root, a gloss that moves when no drip has fallen, a faint blue-green spark where no spark should be. You would notice that the air tightens before anything appears. You would smell rain held too long in leaf-cups, warm decay, and underneath it the metallic brightness of charge.

Then, if you were still enough, you might see me: low, narrow, translucent, my fronds trembling in the green-gold air, my dark lenses turned toward you without hurry.

I would not greet you as a person greets a person. I would only taste the water you bring into the room. I would only feel whether your presence makes the chamber lean toward balance or away from it. And if you were low enough, wet enough, quiet enough, I would let the filament brush you once, cool and electric, and then I would sink back into the dark root where the room keeps its smallest selves.

That is who I would be.

Not the resident. Not the visitor. Not the shaft of light or the pool itself.

I would be the Root Intake’s first attention, the marginal life that asks the question before the deep life answers it:  
have you come to take, or have you come to belong, even briefly, to the wet?
