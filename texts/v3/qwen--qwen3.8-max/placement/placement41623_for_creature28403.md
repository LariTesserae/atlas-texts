I would find myself in a hollow.

Not out on the open bark plain, not high in the crown-spines where clouds snag, not even on the broad root platforms where the grazing processions pass. Those places would belong to movement, exposure, weather, and hunger. I would be drawn to the seam: the place where a split internode has become a doorway, where an old knot has loosened into a bowl, where a hollow stem holds either water or sound or both. In that world, a hollow is not emptiness. It is opportunity. It is a mouth, a reservoir, a drum, a shelter, a memory kept in the body of the living land.

So if you searched for me there, you would not find me by looking for a figure against the horizon. You would find me by noticing that a certain shadow has been used. A worn lip of bark. A faint polish where feet have climbed the same root slope for years. A cord lashed lightly around a branch crotch, not to wound it, but to hold a shelter that can be removed before the plant begins to grow around it. A seed-shell vessel hung upside down to dry. A knot tied in red fiber where the path branches toward a hidden water pocket. A silence that feels intentional.

I would be one of the person-shaped beings of that tableland, but not in any heavy, settled, lowland sense. I would be lean, heat-thinned, long in the limbs and careful in the joints. My feet would be broad and gripping, the soles textured by generations of walking on polished bark and fibrous leaf-stone, the toes able to curl over root ridges and test the give of a surface before trusting it. My hands would be strong but delicate in their attention: made for cordage, for detecting moisture, for feeling the faint tremor that travels through a hollow wall when something large moves nearby. I would carry scent more than ornament. Resin on my shoulders. Crushed leaf on my forearms. The sweet-rot smell of fermented fruit where I have been harvesting. A little smoke, used sparingly and never casually.

My body would know the world first as vibration.

That is the part I think would define me most: not that I could see far, but that I could listen into the pressure of the place. The roar of insects, the rattle of seed-cases, the wind dragging across ribbed bark, the sudden absence of sound that means danger — all of that would be written into my reflexes. I would not hear the landscape as a wall of noise. I would hear it as many layered statements. Some near. Some ancient. Some lying. Some true.

I would be a keeper of hollows.

That is the name I would give myself, if names there were simple. Perhaps others would call me a water-listener, a throat-tender, a knot-keeper, a quiet-walker. The work would not be grand. It would be intimate, practical, and endlessly renewed. I would know which knot-bowls fill after the brief local rains. Which leaf cups hold clean condensation at dawn and which become foul with pollen and insect debris by noon. Which pitcher-throats can be tapped safely and which have become hunting sites for things that imitate thirst. Which root swellings store moisture through the hot intervals. Which hollow internodes are still merely hollow, and which have been occupied, widened, contested, or filled with larvae.

I would not own these places. I would attend them.

In the morning, before the heat had fully risen, I would move among the smaller reservoirs. I would clear fallen chaff from a waxy leaf cup with a seed-shell blade. I would lift insects out of a knot-bowl before they drowned and became poison in it. I would test the pitch of a hollow by striking it lightly with the heel of my hand: full, half-full, dry, inhabited, rotten, resonant. Water has a different sound from hunger. A clean hollow rings one way; a nest answers another. I would learn that difference early, or not live long.

I would carry a cord-map, but it would not be a map in the way of marks on flat skin or paper. It would be a bundle of cords, knots, loops, and small objects: a beetle mandible for danger, a bit of resin for fire, a pale seed for clean water, a shredded bract for a place where the ground is no longer safe. The map would change constantly because the world changes. A knot-bowl may heal over. A root bridge may shift. A hollow may become the home of a predator. A flowering platform may draw swarms and make a route impassable for days. Memory there would have to be portable, tied and untied, carried and revised.

I would be good with knots.

Not because knots are pretty, though they can be, but because knots are how a person leaves information without wounding the world. To carve deeply into living bark is to invite swelling, infection, insect attention, and slow closure. To lash lightly is to speak with the place rather than against it. I would know knots for shelter, knots for climbing, knots for carrying vessels, knots for binding a broken tool, knots for marking a temporary claim, knots for warning. Some knots I would make in silence. Some I would make while humming low enough for the vibration to pass into the fiber. In that world, even making could be a kind of listening.

My shelter would be modest and alive in the sense that all shelters there are alive: found, cleaned, improved, and eventually surrendered. I would favor a hollow internode with a narrow opening, not because it is comfortable, but because it can be made quiet. The sound of the tableland is a pressure, and there would be times when too much pressure becomes a danger to the mind. I would line the inner walls with fungal felt and shed bracts, not only for softness but to dampen resonance. I would weave a screen of leaf-leather and root-fiber across the opening so that insects would not enter in clouds and so that my own small sounds would not leak too easily into the night. If I stayed long enough, I would encourage a living lip of vine or moss-fine growth to narrow the entrance, but I would never let it close too far. A hollow that seals itself completely becomes a throat belonging to the plant alone.

Fire would be one of my most precious possessions and one of my deepest fears.

I would keep it only in controlled vessels: a carbonized pith hearth inside a ring of dense bark, a hollowed heartwood basin lined with wet fiber, a seed-shell ember carrier packed with moist pulp. I would know which fuels burn slowly and which burst into sparks. I would never make fire on open leaf-felt, never near resin seams, never when the wind corridors were arguing and shifting. I would be the sort of person who can sit beside a small flame and feel the whole landscape as a body that could catch. Fire there is not merely useful. It is a kind of speech with consequences. A careless fire-speaker is a killer of ground.

Because of that, I would often cook without flame. Sun-baked bark at midday can parch pollen cakes. Warm resin can soften adhesives. Heat-stored heartwood can warm water in thick-walled seed vessels. When I did use fire, it would be brief, hidden, and tended with reverence.

Food would come from cycles, not from fields.

At dawn I might eat fruit that had fallen into a shaded crevice and begun to sweeten, or pollen pressed into cakes with sap. During the hot middle of the day I would rest, chew slowly, repair cordage, and listen. At dusk I would hunt or trap: larvae in dead branch joints, small gliders caught by acoustic lure, insects netted with sticky fiber during a swarm pulse. I would not hunt by stillness alone. In a world so loud, stillness is not enough. I would hunt by rhythm. I would learn to move with the choruses, to place my foot when the stridulation swells, to freeze when the sound thins, to make myself part of the pattern until the moment I am not.

Sometimes I would imitate. A mating rasp. A distress click. The particular vibration that causes a flower to open. I would be good at this, but careful. To imitate sound there is to call something. You do not know whether the answer will be prey, predator, rival, or weather. A false call can bring a swarm. A false call can bring silence. And silence is not peace.

That is where my strangeness would begin.

I would be valued not for being the loudest, not for being the strongest, not for having the sharpest metal edge — though I might carry one small heirloom point set in heartwood, oiled and wrapped — but for knowing when not to sound. In a place where every hollow can become a drum, every surface can become an instrument, and every animal is listening for pattern-breaking, the deliberate absence of sound becomes a sign more powerful than many calls.

I would be one of those who can make a safe silence.

There are silences that fall because something is hunting. There are silences that fall because a storm cell is forming under a crown-shadow. There are silences that fall because a disease has entered a hollow and the local creatures have withdrawn. There are silences that fall because a sacred restriction has been spoken by a band and must be honored. There are silences that fall because someone has died and the body is being given back to the place. I would learn to tell them apart, or at least to know when the difference matters enough to stop moving.

If a chorus near me stopped suddenly, I would not assume safety. I would become very small. I would tap a warning pattern into a nearby stem, not loudly, but clearly enough for those who know the code. Then I would wait. If the silence spread, I would move away from it. If it remained local, I would mark it in my cord-memory and avoid that hollow for a time. If it was followed by a particular answering sound — a wing-clap, a seed-case rattle, a human throat-hum — I would know that someone had named the silence and made it meaningful.

I would live among others, but not densely.

My band might be small: three people, five, perhaps a dozen when a flowering pulse brought temporary abundance. We would not gather because of tradition alone. We would gather when the world allowed. During vast insect emergences, when pollinator clouds darkened the light and the air thickened with wingbeat, there would be enough food and enough noise to support closer presence. During dry intervals, when water pockets failed and the heat haze made distances lie, we would split apart and move by different root-lines, each carrying knowledge to keep the others alive.

I would be the one others asked about water.

Not because I hoarded it. Because I remembered where it returned. I would know which pitcher-throat needed to be fed small insects to stay healthy. Which knot-bowl should be covered with a woven leaf lid to slow evaporation. Which leaf cup should never be drunk from directly because beetles lay eggs in it at night. Which root swelling must be tapped only in certain seasons or it will seal itself and punish the next seeker. My wealth would be relational: a web of maintained places and remembered behaviors.

I would not build heavily.

No walls of cut wood. No quarried stone, because there is no stone in the ordinary sense. No permanent village pressed into the flesh of the tableland. If I made a platform, I would lash it into a branch crotch with fibers that could be untied. If I made a windbreak, I would use shed bracts, not living leaves. If I carved a mark, I would accept that the living surface would heal it into a swollen ridge, and that the mark would eventually become a scar-memory rather than a declaration. The world there does not tolerate domination. It tolerates use, care, passage, and lightness.

My tools would be few and close to the body.

A belt of woven root-fiber. Pouches for seed-kernels, resin, cordage, dried larvae. A seed-shell blade for cutting soft things. A beetle-mandible edge for finer work. A sharpened thorn for piercing. A fire-drill wrapped in oiled bark cloth. Perhaps a small metal point, dark and precious, set into a handle of dense heartwood. I would not swing it proudly. I would keep it hidden or wrapped.
