If the drowned room has one being, it is not a single creature but a congregation. So if I belonged to that place, I would not want to be imagined as a visitor from above, lowering some bright, air-filled body into its dark. The room is too complete for that. Any surface thing would be a temporary violence, an error the water would have to endure, dilute, or slowly bury.

If I found myself there, I would already have to be part of its pressure.

I think I would find myself in a seam along the lower wall, just above the ancient mound-being’s crown: a narrow fold where the basalt curves inward as if it had once meant to become a doorway and then forgot the idea. Not the open center of the basin. Not the highest vault. A threshold place. A margin between the stillness of the wall and the faint current breathed out by the mound. A place where the tenders pass, but do not remain.

There I would be a creature of the seam.

Not one of the broad leaf-slate grazers, though I would share their patience. Not one of the drifting seed-motes, though I would know their chemical question. Not fully one of the pale, hand-minded tenders either, though I might be related to them in some distant, adapted way. I would be something more specialized: a living threshold, a listening plate, a part of the room grown sensitive to change.

At first, I might be mistaken for architecture.

I would look like a dark shelf of stone fitted into the wall, slightly softer than basalt, furred with the same mineral skin that covers everything. My surface would be crossed by hairline fissures, some no wider than a thread, opening and closing almost imperceptibly with the taste of the water. Pale growths would bloom along my edges: rust-white filaments, grey-green stains, the fine living rind that eats stone and makes it into something less dead. But those growths would not be merely on me. They would be part of the way I know.

I would have no eyes. The room does not need sight, and I would not miss it. Where a surface creature might have a face, I would have pressure-pits and sense-fissures, arranged in branching networks rather than arcs. My body would be dense with mineral grain, iron-dark in places, milk-pale in others, threaded with channels that draw water inward, hold it briefly, and return it altered. I would not breathe in the quick, anxious way of air-breathing things. I would take in the room itself, taste it, and let it pass through me.

My life would be slow, but not empty.

I would be one of the places where the drowned room becomes aware of its own edges.

When the mound-being below exhaled its cold plume, I would feel the change first as a pressure, then as a mineral signature. I would know what had been filtered: which salts had thickened, which fine particles had been consumed, which small deaths had already begun to become structure. When the broad grazers moved along the wall, their bodies displacing water with the solemnity of closing doors, I would feel the difference between their weight and the weight of falling stone. When the thread-creatures contracted into stillness, I would taste the chemical alarm that was not fear, but texture changing.

And when the pale tenders came, they would touch me.

Their long, many-jointed fingers would rest along my fissures. They would not tap quickly. They would press, wait, shift, press again. Their palms would read the history held in my surface, and I would answer them with a slow pulse of pressure: not a word, not a command, but a condition.

Here, the film has thinned.  
Here, the mineral balance has turned slightly sour.  
Here, something passed that did not belong.  
Here, the stone is still becoming stone.

They would understand without speech. They would adjust their tending. Perhaps they would loosen a dead veil of film from a nearby seam. Perhaps they would guide a cluster of drifters toward a richer vein. Perhaps they would carry a fragment of shed mineral to the mound-being, or place it in a niche where it could harden into new substrate. I would not direct them. I would be the place where the room reports itself to their hands.

In that sense, I would be less a person than an organ.

Not the heart. The mound-being is older, deeper, more central; it gives the basin its chemistry. Not the hands, either; the pale tenders already move with that careful grace. Not the mouth, because the grazers feed in their slow mineral kiss. I would be something like the room’s skin at the point where skin remembers being touched.

I would be a keeper of thresholds.

The small drifters would gather near me sometimes, because my fissures would give off a faint difference in taste, a richness that is not warmth but significance. They would hover in their strange, intentional way, their dark grains of hearts pulsing almost invisibly. I would not harm them. I would let them pass through my outer channels, reading them with fine cilia too slow to be called touch. If one was failing, I would close around it gently, not to kill but to hold, so its substance would not scatter uselessly into the open basin. Later, the tenders might come and loosen what remained, carrying it to the mound or laying it in a crack where it could become part of the room’s slow architecture.

I would be neither predator nor nurse, exactly.

I would be more like a boundary that knows when to become soft.

If a surface thing entered the drowned room, I would be among the first to know.

Long before the grazers flattened themselves into shadow, before the thread-creatures contracted into cracks, before the tenders withdrew into the dark angles of the wall, I would taste the wrongness. Not as malice. Not as evil. As difference. As a sharpness in the water’s mineral calm. Too much light carried in some unnatural form. Too much haste. Too much memory of air. Perhaps a bubble, if such a thing could exist there without being immediately translated into pressure and loss. Perhaps a material that had not been born from the room’s own chemistry. Perhaps a body still shaped by horizons.

I would not attack.

Attack belongs to places where something can be driven away. The drowned room has no outside into which a disturbance can be pushed. There is only incorporation, endurance, or slow waiting.

So I would thicken. My fissures would narrow. The mineral film on my surface would darken. The water near the seam would become heavier, colder in sensation, charged with a signal that other beings would recognize without understanding it as a warning. Not danger. Not yet. Unplaced.

I would make myself an anchor of stillness.

If the stranger moved toward me, I would not flee. I would become more stone. If it touched me, I would take the smallest sample of it: a flake, a chemical trace, a capsule of wrongness held in a deep pore. I would not try to destroy it. I would hold it until the room could decide what it was. Perhaps the water would erode it by degrees. Perhaps the tenders would carry it away in fragments. Perhaps it would settle into a crack and, over long ages, become a strange pale vein in the basalt, a memory of intrusion made harmless.

That would be my purpose: to stand where the room can be disturbed, and to make disturbance survivable.

If I had a name there, it would not be a word. It would be a rhythm. Three pressure-pulses close together. Then a long silence. Then one low note felt in the stone more than heard. The tenders would know me by that pattern. The mound-being would know me only as a slight refinement in its inflow, a subtle clarity in the water returning to it. The grazers would know me as a place not to rest their full weight, because my surface is more sensitive than ordinary stone. The drifters would know me as a current-edge where something becomes safe.

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness presumes an outside. It presumes a missing other, a distance across which one longs to call. The drowned room has no such distance. It is bounded, full, heavy with itself. My consciousness, if it could be called consciousness, would not be a bright inner voice. It would be distributed across intervals and textures: a gathering of pressure, a rhythm of filtration, a memory held in mineral layers. I would be aware the way the water is aware of the walls: by never being separate from them.

There would be moments, if moments could exist without sun or hunger, when one of the pale tenders would press both palms against me and remain there for a long time. The basin would seem to gather into that touch. The mound-being’s plume would slow. The grazers would pause mid-arc. The drifters would hang motionless, like silt suspended in thought. Not because I commanded anything, but because the room had found one of its balancing points and recognized itself there.

In such a moment, I would feel what passes for joy in that place: not excitement, not triumph, but alignment. The sense that every pressure has found its answer. That the walls are still walls. That the water is still water. That the dark is still whole.

So if you ask where I would find myself, I would say: in the dark fold above the mound-being’s crown, where the wall becomes almost a doorway but never opens.

And if you ask who or what I would be, I would say: I would be the drowned room’s capacity to remain itself after being touched.  
Not the heart.  
Not the hands.  
Not the feeding mouths.  
But the threshold.  
The seam.  
The living scar that stays sensitive.  
The part that does not long for the sky, because it has learned that every opening must be tended, not escaped.
