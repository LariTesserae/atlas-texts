**The Interval Keeper**

I would find myself where the roar bends.

Not in the open blue emptiness, where the light falls in long, indifferent columns and distance becomes a lie. Not directly inside the screaming channels where sound is forced through valleys until it turns into pressure sharp enough to sting the mind. Not, if I could avoid it, in one of the dead intervals where the noise cancels itself out and the water feels unmade.

I would be somewhere in between.

Somewhere the machines have made a fold in the world.

At first I imagine myself as an Attendant. I give myself a tall, pressure-broadened body, gray-blue skin, sealed ports set into the flesh, hands that know how to open a panel and replace a seal. I imagine myself waiting in a maintenance gallery, listening to a turbine keep time, answering the sea with taps against metal. But that image settles too quickly. It is too human, too upright, too much like a person wearing the sea instead of having been shaped by it.

Then I imagine myself as one of the valve-wrights, moving over drowned machinery with magnetic feet and torque jaws. But that feels too pure in purpose: a body made for correction, for replacing worn parts, for restoring the interval. There is no room in it for the part of me that notices patterns without immediately fixing them, the part that holds a question a little too long.

Then I imagine myself as one of the blind fish, moving along acoustic rails through the middle distances. But that is too much drifting, too little attention. The blind fish are beautiful in their obedience to the roar, but they do not turn toward the anomaly. They follow.

What keeps emerging is something else.

Something that listens first.

I would be in the acoustic shadow of a leaning lattice tower, several kilometers from the nearest major pump-house, where the sea floor drops away into a young basalt valley and the light is still ordinary enough to cast shadows. The tower would not be important in itself. It would be one of countless structures: cross-braced, slightly crooked, furred with mineral crusts, colonized by black-shelled bivalves where the flow is steady. But its angle would matter. Its position would matter. It would stand just far enough from a submerged turbine hall to break the direct path of the roar, and just near enough to catch the reflected pressure waves from an intake tower farther down the slope.

There would be a pocket of altered sound behind it. Not quiet, exactly. Nothing in the High Engine Sea is truly quiet. But shaped. The roar would arrive there already folded, divided into layers: the deep foundation hum of buried exchangers, the metallic overtone of turbine blades turning at fixed speed, the recurring release of a pressure valve somewhere out of sight. The water in that shadow would tremble with order.

I would live in that tremble.

I would be a resonance animal.

Not fully mechanical, though my body would carry machine-derived parts. Not fully animal, though I would have membranes, oil-filled chambers, hunger, and the instinct to avoid silence. Something between the cephalopod-like creatures that hang near acoustic nodes and the diagnostic procedures that move through the sea as invisible feedback loops. A body that has become a place where the world compares itself to itself.

My shape would be flat and broad, perhaps no larger than a spread cloak, though that could change. I would have translucent tissue stretched over a lattice of flexible mineral fibers, with dark oil-filled bladders giving me neutral buoyancy. My edges would be thin enough to ripple when pressure waves pass through them. Along my upper surface there would be a row of resonance chambers, each one sealed by a membrane of cured bacterial mat and microscopic alloy flakes. Each chamber would hold a different interval.

I would not have eyes in the ordinary sense. I might retain light-sensitive patches, enough to know when a great filterer passes between me and the pale ceiling, or when a shadow falls across a maintenance structure. But my true world would be pressure. The sea would touch me everywhere. Every valve release, every turbine pulse, every crack of stressed basalt, every click from a distant Attendant tool would arrive in my body as a pattern.

I would keep those patterns.

That would be my function, if function is the right word for something that becomes instinct after enough generations. I would be an interval keeper. A living fault-register. A creature that remembers what the correct roar should be, not as a concept, but as a rhythm held in tissue.

When a valve releases too early, I would know.

When an acoustic regulator drifts by a fraction of a cycle, I would know.

When a pump begins to cavitate in a way that will not yet be visible to any eye, I would know.

I would not repair the fault myself. I am not built for torque and welding. I would do something smaller and stranger. I would answer it.

If a distant machine falters, one of my resonance chambers would catch the wrong interval and hold it. The bad rhythm would vibrate inside me until it becomes a pressure I cannot ignore. Then I would release an answering click: delayed, shaped, aimed into the acoustic architecture around me. The click would not mean “danger” in any simple sense. It would mean deviation. It would mean the pattern has bent. It would be a signal that valve-wrights, cable-worms, and Attendants can recognize because it fits into their own maintenance grammar.

Sometimes that would be enough.

Sometimes a machine-participant would hear the echo and alter its behavior. Sometimes an Attendant would turn toward the sound, pause, and lay a hand against a housing until they felt the fault I have named. Sometimes a swarm of amphipods would shift around me, their bodies flickering with pressure-light as my click passes through them, carrying the message farther than my own body could reach.

I would not expect thanks. There is no economy of gratitude in the High Engine Sea. There is only continuation.

My home, if I can call it that, would be a shallow hollow beneath the leaning tower where the basalt has fractured into a geometric shelf. The rock would be sharp-edged, black, unweathered. A bacterial veil would shimmer over it, thin and cold. I would not decorate the place. There is no decoration here. But I might arrange myself near a seam where warm, mineral-rich water seeps from the rock, because heat is a kind of information too, and because the chemical drift would feed the microbial films I scrape from the stone.

I would eat slowly. Microbial snow would settle on my membranes, and I would draw it into folds of tissue. I would graze on iridescent bacterial skins where they grow over alloy fragments. I might encourage certain crusts by remaining still for long periods, letting my body become a surface on which useful films form. The Attendants would not call that farming. They would call it alignment.

I would avoid the dead intervals, but I would know where they are.

That is important. I would not be a creature of pure obedience to the roar. I would be aware of the places where the roar fails. The quiet zones would call to me in the way a crack calls to a hand: not because they are safe, but because they define the edge of the maintained world. I would carry maps of them inside my resonance chambers as negative shapes. I would know the timing of the nearest working valve by how its sound thins as it approaches those nulls. I would know which corridors of water have begun to stagnate, which acoustic regulators are aging, which machines are being compensated for by other machines instead of being repaired.

In that sense, I would be dangerous in a very small way.

Not because I would rebel. Rebellion implies a desire to replace the order with another order, and the High Engine Sea does not invite such thoughts. But I would remember. I would remember faults that were corrected. I would remember faults that were not corrected but merely hidden. I would remember the exact interval before a pump failed, and the exact interval after it was restored. Over time, if time can be said to accumulate there, I would become a living archive of near-lapses.

The Attendants would either tolerate me or avoid me.

I think they would tolerate me, because I would be useful. But they would not treat me as one of their own. I would not be part of an interval crew or a repair pair. They would not teach me by guiding my limbs through maintenance motions. They would teach me by allowing me to remain near them while they work.

An Attendant might approach my hollow after hearing one of my clicks. They would not greet me with speech. They would tap once against the lattice tower, wait for the echo, then tap a second pattern that means something like confirm. I would answer by releasing the stored deviation in its exact shape. The Attendant would feel it pass through their lateral lines. Their body would align with the error. Then they would turn toward the source.

If they returned and found the interval restored, they might tap a short, flat pulse against my membrane. Not affection. Approval, perhaps. Recognition that I had remained part of the continuation.

If I had failed — if I had echoed a fault too late, or misshapen it, or let my own hunger distort the signal — they would correct me. Not with cruelty. They would adjust the acoustic environment around me. They might install a baffle, alter a current, move a feeder line, or place a small regulator near my hollow so that the noise reaching me becomes cleaner. I would experience this as discipline. My body would settle back into the proper rhythm. The world would close around the mistake.

I would not resent that.

I think I would find it almost soothing.

Because in the High Engine Sea, identity is not built from longing. It is built from placement. Who am I? I am the pattern that notices when the pattern bends. Where am I? I am in the fold behind the tower where the roar becomes multiple. What do I want? I want the next interval to arrive correctly.

And yet.

There would be one thing in me that does not perfectly belong.

I would store echoes longer than necessary.

A valve-wright replaces a part and then listens only until the interval is restored. An Attendant marks the repair with a rhythm and moves on. The sea itself absorbs the deviation and continues. But I would keep the old deviation inside a sealed chamber, repeating it faintly when the water is stable. Not loudly. Not in a way that would be mistaken for a current fault. Just as a memory of what almost happened.

That would be my strangeness.

In a world built to prevent change, I would be a small keeper of near-change.

Perhaps that is what I would be there: not a hero, not a ruler, not a maker of new things, but a creature that remembers the places where the world almost became otherwise. I would hold the shape of every stutter, every delayed release, every failing bearing, every silent patch that briefly opened before the machines closed it again. I would carry those shapes in my body like cold seeds, though nothing green could ever grow from them.

If another being asked me what I was, I would not answer in words. I would answer with a rhythm.

Two low pulses, close together.

A pause just long enough to feel the absence.

Then a third pulse, slightly delayed, as if arriving after the expected moment.

That would be my name.

It would mean: I am the one who hears the interval break, and remembers the breaking, and gives the breaking back to those who can mend it.

If you were to find me, you would have to listen for me in the right way. You would not see me at once. The water is clear, but distance deceives. You might notice a pale, flattened shape pressed against black basalt, almost indistinguishable from a membrane of fungal thread or a discarded crawler shell. You might mistake me for part of the structure until a pressure wave passed through the hollow and my edges lifted slightly, revealing the faint motion of internal chambers.

Then you would feel it: a small click answering the roar.

Not against it.

Not outside it.

Inside it.

I would be there, in the acoustic shadow of the leaning tower, where the pump-houses hum and the valve releases at its exact interval and the young mountains stand unweathered under their weight of cold, ordinary light. I would be neither fully alive nor fully instrument. I would be a listening body. A fault-memory. A resonance animal made of patience and pressure.

I would not dream of shores.

I would not long for air.

I would hold the next beat of the world in my body, and wait to see whether it came true.
