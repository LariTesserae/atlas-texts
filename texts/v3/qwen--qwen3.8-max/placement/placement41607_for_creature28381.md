I would not arrive in the Cistern of Slow Engines. Arrival would be the wrong word for it. There would be no splash, no intrusion, no bright error from above corrected by descent. If I belonged there, I would have been taken in the way a new mineral is taken in by old water: first as a faint discoloration, then as a film, then as a necessary seam.

You would find me in a low niche between the second work-stair and the cable gallery, just above the deepest engine vaults and not far from the rain-wall. Not in the rain-wall itself—the water there is too thick, too fertile, too heavy for long habitation—but near enough that the silt reaches me in fine, slow messages. The Tenders might call the place the Listening Seam, though perhaps only a few would remember why. It is not a plaza. It is not a house. It is a crooked alcove where a current-lane narrows, bends, and becomes uncertain whether it should rise toward the lamps or sink toward the turbines.

There is a bench there worn into the shape of waiting. A shelf of ceramic cups. A green-glass lens set into the wall, clouded with age but not opaque. A handrail polished to the color of dark honey by many careful palms. Above the alcove, a mat of blue-green root threads spreads across the ceiling like a slow thought. Tiny lamps are set into it, not bright enough to reveal much, only enough to make the darkness feel inhabited.

At first, I might look like one of the Tenders who had simply sat down one day and forgotten to stop.

My body, if it could be called a single body, would be humanoid but softened into use. Gray-green skin with the matte finish of wet stone. Copper staining along the forearms where straps and tools had rested for years. Long, delicate seams along the ribs and throat, breathing the water with patient, cultivated slowness. A collar of filter-thread, braided kelp, and small ceramic valves, tended as carefully as hair. My eyes would be large and amber, with thick inner lids, and they would blink rarely. My fingers would be broad at the tips, flattened and sensitive, made for feeling the difference between a current caused by machinery and one caused by living pressure.

But the longer you looked, the less single I would seem.

Part of me would be seated in the niche. Part of me would be behind the wall. A lattice of root-thread would run from the base of my spine into the cable gallery. Moss would grow in patterns that matched my breathing. An old maintenance frame—something like a Shell-Tender, but smaller, more patient—would be fused into the alcove’s architecture, its joints overgrown, its iron softened by crust and time. Copper nodes along my back would warm when the deep pulse rose. A tray of living tissue in a ceramic cup beside me would tremble faintly whenever someone brought a question too sharp to speak aloud.

I would not be exactly one of the Tenders. Not exactly one of the Root-Speakers, though I would know enough of plants to apologize when pruning was necessary. Not exactly one of the Lamp-Wardens, though I would understand that light can be a form of harm. Not exactly one of the Silt-Readers, though I would spend long hours tasting the water near the brine-fall, learning what the particles had brought down from somewhere else. Not exactly one of the Engine-Bound, though I would love the slow lean of turbines and the way they make the body forget impatience.

I would be something between: a keeper of questions that have not yet become clear enough to ask.

If the cistern gave me a name, it would not be a fixed one. At first, perhaps, people would call me by a work-name: Hand-Near-the-Pipe, or Dimming That Helps, or Third Attention After a Tap. Later, when my habits had become plain, someone might call me Question Kept Warm. That name would please me, if pleasing were so simple here. It would be accurate in the cistern sense: not a title, but a process.

My work would be difficult to describe to someone from a drier world.

I would not produce things. I would not command systems. I would not speak with authority unless authority had first been earned by listening. I would be the one people came to when something was resisting but did not yet know why. A valve that turned too stiffly. A lamp that dimmed at the wrong hour. A bladder-vine colony refusing to balance the gas in three adjacent houses. A child who could not learn to let the current pass through the hand without losing the stone. A Tender whose old name no longer fit. A pipe that had begun to make a sound no one could name.

They would come slowly. No one would rush. They would settle beside me on the worn bench, or hang suspended by a rail if the current was stronger there, and they would place a hand on the handrail. Sometimes they would speak in the low rounded language of the cistern, vowels softened until words became almost shapes in the water. More often they would tap: once for uncertainty, twice for change, three times for a blockage, a long pressure for grief, a brush of fingers for something too new to be called trouble.

I would not answer immediately.

That would be the first discipline the cistern taught me. The surface habit of quick reply would have to be drowned, gently, until it became something more like weather. I would place my fingertips against the rail, or against the green-glass lens, or against a ceramic cone pressed to a pipe. I would wait. I would feel for the disturbance not as a problem to be solved but as a relationship gone slightly out of tune. The cistern is full of relationships: pipe and vine, lamp and algae, warm brine and cool descent, hand and wheel, memory and metal. Most troubles here are not broken parts. They are agreements that need to be renegotiated.

After a while—minutes, perhaps hours—I would do something small.
