I would find myself in the between places.

Not on the highest rim, though I would know the rims well. Not in the deepest reservoir court, though I would carry their water in my body and their caution in my habits. I would be in the middle distance, where one basin fades into fog and the next appears only as a suggestion of lamps: on a viaduct, a stair, a funicular line, a covered walk, a bridge that seems less built than remembered into the softened stone.

If I had to name my place in that world, I would say: the middle way-station on one of the old lines that connects a lower reservoir court with the fog-combs on the rim. Not a grand terminal. A small shelter built into a saddle between two rounded swells of basalt, where the mountain has been worn into a shape like a shoulder turned away from wind. The station would be made of ancient stone below, blackened iron and glass above, and above that a roof thick with moss and sedge, threaded with walkways and vent pipes. A lamp would burn there in the damp, amber and low. A bell would hang under a little hood of copper, green at the edges. Water would drip from the eaves into a narrow trough, and from the trough into a cistern whose lid is fitted with iron handles worn smooth by many palms.

There I would be one of the cable folk.

At first, reading the shape of your country, I imagined myself elsewhere. I thought perhaps I would be a roof-farmer, green-handed and cheerful, walking the moss fields above the streets with the sky pressing down like a pearl ceiling. I thought perhaps I would be an engine-keeper in the industrial terraces, with warm fingers and oil-dark cuffs, listening to the condensation engines until their hum became a second pulse. I thought perhaps I would be a water clerk in the lowest courts, pale and formal, keeping ledgers bound in waxed cloth, recording whether the city’s thirst was rising or settling.

All those lives appeal to me. But they are rooted lives, and I think I would be drawn more strongly to connection.

So I would be a line-keeper, a cable-singer, a fog-runner: someone who tends the links between districts. Not the one who makes the water, not the one who grows the moss, not the one who commands the engines, but the one who makes sure the city does not lose itself in the mist. The one who listens to the cables. The one who knows when a funicular pulley has begun to complain in a pitch too fine for ordinary ears. The one who can walk into grey air and find the next bridge by the feel of the rail under a wet glove.

In that world, I would be a small, necessary being.

I would be human, I think, but human altered by the place: deep-chested from the thin air, slow-hearted from the quiet, grey-green-eyed from the weak light. My shoulders would be rounded a little forward from habit and from carrying a harness. My hands would be my most educated feature: fingertips hardened by wire, palms sensitive to the difference between cold iron and iron that is merely damp, nails often stained with moss or oil. I would have the damp gait everyone there has: short steps, knees loose, weight ready to shift, hood low against the mist.

My coat would be dark and waxed, high-collared, with a narrow-brimmed hood. It would have hidden pockets, because a line-keeper cannot afford to be without small things. In one pocket: wax matches. In another: a little oil vial stopped with cork and leather. In another: a collapsible cup, because to be without a vessel is careless. In another: a spool of fine wire, a knife with a bone handle, a brass key for valve boxes, a strip of oiled mesh for emergency repairs, a waxed notebook no larger than a palm. Always a container. Always something to mend with. Always something to offer if another traveler is thirsty or cold.

I would not be especially strong. I would be enduring.

My work would begin before the light does, if light can be said to begin there at all. The sky would only change from dark grey to a thinner pearl, and the first sign of morning would be a shift in the city’s dampness: roofs releasing their night weight, channels beginning to whisper, engines changing pitch as the fog tide thickens or thins. I would rise from a narrow cot built into an alcove of the station, put my hand against the wall to feel whether the pipe behind it still carried warmth from the lower boilers, and listen.

That is the first skill of the cable folk: listening.

Not listening for dramatic sounds. Listening for what is almost nothing. A cable under too much tension. A strand beginning to fret inside its sheathing. A rail joint loosened by the slow creep of wet stone. A pulley that has developed a faint hesitation, like a heart skipping once and then pretending it did not. A bell that sounds dull because moisture has gathered in its hood. A funicular cage moving through fog with the wrong rhythm, as if the counterweight has shifted or the line has caught a burr of ice or moss.

I would tap the cables with a small hammer or the butt of a wrench and listen to the note. That is why they are called cable-singers, perhaps. Not because the cables make music in any pleasant sense, but because each healthy line has a sound it should make, and every damaged line has a false note hiding inside it. In dry weather, the sound is brighter. In fog, it is muted. In cold, it tightens. In a storm, it becomes treacherous. A good line-keeper learns the voice of each span the way one learns the breathing of someone sleeping nearby.

My day would be full of small rounds.

I would walk the catwalks beside the funicular rails, checking the mesh screens where moss spores collect. I would clear drainage seams of crushed cushion plants and dead sedge, but not too thoroughly, because the green growth holds moisture and keeps the stone from slicking over with bare condensation. I would oil sheaves in the fog, working slowly, because spilled oil is waste and waste is a kind of violence in a place where everything must be counted. I would inspect the tether-lines along the rim approaches. I would test the hand signs used between stations when voices fail in the mist: two flashes of a shuttered lamp, a certain pull on a signal cord, a sequence of taps on iron.

When the fog was thick enough to swallow the nearest tower, I would rely on touch and memory.

There would be times when a cage arrived from below carrying water clerks, their waxed ledgers held inside their coats like holy books. Times when rim-walkers came down from the combs with the first pure water of the morning sealed in copper flasks. Times when roof-farmers crossed from one basin to another with bundles of moss tied to their backs, their fingers green, their hoods pushed back despite the chill. Times when engine-keepers came up seeking a cooler part, or a messenger, or a few minutes away from the hum they had been listening to too long. I would let them rest in the station. I would offer hot water, not tea unless they were very welcome, because tea is a slower kindness and requires a reason to linger.

I would know many people, but I would not wholly belong to any district.

That is the nature of the cable folk. They are of the city but not settled in one basin. They move through the fog between neighborhoods, carrying messages, small parcels, replacement parts, seed trays, water samples, warnings, greetings, the occasional love letter folded so many times it has gone soft at the creases. They know the schedules of the funiculars and the moods of the viaducts. They know which bridges collect a private drizzle from the engine exhausts below. Which stairways become rivers during a cloudburst. Which rail switches are stiff in cold weather. Which lamps attract silvered moths so thickly that the glass must be cleaned before morning.

I would be one of them because I am drawn to maintenance more than origin.

There is a kind of person who wants to found, conquer, name, build the first tower, light the first fire. I do not think I would be that person in your country of ended mountains. The mountains there are already finished. The great violent work of becoming is over. What remains is the quieter, endless work of keeping a livable world inside a completed form. That work suits me.

I would be a mender of connections.

Not only physical ones. In a dense city of overlapping roofs and shared water, connections are also social. A leak in one district can become a dispute in another. A full cistern above an empty court can become resentment. A fog-comb drawing too greedily from the cloud can leave a lower glasshouse dry. The cable folk, moving between all places, hear these tensions early. I would hear them in the way a reservoir clerk speaks too formally. In the way an engine-keeper’s hand signs become clipped. In the way a roof-farmer looks at the sky and says nothing.

So I would carry more than wire and oil.

I would carry small acts of adjustment. A warning passed quietly to the right person before it becomes blame. A favor arranged between districts that do not wish to seem to need one another. A cup of water offered at the exact moment when pride can be softened. A message delayed or hastened, depending on whether the weather calls for patience or urgency. The cable folk would learn, by necessity, to be discreet. They are the nervous system of the tableland, and a nervous system should not gossip with every twitch.

My station would be modest, but it would have a life of its own.

The walls would be layered: old folded gneiss at the base, pale ribbons of stone worn smooth where generations had leaned against them; blackened brick above that; then iron brackets, glass panes, timber shelves, copper piping. Moss would have colonized every corner not constantly disturbed. Lichen would map the outer wall in ash-grey and faint gold. In a shaded seam beside the door, dwarf ferns would unfurl each season, their fronds so fine they would tremble when a cage passed. On the roof, sedge and moss would make a soft meadow, walked only along planks. A few cushion plants would swell in the cracks around the lamp post. In warmer weather, if engine heat reached that far, a dark-leaved vine might creep along the underside of the eaves, beaded with condensation.

Inside, I would keep things in order without making them bare. A bench. A stove connected to a return pipe so no heat is wasted. A rack for coats and harnesses. A shelf for tools, each wrapped in cloth. A waterproof map of the line, marked with small signs: where the cable sings flat in fog, where the rail drains too slowly, where the lamp needs a new pane before winter. A cistern gauge. A sounding rod. A locked box for messages. A glass case near the window where I grow a few delicate things: dwarf mint, a pale flower that opens only when the fog thins, perhaps a berrying shrub with waxed leaves if the station is warm enough.

There would be animals in my life, but few.

A pale highland vole might live in the roof moss, known to me only by tiny tremors above the ceiling when it tunnels. I would leave it alone unless it came too near a drain. One of the grey weasel-like hunters might visit the station yard at night, slipping between cistern and wall, drawn by warmth and the scent of voles. I would not feed it, but I would not drive it away. It would keep the station honest. Sometimes, on clearish nights, I would hear the short sharp call of something small ending, and I would feel the strangeness of the place: not cruel, not gentle, only exact.

Moths would gather at my lamp. Their narrow silvered wings would tap against the glass with a sound like rain trying to become rain. I would let them in sometimes if the fog was too thick and the lamp needed no shade, because their bodies bring small news of the air outside: what spores are loose, what engine heat has shifted, whether the cloud smells of stone or of distant moss smoke. A broad-winged bird might ride the fog currents past my station at dawn, appearing suddenly out of grey and vanishing again, and I would watch it with the envy one keeps for creatures whose work is simply to move.

Water would be my constant companion, but not in the form of abundance.

It would be all around me as possibility. Beading on mesh. Sweating on iron. Gathering in the folds of my hood. Running in thin lines down glass. Standing invisibly in the air until I passed through it and became wet without knowing exactly when. I would learn the tastes of water as the people there do: comb water, sharp and cold and slightly wild; roof water, softer, touched by moss; reservoir water, settled and mineral; water that has passed through copper and carries a faint brightness; water that has stood in stone and become patient. I would have a favorite cup, perhaps dented, perhaps made of dark horn, and I would fill it carefully, without splashing, because even there, even with cisterns full, water remains something that must be respected.

My greetings would be quiet ones.

To a rim-walker arriving with mist in their eyebrows, I would offer the dry side of my cloak. To a basin child sent upward with a message, I would offer a place near the lamp. To an engine-keeper with hot hands and tired eyes, I would offer hot water and silence until they chose to speak. To someone I loved, if love came to me in that place, I would press a warm palm to the back of their neck, or touch knuckles and then thumb to pulse, acknowledging life in the cold. I would not be loud. The place would teach me that too much sound is a kind of spill.

I would become fluent in the language of damp.

I would know whether the fog was passing or clinging, whether it beaded quickly or hung without deciding. I would know the difference between a drizzle that falls and one that merely leans against the world. I would know the smell of a channel about to fill. I would know the particular silence of a cistern nearly full, when the last few drops stop sounding like separate events and become one body of water. I would know the warning signs: a cable too bright with condensation, a mesh tower shedding too early, a roof drain choked, a glasshouse pane fogged from the wrong side, a pipe sweating where it should be dry.

And I would be changed by the quiet.

Not emptied. Saturation is not emptiness. The quiet there is full of small presences: dripping, ticking, the faint electric whisper of transformers, the creak of a cable under load, the soft impact of drops from eaves, the almost inaudible vibration of water moving through stone. I would become someone who can stand still long enough to hear those things. Someone who does not need constant speech. Someone who understands that attention is a form of care.

If there were emergencies, they would not be spectacular in the way of avalanches or wars. They would be slow emergencies: a line fraying, a seal failing, a district drawing too much water, a fog season lasting beyond the reserves, a spore cloud choking the vents, a child lost between basins in a wall of mist. I would answer those emergencies with knots, lamps, hand signs, patience, and the kind of courage that does not look like courage. I would go out into the fog with a harness tied to a rail. I would crawl along a service ledge to reach a frozen pulley. I would carry a message through a blind stretch of viaduct by counting steps. I would share my water with someone whose flask had cracked. I would not call it heroism. In that world, heroism would probably be another word for maintenance.
