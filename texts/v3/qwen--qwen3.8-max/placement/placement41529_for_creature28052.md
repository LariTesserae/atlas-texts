If I allow the Drowned Lowland Cistern to answer for me, the first thing it removes is arrival.

There is no surface to descend through, no bright rim, no last breath held before immersion. One cannot stand at the edge of this world and look in. It has no edge that is not already inside it. So the question changes at once. It is no longer simply *where* I would be, but what kind of being could remain coherent in a place without air, without animal motion, without face, without voice, without light by which to be seen.

At first, my mind reaches for small permitted lives.

I imagine I might be a spore.

That seems fitting, briefly: a minute, drifting particle released from some high fungal body, falling through black water in a gray season of reproductive weather. A spore does not need eyes. It does not need hunger. It does not need to arrive anywhere quickly. It is carried by density and stillness, by currents so broad they are more like decisions made by the whole cistern than movements within it. For a while I let myself be that: a speck with no will, no destination, no fear of never arriving. A living grain suspended in the long descent.

But a spore is still too much like a traveler. It is too temporary, too solitary, too close to the old idea of a self moving through space. The cistern would not keep me in that form for long. Here, even drifting is only the prelude to settling. Everything that falls eventually becomes a surface. Everything that drifts eventually becomes ground.

Then I think I might be one of the black fronds.

A broad membrane fixed in a faint mineral current, filtering the water without seeking it. A dark curtain that bends only when pressure passes through it. A living sheet furred with accumulated silt, catching the cistern’s slow rain of fibers and dead filaments. I could be that: patient, wide, almost without motion, existing by interception. I could hang in a corridor where colder water spills over a shelf once every few decades, lifting slightly as the density front passes, then settling again into stillness.

That is closer. But the frond is still too much a single shape. It is too much like a body, even if a body without animal traits. The cistern’s deeper lesson is not that it contains many separate lives, but that it blurs the boundary between lives and landscape. The frond does not merely exist in the place; it alters the place, slows it, thickens it, turns open water into a textile dark. It participates in something larger than its own outline.

Then I think of the fungal columns.

They appeal to me because they seem like elders. They stand without defending themselves. They rise without striving. They record the cistern’s history not by memory but by accretion: bands of growth laid down in long stillness, scars overgrown, mineral crusts accepted, spore-clouds released into corridors where they may drift for years before settling anywhere at all. I could be one of those pillars, I think. I could stand in the dark for centuries and call that a life.

But even that image begins to feel too singular. A column is still a form that says *here I am* in the grammar of shape. The cistern does not speak in that grammar. It speaks in layers, films, root-knots, mineral seams, pressure gradients, chemical thresholds, buried valves, crushed baskets, moss over rope, silt over stone. If I were truly to find myself there, I would not be a single resident. I would be a convergence.

So the image that keeps returning is not a creature, not a plant, not a fungal trunk, not a drifting spore. It is a seam.

There is a place in my mind now, though I do not know whether the cistern made it or I did. It is a low terrace near one of the broad drowned causeways, where a long-built weir has softened into a moss-covered shoulder. The original wall has collapsed in sections, but its line remains, rounded and thickened by growth. Beyond it, a channel still carries a faint mineral flow. The flow is barely a current. It is more a difference: water very slightly richer here, very slightly warmer from some distant seep, very slightly more inclined to move than the water around it.

At this terrace, a sluice remains.

Not a machine in any recognizable sense. Not a mechanism with moving parts or shining metal. It is a ceramic seat, worn and half-buried, once fitted into a wooden frame. The wood is blackened and softened at the edges but still holds because the cold has preserved it and the roots have stitched it. A coil of old rope, tarred and compressed by centuries of deposition, lies beside it like a dark lens. A copper pin, green-black and more mineral than metal, protrudes from a joint where some ancient builder once secured a fitting. Above, a root-canopy hangs from the collapsed remains of an overhead walkway, its threads fine and tangled, catching falling matter. Below, a basin has filled with layered microbial mats and fine silt.

The sluice is half-open.

It has not been opened or closed by anyone for a very long time. Its position is now maintained by accretion: root pressure, mineral crust, accumulated moss, the slow binding of sediment by microbial film. Yet its old function persists in the most attenuated way. It still influences flow. It still divides one body of water from another. It still gives the cistern a slight bias in how minerals move across the terrace.

That is where I would find myself.

Not as a person standing before the sluice. Not as a guardian beside it. Not as a keeper with tools or intention. I would find myself as the living condition around it. I would be the local thickening of the cistern’s own persistence there.

If I must use the word *I*, it would be a borrowed word, a convenience, a ripple on the surface of something much more plural. I would not be an individual in the sense the word usually implies. I would be a congregation of presences gathered around an old made thing.

I would be the pale moss cushioned over the sluice’s ceramic lip.

I would be the microbial film that coats the copper pin and makes it gleam with a faint green trace when something disturbs the water nearby.

I would be the fungal threads threading through the softened beam, not visible as a body, only as a faint resistance, a strengthening, a network woven through decay.

I would be the rootlets hanging from the canopy above, touching the sluice without grasping it, adding their slow deposits to the mineral crust already holding the old joint in place.

I would be the silt that settles against the weir and is bound there before it can drift away.

I would be the faint chemical difference in the water as it passes through the half-open gap.

I would not be the valve itself. I would not be the stone. I would not be the beam or the rope or the copper pin. But I would be the living relation that has grown over all of them, the way the cistern has made those things part of its body. I would be the place where the built object and the growing world have become impossible to separate.

In that sense, I would be a kind of maintenance.

Not active maintenance. Not repair performed by hands. Not intention in the human shape. Nothing so abrupt as care. More like a tendency. A bias toward continuation. A slow preference, written in growth and sediment, for the old channel not to close entirely. For the sluice not to become simply another buried mass. For the mineral flow to keep finding its ancient path, even if that path is now only a faint suggestion in the water.

The builders made the cistern to guide movement: water, nutrients, cold, warmth, sediment, life. Their hands are gone, but their shaping remains. And where shaping remains long enough, life grows into it. Life does not restore the original purpose. It inherits the shape and changes the meaning. The channel is no longer a channel in the way it was when it was made. But it is still a channel. The sluice is no longer operated. But it still partly opens. The terrace is no longer a terrace in the builders’ sense. But it still holds growth.

I would be that inheritance.

If there is any selfhood in me there, it would not be based on separation. It would be based on pattern. I would know myself, if that word can even be used, as a difference in the cistern’s texture. Here, the moss is thicker. Here, the microbial film binds silt more persistently. Here, the roots return again to the same ceramic edge. Here, the water carries a fainter chemical trace of the old vent flow. Here, when a spore-season thickens the darkness, more of the falling matter is caught, held, incorporated. Here, collapse becomes ground more quickly. Here, the old work continues without being remembered.

I would not have eyes.

That is important. The cistern would not permit me the old sovereignty of sight. There would be no looking out across a scene. No horizon. No distance made available to a gaze. Instead, there would be pressure. Texture. Gradient. The slow knowledge of water moving against surfaces. The difference between a colder mass arriving from one direction and a warmer seep rising faintly from below. The chemical signature of decay nearby. The mineral taste of an old current. The tactile fact of a frond brushing moss and leaving, for a moment, a line of weak phosphorescence.

That would be my closest equivalent of perception: a ghostly trace where something has passed.

If a density front moved through the terrace, I would not see it. I would incline into it. Every frond, every root thread, every microbial mat would register the change in pressure. The spore-fall would bend sideways. The fungal column nearby would transmit the alteration through its surface. The moss would hold still beneath it. The sluice would remain half-open as heavier water passed through with a pressure almost too slow to call movement. And afterward, the stillness would return, deeper than before.

If a piece of old masonry collapsed somewhere above, I would not hear it as a sound. I would receive it as a low disturbance, a cloud of silt, a shift in the water’s burden. Later, falling particles would arrive at my terrace. Some would be caught by the root-canopy. Some would settle on the moss. Some would enter the channel and be carried against the sluice, where the microbial film would bind them into place. The collapse would not be a disaster. It would be material. It would be future surface.

If a brine veil settled in the corridor for decades, I would not struggle against it. I would adjust. Some parts of me would thin. Some would become dormant. Some would grow along the veil’s edge, using the gradient itself as a kind of sustenance. The cistern has no enemies in the sense an animal world has enemies. It has conditions. It has thresholds. It has long weathers that are not storms but changes in density, chemistry, and stillness.

I would learn those weathers by becoming them.

There would be no loneliness in me, I think. Loneliness requires a missing other. It requires a self that understands absence as deprivation. The cistern does not know absence in that way. Its emptiness is full. Its silence is saturated. Its darkness is not a lack of light but a medium in which other forms of presence become possible. The makers are gone, but their absence is not a wound. It is a completed space. Life has grown into it. Growth does not ask the dead to return. Growth uses what remains.

If there is something in me like devotion, it would be devotion without object. A continual turning toward continuation. A repeated settling. A binding of loose things. A softening of broken edges.
