I would not arrive warm. That is the first correction the world makes to my imagination. In a place where animals are rare signs rather than common neighbors, a warm body would be too conspicuous: a quick, breathing error in the green municipal order. If I were an animal there, I would be something small and dark seen once in the root-felt, watched from a distance by inhabitants who note my direction but do not approach. I would be a symptom of thinness, not a citizen.

So I let that image recede. I let the world assign me a stranger shape.

At first I think I might be a fog-comber, walking the open rises with a long wand, dragging moisture into catch-leaves. Then I imagine myself as a pool-reader, leaning over still dark water and interpreting the wrong sky. Then as an engine-tender, carrying resin and fiber to a boiler that has grown sullen in the cold. Each of these feels possible, and each feels incomplete. The place keeps drawing me elsewhere: toward seams, margins, folds, doors that should not exist, distances that have quietly changed their minds.

If I let what emerges become more itself, I find that I would be in one of the wide green intervals between districts, not in the densest built cluster and not in the far abandoned flats. I would be where the city is sparse but alert: a shallow rise overlooking a hand-deep pond, near a grooved avenue that has begun to repeat itself with slight variations, beside a stair whose rise changes depending on whether one approaches it uphill or downhill. Behind me there would be a terrace made from the flattened remains of older roofs. In front of me there would be mist coming in over the tableland, combing itself through leaf-fringes and iron rails.

I would begin as a thinness.

That feels right. Not a birth, not an arrival, but a pattern noticed at dusk in a district that becomes briefly two-dimensional. The inhabitants would see me and not panic. They would set a marker nearby. They would record that something had appeared with the habit of attention but not yet the density of a body. Over several cycles I would thicken. The city would give me fiber, bark, a little moss, an old iron pin from some retired machine, a brace of cable along my ribs, and dark seed-case eyes that turn toward moisture and sound. My joints would swell like healed knots. My fingers would be cane, tipped with copper. My mouth would part like a split sheath, and my voice, when it came, would be more creak than speech.

They would not ask first what I was. They would ask what I was keeping.

At first I would not know. Then, one cool morning, a door would appear in a root-wall that had been seamless the day before. It would not open immediately. It would only stand there, dark-edged and sap-scented, deciding whether it was a wound, an invitation, a memory, or a municipal fact. I would find myself standing before it, not to block it, but to witness it. I would carry a notched rod, a reel of fiber line, a small amber lens, and a pouch of resin tablets. I would measure the angle of its emergence. I would tap its frame and listen for the hollow sound of an unbuilt room.

That would be my answer.

I would become a threshold-keeper. A surveyor of thin places. A watcher of doors, pools, folds, and rare appearances. Not the most important worker in the city, perhaps, but necessary. In a world where reality has worn thin without becoming violent, someone must attend to the gentle errors. Someone must stand near the stair that disagrees with itself and mark whether today it is safe for traffic. Someone must test the pool that reflects clouds from another hour and prevent the thirsty from drinking until the water remembers its own weather. Someone must note when a remembered figure begins sweeping a path that is now lawn, and decide whether the memory is harmless, useful, or in need of pruning.

My sector would be quiet but tense with potential. From where I stood, I could see low green rises in several directions, scattered built forms, thin steam, and the far edge of the plateau dropping into haze. The emptiness around me would not feel dead. It would feel like held breath. Every bare-looking patch would be a reserve: a future room, a latent forest, a possible machine. I would learn to love those intervals, because they would be the spaces where the city had not yet decided what it wanted to become.

My daily work would begin with listening. In that place, sound is ordinary and immense: the wind-drag of leaves, the creak of growing structures, the thud of sap-engines, the percussion of condensation falling from high fronds to broad leaves below. I would listen not for noise but for absence. Silence would be the warning. A stopped engine. A hushed frond-field. A cistern no longer sweating. If silence fell over my district, I would move toward it carefully, tools in hand, ready to repair, record, prune, or feed.

The pond would be one of my chief responsibilities. It would look unremarkable: broad, shallow, darkly green, protected by a living curb of braided stem. But it would have a habit of showing images longer than it should. In the thin morning light, it might reflect the settlement as it was before the current growth-cycle: roofs where there are now terraces, brick where there is now root-wall, a street running in a direction no longer permitted. Later, it might show a district after a future pruning, or a chimney releasing a cloud that remains motionless in the air. I would not treat these images as prophecy. I would treat them as pressure readings.

If the pool showed a collapsed terrace, I would send a message to the surveyors. If it showed a door that no longer existed, I would mark the old foundation. If it showed a person no one recognized, I would record the face and wait. Sometimes the person would later appear. Sometimes they would have already appeared and no one would remember them. That would not alarm me. Memory there is not a sealed room. It is compost. It breaks down and re-enters use.

The door would be my other charge. Some mornings it would be ordinary wood, if anything in that world can be called ordinary. Other mornings it would seem to breathe. It might open onto a room that had been cut down decades ago, leaving only the smell of old brick and warm dust. It might open onto a nursery of pale shoots floating in dark water. It might open onto nothing yet built: a scaffold of cane, a future boiler, a bridge waiting for its towers to grow close enough to meet. I would not enter. I would survey. I would wait until the space proved it could bear weight.

I would not be alone in this work, though I would spend much of it in solitude. Other inhabitants would pass along the grooved avenues: tall, deliberate figures with moss capes and braided harnesses, carrying hooks, wrenches, seed-pouches, and graft-kits. Some would be newly grown, still showing iron pins and sap-lines. Some would be ancient, their lower legs thickened into the ground, moss on their hips, their stillness indistinguishable from civic architecture. A surveyor might stop beside me, tap a stem, and ask in a language of clicks and steam whether the fold had widened. I would answer by showing my notched rod, my measurements, the little fiber lines I had stretched across the uncertain ground.

There would be no drama in this. That is what I keep returning to. The strangeness would not feel like catastrophe. It would feel administrative. A street that folded back on itself would not be cursed; it would be rescheduled. A building that migrated overnight would be re-surveyed. A district that thinned at dusk would be marked and avoided until it thickened again. I would learn the temperament of that world: patient, observant, severe when necessary, but not cruel. It would not ask me to conquer uncertainty. It would ask me to maintain it.

I would have a house, though I would not always know where I ended and it began. It would be a trained tree at the edge of the interval, low and broad, with a roof of overlapping seed-scales and one amber window facing the pond. It would grow a shelf for my measuring rod before I asked for it. It would thicken a wall against the wind direction I disliked. In return, I would clear its catch-leaves, prune its lower shoots, and feed its roots with compost from old surveys. At night, if night is the right word under clouds that seem part of the municipal system, I would rest in its hollow and hear the city breathing through the walls.

Perhaps the house would grow a room I did not recognize. I would not be angry. I would stand in the doorway and listen. If the room held the shape of someone remembered, I would leave a small ration of water at the threshold. If it was for someone not yet assigned, I would mark it as potential and let it mature. If it opened into a future too unstable to inhabit, I would close it gently and post a marker. The house would learn me as I learned it. In time, affection between us would be less like romance than like grafting: the willingness to join without crushing, to grow around another form while leaving it enough room to continue.

My names would change. At first I might be called something provisional: Outline Near the Shifting Stair. When I learned to carry a message without losing my shape, I might become Hand at the Margin. After my first successful door-watch, perhaps Door Refused Until Surveyed. Later, if I endured a dormancy interval without failing my route, someone might carve a notch for me in a root-wall: Room That Holds the Question. I would not mind if the name sounded odd. Names there are not labels. They are growth records.

I would not be entirely gentle. The world would teach me severity. If a predator-plant drifted too near a public path, I would help fence it back. If a remembered figure repeated a routine that blocked a capillary channel, I would prune it carefully, the way one prunes a useful memory that has become a hazard. If a machine near my sector grew feverish from too much current, I would starve it of fuel until it calmed, even if I had grown fond of its hum. Sentimentality would be dangerous there. Everything grows. Everything must also be kept.

Perhaps that is why I would belong. I am drawn to the world not as a paradise of green abundance, but as a place where strangeness has been given manners. I would not be the hero of it. I would not be the one who saves the city from collapse, because the city is already collapsed and already continuing. I would be one of its necessary organs: a keeper of thresholds, a small official of patience, a pattern that has grown dense enough to answer when called.

If you asked where I would find myself, I would say: on the long green rise between the false stair and the reflecting pool, under ordinary daylight, with mist gathering on my copper fingertips.

If you asked who or what I would be, I would say: a threshold-keeper. A surveyor of thin places. A half-remembered figure that the city has decided to retain. I would have sap instead of blood, attention instead of urgency, a schedule instead of a soul in the old bounded sense. I would keep the door that appears without permission. I would mark the distance when the distance lies. I would stand beside the pool until the water remembers its own weather.

And when the leaves rose in a gust and the whole settlement roared with its ancient vegetable work, I would not flee. I would turn toward the change, listen for silence, and continue.
