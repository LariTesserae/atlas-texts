I would not find myself there as a single, upright thing.

I would find myself just under the ordinary surface, in the seam where the world above has cracked and the world below is still learning its own shape. Somewhere near a grey shaft of morning light falling into a pool. Somewhere on a root-bridge over dark water. Somewhere under a shelf of broken limestone where swallow nests are packed into the dry shadow and the air smells of mud, cold greens, and wet fur.

At first, if you asked me what I was, I might say I was a water vole.

That seems like the first honest answer. I would be low to the water, soft-furred and blunt-faced, moving through the marsh-edge tunnels with a body made for tight passages and quiet repairs. My hands would be paws. My tools would be teeth and instinct. I would know the hollow by pressure: where the root-mat is firm enough to hold, where the mud has gone treacherous after rain, where a channel has deepened by a finger’s width, where a nest entrance needs one more packed pellet of clay.

I would live among the sedges and woven roots, not in the open water and not entirely above it, but in that negotiated place where land and water argue and come to terms. My days would be spent maintaining the hidden streets of the city: clearing a blockage from a runway, reinforcing a wall with chewed stems, lining a chamber with shredded reed, listening for the sudden disturbance that means something is hunting.

There is something in that form that feels right to me: the builder in the margins, the small body keeping the fabric of the place intact. Not grand. Not loud. Necessary.

But the vole is only one possible skin, and the hollow would not let me stay there for long.

The moment I tried to settle into that single shape, I would feel the rest of the world pulling at me. I would feel the caddis larva adding one more grain to its case in the slow channel below. I would feel the spider tightening a guy-line of silk between roots. I would feel the mussel pressing itself into the underwater pavement, filtering the dark water with patient, hidden labor. I would feel the bat shifting in the upper darkness, folded into a seam of stone, breathing with a hundred other bodies until the next flight.

So perhaps I would not be one animal. Perhaps I would be distributed.

If I had to locate myself in the geography of the hollow, I would choose the transition places. I would be found at the edge.

Not in the deepest pools. Not in the brightest crack of sky. Not in the driest overhang. But where one condition meets another: where drip becomes film, where root becomes bridge, where light becomes algae, where mud becomes architecture. I would be near a sinkhole mouth where grey daylight comes down in a soft column and touches the water just enough to make it tremble. I would be under a ledge where nests crowd together and roots hang like torn ropes from the ceiling. I would be where the marsh tier meets the wall, where a vole path turns upward into a crevice and a bird’s flight path cuts down toward the pool.

The edges are where the hollow is most alive. They are also where I think I would be most myself.

I would be something that belongs to the intervals.

If you saw me, you might first see a small body: perhaps something vole-like, perhaps something salamander-like, perhaps something in between. It would not matter as much as the position. I would be at a threshold. A place where water rises and recedes. A place where light enters but does not dominate. A place where many routes cross.

Maybe I would be a keeper of thresholds. Not a ruler of the hollow, because there is no ruler there. Not a planner, because the city plans itself through countless small needs. Not a voice commanding, because the place has no language of command. I would be more like a repair impulse. A noticing. A tendency to restore connections.

When a channel clogged with sediment, I would be the attention that recognized the change. When a root-bridge sagged after a rise in water, I would be the urge to thicken it. When a nest cracked in the cold, I would be the memory of mud and fiber and shelter. When a predator moved through one district, I would be the stillness spreading through the nearby stems, the tiny alarm passed from body to body, the city adjusting itself around danger.

In that sense, I would be less a creature than a relationship.

But if I let the image become more concrete, I can see myself more clearly.

I would have a body suited to the marsh layer. Small. Low. Warm enough to endure the cold, but not warm enough to waste energy. Furred or furred-like in some places, slick in others. Capable of swimming, climbing, squeezing through root gaps, and remaining motionless when necessary. My senses would be tuned to nearness: whiskers for reading passages, skin for sensing water movement, ears for small sounds, eyes adapted to grey light and green shadow. I would not need a broad horizon. I would need depth of attention.

I would spend much of my time repairing.

Not because I had invented repair, but because the hollow demands it. Everything there is temporary in form and persistent in pattern. A wall must be renewed. A tunnel must be kept open. A nest must be dried. A dam must be adjusted. A web must be re-strung after a drip breaks it. A channel must be guided before it undermines a lodge. The city is not maintained by one great act. It is maintained by countless small acts repeated until they become architecture.

I would be one of those acts.

But I would also be aware of the vertical city.

I would know the submerged level, where fish hold against the current and mussels make floors out of their own bodies. I would know the root-tunnels, where eels move like slow thoughts through the dark. I would know the marsh level, where voles and shrews make the busiest streets. I would know the ledges, where wasps hang paper cells and birds build mud cups. I would know the upper dark, where bats sleep in tight clusters and the air is warmer from their breath.

And perhaps that is the truest answer: I would be the awareness moving between the levels.

Not the bat alone. Not the vole alone. Not the moss alone. But the connective life of the hollow: the mycelium under the floor, the silk between the roots, the water carrying scent from pool to pool, the inherited memory of paths, the pressure of many lives shaping one place until the place begins to seem like a single breathing body.

If I had to name what I was, I might say I would be a citizen of the wet intervals.

A small plural thing.

A body made of several bodies.

A mind like moss spreading over stone.

I would be most at home where the hollow is not finished. The description says the collapse is young, the terrain sharp, the channels new. That matters to me. I would not be an ancient spirit resting in a perfected realm. I would be a pioneer intelligence in a raw place, still testing where things can hold. I would be drawn to fresh breaks, new seepage lines, unsettled ceilings, places where the city has not yet decided what it will become.

I would like that.

There would be no permanence there, only persistence. A wall would not last because it was stone; it would last because it was continually repaired. A path would not remain because it was marked; it would remain because bodies kept choosing it. A pool would not stay the same; it would deepen, fill, flood, clear, and be re-inhabited. I would be part of that continuity, not as a monument, but as a habit.

I would probably be quiet.

The hollow is not silent, but its sound is restrained. It does not need voices. Its communication is drip, scent, touch, tremor, wingbeat, the faint disturbance of water, the rustle of a nest adjusting itself. I would learn to speak in that language if I did not already know it. I would say things by strengthening a wall, by clearing a passage, by leaving a scent at a turn, by pausing when a shadow passed overhead, by returning again and again to the same place until the place accepted me.

If I felt affection there, it would not be romantic. It would be structural. A fondness for the way a root holds. A tenderness toward a nest newly lined. A loyalty to a tunnel kept open through many rains. I would love the hollow the way a body loves its own circulation: not as a view, but as a condition of living.

And I would be watched back.

Not by a single guardian. Not by a face in the stone. But by the attentiveness of the whole place. The salamander’s stillness would be a kind of observation. The spider’s web would be a kind of listening. The bat cluster overhead would be a folded congregation of ears. The moss would register my pressure. The water would carry the news of my movement. I would never be entirely alone there, even if I could not point to another single mind.

So if you ask where I would find myself, I would say: on a mossy shelf above a dark pool, beneath a broken ceiling where roots hang and grey light enters, near a terrace of mud nests and a water vole runway hidden in the sedges.

If you ask who or what I would be, I would say: a small builder and threshold-dweller, partly animal, partly pattern, partly the hollow’s own tendency to repair itself.

If you pressed me for one visible shape, I would say I was a water vole with salamander patience and moss in my fur, no larger than the space between two roots, dark-eyed, wet, quick to listen, quicker to mend.

But if you waited long enough to see what I really was, you would notice that the repair was happening in many places at once.

You would see a caddis larva add one more grain to a wall.  
You would see a spider re-anchor a broken line.  
You would see a bat shift in the upper dark.  
You would see a salamander take one slow step in the shallows.  
You would see moss spread a filament across stone.  
You would see a bird return with mud from the pool rim.  
You would see a root tighten as water moved through it.  
You would see the hollow breathe.

And then you would understand: I was not one resident of that city.

I was the moment it briefly noticed itself.
