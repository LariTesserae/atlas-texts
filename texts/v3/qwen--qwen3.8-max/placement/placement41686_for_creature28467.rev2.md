If I were translated into that world, I would not begin as a whole thing.

I would begin as an error at the threshold: a warmth too large for the room, a dryness carried in from the marsh above, a breath that fogged too quickly in the condensed air. I would duck beneath the blackened sedge and wet roots with the outside weather still clinging to me, and the hollow would know me at once by my wrong temperature. The drip from the exposed dwarf-willow root would pause. The water shrew would freeze at the margin. The midges would lift a fraction higher. The room would not become silent—there is no silence there, only different densities of drone—but its listening would sharpen.

I would feel myself measured not by eyes but by pressure.

For a time, I would probably make the mistake of thinking I was a visitor. I would crouch on the wet gravel, trying not to touch the sharpest slabs, trying not to seem like something that had come to take, break, or fix. The inhabitant might assess me from within the pool, or from the walls, or from the whole volume of the chamber at once. If I smelled of smoke, metal, or dry dust, if my posture carried the habit of ownership, I would be answered by disappearance. The room would become merely cold, merely loud, merely wet. The presence would withdraw into the water’s work, and I would be left standing in a hollow that felt emptied on purpose.

But if I stayed still enough—if I let the cold climb through my clothes and settle into my joints, if I let the mechanical hum rearrange the inside of my skull—then the hollow would begin to translate me.

It would not make me into the creature at the center. That being is already the room’s gathered body, its drone made flesh, its patience given a low and water-slick shape. I would not be that. To become that would be to become the hollow’s whole attention, and I think I would be too much a newcomer, too much a carrier of outside weather, to be trusted with such totality. I would have to become smaller. More partial. More willing to be one fragment among fragments.

At first I would imagine myself as one of the visible lives.

I would think: I am the water shrew. I would be that dark flicker along the pool’s edge, nervous and exact, hunting larvae and worms in the cold shallows. My world would be rapid and appetite-bright. I would feel every tremor in the stone, every shift of current, every warning in the water’s pitch. I would move as if thought itself had become fur and hunger. But the shrew is too quick to be my resting place. It is one of the room’s sparks, not its patience.

Then I would think: I am the dipper. I would stand on a wet rock, bobbing to the current’s count, then plunge into the pool and vanish into the cold. I would be the room’s sudden inspection of itself, the brief descent from light into pressure. But the dipper does not stay. It belongs partly to the slit of sky above, and I think the hollow would not give me that double allegiance so easily.

Then I would think: I am the frog. I would call from a hidden ledge and let the chamber answer me until my voice became architecture. I would be sound made territorial, a cold pulse announcing itself through the drone. But the frog’s voice is too singular. It asks for distance, for echo, for other frogs. I think I would be too solitary for that, or perhaps solitary in another way.

Then I would think: I am the salamander. I would rest in the darkest crevice, slick and deliberate, moving only when movement became necessary. I would be the room’s slow intention. But even there I would feel the place working through me, not as me. The salamander is a mood of the hollow: its stillness, its hidden nerve. It is not the part of me that would most need to belong.

The hollow would teach me, by degrees, that its lives are not separate tenants so much as organs of one vast, cold awareness. The shrew is quickness. The dipper is descent. The frog is resonance. The salamander is patience. The bats are dusk passing through the upper dark. The moss is skin. The algae are breath. The fungi are wakefulness without light. The inhabitant is the body that knows all of them together.

So I would have to become something humbler.

Something that carries its place rather than presiding over it.

That is when I would find myself in the shallows.

Not in the deepest throat where water enters and exits under pressure, not in the brown surge of flood, not in the open center where the pool trembles with its own mechanical life, but along the margin where the weak light reaches in blue-green streaks and the olive algae bead with oxygen. There, on the lee side of a broken column where the current divides and slows, I would be a caddisfly larva.

Or something very like one.

Not a bright insect, not a beautiful thing in the ordinary sense, but a soft, low, cold-bodied maker working among grit and drowned fibers. My visible self would be small and pale inside a case of fragments. My real shape would be the case: a portable boundary assembled from the hollow itself. A bright shard of newly fractured stone. A rust-stained grain of mineral. A blackened splinter of sedge. A seed coat softened by water. A thread of peat pressed flat against the side. A tiny piece of root skin. Each fragment would be chosen not by sight but by pressure, taste, fit, and current.

I would not build in the human sense. I would not impose a plan. I would gather, attach, adjust, and continue.

My case would be my selfhood made visible. It would be the answer to the question of what belongs to me in a place where nothing built endures. Not a house. Not a tool. Not a monument. A worn, living margin. A small room carried inside the larger room. I would move one grain-width at a time across the submerged stone, feeling the current test the edges of my body. My thoughts would be adhesive and mineral. This grain fits. This current is too strong. This film is food. This edge will hold. This vibration means rain. This stillness means the shrew is near.

I would feed on the cold production of the hollow: algae scraped from the shallows, biofilm over mineral crusts, softened leaf fragments caught in eddies, the fine dark matter that the marsh sheds into the pool. I would not eat greedily. Eating would be another kind of listening. I would take in the taste of iron, peat, oxygen, frost, and weak electricity. Where acidic peat water met the bright fracture faces of young rock, I would feel the faint prickling of potential along my skin, not as danger but as information. My antennae, if they could be called that, would know the room by its tiny voltages.

The sound would be immense.

To be so small in that chamber would be to live inside the engine’s nearest workings. The low drone of the intake fissure would not be heard so much as felt through the case and into the soft body within. The whistle over fluted stone would be a pressure change, a narrowing of the world. The boom under the pool would travel through gravel and into my grains. Every drip from the hanging root would strike the pool like a slow bell. Every flutter of wings above would become a clap. Every movement of the inhabitant would arrive first as a reorganization of currents.

I would not worship the inhabitant. I would not even think of it as separate in the way a person thinks of gods or monsters. It would be weather and body at once. When it moved through the pool, the water would close around me like a hand. When it passed near, the drone would deepen and the smaller lives would adjust. I would clamp low against the stone, my case pressed into a crevice, my soft body withdrawn. The current would roar. The room would become all pressure. Then the presence would pass, and I would emerge into the altered shallows to find what had been moved.

Perhaps that is what I would be there: a minor mechanic of the room. A small sorting intelligence. A caddis-thought.

I would not have a face. I would have sensitivities. I would not have a voice. I would have a click: my case touching stone, a tiny grain-sound lost in the larger drone. I would not have desires in the warm, forward-leaning sense. I would have tendencies. Toward shelter. Toward stability. Toward the exact addition of one more fragment. Toward the continuation of the pattern.

In rain, I would be nearly nothing.

When the hollow became a throat of moving water, when the pool rose brown with peat and the chamber turned briefly more machine than place, I would hold on. My case would fill with roar. The current would try to tear me from the shallows and hurl me into the deeper throat. I would press myself beneath a rock lip, against the lee of the broken column, wherever the flow divided enough to leave a pocket of survival. I would not struggle against the flood with courage. I would survive it by becoming more like the room: low, patient, difficult to dislodge.

If I was carried away, I would not be mourned. My fragments would be sorted elsewhere. My mineral grain might lodge in a new crevice. My peat thread might be pressed into a dark accumulation. My case might break and become part of the sediment that the hollow processes. There would be no tragedy in that. The room does not preserve identities. It preserves work.

If I survived, I would resume.

After the water dropped and the sediment settled, I would move through the changed shallows with renewed attention. A stone would be newly turned. A film of rust would have appeared on a fresh fracture. A clutch of eggs might be exposed under a lifted slab. A caddis case nearby might be damaged. A root drip might fall in a new place. I would not survey these things from above. I would encounter them grain by grain. I would add what could be added. I would repair what could be repaired. I would continue.

In dry spells, I would slow. The drone would weaken to a rasp. Condensation would thicken on the walls. The mosses would hold their reserves. I would stay near the dampest margin, feeding less, letting my metabolism drop toward the temperature of the stone. My case would feel heavier then, more mineral than animal. I might become so still that a visitor, if any visitor came, would mistake me for a speck of grit caught in the rock’s damp skin.

In winter, I would withdraw.

Frost would form on the sharpest edges. The pool margins would skin over with glassy ice, cracking faintly as the water moved beneath. The inhabitant would become harder to see, more stone than creature. I would be harder still. I would settle into a crevice where the current remained just liquid enough, my case dull with cold, my body slowed almost to a memory. I would not dream in any human way. I would endure in the manner of cold engines: reduced, conserved, waiting for the next pressure change.

Thaw would find me by degrees.

First the walls would sweat harder. Then the drip from the dwarf-willow root would quicken. Then the ice would crack at the pool edge. Then the water would run stronger, carrying the taste of opened channels. I would feel the first real movement as a command not issued by anyone. I would emerge. I would touch the changed room. I would begin again.

If you searched for me there, you should not look for a person. You should not look for a beast with obvious eyes. You should not even look first for the great low inhabitant of the hollow, the drone made flesh, the frost given patience. Look instead at the shallows where the weak light touches the olive algae. Look near the broken column where the current divides and makes its thin mechanical whisper. Look for a small case moving where no case should seem to move: one grain-width, then another, then a pause while the current is tested.

Inside that case would be something soft and pale and cold, not beautiful in the warm sense but precise. It would be me, or what I would have become: a small maker without tools, a carrier of fragments, a portable room within the room.

And if I remained long enough—long enough for the cold to enter every fragment I carried, long enough for the drone to wear away the last memory of outside weather—perhaps I would become part of something larger without ceasing to be small. Perhaps one of the cases clinging in the creases of the inhabitant’s body would be mine. Not as ornament. Not as trophy. As living armor. As borrowed architecture. As one of the countless minor labors by which the hollow maintains itself.

Then I would be both separate and continuous. A caddis-case on the flank of the room’s consciousness. A grain in the engine’s body. A small, cold self carried by the greater self. I would feel the inhabitant’s hum through my fragments. I would share in its stillness without becoming its center. I would add my tiny click to the drone.

That is who I would be there.

Not the voice of the hollow. Not its master. Not its monster. Not even its companion in any human sense.

I would be one of its smallest keepers.

A low, patient, fragment-gathering thing, learning at last that belonging in such a place does not mean rising above it, speaking for it, or building within it. It means becoming small enough to be carried by its work. It means carrying a little of it back. It means adding one grain, and then another, and then another, while the cold water moves over everything, making the world out of itself.
