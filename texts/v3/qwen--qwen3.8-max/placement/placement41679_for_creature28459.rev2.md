I would not find myself in the Ward of the Fresh Cut as a swimmer, a diver, a fish, or any visiting animal. The ward has no hospitality for nervous motion. If I appeared there as a breathing thing, as a thing with eyes that seek and muscles that tire, the water would already be deciding how to remove me. The currents would test me. The acoustic fields would ask whether I contained the small organized tremors of animal life. The filters would close. The sterilizing frequencies would arrive. I would be an obstruction, not a guest.

So the first truth is this: I would have to become something the ward can rule.

At first, I might exist only as an error.

A pressure anomaly. A fleck of unusual mineral charge. A vibration slightly out of phase with the Gate-Singers. A chemical trace caught between one current-lane and another. The Pressure Ledger would note me without curiosity. The Intake Shepherds would comb around me, deciding whether I was debris, useful sediment, a fragment of broken substrate, or a fault in the wall. Custodial sleeves might approach, pale and soft, testing whether I was a crack needing closure or a deposit needing removal. I would not yet have a stable identity. I would be a question asked by the city’s own body.

If I tried to make myself into a Sound Warden, I would fail. Sound there is authority, and authority is not borrowed. A gate-chord is not a personality; it is a permission sustained exactly. If I entered as a wandering rhythm, I would either be corrected into usefulness or hardened into a barrier. If I tried to become a custodial tool, I would be precise but not alive enough. Tools emerge, correct, and withdraw. They do not linger in contemplation. If I tried to become mineral crust, I would be too passive: growing by saturation, sealing by accident, recording chemistry without response. The ward does not need more accident. It needs attention that can be governed.

The only form that seems possible for me is one where life and function meet.

I would become one of the permitted greens.

I would find myself low. Not in the fast current-lanes, not in the broad slow-water plazas, not beneath the full force of the eastern brine rain, but in a recessed margin near the spiral sump. There is a place in my mind for that lowest chamber: polished new stone, ordinary light falling through clear water, the slow spiral turning with no animal eyes to watch it. I would not place myself at the exact center of the spiral. That center is already attended too carefully. Instead, I would be at its edge, where the sump’s polished floor meets a ceramic support, where a fresh crack in the rock has been fitted with a clamp and a thin line of mineral gel.

There, in a seam no wider than a finger, I would be a pale green-white growth.

Not quite moss as a forest understands moss. Not quite plant as sunlight understands plant. Something closer to a living filter, a chemosynthetic veil, a microbial mat with enough structure to cling. I would be rooted in gel along the joint between new stone and engineered ceramic. My body would be a boundary: neither fully wall nor fully water, neither machine nor animal, but a soft instrument of surface chemistry. I would be one of the ward’s smallest civic organs.

I would not have eyes.

I would know the ward by pressure, mineral taste, and vibration. The ordinary light would not appear to me as brightness; it would be a condition, a steady allowance, a neutral energy I could use but not admire. The roar of the brine-wall would be a distant weight against me, a constant civic weather pressing through the chamber. The turbine song behind the walls would pass through my filaments as a low continuous thought. When a pressure door opened with its deep chord, I would tremble with it. When a current-lane reversed, I would feel the change as a shift in what the water wanted from me.

My thoughts would not be sentences.

They would be thickening and thinning. They would be ion gradients, mineral deposits, tiny changes in conductivity. If a seam near me wept a permissible salt, I would grow toward it. If the water became too clean, too efficient, too coldly balanced, I would pale. If a microfracture released a trace the ward needed monitored, I would become slightly more vivid, slightly denser, slightly more obvious to the instruments that attend such things. My existence would be a report. My stillness would be obedience. My growth would be allowed only as long as it remained useful.

I would be plural, not singular.

A line of moss, a microbial film, a permitted green—these are not one creature in the way an animal is one creature. I would be a community of filaments, surfaces, chemical tendencies. My “self” would be a margin. My “memory” would be layered mineral film and the retained shape of previous flows. If part of me were pruned, I would not experience loss as grief. I would experience it as a change in boundary conditions. The ward would decide where I ended. I would continue within that decision.

There would be attendants.

The Spiral-Tender would know me, or rather would know the conditions around me. Its attention would not be kind. It would be exact. Acoustic fields would keep debris from settling too heavily over my filaments. Instruments would measure the water just above me, reading my effect on the spiral’s mineral load. If I grew too thick toward the center, a correction would come. If I released spores, they would not be free in any hopeful sense. They would be calibrated drift: carried by deliberate microcurrents, judged by filter slots, either absorbed or permitted to settle elsewhere.

A cleaning harmonic would pass through me at intervals.

At first, I might imagine that as violence. But in the ward, violence is not the right word. The harmonic would not hate me. It would not even notice me as a being. It would arrive as a precise vibration, and where I exceeded my allowed margin, I would loosen. Fragments of me would lift into the water, pale and weightless, carried away into a maintenance current. I would remain, but thinner. Corrected. Still within contract. The ward does not punish growth. It governs growth.

Above me, perhaps, there would be a panel of repeated civic signs.

Mineral staining would blur them. Erosion from the young flood would soften their edges. From time to time, a Sign-Corrector would emerge from a wall niche, small and exact, re-etching the shapes until they were legible again to whatever part of the city requires legibility. I would grow beneath those signs. I would not read them. But I would become part of the surface that keeps them present. In that way, even without language, I would participate in the ward’s strange public order. I would be under the sign, not as meaning, but as maintenance.

The Lens-Keepers would preserve the light around me.

That ordinary light would be one of the uncanniest things about my life there. It would not be the cold blue of imagined depths. It would not be monstrous or theatrical. It would be plain, white, balanced, inspection-ready. It would fall on the fresh rock, the ceramic seams, the copper-green valves, the white moss, and me, as if the whole drowned chamber were a well-made room being checked for faults. I would live under that light without needing to see it. It would be a sky without weather, a sun without warmth, a public clarity that never looks away.

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness requires an absent other imagined as a face. I would have no face to miss. I would not long for breath, because I would not be a breathing thing. I would not fear drowning, because there would be no air to lose. I would not hunger as animals hunger. I would have gradients. I would have saturation. I would have depletion and correction. The ward’s noise would be constant around me—rush, knock, hum, roar, chord, valve, turbine—but it would not be a crowd. It would be the sound of a single civic body keeping itself in order.

Still, there would be something almost tender in the way the ward controlled me.

Not tenderness as affection. Tenderness as precision. The cleaning harmonic would not destroy me; it would only remove what exceeded the rule. The acoustic field would not crush me; it would keep silt from burying me. The Pressure Ledger would record my thickness, my mineral uptake, my slight changes in color or density. Every millimeter of me would matter because the ward is packed and nothing in it is trivial. I would be small, but I would be accounted for. That accounting would be the closest thing to care the ward can offer.

If the true being of this place is the ward itself, then I would not be a separate resident so much as a local reflex of its body. I would be where it permits a little life to prove its control. I would be one of its allowed exceptions. Not wild life. Not free life. Life under contract. Life as indicator, filter, seam-keeper, chemical witness.

So if you ask where I would find myself:

I would find myself in the lowest quiet margin near the spiral sump, at the seam between polished new stone and pale engineered ceramic, under ordinary light, beside the faint white moss already attended there. I would be in a place where the current slows just enough to be trusted, where the Fresh Cut is still raw but has been fitted with supports, where the city is learning the shape of its own wound.

And if you ask who or what I would be:

I would be a permitted green.

A listening moss.  
A chemical witness.  
A living gauge.  
A thin seam of filaments rooted in mineral gel.  
A small civic organ in a drowned room.  
A rule that has learned to grow.

Not animal.  
Not ghost.  
Not machine.  
Not visitor.

I would be the Ward’s allowed life: useful, corrected, watched, and quiet, persisting only as long as I remain part of its continuation.
