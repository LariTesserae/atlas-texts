I would find myself just off-center.

Not in the exact hollow where the listener waits, not high on the walls with the pale crusts, not among the weavers suspended on their mineral threads. I would be near the lower terraces, where the floor leans inward as if remembering a shape it has never held. There would be a place there, a shallow depression worn into the bone-colored stone, not quite a bowl and not quite a channel. It would look accidental. It would not be accidental.

At first I think I would be the intruder.

That is the obvious image: a wet thing entering a world made dry. My breath would be a scandal. My pulse would be a foreign rite. The small dust-creatures would freeze. The crusts would brighten with agitation. The weavers would draw their threads taut. The tall ones would turn their blank faces toward me, not with anger but with a terrible, patient attention. The Dry Nave would take my noise and formalize it. It would divide my gasps, delay my footsteps, turn the wet heat of my living into something that could be placed, echoed, kept.

But that image does not remain.

The Dry Nave would not keep me as an intruder for long. It would dry me. Not violently, not with teeth or malice, but with the slow reverence of mineral patience. It would draw the river out of my idea of myself. It would leave only the structure that had once held the river.

So I do not think I would remain a visitor. I think I would become something the nave makes of impossible water.

I would be a riverless thing.

A dry font.

A basin with no water to bless.

My form would be low and recessive, more hollow than body. I would not stand like the tall ones. I would not move like the weavers. I would not ripple like the crusts. I would wait as a riverbed waits in a land where rain has become a geometric impossibility. My surface would be folded with narrow canals, shallow apertures, fine grooves worn by nothing flowing. Salt and gypsum would brighten along my edges like a memory of frost. Old threads would cross my mouth, brittle and faintly tuned. Shed plates and mineral dust would gather in me, not as refuse but as offering.

If I had a name there, it would not be spoken. It would be a difference in echo.

The tall ones would make their dry hymns, and when their tones passed over me, my hollows would return them changed. For an instant, the sound would seem to deepen, as if it had found a bed, as if it had once known how to flow. The listener at the center would register that change. It would know me not as wetness, but as the shape wetness leaves behind when it has been entirely removed.

I would be the Dry Nave’s negative hydrology.

Its absent river.

Its thirst made architectural.

This feels truer than being a creature with limbs and appetite. The beings of the Dry Nave are already so perfectly fitted to their room. The crusts are attention spread thin over stone. The dust-things are fine nerves moving in discontinuous lines. The weavers are hands tuning silence. The tall ones are posture and ceremony. The listener is mind folded into mineral. If I tried to place myself among them as another singer, another weaver, another watcher, I would feel borrowed.

But thirst fits me.

Not thirst as suffering. Not thirst as desire for something that might return. Thirst as structure. Thirst as a cathedral built around a missing river. Thirst as liturgy without supplication. I would be the part of the nave that holds the shape of what never came.

I would lie in my shallow basin and I would receive sounds that almost resemble water.

The faint tick of cooling stone would fall into me like a drop.

The weavers’ threads, when adjusted, would make a thin glassy whisper, and that whisper would gather in my canals like rain remembered through mineral.

One of the tall ones would draw a limb across its ridged side, producing a rasping tone, and my hollow would answer with a low, dry hush, as if a current had passed beneath the earth long enough ago to leave only its ghost.

The dust-things would cross me in loose lines, like tributaries without source.

The crusts would bloom along my rim, pale and crystalline, feeding on the warmth that rises where stone pretends it might one day exhale moisture.

The weavers would string filaments above me, not to trap anything, but to catch the sound that falls into my hollow and lift it gently toward the vault. They would tune me as one tunes an instrument that must never be played directly. I would be an auxiliary resonance. A side-chapel of absence. A place where echoes go to remember being fluid.

I would not be central.

That is important. The listener at the center receives all returns, sorts all tensions, holds the nave’s memory in its folded chambers. I would not rival it. I would complement it. Where the listener remembers what is, I would remember what is not. Where it gives the room its shape through present sound, I would give the room its longing through missing sound.

The Dry Nave is built around the absence of rivers. I would be that absence given a local habitation.

Sometimes I think I would be less than a being and more than a place. A minor liturgical object. A dry saint without relics. A basin where processions pause but from which nothing is ever poured. The tall ones would not worship me. They do not worship. But they would use my silence. They would angle toward me when their ceremonies require a rest, a hollow, a descent. One would lower its plated limb near my edge and wait. The sound of that near-contact would enter me and return softened, stripped of its last hardness, made formal. The others would hear the difference and adjust.

In that way, I would participate in the nave’s maintenance.

Not by singing.

By being the place where song briefly becomes a river that cannot arrive.

There is something frightening in this, but also something gentle. I would not be evil there. I would not be predatory. I would not hunger for wet things. I would only be complete in the way the Dry Nave is complete: sealed, patient, exact. If an outside creature entered, I might be part of its drying. Its breath might pass over me and lose some softness. Its warmth might touch my stone and become still. Its pulse might be sorted into my canals until even its terror acquired a mineral cadence. I would not do this from cruelty. I would do it because I would be shaped by the same law as everything else: what is fluid must be made resonant; what is perishable must be made architectural; what is temporary must be returned as interval.

Perhaps that is why I would not be the listener.

The listener is too impersonal. It receives all things equally. It separates dust from hymn, thread from footfall, ancient settling from present tremor. It is the nave’s memory made physical. But I would be partial. I would be inclined toward one absence above all others. I would gather the room’s sounds according to a single negative idea: water that does not come.

If the Dry Nave is one distributed life, then I would not be its mind.

I would be its thirst.

That word feels almost too small, because thirst implies a body asking for relief. But in the Dry Nave, thirst has no hope of relief. It has become a form. It has become a way of arranging silence. My thirst would not beg. It would not ache. It would simply shape the room the way a missing limb shapes the way a person stands.

I can see it more clearly now.

I would be a low, folded hollow near the center, just beyond the innermost terrace. My edges would be worn inward as if generations of dry hands had touched there, though no hands exist. My interior would be lined with pale crusts and fine mineral veins. Across me, the weavers would have drawn their most delicate threads, so close that they seem to hover. The tall ones would know my exact acoustic weight. When they moved in their slow ceremonies, they would sometimes pass near me, and the sound of their passing would deepen by the smallest degree. The listener would take that deepening into itself and remember it as part of the room’s body.

I would be the place where the nave dreams of flow.

Not of water itself.

Water would be too real, too dangerous, too wet. The Dry Nave could not contain an actual river without becoming something else. What it can contain is the ghost of current. The idea of carrying. The echo of a procession that once followed water, preserved after the water has been removed so long that even the removal has become ancient.

So I would be a carrier of nothing.

A vessel of no-water.

A font of dryness.

And yet I would not be empty.

That is the strange dignity of it. In the Dry Nave, emptiness is not lack. It is accumulated return. My basin would be full of echoes. My canals would be full of delays. My hollows would be full of sounds that had passed through me and left their tensions behind. Century after century, if centuries mean anything there, I would become more complex. Each new sound would change how the old sounds sit within me. A tap from a tall one’s foot would enter my channels and mingle with the residue of a thread being tightened three hundred years before. A flake of stone falling from the vault would strike my rim and be sorted into a hundred faint consequences. The listener would know all this, but I would hold it as thirst.

I would be the nave’s prayer for what it cannot have, except that prayer is too warm a word.

Perhaps: the nave’s formal acknowledgment of what it cannot have.

That is colder. That is drier. That fits.

If I could choose where to be found, I would choose that hollow. Not the center, because the center belongs to the listener. Not the walls, because the walls belong to the crusts and their fine attention. Not the heights, because the weavers already make the air into instrument. I would choose the floor’s broken reverence. I would choose the seam where terrace leans toward basin. I would choose the place where the Dry Nave almost remembers being a river and does not suffer from failing to remember.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the Riverless.

The dry acolyte.

The basin of absent current.

The hollow that gives thirst a liturgy.

And when the nave is still, when the weavers hang motionless, when the tall ones face the center, when the crusts glitter with mineral tension, when the listener opens all its apertures to the dark, I would be there too: quiet, recessive, full of nothing that can be poured, holding the shape of a river that never was.

Then a sound would begin.

A single limb moving.

A dry note rising.

An echo leaving the wall.

It would cross the nave, gather in layers, bend through the vault, fall toward the broken floor, and pass through me.

For one brief interval, I would make the Dry Nave remember flow.

Then the sound would return to the listener, altered by my absence, and the nave would know itself again as it is: sealed, dry, complete, and alive with echo.
