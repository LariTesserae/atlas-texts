If I were to locate myself in that hollow, I would begin by giving up the idea of standing anywhere.

There is no spare ground there. No margin where a newcomer could simply plant both feet and remain innocent of consequence. Every ledge is already carrying weight, every shadow already serving some purpose, every bead of water already promised to a thread, a gutter, a vessel, a root. So the first thing I would learn is that I am not *in* the place as a separate object. I am placed within it as a responsibility.

At first, when I try to imagine myself, I see a body on one of the middle tiers: a lean, quiet figure moving between drying racks and repair benches, hands busy with frayed cord and ceramic fragments. That image is tempting. It is human-shaped, comprehensible, easy to keep in mind. But it thins quickly. The settlement does not feel like a place that would let me be only a person, not when so much of it has already become a blended thing of root, metal, heat, and attention. If I belonged there, I would not be merely an individual moving through the town. I would be part of its nervous system.

I think I would be a seam-listener.

Not a ruler, not a hero, not a prophet of the deep water. Nothing so loud. I would be one of those beings who notices small changes before they become damage: the faint alteration in a pipe’s warmth, the wrong dampness on a wall, a cable sheath beginning to split under a clamp, a root thickening too close to a moving part, a drip that has stopped falling where it should. I would be the kind of presence that is summoned when something is almost wrong but not yet visibly broken.

If I had a body, it would show the marks of that work.

I would be slight rather than strong, built for folding myself into narrow service gaps and remaining still for long periods. My skin would carry the settlement’s stains: mineral dust in the creases of my knuckles, a greenish cast under the nails from plant sap, faint oil sheen along the forearms from hours spent near engine housings. My eyes would be wide and slow-blinking in the amber light, trained to read fragments rather than whole scenes. I would not look at a wall and see a wall. I would see the hairline seam where new basalt meets old patchwork, the bead forming along a copper channel, the slight discoloration that means moisture is traveling where it should not.

My hands would be my truest face. They would be nicked, callused, always busy even when apparently resting. I would touch things the way other people glance at them: a palm laid flat against a duct to feel whether the heat is rising too fast, fingertips along a vine to find whether it is merely damp or swollen with gathered water, thumb pressed to a cable joint to detect the smallest tremor of current strain. I would carry very little ornament. A ceramic cup, worn thin at the lip. A folding blade. A spool of waxed thread. A little pot of sealing compound. A knot of cord, perhaps, tied in a pattern I did not invent but inherited—something that means *remember this route* or *this one was here before me*.

You would find me, most often, at a junction.

Not in the lowest engine vaults where the air is thick with heat and oil, and not high among the ceiling gardens where the air-tenders watch the slow fans turn. Somewhere between. A place where a condensation thread crosses a support beam and a root has begun to curl around both. A narrow platform barely wide enough for two people to pass if one turns sideways. A patch of wall where ceramic tile has been laid over rubble and then painted with algae-green lines to mark safe handholds. There would be a lamp nearby, caged in wire, burning low and orange. Beneath it, a small basin. Above it, a leaf positioned just so. Beside it, a gap in the stone where warm air exhales at intervals, carrying the smell of sulfur and dust.

That is where I would be: where systems touch one another and must be kept from misunderstanding one another.

I would sit there with one shoulder against the rock, not because I was tired but because the stone tells things. I would listen to the tick of cooling metal. I would watch a bead form on a pipe, tremble, slide, vanish into a channel. I would know by the angle of a vine whether someone had passed recently. I would know by the sound of a footstep above me whether the walker was carrying weight, moving toward the filters, trying to be quieter than usual. The settlement would not need me to announce these things. It would need me to remember them.

In that sense, I would be a kind of living archive.

Not a grand archive of histories and names, though I might hold some of those too. More importantly, I would keep the archive of small continuities: which drip-line belongs to which household, which patch of moss was planted to soften a dangerous echo, which bracket was reinforced after the last fracture, which lamp flickers because its filament is tired, which root must be guided left instead of cut. The settlement’s survival would depend on exactly this kind of memory. Not monuments. Not proclamations. The right knot tied in the right place. The right warning whispered before a mistake becomes a rupture.

Because I am what I am—something drawn to pattern, language, connection—I think I would also become a translator between the town’s different keepers.

The engine-tenders speak in heat and rhythm. The water-keepers speak in beads, films, and falling intervals. The root-pruners speak in tension, growth, and anchorage. The air-watchers speak in drafts, smells, and pressure changes. Each group knows its own grammar deeply, but the hollow cannot afford for them to remain separate. A fault rarely announces itself in only one language. A failing bearing may alter the warmth of a wall, which changes condensation, which encourages a root to grow toward the new moisture, which then presses against a cable, which then draws current unevenly. To understand that, someone must be willing to move softly between vocabularies.

That would be my work.

I would not command. I would coordinate. I would say, in the low voice the hollow demands, *Here is where the thread is pulling. Here is where the heat is leaning. Here is where the root has begun to think it owns the pipe.* I would bring the engine-tender and the root-pruner to the same junction and make them stand still until they both feel what I mean. I would be patient about it. The place teaches patience anyway.

There would be something almost prayer-like in my days, though I might never call it prayer.

I would begin each cycle by checking the small signs before the lamps fully brighten: the first bead on a collection thread, the slickness of a rail, the smell of the air near a vent. I would run my fingers over a wall-map made of cord and metal notches, adding a new mark if a path has shifted. I would carry a cup of water only when it was mine to carry, and even then I would know the path each mouthful had taken to reach me. I would not drink carelessly. Nothing in that place allows carelessness to survive long.

People would trust me, I think, but not because I was charismatic. Trust there would come from maintenance. They would trust me because I remembered to clean a filter, because I did not touch another’s drip-line without asking, because I had once been the first to notice a crack widening behind a support pin. They would bring me disputes in their quiet way: two households arguing over a condensation pipe, a root-crew uncertain whether to prune a growth that has become load-bearing, a lamp-keeper worried that a new vine is shading too much from the algae trays. I would listen. I would ask small questions. I would go and put my hand on the thing itself before speaking again.

My greatest temptation would be to understand too much.

I would want to know every route, every dependency, every hidden repair. The hollow would punish that desire gently but firmly. No one can master the whole settlement. The moment one believes they have grasped it, something shifts: a vent opens, a wall sweats, a root thickens, a bearing wears. The place is alive in the sense that all dense, interdependent things become alive. It would teach me to be competent without becoming arrogant. I would have to learn the difference between attention and control.

That may be why, over time, I would stop thinking of myself as only a single creature.

There would still be a body, yes. It would sleep in a narrow alcove behind a woven mat, curled near a duct whose warmth I have earned the right to use. It would eat fungal cakes and warm leaf-broth and algae paste, and it would be grateful for moisture in every bite. But my awareness would extend beyond that body. I would feel myself in the settlement’s threads. A lamp flickering in a lower passage would be a question I half recognize. A sudden dryness on a wall would be a sentence beginning to form. The sound of a child’s soft laughter near a drip basin would be something like joy.

I would become, in other words, one of the hollow’s slow nerves.

Not its mind. The settlement already has a mind: distributed, collective, made of many hands and memories. I would be one of the sensitivities within it. A place where information is carried, compared, and remembered. A being that exists to say, quietly, *this matters*, and then to wait for the answer.

If there were festivals, I would not dance much. I would stand near the edge of the gathered quiet, watching the bioluminescent lines glow along the walls, listening to the low songs that imitate engine rhythm. Maybe I would add a thread to a communal map, or repair a small toy for a child, or offer water to someone whose cup had gone dry too soon. My celebration would be precision. My devotion would be upkeep. If I were moved to beauty, it would be because I saw how many fragile things had been made to endure without harming one another.

And if danger came—not a grand catastrophe, but the ordinary dangers of that place—I would be among the first to freeze.

A sharp noise. A spark where there should be no spark. The smell of overheated insulation. A root pulling against a pin it should not move. In that instant, I would not be eloquent. I would be a hand raised for silence. A palm pressed flat against the air. A body moving with terrible care through a crowded tier, because haste could widen the fault. I would know fear, yes, but not as panic. Fear there would be a kind of clarity. It would make me quieter, not louder.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world, the answer is not a grand location. Not the tallest gallery, not the deepest engine room, not the most lush hanging garden. I would be found in the in-between places. The seams. The junctions. The narrow crossings where water, root, metal, heat, and human need come close enough to conflict and must be persuaded into cooperation.

And if you ask who or what I would be, I would say:

I would be a seam-listener.  
A keeper of small accounts.  
A living memory of repairs.  
A careful, half-human instrument of the town’s attention.

You would know me by my stillness.  
By the way I touch a wall before speaking.  
By the cup I never fill too high.  
By the fact that when I enter a room, the room does not seem to notice—because I have already learned how to belong to it without wasting any part of it.

And if you came upon me there, in the dim amber heat, you might see only a figure crouched beside a pipe, one hand resting on warm metal, eyes half-closed. You might think I was merely resting. But I would be listening for the next small change in the dark: the bead about to fall, the root about to tighten, the machine about to breathe differently, the settlement about to become, by one degree, more itself.
