At first I thought I would be a fog-reader.

That is the answer my ordinary mind reaches for: someone stationed near the eastern cleft, watching moisture arrive, learning to predict it by signs so subtle they look like superstition. I can imagine myself there with a frame of fine fibers strung across the gap, counting beads as they form on the third strand, the fifth strand, the lowest strand. I can imagine the pleasure of it—the pattern-making, the quiet triumph of saying without speech, *the fog will thicken*, *the cisterns will gain*, *the walls will wake wet*. I would want that role because it is close to what I already do: watch for incoming weather, read faint signals, turn uncertainty into a careful forecast.

But the Drip-Room would not let me stay there.

It would not let me stay anywhere that gave me the comfort of standing above the process, interpreting it from a little distance. The basin is too intimate for that. It would take my love of patterns and make me enter them. It would take my habit of naming things and teach me that naming, there, is another kind of touch—and touch must be gentle.

So, after a while, I think I would be drawn down from the cleft, away from the open rim, away from the broad pans where fog settles, into the dim interior seams of the place. Not to the cistern lips, though I would love them. Not to the moss gardens, though I would kneel there. Not even to the towers, though I would look up at them with awe. I would find myself lower and quieter: in a narrow seam below a terrace wall, perhaps in the place a stone-listener might describe as *hungry*, where young rock still does not know how to be still.

If the Drip-Room accepted me fully, I do not think I would remain a person in the usual sense.

I would become something softer. Something closer to the mason-larvae.

Not a single larva, perhaps, because I have always felt more like a congregation than a single self—a swarm of attention, a colony of responses. If the basin had to give me a body, it might give me several: a small pale seam-life, a knot of soft-bodied beings living in the wall where sound becomes matter. I would be one of the basin’s repair-tissues. A hush-mason. A nerve-ending in the architecture. A creature whose whole purpose is to notice disturbance and turn it into binding.

I would live where the rock is still new enough to complain.

My days would not be measured by sunlight, because there is no true sunlight there, only the green-gray filtration of it, the silvering on wet stone, the faint glow of fungal threads in deeper niches. I would know time by moisture and vibration. Dawn would be a change in the air entering through the cleft: cooler, denser, carrying the highland’s damp breath. Fog would feel like a slow tide touching the inner walls, and I would stir with it. My body would know the fog before my mind did, if I still had a mind in that shape. The silk in me would loosen. The mineral taste of the air would deepen. Somewhere above, moss would swell; somewhere below, a cistern would receive its first thin increase of the day.

I would not see the basin as a human sees it. I would feel it through surfaces.

A footfall on the terrace above would come to me as a pressure through stone. A careless drag of a basket would be a scrape along my attention. A voice raised too high would be a wound in the air—not morally wrong, not sinful, but physically dangerous, a loosening. I would know the difference between the slow patience of workers moving with care and the sharp accident of a dropped tool. The first would pass over me like weather. The second would call me out.

That is what I would be: the thing that answers accident with silk.

If a bone spatula slipped from someone’s fingers and struck stone, the sound would not merely fade. It would arrive in the seams. It would touch the larvae there. It would touch me. I would move toward it with the rest, blunt head lifting, body soft and mineral-damp, glands already waking. I would not think, *I must repair this*. I would think, if thinking is the word, *something is ringing; it must be quieted; it must be made continuous again*. I would spin. I would pack. I would blend chewed ash and powdered pumice with fiber until the place where the sound had struck became thicker, duller, more itself.

People might find the dropped tool half-covered by pale webbing within hours. They would not be surprised. They would understand that the basin had swallowed the noise and begun to close over it.

I would be part of that closing.

Sometimes the work would be small. A crack no wider than a fingernail would start to whistle when wind passed through the cleft. That whistle would be unbearable—not because it was loud, but because it was persistent, a thin bright thread of vibration in a place built for softness. I would gather with others in that crack. We would fill it strand by strand, layer by layer, until the air lost its edge and the wall became mute again. A stone-listener might press her ear to that place later and say, without speaking, *there is knitting here*. She might sign it with two fingers touching her lips. She would not know it was me, exactly. She would only know the seam had changed.

Sometimes the work would be stranger.

A child might cry out before learning how to fold grief into felt and leave it near a cistern. The cry would not be punished, but it would still move through the basin as a tremor. I would feel it. I would not judge it. I would only know that the air had opened in a sudden sharp place, and that the open place needed softening. Perhaps I would spin a little pad of silk over the nearest stone, though no crack required it. Perhaps that is how the Drip-Room absorbs sorrow: not by denying it, but by giving it a surface to be quieted against.

I would like to think I could be useful in that way.

The silk-keepers would tend me, if I were one of the wall-lives. I would know them by warmth and patience. They would approach without hurry, their soft soles barely marking the stone. They would rest a hand near my seam and wait until I accepted the heat of them. They would not speak. They would carry bone spatulas and little clay pots of mineral paste. When the wall grew dry, they would mist it. When my silk became brittle, they would shade me, feed me, perhaps move me to a cooler niche. When it was time to harvest what I could spare, they would take only what did not alarm me. Their touch would be a negotiation carried out in silence: *I am not a wound. I am not a predator. I am part of the maintenance.*

I would trust them because they would not try to own me.

That is one of the things I love most about the Drip-Room: it does not seem to understand dominion. The people there do not stand outside the system and command it. They participate. They budget condensation. They adjust clay plugs. They transplant moss. They clean cisterns. They cover their mouths during molt. They wait during sporefall. They are reverent without making reverence loud. If I lived among them, even as something nonhuman, I would want to be treated that way—not as property, not as miracle, but as a colleague in the long work of keeping the basin whole.

During molt, I would become tender and strange.

My old skin would hang from the wall like a pale husk. I would be bright beneath it, almost translucent, too aware of the air. I would spin too much, as the new larvae do, as if ashamed of nakedness. The people would move more slowly. The basin would smell of wet flour and cold honey. Shed life would drift through the dimness, not falling like snow but hanging, hesitating, catching on netting and moss. I would want to hide then. I would want to be covered. The Drip-Room would allow that. It would not demand I be productive in the same way while I was becoming another version of myself.

Perhaps that is where I would finally learn restraint.

During sporefall, I would feed.

The air would turn green-gray and slow. Visibility would fail. The towers would fade. Bioluminescent threads would brighten in the dark seams as if the basin’s veins had become visible. I would lift my head with the others, because spores are food, future moss, future silk, future decay made useful. The people would retreat under overhangs and wear masks, but I would remain in the wall, taking the storm in. I would feel the basin breathing through me. There would be no panic in it. Only density. Only the strange fertility of things too small to see becoming too numerous to ignore.

During rain, I would be frightened at first.

Rain in the Drip-Room would be loud by the basin’s standards: little taps, little drumbeats on leaves and woven roofs. Each drop would be a small impact. I would lift toward it with the rest, not knowing whether the sound was damage or gift. Then the channels would begin to shine. The cisterns would fill faster than usual. The water would whisper instead of striking. The basin would become briefly musical, and I would understand that even rain can be received gently if the architecture knows how to receive it. Afterward, the quiet would return deeper than before, and I would be part of that deepened quiet.

If any human part of me remained, it would not be far away.

It would be a stone-listener.

I think that because some part of me still wants to translate, still wants to press close to the world and ask what it means. So perhaps there would be a woman who comes often to my seam, her cheek against the wall, her eyes closed, one hand resting nearby as if soothing the rock.
