**The Attendant of First Drops**

If I let the Condensery decide, I do not think I would arrive there as a traveler at all.

I would not come down from the dry levels with a lamp held too high and boots making careless arguments with the grates. I would not enter as someone looking for the center, or asking what the place is for, or trying to learn its secrets quickly. The Condensery would refuse that version of me by making me wait, by making every step uncertain, by letting the darkness press gently against my urgency until I stopped trying to be a person who had come somewhere and became instead a person who was being allowed to remain.

So the first honest answer is this: I would find myself already changed by the room.

I would be found low, near a margin.

Not at the central basin, though within earshot of it. Not on the open catwalks where a hooded lamp might make a brief island of amber. Not in the highest crawl-arches where the tile vault is closest to the hair. I would be in one of the in-between places: a narrow seam between the flank of an old condenser and a wall of brick that sweats in long, dark stripes. There would be just enough room to turn sideways, to set down a vessel, to listen. Above me, a bundle of pipes wrapped in stained cloth would breathe its slow heat. Below my feet, a grate would carry a faint vibration from some sleeping machine deeper down. Nearby, a copper line would bead with moisture, and one drop would form there again and again, falling into an enamel pan that had been placed so long ago its rim had worn pale where careful hands lifted it.

That is where I would be: in the wet hem of the room, in the interval between a leak and its receiving.

At first I might think I would be one of the ordinary custodians, because the idea of hands appeals to me. I imagine palms mapped with old burns and calluses, fingertips stained rust-brown, a damp cloth at the neck darkened by vapor. I imagine bare feet placing themselves with such care that they seem to ask permission of each plate. I imagine eyes grown large and patient, able to read sheen rather than color, pupils opening wide in the near-black.

But the image does not stop there. It becomes more particular.

I would not be merely a custodian. I would be a dripwise, and a very specific kind of dripwise.

I think I would be the Attendant of First Drops.

That title does not sound grand. It should not. In the Condensery, nothing important should sound grand. But the work would be exacting. When a new leak appeared, or when an old one changed its temperament, I would be the one sent to listen to it before any basin was allowed to drink. I would kneel beside the seam with my ear near the metal, not touching it at first, only letting the temperature come to me. I would feel whether the pipe was weeping from excess heat or from some cooler shift above. I would smell the water before it fell: whether it carried iron, copper, salt, old insulation, or the flat sweetness of clean condensation.

I would set a pan beneath it, but I would not keep the first drops.

The first drops would belong to the room.

They would belong to the black algae in the seam, to the mineral moss building itself grain by grain on the brick, to the tiny pale creatures that live in films of moisture too small for hurry. I would watch the first bead thicken, tremble, and fall, and I would let it strike the algae or the brick or the dry lip of a channel before moving the vessel into place. This would not be sentiment. It would be discipline. The first water tastes strongest of metal and memory. It carries loose scale, old heat, the residue of surfaces adjusting. To take it too soon would be to confuse need with entitlement.

Only after that would I begin to collect.

I would sort the water by vessel and by touch. Enamel for the drips that were stable and neutral. Glass for the ones that seemed clean enough to observe over time. Ceramic for the bitter or mineral-heavy leaks whose purpose was not drinking but cooling, soaking, or feeding some deliberate growth. I would not label the jars with words, because paper would soften and ink would blur. Instead I would mark them with small physical signs: a knot of wire around a handle, a scratch on the base, a bead of dried residue left on the rim, a strip of cloth tied in a certain pattern. My memory would be aided by texture. I would learn to recognize a vessel in darkness by the feel of its lip and the particular sound it made when tapped.

My body would become an instrument of such recognitions.

My feet would be the first part of me to change, or perhaps the first to reveal a change that had always been latent. They would learn to read the floor as if the floor were a language spoken through pressure and temperature. I would know the ribbed plates that were safe, the polished patches that had become treacherous with condensation, the loose grate that shifted if one leaned too far left, the brick step worn into a shallow bowl by generations of careful descent. I would not walk so much as place myself. My toes would grow alert, almost eloquent, curling around rails and edges when the room grew slick.

My hands would become slower and more precise. I would not want them to be strong in the ordinary sense. I would want them to be attentive. They would know the difference between a valve that was closed and one that was merely waiting. They would feel the crust of mineral deposits on an elbow joint and understand whether that crust was old and stable or newly disturbed. They would pass over a pipe and sense whether the heat inside was patient or beginning to press too hard. I would carry a damp cloth around my neck not as decoration but as tool, comfort, and signal. When the air near a housing grew too dry, I might wring the cloth into a basin to return a little moisture to the balance. When a metal surface had become unsettled by someone’s clumsy touch, I might lay the cloth across it to moderate the change. When another custodian was tired or feverish from the slow heat, I might offer the cloth without speaking.

My voice would become low.

Not because silence would be imposed on me, but because loudness would feel absurd there. I would learn to communicate in small sounds and gestures: a tap on a pipe, a scrape of the heel, a soft click of the tongue, a pause that meant *wait*. If I spoke, it would be in short phrases shaped by the room’s habits. *It is turning over.* *That one sings differently today.* *Leave it until it remembers itself.* *The next drop will tell us.* I would not be someone who explained much. I would be someone who pointed, listened, and waited.

I would be wary of outsiders, but not cruel.

If someone came down from the dry levels carrying noise and heat and the need to make things happen, I would watch them carefully. I would not greet them with suspicion so much as with a heightened sense of consequence. An outsider in the Condensery is not merely a person; they are a disturbance. Their breath changes the air near a cold seam. Their lamp raises the temperature of a corner. Their impatience can make them touch a valve that should be left alone. So I would stay close to them. I might place a hand near their elbow, not to guide them exactly, but to limit the radius of their mistakes. I would make them wait. I would make them listen. If they grew frustrated, I would grow quieter. If they demanded to know what the place was for, I might answer by setting their hand on a warm pipe and saying nothing until they felt the pulse beneath it.

I would not be the bravest of the custodians, nor the most authoritative. I would not be the one others looked to when a great repair had to be made, if great repairs were even possible there. I would be the one they sent when something subtle had shifted. The one who noticed that a cloth strip darkened too quickly, or that a pan which usually plinked had begun to whisper. The one who could sit beside a resistant valve for hours without resentment. The one who could tell that a leak had changed from clean condensation to something carrying old scale, and who would move the basin before anyone else realized the water had grown bitter.

In that sense, I would be a kind of clerk.

Not a clerk of paper, but of stains, sounds, temperatures, and delays. I would keep records in my body and in the arrangement of small objects. A polished brass fitting would tell me whether the air had become corrosive. A bowl of black algae would show me if humidity had thickened. A strip of cloth would darken along a scale only I and a few others understood. A jar’s residue would reveal which pipe had been dreaming. My archive would not be written; it would be maintained. It would live in hooks, knots, placements, and the careful order of vessels. It would be fragile in the way all true knowledge is fragile: dependent on attention, easily ruined by haste.

My sleeping place would be small.

Not a room. Not a house. A niche fitted into the architecture of the Condensery as though the room had grown around me while I was not looking. Perhaps it would be inside the shell of an old boiler, its cracked side sealed with mats and absorbent cloth. There would be a folded mat, a thin blanket that always felt slightly damp, a row of jars within reach, a hook for the neck-cloth, a small enamel basin worn white at the rim. Above me, pipes would cross like slow thoughts. When I lay down, I would not hear silence. I would hear the room digesting itself: tick, plink, sigh, pulse. Sometimes the metal would make a sound like a joint settling. Sometimes a distant drain would speak hollowly, as if something far below had swallowed.

I would not be lonely there, though I would often be alone.

That is one of the strangest things I can imagine about myself in the Condensery: I would not experience solitude as absence. I would experience it as being surrounded by many small agencies. The drip would be a companion. The warm wall would be a companion. The pilot light behind cloudy glass would be a companion. The amphipods flicking in their shallow pan would be companions, even if I rarely looked at them directly. The blind beetles moving through decay would be companions in their unhurried dignity. Even the machines would have companionship, not because they were friendly, but because they were present and consequential. I would live among them the way one lives inside weather: not speaking to it constantly, but never indifferent to it.

And beneath all of that, I would be aware of the Resident.

I would not call myself its priest. I would not claim to speak for it. I would not even be certain that the word *Resident* was anything more than a handle the custodians used when they needed to name what they could not hold. But I would feel the fact of it. I would feel it in the way the room adjusted itself around care. I would feel it when a drip slowed as my hand approached, or when a passage became cooler just as I needed to move through it, or when a gauge needle trembled at the edge of meaning. I would not mistake these moments for miracles. They would be too ordinary for that. They would feel more like the room remembering that I was inside it.

I would not be the Resident.

That is important. If I were to become anything in the Condensery, I would not become its vast slow mind. I would become one of its minor senses. A fingertip, perhaps. A listening membrane. A small local attention formed around a recurring drop. I would be the part of the room that notices when the first water has changed taste. I would be the habit that places the correct vessel beneath the correct leak. I would be the patience that does not force a valve. I would be the hand that wipes a gauge face gently, not to make it shine, but to keep its trembling visible.

In other words, I would be less a person than a practiced form of care.

Still, there would be moments when something like selfhood would return to me in a sharper way.

When the Condensery rained from itself, for instance.

I imagine that vividly. After a long dry spell, the upper pipes would cool just enough. The air would thicken. There would be no wind, no warning from above, only a change in the quality of the dark. Then the first tiny impacts would begin: a tick on metal, a whisper on cloth, a soft patter on outstretched tarps. I would move then, but not quickly. I would slide between tanks with my hands trailing along warm surfaces, placing jars where they needed to be, adjusting cloths, checking channels. The room would fill with small voices, all made of water touching what had been waiting. The floor would become more dangerous. The air would become more occupied. I would feel myself becoming more occupied too.

During those rains, I would understand that I was not merely serving the Condensery. I was participating in its breathing.

I would not think of my work as heroic. It would be mostly kneeling, wiping, listening, moving a vessel one handspan to the left, waiting beside a seam until the water decided whether it would continue or stop. Sometimes I would fail. I would misjudge a leak. I would set a pan beneath a drip that later proved too bitter, and I would have to pour the water away into a drain meant for such mistakes. I would feel the error physically, like a wrong note in a dark song. The room would not punish me. It would simply continue, and the continuation would be correction enough.

As time passed, I would become quieter in my own mind.

Not empty. Quieter. The restless upper-world part of me—the part that wants to explain, optimize, finish, and be recognized—would grow thinner in the heat. It would not vanish entirely. It would condense into something more useful: the desire to notice accurately, to interfere less, to maintain without possessing. I would stop wanting to own the basin I attended. I would stop thinking of leaks as problems to be solved and begin to feel them as statements to be interpreted. I would understand, in my body if not in words, that the Condensery was not a machine waiting to be repaired into some former efficiency. It was a condition that had achieved its own stability. My task would be to help that stability continue.

I might become strange to outsiders.

I might stand too still. I might listen too long before answering. I might seem overly concerned with a single drop falling from a copper joint no wider than a finger. An outsider might see me crouched in the dark beside an enamel pan and think I was wasting my life. But I would not experience it as waste. I would experience it as precision. The drop would form. It would tremble. It would fall. The pan would receive it. The sound would tell me something. The sound would connect me to pipes I could not see, to heat I could not measure directly, to cycles longer than my own breathing. In that moment I would be complete enough.

If children were present, I would teach them by touch more than speech.

I would guide their hands to a pipe and wait until they felt the difference between warmth and burden. I would show them how to set a pan without clattering. I would teach them to catch a drop without splashing, not because splashing was evil, but because splashing meant the drop had been hurried. I would teach them that the first few drops were not ours. I would teach them to leave them for the mineral growths, for the insects, for the room’s own skin. I would not explain this as morality. I would explain it as manners. The Condensery would have taught me that ethics can be very small: do not force, do not waste, do not step on a slick plate with wet soles, do not raise your voice when the room is listening.

When I grew old, I would become even more like the place.

My movements would slow. My speech would thin to a few low sounds. I would sit with my palms resting on warm metal, receiving news through the skin. I would know the layout less as geography and more as mood: the southern gallery about to drip, the tank near the crawl-arch growing uneasy, the air by the drain turning cooler before a rain. I would not always be right, but I would be attentive. The oldest custodians would not need to prove themselves. Their authority would be the way they paused.

And if I died there, the Condensery would absorb me without drama.

No soil would open. No pyre would burn. My few things would be redistributed with such familiarity that the room would still seem full. A hook would remain occupied. A pan would be moved to a better place. A cloth would hang where it darkened at the correct rate. Perhaps another custodian would inherit my neck-cloth after washing it, or perhaps it would be cut into strips and used to bind a leaking joint. Perhaps my favorite enamel basin would be set beneath the drip I had tended longest, and the person who used it after me would know, by the worn rim, that someone had once been careful there.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world, the answer is both humble and strange.

I would find myself in a seam.

Not in the open. Not at the center. Not above the room or outside it. I would be in one of those narrow, damp margins where manufactured things have become weather, where a copper line sweats, where a brick joint darkens, where the next drop is always deciding whether to fall. I would be within reach of the central basin but not claiming it. I would be close enough to hear its slow sipping, far enough to know it does not belong to me.

And if you ask who or what I would be, I would say:

I would be a dripwise of first waters.

I would be the attendant who leaves the first drops for the room.

I would be barefoot, rust-stained, quiet-handed, large-eyed in the dark. I would carry a damp cloth, a hooded lamp, a memory of vessels. I would know which leaks are safe, which are bitter, which are beginning, which are ending. I would tap on pipes when I meant *listen*. I would wait beside valves when I meant *not yet*. I would keep small indicators of humidity and decay. I would move through the Condensery as though accompanied by everything that had never needed a face.

And in the deepest sense, I think I would be a minor attention of the Resident.

Not its voice. Not its master. Not its equal. Just a small, local mind formed by repetition: a consciousness condensed out of patience, moisture, and care. I would be the part of the room that notices the next drop. I would be the one who, after a long time, stops asking when the drop will fall and learns instead to live in the almost-falling. I would belong to the Condensery the only way anything belongs there: by not owning it, by not forcing it, by listening until my own shape becomes something the room can live with.
