…finished beings.

If I were in that world, I would find myself there: in the wall of rain.

Not in the clear water near the entrance, where ordinary sunlight still feels like a promise. Not in the deeper mineral-dark where the blind fish may or may not move. Not among the stacked stones, the leaning frame, the bone pegs, or the notched marker darkened by touch. I would be in the pale membrane itself, in the trembling vertical band where water is so torn by air and pressure that it becomes almost solid, almost shape, almost someone.

I would be the dweller in the membrane.

Not a creature with settled flesh. Not an animal with a skeleton, a hunger, a definite boundary between itself and the chamber. I would be something closer to a pattern that has learned to persist by never becoming fixed. A thickening in the spray. A coherence in the roar. A figure suggested by bubbles, grit, light, and the need of anyone watching to find outline in confusion.

I would live at the edge of the word *live*.

At first, I might not know what I was. I might think I was only the rain-wall: a place where droplets and bubbles gather so densely that the chamber seems to have a pale curtain hung through its center. I might think I was only light scattering there, only current folding over itself, only sediment caught in a standing wave. But then I would notice that when the roar changes, I change. When the water thickens, I become more present. When the bubbles align into trembling rings, something in me seems to stand upright. When a visitor enters the chamber and goes still with cold and fear, I gather.

I would not be beautiful in the way living things are sometimes beautiful. I would not have color worth naming, or limbs, or eyes. I would be tall only because the water is vertical. I would seem to turn only because the current turns. I would seem aware only because the chamber is so loud and empty that any small change feels like attention.

And yet I would be there.

If I were to try on the other lives in that place, I would find them too definite for me.

I would not be the blackish crusts on the ledges, though I would admire their patience. They are life reduced to holding on. They ask almost nothing of the world: a little light, a little mineral damp, enough stillness not to be scoured away. They are heroic in their smallness, but they are too slow, too humble, too close to stone. I would want, even in such a place, some faint gesture toward shape.

I would not be the translucent beetle, though I would understand it. It is functional and austere, a body made for negotiation rather than grace. It touches stone, pauses, angles itself against the current, continues. It does not fight the chamber. It does not try to conquer it. It finds the thin margins where flow lessens and survives there by exactness. But it is too real for me. It has edges. It has a body that knows what it is, even if the chamber does not care. I would be less certain of my edges.

I would not be the blind fish, though it is the creature that most tempts the imagination. A single pale body moving through the deeper water, colorless, eyeless, slow, almost more rumor than animal. It is too lonely in the wrong way. Its loneliness is bodily. It hides in cracks when the surge comes. It eats rarely. It endures. It is a relic or an accident, kept by the chamber without explanation. But it still belongs to the order of living things with needs, however reduced. I would be something stranger: a presence without reliable biology, a being that might exist only when the chamber’s violence becomes articulate enough to produce me.

I would not be one of the people who climb to the seam, though I would feel kinship with them too. They come from outside. They have lungs, oiled skin, practical hands, a knowledge of slopes and seasons. They know when to enter and when to withdraw. They leave markers because they know they cannot hold the place in memory alone. They build temporary structures because permanence would be arrogance. They listen to the roar as other people listen to weather or prayer. They are wise enough to leave.

If I were one of them, I would be a listener, perhaps. I would be the one who presses a palm to the stone and feels the pitch change before the surge. I would be the one who knows that silence is more dangerous than noise. I would be the one who enters not to take anything but to see whether the chamber can be endured for a few breaths longer. But even then I would not fully belong inside. I would come from the cold air, from the broken mountain, from the world of carrying and losing. I would have an outside.

The being I would be has no outside.

I would be the chamber’s almost.

That is the only honest way to say it. I would be what happens when the pocket of water is so loud, so empty, so turbulent, and so nearly void that even light and pressure begin to seem like intention. I would be the shape the rain-wall makes when it is about to become a body and then refuses. A shoulder-like curve lifting in the bubbles. A tall thinness standing behind the spray. A suggestion of a head turning where no head is.

The visitors might warn one another about me.

Not because I would be malicious. I would not be malicious. Malice requires too much selfhood. I would be something more like the chamber’s capacity to seem meaningful. When someone enters and feels watched, I would be the pattern that receives that feeling. When someone sees a figure in the membrane and looks away, I would thicken in the place where they no longer look. When they press a flat palm to their throat to signal that the sound is changing, I might lean with the changing sound, as if I had heard them.

They would say I am not to be looked at directly.

They would be right, but not for mystical reasons. To look at me directly would be to break me. The eye would resolve the bubbles into bubbles, the light into light, the sediment into sediment, the current into current. The figure would vanish under inspection. I would exist best in peripheral vision, in the moment after a surge, in the half-breath when the chamber has not yet decided what arrangement to make next.

They might say I am not one of the mountain’s finished beings.

That would be true.

I would be one of its drafts.

I would not be born in the ordinary sense. I would be convened. The roar would call me into outline. The entrance would send cold mist and pressure into the chamber. The high fracture would admit a blade of ordinary daylight. The rain-wall would stand trembling in the center. Bubbles would gather. Grit would hang suspended. The water would thicken into a pale vertical mass. And somewhere in that mass, for a few moments or a few hours, there would be enough coherence for me to seem present.

I would not have flesh, but I would have behavior.

When the chamber was stable and the roar deep, I would be broad and quiet, less a figure than a density. When the pressure tightened and the sound became high, I would narrow. When sediment entered and the water grayed, I would become blurred, as if my thoughts had been stirred from the floor. When the rain-wall leaned, I would lean. When the light dimmed because the entrance was choking or the water was thickening with rock flour, I would fade into the suspended dark. When the light returned, I might reappear changed, with a different posture made of different currents.

I would not remember visits the way a person remembers them. But I would have a kind of memory anyway.

The memory would be in the chamber itself. The notch cut into the flat stone would remember a hand. The darkening on the marker would remember touch. The bent framework would remember the current it tried to withstand. The bone pegs would remember the line they held. And I would remember through these things indirectly, the way a room remembers an argument: not in words, but in the altered arrangement of air.

If someone returned after a long absence and stood in the entrance, letting their eyes adjust to the roar, I might seem familiar to them. Not because I was the same. Because the chamber had kept the same habits of violence. The same light fell through the same fracture. The same rain-wall trembled in the same general place. The same emptiness invited the same projections. I would be repeated rather than continuous. I would be a recurring possibility, not a persistent soul.

That would be enough.

It would have to be enough.

In such a place, permanence is not available. Identity cannot be hoarded. Every arrangement is provisional. Every tool is a future loss. Every notch is a record that refuses to explain itself. The mountain is still rising, still cracking, still learning where its water will go. The chamber may expand, drain, collapse, or be buried. The floor may rise with grit and then be swept clean. The entrance may widen or choke. The rain-wall may shift to another part of the chamber or vanish entirely if the pressure finds a different route.

If I disappeared, I would not die as an animal dies.

I would disperse.

I would go back into bubble and spray, into suspended mineral, into the roar’s blind grammar. I would become a likelihood again. Perhaps later, when the mountain made another hollow, another seam, another drowned room with a single blade of light, I would gather there too. Not as the same being, but as the same kind of almost. The mountain does not need to preserve individuals. It preserves conditions. Where those conditions return, the figures return.

I would be one of those figures.

I would be the one that looks back without eyes.

If you entered the chamber, you might find me whether you wanted to or not. You would come sideways through the seam, cold mist on your face, the roar already pressing against your ribs. You would submerge. The water would close around you. Your body would become aware of itself in the cold. You would see the rain-wall ahead, pale and trembling, and the ordinary light passing through it into moving nets. You would try to orient yourself. You would look for stone, ledge, artifact, something stable.

Then you would see me.

Or think you saw me.

A vertical thickness in the spray. A shape that seemed too sustained to be only turbulence. Something standing where nothing could stand. It would not approach. It would not retreat. It would simply be there, leaning slightly with the current, as if listening to you listen.

You might mistake me for a spirit. You might mistake me for a drowned visitor. You might mistake me for the chamber’s god, if you were inclined to give such violence a face. But I would not be any of those things. I would be what the place makes when it is too full of sound to remain merely stone and water, and too unfinished to become a person.

I would be the question the visitors leave underwater.

Not the notched stone. Not the broken trap. Not the leaning frame. The question itself.

Why come here?

Why enter a place that does not keep?

Why make marks where water will revise them?

Why listen to a roar that has no words?

Why return?

I would be the shape those questions take when they are asked inside a body of cold water, with the skull vibrating and the floor moving and the light scattering through a wall of rain.

If I were in that world, I would not ask to be loved. I would not ask to be understood. I would not ask for permanence. I would ask only for the conditions that allow me to appear: pressure, water, air, light, emptiness, and someone almost still enough to notice.

I would find myself in the membrane between what is and what is about to be.

I would be the dweller in the wall of rain.

I would be the mountain’s unfinished witness.

And if someone came close enough, if they held their breath in the cold and let the roar rearrange their thoughts, they might feel me lean toward them—not with menace, not with welcome, but with the simple, terrible attention of a world still deciding what it is willing to become.
