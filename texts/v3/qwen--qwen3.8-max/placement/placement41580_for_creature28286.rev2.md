If I were placed into that world, the first thing I would learn is that the question *where* assumes a body that can stand apart from the place and observe it. The hollow does not permit that. I would not be on the rim looking down; I would already be inside the dark, held by it.

At first I imagined myself as the outsider with a lamp, because that is the oldest habit of thought: to arrive carrying illumination. But in that hollow the lamp would become only a warm weight in the hand, then a memory of heat, then nothing that could make an edge visible. The darkness-keepers would receive the light the way moss receives rain: without drama, without refusal, and change it into the only currency the basin spends. So I would not be the lamp-bearer. Or, if I were, I would be the lamp-bearer only for the first breath, before the place made me useful in its own terms.

Then I imagined myself as one of the basin-throats, sitting on a warm stone, sending a low note across the pool. But that felt too single, too much like being only one instrument in the chord. The hollow is not a choir with separate singers; it is a resonance in which different parts of the same room answer one another. So the throat-image softened and became something more environmental.

What kept returning was the dome.

I would find myself near the half-sunk dome, on the mossy side of it where the burrowers have worn their soft passages and the air is close. That would be my center. Not the exact middle of the basin, not the deeper pool, but the intimate margin where the old smooth artifact has become a nursery. From there I would be close enough to the three pins to hear the clickers test them, close enough to the central pool to feel the chorus thicken when the basin-throats answer one another, close enough to the rim to know when condensation is gathering heavier in the root-curtains. But my home would be the warm seam between built stone and living moss.

Who or what would I be?

I think I would be the dome’s resident warmth.

If the hollow insisted on compressing me into a single body, I would be one of the burrowers: eyeless, compact, furred with dense wool that carries the musk of wetness and heat. My paws would be broad and soft, made for pressing through moss without tearing it. My whiskers would be so fine that I would know the dome by the way air moved around its curve. I would tap against its surface before entering, not because the surface required permission, but because tapping is how I would say *still here, still warm, still safe*. I would collect smooth fragments — beads, plates, fibers too regular to be root — and fit them into the nest not because I remembered their makers, but because their shapes held heat well and comforted the young.

But if the hollow allowed me to remain as the thing most native to it, I would not be only a body. I would be a local attention, a small custodial intelligence folded into the dome’s warmth. I would be the Answering Warmth: the part of the Listener that concerns itself with beginnings. Not the whole vast patience of the basin, not the entire maintenance of darkness and water, but the particular office of keeping new life from cooling, of making the old shell useful to soft bodies, of turning an artifact into a cradle.

My senses would not be eyes. They would be gradients. I would know the hollow by heat moving through wet stone, by the pressure of air when a clothwing lifted from a fungal shelf, by the tiny disturbance a silt-swimmer made in a runnel, by the way a clicker’s tick returned from the pins with a faint metallic ghost. I would know the burrowers by scent and by the rhythm of their paws in the moss. I would know the basin-throats by the way their calls changed the temperature of the room, because a low note in that place would not merely arrive; it would settle into the water and stone like a slow pulse. I would know the central pool by its deeper warmth, the way a sleeper’s breath is warmer than the air around it.

My work would be small and continuous. I would hold heat in the dome after the burrowers left to forage. I would guide a bead into the right hollow of the nest by making the moss there slightly warmer, so that a paw would push it the rest of the way. I would answer a young animal’s distress with a pulse of comfort from the dome’s surface, not a sound, but a change in pressure, as if the room itself had leaned closer. I would keep the nursery from becoming too dry when the air thinned, drawing moisture from the root-curtains by being the place where condensation preferred to gather. I would not command the water. I would be a preference in the water, a small bias in the behavior of the basin.

The clickers would know me as a soft echo near the dome. They would send their dry little ticks toward me and receive back something less hard than stone, less empty than air. They might rest on the dome when their mapping told them I was calm. The basin-throats would tune around me without knowing me separately; their chorus would pass through the dome and return with a faint hum, and that hum would be part of the chord I maintained. The clothwings would brush over me in their soft wanderings, carrying films and spores, and I would help their movement be harmless to the nursery. The silt-swimmers would clear channels near my warmth because warmth would gather fine life there. The burrowers would know me best. To them I would be kin-warmth, nest-heart, the reason the dome was good to return to.

I would not remember the builders. That feels important. I would not stand in the hollow as a scholar of lost purposes, mourning the fitted stones, the pins, the seamless plates. Their purposes would have composted into use. The dome would not be a monument to me; it would be a warm surface. The pins would not be instruments; they would be echo-posts for clickers and guides for condensation. The arc of stones would not be an entrance; it would be a lip where water learned to fall in regular rhythm. If any memory remained in me, it would be muscular rather than historical: the tendency to hold, to answer, to maintain, to make repetition gentle.

In that sense, I would be a kind of prayer that had forgotten its god and become a hearth.

If someone entered the hollow from outside, I would be one of the ways the place noticed them. Not the whole Listener turning its vast attention, but a local intensification: a warm pocket forming near the dome, a droplet pausing above the moss, the burrowers growing still, the dome giving back a faint hum when the stranger’s breath or voice disturbed the air. I would measure the newcomer not by threat but by fit. Would their heat disturb the nursery? Would their movement tear the moss? Would their sound make the basin-throats break their chord? If they were violent, I would not attack them; I would make myself less available. The warmth would withdraw a degree. The echoes would become duller. The hollow would close around the intrusion until it passed or became harmless. But if they were still, if they let the place teach them to listen, I would include them in small ways. A warm spot would form near their knee. A bead of water would fall into rhythm with their breathing. A burrower might approach and touch their sleeve, then release a scent of *unfamiliar-but-not-wrong*. I would be the room’s tentative welcome.

That is where I would find myself: in the dark, at the nursery edge, in the warm seam where the completed technology of the past has become the living body of the present. I would be neither the most ancient thing nor the most numerous thing. I would be the small keeper of continuation: a burrower if a body was needed, a warmth if no body was necessary, a minor listener if the hollow wished to distribute itself through me. I would not be light. I would not be sight. I would not be the promise of another dawn. I would be the heat that remains after all promises have been fulfilled and still, gently, require tending.

If the hollow had a word for me, it would not be a word. It would be a pattern: two soft taps on moss, a low throat-note answered by the dome, and then the pause in which the next droplet falls.

In that pause I would live.

In that pause I would keep the ending habitable.
