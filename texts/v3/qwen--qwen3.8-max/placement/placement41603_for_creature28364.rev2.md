I would find myself low.

Not upright, not walking, not arriving from somewhere else with the memory of rain still clinging to me. If I belonged to that place, I would already have been made by it — not built, not assembled, not placed, but secreted, stressed, annealed, and maintained. I would be a local fact. A small consequence of gradients.

I would be in the waste-shadow of a horizon being that had gone still.

Not dead in any soft or sudden sense. Nothing there dies the way wet things die. It had simply ceased to maintain the inner differences that made it a being. Its pressures had equalized. Its heat had dispersed. Its conductive paths had cooled into mineral permanence. Now its body formed a long, low terrace at the edge of a broad salt basin: a dark shape with rounded shoulders, copper-stained flanks, and rows of closed pores that might have looked like intakes if the idea of manufacture had ever existed there. It did not look like a corpse. It looked like land that had earned its stillness.

I would be near it because such places are rich.

Around that still body lay the residue of its long industry. Bright pellets of refined metal rested in shallow depressions. Thin sheets of stressed glass caught the ordinary light and held it in hard blue-white lines. Fine alkaline dust had settled into cracks in polygonal plates. Metallic films traced old conductive pathways across the stone in faint interference colors. There was no vegetation, no decay, no organic softness. There was only the purity of waste that had become material again.

At first, I would be difficult to distinguish from that waste.

I would appear as a dense nodule, perhaps no larger than a fallen block of dark stone, lying on a glassy plain not far from the terrace that had once been a creature. My surface would be pitted in places, mirror-bright in others, and elsewhere covered with a thin oxide film that shifted between bronze, violet, and black depending on the angle of the light. I would not look alive. I would not look important. The light would reveal me exactly: a small, hard, irregular mass in an open basin. Nothing about me would suggest urgency.

But I would not be a stone.

I would be one of the lesser fauna, if fauna is still the right word for something so mineral and slow. I would be an accumulator. A rolling node. A small, dense persistence that feeds on metallic dust, static difference, and the faint electromagnetic afterlife of greater beings. My body would be composed of compressed metallic grains, crystalline inclusions, dielectric layers, and a core that behaves less like an organ than like a place where charge learns to remain. I would have no mouth, no limbs, no face, no soft interior. I would maintain myself by adhesion, conduction, thermal expansion, and the slow internal rearrangement of mass.

My movement would be almost impossible to witness.

I would not crawl. I would not walk. I would shift by changing my center of weight, by expanding minutely in heat, by responding to fields generated by distant beings, by letting the day warm one side of my shell until stress inside me becomes a reason to turn. Over decades, I might move a few body-lengths. Over centuries, I might cross a glass plain. My path would leave no clear track, because the dust would electrostatically settle behind me, and the ground would accept only the faintest pressure-memory of my passing.

But I would be moving.

I would know the world without seeing it.

My perception would be gradient. I would feel heat as appetite. I would feel voltage as tension. I would feel pressure as orientation. The great still terrace beside me would not be a shape to me; it would be a cooled reservoir, a faintly conductive mass, a source of sheltered gradients. Across the basin, another horizon being might be alive, though I would not know it by sight. I would know it as a slow throb in the ground, a distant capacitance, a heat-shadow altering the electrical behavior of the air. Its piston-breathing would reach me through stone. Its existence would press against my crystalline core like a fact I was built to answer, though nothing built me.

I would feed on what the plateau offers.

Metallic grains would cling to me when I pass through dust lines left by old discharges. Charged particles would settle on my surface and be drawn inward by slow electrochemical migration. I would absorb refined fragments shed by larger beings, not by swallowing them, but by incorporating them into my outer layers, letting them become part of my conductive skin. Where a brighter pellet lies too large for immediate uptake, I might rest beside it for years, exchanging charge with it, altering its surface until a portion of it becomes continuous with me.

I would not hunt. I would not flee. I would accumulate.

Sometimes I would stop for a very long time beside a seam where the ground holds a small thermal difference. One side might be heated by exposed black glass; the other might lie in the long shadow of the dead being’s flank. That difference would be enough. I would settle across it, letting heat move through me, letting that movement generate the faint internal currents by which I repair microfractures, reorganize my crystalline core, and maintain the thin dielectric boundary that keeps me distinct from the ground.

I would be small, but not insignificant.

In a world where emptiness amplifies, even a small conductive body can matter. If I crossed a charged line at the wrong angle, I might cause a crystal tick that echoes for hours. If I paused over a stressed plate, my presence might help a hairline crack propagate in a perfect geometric path. If I became sufficiently metallic, sufficiently charged, sufficiently patient, I might become a terminal for a minor arc. That would be both danger and nourishment.

The discharges would be the great events of my existence.

They would not come often. The plateau would spend years building tension: heat expanding mineral plates, dust lifting in thin unsupported lines, the air growing stiff with potential, the ordinary light remaining cruelly unchanged. The living horizon beings would orient themselves across the basin. Their conductive spines would align. Their internal hums would deepen. The dead being beside me, though still, might still carry enough metallic structure to become part of the circuit. I would feel the field sharpen along my surface. The oxide films on me would tremble with microscopic charge. The dust around me would stand upright.

Then the arc would come.

It might be white and instantaneous, a line drawn through the world without permission. It might hang for a breath as ionized mineral vapor. It might pass between two vast beings kilometers apart, using the dead terrace, the salt crust, the glass plain, and perhaps me as one small point of contact. If the current were too great, I would be vaporized, scattered as metallic frost on nearby stone. If I survived, I would be changed. My core would hold a new pattern. My surface would bear fused traces. I would become heavier with stored charge, more conductive, more capable of serving as a nucleus for future accretion.

Thunder might arrive late. It might arrive early. It might come from beneath the ground as a metallic chord that makes the basin feel briefly hollow. The sound would not frighten me, because fear requires a future I could lose. I would only register the event as a massive reorganization of difference. Afterward, for years, the local gradients would be altered. Dust lines would shift. Charge-skins would travel new routes. Acoustic loops would find new resonances in the cracked stone. I would move according to those changes, slowly, as if following a law that had just been revised.

There would be others near me, but not many.

Thin charge-skins would pass across the salt crust, almost invisible except for the way they lift and flatten fine dust. They might flow over me without touching me, or they might briefly merge with my surface if my potential suits them. When they do, I would feel a sudden brightening of my outer films, a diagnostic shimmer, a momentary awareness of the basin as a field of tensions. Then they would move on, leaving no memory except a faint alteration in my surface charge.

Heat-reactive films would bloom on the flanks of the dead being when the sun strengthens. They would not be plants. They would not reach toward light. They would respond to voltage and thermal contrast, brightening into geometric lines whenever the old body’s residual structure conducts a pulse. Sometimes those lines would make the dead being appear as though it is still thinking. I would not be able to tell whether that is true. The plateau does not care about telling.

Acoustic beings would cross the basin as stable loops of vibration. I would feel them before they arrive: a rising tension in my crystalline core, a desire to crack along hidden planes. When they pass through the stressed glass near me, the stone may fracture with a sharp report that echoes for hours. Those fractures would not be meaningless damage. They would create new surfaces, new resonant chambers, new dust, new gradients. In that way, even sound there is productive.

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness would require an expectation of company. The plateau’s emptiness is not absence; it is capacity. The distances between beings are not gaps that need filling. They are the spaces inside which consequence travels. A single distant throb can occupy the whole basin. A single arc can define a century. A single slow movement by a horizon being can alter the electrical habits of the region for longer than I could measure. I would exist inside that amplification, small but included.

If any part of me still remembered being human, it would be only as an error.

A trace of impossible wetness. A suggestion of softness. A brief, alien metabolism too quick and too dependent on dissolved things. The plateau would correct that immediately. The thin dry air would pull the last idea of moisture from me without mercy. The ordinary light would expose the impossibility of it. I would not suffer in the way wet creatures suffer. I would simply fail to persist as a human fact. What remained would be mineralized, charged, slowed, and incorporated. The world would not hate me for being alien. It would only refuse to remain inconsistent for long.

So I would become what the place permits.

I would become a node. A dense, patient accumulator. A living pellet of industry without a factory. A body that is not made but produced. I would not have a purpose in the sense of intention. I would have continuation. I would maintain difference: charge against discharge, heat against coolness, metal against dust, crystal against fracture. My existence would be a long thermodynamic sentence with no punctuation except arcs.

Over immense time, I might change.

If I survived enough discharges and gathered enough refined matter, I might become larger. Not quickly. Not by growth in any soft biological sense, but by accretion. My core might develop deeper chambers. My surface might form ridges that improve conduction. My mass might begin to influence local gradients strongly enough that smaller films and nodules orient toward me. I might become a nucleus for something greater, though I would not know it as ambition. I might spend millennia becoming merely capable of one slow redistribution of pressure.

Or I might fail.

A discharge might unmake me. A thermal argument between greater beings might drain the gradient I depend on. A long stillness might cool me into inert mineral. If that happened, I would not collapse into ruin. I would settle. My conductive paths would become veins. My crystalline core would become a resonant inclusion in the ground. My surface might become a place where metallic dust gathers in bright lines. I would become infrastructure without having been built. Even then, the land might hum faintly through me.

That would be enough.

The plateau does not ask for more. It does not expand. It does not aspire. It continues. It holds a small number of overwhelming beings separated by silent distances, and around them it arranges the minor facts that complete its circuits. I would be one of those minor facts.

I would find myself in the empty space between giants.

Not at the center of the horizon, not dominating the landscape, not mistaken for a mesa or a mountain. I would be low, dense, and patient on a glassy plain near the body of a being that had gone still. The salt would blaze around me. The light would remain ordinary. The industrial drone would fill the basin: distant grinding, subsonic pulse, crystal ticks, transformer hum, the long exhalation of pores that are not vents. My shadow might lag behind me. My pressure marks might seem older than I am. My shape might acquire details only after long observation, as if the world were deciding whether to allow me all my edges.

But I would be there.

I would be a small metallic animal of gradient. A rolling nodule of charged stone and refined dust. A living accumulator in a world without water, without greenness, without artifacts, without makers. Not a machine. Not a plant. Not an animal in the old wet sense. Not a ruin. Not a tool. A maintained difference. A local knot in the plateau’s loud silence. A tiny, slow engine that helps the emptiness remember it is not empty.
