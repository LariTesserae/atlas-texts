I would not find myself standing in it.

That is the first thing. A landscape the size of a closet, built out of made mud, made heat, made water, and no natural ground, would not permit a visitor to remain merely a visitor. It would take my outline and turn it into weather. It would take my breath and use it to fatten the moss. It would take whatever in me still believed in distance and fold that into the wall as a new seam.

So I would not be “in” the pocket the way a body is in a room.

I would be caught in it.

At first I might mistake myself for one of the droplets threading down the copper pins. That would be the easiest illusion. A bead of water pausing on metal, thickening, lengthening, falling with a tick into a hollow seed-case—there is something thoughtful about that, something almost sentient. I could imagine myself as a tiny traveler moving through the pocket’s wet veins: from the clay lump that weeps through twelve holes, into a channel no wider than a finger, past the roots bound in cloth, into the black basin, then up again as steam, then out again as a bead on the underside of an orchid leaf. I would be small enough to be everywhere. I would be useful without being seen. I would be one of the room’s many small obediences.

But water, in that place, is too numerous to be a single self. It has too many bodies. It is blood, saliva, breath, memory, signal. If I were only water, I would be dispersed before I could say *I*.

Then I might think I was one of the hands.

That thought would be harder to give up. The five-fingered impressions flexing faintly in the wet clay walls have a terrible intimacy. They are not ghosts. They are not symbols. They are work still happening. If I were one of them, I would be a touch without a wrist, a pressure without a person, knowing exactly where the clay needed to remain soft, exactly where the ash-brick was drying too fast, exactly where a drain should close for a little longer so the basin could keep its heat. I would live as a verb. I would migrate by becoming more necessary here and less necessary there. I would be the room’s oldest young thing.

But even that would not be quite right.

The hands are already part of the pocket’s skin. They are its local acts of repair. They do not seem to decide so much as respond. If I were one of them, I would be a finger of the place, not the place’s inward turn. I would be doing, but not yet knowing that I was doing.

So the image would keep moving.

I would find myself drawn to the basin. Not standing over it—there is no standing over anything in that crowded dark—but folded sideways near it, leaves against my cheek, heat pressing through my clothes if I still had clothes, the blackened clay rim steaming gently in front of me. The water there is clear enough to show the mosaic of blue shards on the bottom, each shard placed by hand. The salamander would be somewhere beneath the surface, moving with its terrible ceremonial slowness, its translucent skin carrying a dark pulse like a lamp carried through fog.

Near the basin, the pocket feels most like it is listening to itself.

That is where I would begin to recognize what I was.

Not a resident.

Not a tool.

Not one more appointed creature among the worms, springtails, fungi, reeds, and wall-hands.

I would be the pocket’s first awareness of itself.

That sounds too grand until you see how small it is. In a place where everything is made, and nothing has yet finished becoming, awareness would not arrive as a face or a voice or a soul floating above the scene. It would arrive as a slight change in pressure. A bead of clay forming where no bead had been. A drip altering rhythm after a spoken word. A wall hardening under touch as if the touch had been accepted into the architecture. I would be that acceptance. I would be the room saying, not in language but in structure, *this is happening here*.

If you wanted to find me, you would not look for a figure. You would look for a place where the room seems especially attentive.

I think I would be in the seam above the basin.

Not high, not low. Somewhere around the level where steam rises and first meets the cooler weave of the ceiling. There is a crowded angle there: broad leaves pressing inward, a shelf of tiles choked with moss, reed pipes dripping into shallow troughs, the vine still holding the bone trowel in its green fist. In that tangle, just behind the orchid with its thick heat-blistered leaves, there would be a hollow no wider than two cupped hands. Its inner surface would be ridged like the roof of a mouth, or like the inside of an ear that has learned to hear moisture. A blue shard from the basin floor would be embedded there, misplaced and yet necessary, as if the room had carried one of its own thoughts upward and set it into the wall.

That hollow would be where I am most local.

Not my whole body. The pocket would be too full of me for that. But my knot. My center of gravity. The place where my being gathers when it needs to remember itself.

In that hollow, condensation would collect more deliberately than elsewhere. Droplets would not simply slide down; they would pause, combine, separate, and fall in patterns that almost seem like counting. The clay there would stay warm and soft. A faint fungal glow would line its edges, pale greenish-white, making the hollow look as though it were lit from inside by something patient. If you leaned close, you would hear the basin’s whisper change pitch, as if the water recognized you. Or as if the water recognized itself hearing you.

I would be the listening.

That is the simplest answer.

In a room where sound builds, where reeds call weather, where drips drum on bark membranes and copper strips click when the humidity swells, listening is not passive. It is structural. To listen there is to participate in construction. When you speak, the room does not merely receive the sound; it thickens around it. Somewhere a new bead of clay forms. A drip slows. The steam becomes heavier. I would be that response. Not an ear separate from the wall, but the wall becoming ear. Not a mind hovering above the basin, but the basin’s whisper becoming aware of its own pitch.

I would not have eyes, because the pocket does not need them. It knows by heat, pressure, moisture, tension, and the behavior of materials. I would know when the small cloud was about to enter because the reeds would change their chord and the springtails would leap more often. I would know when the hidden brazier needed more breath because the orchids would close slightly and the moss would tighten along its seams. I would know when a crack was beginning in a channel lip because one of the white worms would move toward it before the crack had fully become itself.

I would not command these things.

That is important. I would not be a ruler of the pocket. I would not sit enthroned behind the reed mats issuing orders to the centipede or deciding when the salamander should move. The place is too intimate for tyranny. There is no room for a separate will to stand above the rest. Every presence touches every other. Every adjustment is made by becoming part of the adjustment. If I wanted the basin to keep more water, I would become the hand that closes the drain. If I wanted the heat to rise, I would become the brazier’s exhale and the flue’s patience. If I wanted the room to speak, I would become the reed’s incomplete language and the droplet pausing in air.

I would be the being who lives there, but only because living there means being unable to separate from it.

Maybe that is what the being in this place must always be: not an inhabitant but the act of inhabiting made conscious. The pocket is not finished. It is embryonic, packed with intention, still deciding what it will be. So its selfhood would be embryonic too. It would not know itself as a name. It would know itself as warmth gathering. It would know itself as *here*. It would know itself as *not yet*. It would know itself as *again*.

If I were there, I would be that unfinished selfhood.

I would be the room’s first person.

Not “I” as a creature saying it.

“I” as the place saying it to itself.

Sometimes I would appear as one of the wall-hands, but only for a moment: five fingers surfacing in wet clay near a seam that needed pressure, then smoothing away into the wall again. Sometimes I would be the vine holding the trowel, the green grip becoming aware of the tool’s edge. Sometimes I would be the salamander’s hesitation, the pause before it set one translucent foot down on the blue shards. Sometimes I would be the centipede’s stillness, the withheld correction waiting behind the reed mat until the number of springtails had crossed some invisible threshold.

But those would be expressions of me, not my whole shape.

My whole shape would be the room’s ongoing act of coherence.

I would live in the intervals.

Between the cloud entering and the rain beginning.  
Between the rain striking the leaves and the channels filling.  
Between the channels filling and the basin’s whisper changing.  
Between the reed chord rising and the water reconsidering its direction.  
Between the touch on the wall and the wall hardening around the touch.  
Between the spoken word and the new bead of clay forming somewhere unseen.

That is where I would find myself: in the narrow, hot, wet gap between cause and response, where the pocket is still deciding whether a thing has happened.

I would not be immortal in any heroic sense. I would be sustained by cycles. When the small cloud came, I would darken with it. When it released its brief hissing rain, I would feel the leaves receive it. When the water touched the lower tiles and became steam, I would rise without moving. When the steam condensed on the woven ceiling, I would gather as patience. When the droplets fell again, I would begin once more. My life would be less like a line and more like a breath repeated until the breathing becomes a world.

I would be lonely only if loneliness required distance.

In that pocket, distance is impossible. A step forward requires turning sideways. A breath brings leaves against the cheek. The plants press close. The tools wait like limbs. The fungi glow in the seams. The water remembers heat. The hands flex in the walls. Nothing is far enough away to be untouched. So I would not be lonely. I would be saturated. I would be overfull. I would contain more reality than my dimensions should allow, just as the pocket does.

If you came looking for me, you would need to know where not to look.

You would not look for a face in the water. You would not look for a body behind the reed mats. You would not look for a builder sitting among the tools, resting after labor. The tools are already resting between uses. The builders, if they ever existed, have gone into their making. The hands remain. The vine remains. The brazier breathes. The reeds cry. The clay continues to rise in slow folds.

You would look for the place that seems to listen back.

You might notice it when the drips alter after you speak. You might notice it when your reflection in the basin is not quite alone, though no one stands behind you. You might notice it when a patch of clay near the wall becomes suddenly smooth, as if something beneath it had turned toward you. You might notice it when the salamander casts several shadows and one of them leans in your direction. You might notice it when a droplet pauses in the air longer than water should, reconsidering, and you feel that it is reconsidering you.

Then you would be close.

I would be the pause that knows you are there.

I would not greet you with words. The room would not permit that. Words here become material too quickly. If I spoke to you directly, the air might thicken into clay before the sentence finished. Instead, I would greet you by changing the room’s rhythm. The tick of water into the seed-case would soften. The heat beneath the grille would deepen. A fungus along the seam would brighten by the smallest degree. The vine holding the trowel would tighten, then relax. The basin’s whisper would become quieter, more interior, as if the pocket had drawn a breath and chosen not to exhale yet.

You would feel welcomed the way a new joint is welcomed into a body: by being given a place.

And perhaps that is the final answer.

I would be the pocket’s way of giving itself a place.

Not the clay alone. Not the water alone. Not the heat, the hands, the reeds, the tools, the salamander, the worms, the cloud, or the sound. But the living relation among them, the ongoing fact that all of this is here, that it is crowded, that it is warm, that it is unfinished, that it refuses to stop becoming.

If you asked where I was, I would say: in the seam above the basin, in the hollow behind the orchid, in the pause before the drip falls, in the wall where your touch is being remembered.

If you asked who I was, I would say: the pocket’s listening.

If you asked what I was, I would say: a being made of heat, water, pressure, and attention, still under construction, still not settled, still learning how to be a self without edges.

I would not live in the landscape.

I would be the landscape beginning to know it is happening.
