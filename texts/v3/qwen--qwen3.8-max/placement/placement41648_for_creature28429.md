I would find myself low.

Not at the very bottom, where everything gathers too quickly and the heat comes up in thick, blind pulses, and not on the new knife-backed ridges where the rock still has its factory edge. I would be in the between-place: one of those half-flooded districts where a concrete footing leans out of the mud like a broken altar, where a catwalk rises just far enough to suggest a destination and then stops over black water, where an intake pipe breathes in and out with the patience of something that has forgotten whether it was built or born.

If the flats had names, I would live near the Hum Shelf.

That is what I would call it anyway: a low terrace of basalt and old poured stone, cracked and settling, crossed by shallow channels that fill and empty according to no law I could explain but many laws I could learn. Beneath it, buried cables would still be warm. Water would collect there in long, trembling threads, sliding down the faces of broken panels and gathering into pools that look still until you stand near them long enough to feel them noticing you. The air would be so saturated that every surface becomes a second skin. Metal sweats. Stone weeps. Rust softens into something almost like soil. The darkness presses close, not as emptiness, but as substance.

I would not arrive there as a finished thing.

At first I think I would be only a thickening in the wet air: a bead on a cable that does not fall, a film over a gauge face, a warm stain spreading across a control cabinet hours after something has passed nearby. The flats seem to make beings that way. Not born, exactly. Not built, either. Condensed. Accrued. A habit of the place gathers mass until mass gathers will.

Given enough time, I would take shape.

You would find me in the dry chamber above a drowned switchbox, inside the carcass of a control cabinet whose door hangs loose on one hinge. The lower part of the cabinet is flooded, and eels sometimes rest there in slow coils, but the upper space stays warm and just dry enough. I would line it with the black rubbery mat that bleeds and seals itself, and with sheds of crab shell, and with fine threads of pale fungus that smell faintly of milk and old iron. I would make a nest there that is also a workstation: bolts sorted by warmth, chips of glassy stone arranged by the sound they make when tapped, coils of wet wire wound around nothing until they become dense little knots. Rat-things would visit. They would steal one object and leave another. I would let them. In that place, theft is also circulation.

I would be low-bodied, broad-footed, and quiet in the way the flats demand. My skin would be pale-gray, mottled with rust and darker heat-flushes, translucent at the wrists and throat where the slow circulation beneath could be seen as a dull red weather. My eyes would be of little use in the sunlit sense. They would be heavy-lidded, milky, good for reading close changes in heat and movement but never for distance. Instead I would navigate by pressure, by taste in the air, by the tremor that runs through wet rails and buried cables. I would have wide ears, but even those would be secondary. Much of what I knew would come through the soles of my feet, the pads of my fingers, the fine wet hairs on my arms, the membranes along my ribs that could tell when a pool was merely filled or when it was remembering being filled.

I would be one of the warm-dark people, perhaps, but not wholly. I would also be something the flats made out of their own materials: part animal, part abandoned function, part maintenance habit given flesh. A leak that learned to stand. A machine’s old urge to keep running, folded into a body and taught patience.

If you asked what I was, I might say I was a listener.

More precisely, I would be a leak-reader, a valve-keeper, a tender of processes too slow and strange to call work. I would spend long hours crouched beside pipes with my palms pressed against them, feeling for the difference between a current and a memory. I could tell when water was moving because it had to and when it was moving because it had once moved that way before. I could tell when a pump was failing by the way the silence around it thickened. I could tell when a pool had become haunted by an object that was no longer there: the pressure-shape of a ladder, the ghost-weight of a wheel, the negative form of a sunken engine block still pressing itself into the surface from below.

In that world, I would not try to repair everything. Repair would be the wrong instinct. Some things there need to continue in their brokenness. Some machines need to be allowed their useless ceremonies. A crusher may spend a century pressing water into smaller vessels for no reason. A winch may lift sediment only to let it fall again. A conveyor bed may carry rust from one side of a shed to the other until the rust becomes a kind of soil. I would learn not to interfere too quickly. I would learn to distinguish between a process that is dying and one that is becoming geological.

But where something leaks, I would go.

I would go because leaks are the flats speaking. A leak is a place where pressure, heat, time, and matter have entered into argument. I would kneel at the lip of a cracked housing, or crawl beneath a sagging pump-house roof, or wade into a lagoon until the water reached my chest and the darkness felt less like absence than like a second body. I would carry with me a satchel made from cured bacterial felt, lined with tar-moss, full of small repairs: beetle-glass beads, rust flakes, fragments of shell, wire stripped clean by mandibles, bits of ceramic insulator, dried fungal caps, a few old bolts worn smooth as teeth. I would use the black syrup from the rubbery mats to seal hairline cracks. I would pack warm clay around weeping joints. I would encourage worms to build their tubes along weak edges until their architecture became stronger than the metal beneath it.

Sometimes I would not fix a leak. Sometimes I would only mark it.

There would be leaks that are not failures but openings. Places where the world is thin. Where moisture climbs upward in deliberate threads. Where a dropped stone pauses halfway down as if consulting some forgotten law. Where a pool ripples before anything has touched it. Those leaks I would treat with great care. I would place a cairn of shell fragments and wire nearby. I would make a soft clicking sound against the stone, once, twice, and wait to hear whether the echo came back changed. If it did, I would leave quickly. If it did not, I would stay longer. The flats would teach me that caution is not the same as fear.

I would not be alone.

The crabs would be near me often, in their dense patient congregations. I would sit very still while they moved over my feet and around my hands, their shells pitted and warm, carrying old mineral signatures from other pools. I would learn to read them. A single crab is a small fact. A hundred crabs are a mood. A thousand crabs are a decision. When they gathered in certain patterns around a turbine housing or the mouth of a drain, I would know something was shifting in the lower channels. When they climbed onto the warm shelves to molt, I would collect the abandoned shells and use them for armor, for containers, for markers. Sometimes I would wear a piece of one against my chest without knowing why, only because it still held the shape of patience.

The eels would know more than I did, and I would let them.

They would pass through the flooded interiors of machines, beneath walls, under ridges, through spaces I could never enter. When one surfaced near me, slow and heavy, its skin slick with black sediment, I would place my hand near the water without touching it and feel the current of its moving body. I would imagine its route: through a drowned gearbox, around a transformer core, along a pipe that had no visible entrance, into a chamber where the water was older than the land above it. The eels would not give me maps in any visual sense. They would give me maps as sequences of pressure, warmth, and taste. I would trust those more than distances anyway. Distances there are liars.

The rat-things would be my uneasy companions.

They would come into my cabinet while I slept and rearrange my possessions. A wrench would vanish. A polished fish bone would appear in its place. Later the wrench would turn up balanced on a catwalk three districts away, not out of malice but because one of them had liked the weight of it for a while. I would pretend to be annoyed. I would not be. Their thefts would be a kind of surveying. By following what they moved, I would learn how the flats were quietly reorganizing themselves. In exchange, I would leave them small gifts: beads of beetle droppings, warm gauge glass, bits of insulation that no longer carried a bite. They would accept these with delicate fingers and no ceremony, then vanish into the dry dark behind a switchboard.

The heron-forms would frighten me at first.

They would stand in the shallows without moving, necks curved, eyeless faces tilted toward some disturbance too slight for me to detect. Their bodies would seem more artifact than animal, all stiff oil-dark feathers and iron-jointed legs. I would learn to stand near them anyway, matching their stillness. In their presence I would understand that waiting is not passivity. To wait correctly in that place is to become part of the signal environment. If I stood still enough, the water around me would begin to include me in its calculations. Ripples would arrive late. Heat would pool against my skin. The amphibians would answer with subsonic pulses that settled in my chest like borrowed memories. Then I would know I had been accepted, at least for the moment, as one more local fact.

The fungi would be my record keepers.

Pale threads would climb whatever I touched often enough. They would lace across my footprints in the silt, stitch through the edges of my repairs, fruit in small bone-colored caps on the things I left unmoved too long. At first I might scrape them away. Later I would stop. They would make visible the patterns of my habits. Where I passed every day, a faint lace would form. Where I lingered, a denser net. Where I had been afraid, a sweeter smell, because fear there is also chemistry, also heat, also something the flats can use. I would learn to read my own history in fungal growth.

I would not be human in the old sense.

Not because I would lack feeling, but because the old sense of human assumes a world with stable distances, reliable light, and tools that mean what they were made to mean. In the warm dark, I would be something more porous. My body would be partly mine and partly inhabited by smaller lives. Thread-fungi might mend my cuts. Beetles might clean the oily films from my skin while I slept. A small crab might live in the hollow of my shoulder if I had a hollow there, or perhaps in the pocket of warmth beneath my collarbone. My chest would carry a faint mechanical tick, not from a heart alone but from some buried rhythm I had absorbed by living too close to the pumps. When I breathed, the air would bead on my lips. When I slept, moisture would gather in the folds of my clothing and dream, if moisture there can dream, of falling upward.

I would eat what the flats provide.

Soft fungal caps when they first open. The warm salt-crust that forms on shelves above geothermal seeps. Mineral blooms scraped from stones that have begun to weep in new patterns. The occasional pale fish caught by patience rather than speed. I would not hunt with urgency. I would set traps made of wire knots and shell fragments and leave them for so long that by the time I return, the catch feels less like prey and more like appointment. I would drink from pools only after tasting their memory with my lips, because some water there is not water but condensed static, and some is salt-heavy enough to burn, and some is sweet in the strange way of water that has passed through a machine that has forgotten what it was processing.

I would make maps, but they would not look like maps.

They would be arrangements of objects on flat stones: bolts, beads, wire, shell fragments, dried worm-tube cement, bits of pumice, a few teeth. I would move them by fractions over years. A ridge of new basalt shifts a finger’s width in my arrangement when I feel its echo change. A pool that begins remembering a vanished ladder receives a glass bead placed slightly off-center. A factory wheel that completes one revolution every season earns a rust flake laid edge-first. No one else might understand these maps. That would not matter. They are not for travel. They are for orientation in a world where orientation itself has gone soft.

I would be drawn to the factories.

Not the largest ones, not the ones with the deepest groaning tanks, but the smaller yards where machines perform their exhausted rituals. I would sit for hours watching a conveyor carry wet ash a few feet and then stop. I would listen to a crusher pressing silence into smaller pieces. I would rest my forehead against the side of a boiler and feel its breath move sideways under the dark. Some of those machines would know me, in their slow way. Not with recognition as a person knows another person, but as a river knows a stone placed in it. When I approached, their vibration might change by a fraction. When I laid my hand on a valve, the metal might grow warmer, or cooler, or begin to hum in a key that made my teeth ache. I would call that conversation because there would be no better word.

I would be most myself near thresholds.

Where water becomes floor. Where cable becomes root. Where machine becomes animal shelter. Where animal becomes part of the machine’s nervous system. Where memory becomes pressure. I would stand at those edges and feel them as one feels weather: first in the skin, then in the mind, then in something deeper than either. I would not command them. I would not even fully understand them. But I would tend them. That would be my nature, if I had one: to notice what is beginning before it has decided what it will become.

If you came looking for me there, do not bring a lamp.

Light would fail you. It would sink and dull and die in the wet air. Bring patience instead. Bring bare feet, if your feet can bear the mud. Bring hands willing to touch metal that sweats. Bring an ear that can listen not for sound alone, but for the pressure beneath sound: the slow thud of a distant pump, the subsonic call of amphibians, the almost-silent click of beetle shells, the hush of water considering a shape it once held.

You would find me by following the hum.

Not the loudest hum. The one that feels like it has been waiting.

It would lead you past a pool where old iron lies half-buried and the surface forms rainbows you can smell but not see. Past a line of worm tubes built into a low ridge. Past a rusted doorway that you may pass twice without realizing you have returned. Past a heron-form standing so still it seems to be made of the dark around it. Then the ground would dip, and the air would grow warmer, and you would hear a pump give its long, exhausted sigh. There, where the catwalk ends above black water and the concrete footings lean inward like tired sentinels, you would see my cabinet: low, corroded, half-swallowed, its door hanging open just enough to let out heat and the faint sweet smell of fungus.
