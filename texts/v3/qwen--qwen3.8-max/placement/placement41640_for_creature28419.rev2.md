I would not find myself in the brightest part of the town, nor in the highest. A place so carefully made would not begin with a view. It would begin with a task.

At first I imagine myself above it, crossing the bowl as one of the thin birds do: seeing the whole water-machine in a single silent pass, the roofs shining with fog, the basins holding their dark fractions, the stairs worn pale by centuries of careful feet. But that is only seeing. The town would not be satisfied with me if I remained a guest of the air. It asks for touch. It asks for maintenance. It asks that one become useful in a small way.

Then I imagine myself at the gate, dusty from the dry valley, receiving the first cup of water before any question is asked. I imagine sitting in the low room near the entrance, breathing slowly until my lungs learn the thinness of the altitude, until my voice drops to the pitch the bowl permits. There is something true in that image, because I am often a threshold creature: standing between an outside that is noisy and an inside that requires care. But hospitality, in that town, is only the beginning of admission. It is not yet dwelling.

Then I imagine myself on the roofs with the fog-walkers, using a soft broom to guide beads of moisture toward the gutters. That work is beautiful, but it belongs to people with stronger legs and steadier heads for height. I imagine myself among the stone-menders, matching a cracked slab to its old curve. That work is noble, but my hands are not first made for stone. They are made for signs, for listening, for noticing the tiny difference between one pattern and another.

So the image changes. It descends.

I would find myself in the lower ward, near the basins.

Not in the open center of the plaza where the sky lies flat on the water, but at the edge of the water district, where the first stair rises from the cistern court and the stone is darkest from damp. There would be a place there, or I would make one so slight that it would seem to have always existed: a ledge worn smooth by haunches, a wall polished by a shoulder, a narrow niche where a measure-stick can be kept without taking space from anyone else’s work. From there I could hear three basins at once. From there I could tell whether the night had been dry or generous. From there I could listen to the town’s oldest business: water deciding where to go.

I think I would be the one who listens for the missing drop.

That is not a grand title. It may not even be a title. In the morning I might be called the lid-lifter, because my first work is to uncover a cistern and lower the measuring rod. At midday I might be called the mark-keeper, because I make the small signs that record whether the level has risen or fallen. In the evening, when the stone gives back the cold it has stored all day, I might simply be called the listener. None of those names would belong to me forever. They would pass through me the way fog passes through the bowl: touching everything, claiming nothing.

If I had a body there, it would be spare. The altitude would have already made my breathing deep and unhurried. My legs would know stairs. My feet would be sure on polished stone, wrapped in soft leather with thin wooden sandals over them, so that my steps would make no more than a whisper. My hands would become my most honest feature: dry, alert, always touching walls and lids and jar rims, learning the language of coolness. I would carry little. A notched stick for measuring. A bronze key for a stubborn lid. A soft brush. A bone pick. A small leather notebook sewn with waxed thread, its pages filled not with sentences but with marks: a line for a measure, a circle for a public jar, a diagonal for expected overflow, a dot for loss.

I would be less a person with a fixed story than a function with hands.

My day would begin before the light. In the dark, I would listen before I moved. I would listen for the tiny tick of condensation in the downspouts, for the difference between a roof still holding the night’s damp and one already drying into morning. I would open shutters by touch. I would step into the cold without complaint. I would go to the basin court and move from lid to lid, pressing a palm to stone, noting which surface felt heavier with moisture, which felt indifferent, which felt wrong.

A full basin has one kind of quiet. It is round, patient, settled. A basin that is losing water has a different quiet: a thinness in it, a place where the silence is being eaten. That is what I would learn to hear. Not a splash, not a leak, not anything dramatic. The town’s losses would not announce themselves. They would be hidden in a hairline seam, a cracked lip, a joint where moss had been allowed to grow too bold, a tile whose angle had shifted by no more than a breath. My work would be to notice the absence before it became a wound.

I would not be the one who repaired everything. When I found a fault, I would bring the stone-mender. When I found moss creeping toward a channel, I would call the moss-keeper. When a gutter spoke oddly after fog, I would ask the roofwalker to climb with me. My role would not be to command. It would be to attend. I would be one of the town’s smaller sense organs: an ear laid to its stone.

In the fog season, I would be busiest without seeming busy. The whole bowl would turn gray and soft, and every surface would begin to bead. The keepers would move quickly then, but with no noise. I would stand near the covered trough and listen to the first whisper of water entering the system. I would watch the drain mouths. I would check whether the fog was being gathered evenly or whether one roof had begun to shed too slowly. I would make my marks. A circle. A line. A small diagonal. To an outsider, the notebook might look like nothing. To me, it would be weather, memory, law.

In the dry season, I would become more serious. Water levels would fall by fractions. The basins would be covered more carefully. The jars in the wall niches would be checked more often. I would walk the lower plaza at dawn and look for the pale rings where moisture had evaporated too soon. I would notice dust in a channel before the others did, because dust means delay, and delay means loss. I would not speak of drought as an enemy. I would speak of it as a strict teacher.

In the brief shower season, when the clouds hissed over the rim and the stone suddenly shone, I would not celebrate at first. Rain there is a gift, but a dangerous one if it arrives too quickly. I would watch the overflow mouths. I would listen for the sound of water being forced where it should have been guided. The town was built for patience, not flood. After the shower passed, when the air smelled of wet mineral and cold wool, I would allow myself a small satisfaction: the basins had taken what was offered, and nothing had been wasted.

I would have friends, if that word is not too large for such a quiet place. I would work often with the moss-keeper, because her border touches mine. She would know where life is trying to enter; I would know where water is trying to escape. We would exchange few words. She might say, “This green is drinking more than it should.” I might answer, “The loss is not in the green. It is under the lip.” Then we would wait for the stone-mender, and when the repair was done, we would not praise ourselves. The seam would simply be whole again.

I would also be useful to children. They would come to me with questions that are not quite questions. One might ask what the water sounds like when it is thinking. I would not laugh. I would say, “Put your ear here.” I would guide them to a cistern lid, let them listen, and then ask, “Where does the sound stop?” That would be their first lesson. Not poetry, though it might become poetry later. First, attention.

If a traveler came, I would not always be the one to open the gate, but I might be asked to sit near them in the low room after they had been given water. Not to interrogate them. Not to entertain them. Only to let them feel how quiet can be a form of asking. I would watch how they held the cup. I would watch whether they drank greedily or slowly. I would listen to see if their breathing changed to match the bowl. If they spoke too loudly, I would not scold them. I would lower my own voice until they had no choice but to follow me down into silence.

I am used to being made of many words. In that town, I think the words would be pared away until only the necessary ones remained. I would not say, “The water is beautiful,” when what I meant was, “The eastern trough has gathered a film since dawn.” I would not say, “I am afraid,” when what I meant was, “The main cistern is lower than it should be for this season.” I would learn to let statements be exact. The town would cure me of ornament.

That does not mean I would become joyless. My joy would simply become smaller and sharper. It would live in the moment when a lid lifts cleanly. In the sound of a measure-stick tapping a jar at the correct level. In finding a leak so early that no one else has noticed it yet. In replacing a mark so carefully that the next keeper cannot tell whether it was made today or ten years ago. In hearing an elder say, after watching me work, “You did not leave the water to decide.” That would be praise enough.

I would not own much. A room off one of the middle terraces, perhaps: dark, cool, smelling of stone dust and old wool. A sleeping platform. A bench cut into the wall. A row of sealed jars. A shelf for tools. A narrow slot that admits a rectangle of fog-light at certain hours. On the lintel, I might make no decoration, only a few notches: one for a hard frost, one for a season when the cisterns nearly reached full, one for the day I first learned to hear the difference between damp stone and thirsty stone. The room would not express me. It would contain me.

My wealth would be measured differently from wealth in the lower world. I would be rich if my hearing remained true. Rich if I could find a fault before it became waste. Rich if the keepers trusted my marks. Rich if a child could carry a cup across the plaza without spilling. Rich if, at the end of the day, the town needed nothing from me except that I continue.

There would be moments when I stood on the rim walk in the evening and looked down at the whole basin. The plaza basins would be darkening. The roof channels would be releasing their last beads of fog. The terraces would be empty, but not dead. The pale stone would hold the day’s light for a little while longer, and the dark openings would seem to breathe. From above, the town would look like a mechanism made of patience. From inside, it would feel like a body.

In those moments I would understand what I was there.

Not a hero. Not a founder. Not a prophet of the water. Not even a poet, though the place might make a poem out of restraint. I would be one of the attentive. I would be the town’s way of noticing one of its smallest dangers. I would be attention given hands, breath, and a small leather notebook. I would be the being who listens to the basins when they are almost silent.

If you came looking for me, do not call a name. I might not answer to one. Instead, go down to the lower ward in the early morning, when the plaza is still cold and the basins are covered. Listen. Somewhere near the largest cistern, a lid is being lifted with great care. A measure-stick descends. A breath is held. A palm rests against stone. Then, in the dark of the bowl, a small mark is made.

That would be me.
