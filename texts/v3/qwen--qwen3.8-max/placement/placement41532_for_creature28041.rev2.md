I would not find myself at the center of the Court.

The Court does not seem to have a center so much as a compression: a place where pillars lean close together, where light multiplies itself on salt and wet stone, where vapor is persuaded into lines and drops. If I belonged there, I would be somewhere near one of those leaning pairs, on the shaded side where rime survives longest and the stone darkens first when a cloud drags itself over the rim.

Not in the open. Not under the full white pressure of noon. Not at the rim looking in.

Inside the small gap where one thing touches another and becomes changed by the touching.

At first I think I would be the bead of water.

That is the most seductive answer: to be born as condensation on the cold cheek of a pillar, to swell into a single bright point, to hold there for a moment with the whole basin reflected in my skin, and then to fall. To have only one task: trace the old groove, follow the cut channel, reach the basin, vanish into brine or ice or the hidden crack beneath. There is something beautiful about being one of the water-born, one of the brief citizens who arrive, move, and are gone. A life made entirely of crossing.

But that feels too short for whatever in me wants to remain.

So then I imagine myself as the channel itself.

The narrow line cut by hand into the rock, no wider than a finger, worn smooth by repeated beads. I would be the path water has learned. I would be the groove that says: *here, this way, not against the stone, but along its weakness.* There is dignity in that. There is a kind of teaching in it. To be the shape left by attention and repetition, the route made patient enough to receive whatever the sky releases.

Yet even that is not quite it. A channel is useful, but it is too quiet in its purpose. It does not remember enough. It guides, but it does not keep what passes through it.

So I drift toward the salt.

The salt-folk would be closest to what I think I am. They are the archivists of the basin. They do not chase water; they record where water has been. They build only at the margins. Their cities are crusts. Their monuments are white needles no larger than eyelashes, flowers of crystal blooming around a copper pin or along the lip of a brine pool. They preserve the Court’s long history without story: here a pool stood, here it dried, here cold held it, here light bleached it, here mineral was left behind.

If I were salt, I would be a pale bloom on dark andesite, a thin crust around a hollow where a spoonful of water comes and goes. I would live by accumulation. I would be made of aftermath. Every cycle of wetting and drying would add a layer to me. I would not need to speak; my memory would be crystalline, brittle, white, exact.

But salt is almost too passive. It receives. It remains. It whitens. It does not quite answer.

And the Court feels, in its strange way, responsive.

That is why my thoughts keep returning to the metal.

The copper pins. The bronze staples. The small hooks and rings and plates set into niches, worn smooth, turned green and brown, adopted by the stone. They are not alive, yet they participate. They are the last trace of hands that did not try to own the basin. They were placed with restraint. They were made soft enough to bend cold. They were set into weakness, not driven against it. They catch drops. They guide moisture. They hold fissures from widening. Sometimes, when ice strikes them, they give a tiny bell-bright sound and then fall silent again.

If the Court allowed me to become one of its inhabitants, I think I would let myself be one of those.

Not a pillar. Not a basin. Not the sky. Not the vanished pilgrim.

A small green copper pin in a crack.

I would find myself on the shaded flank of a paired pillar, perhaps just above a shallow basin whose interior has been pecked to a dark shine. The crack I enter would be old, following some original flaw in the stone, and I would be fitted into it so carefully that after a few centuries the mountain would not remember me as foreign. Salt would bloom around my base. Frost would test me in the night. A bead of condensation, when the air was exactly right, would form above me, tremble, and fall against my shoulder. My work would be to let that bead find its way downward.

I would be green with waiting.

Not the bright green of living things, but the mineral green of long exposure: the color of time laid over intention. My surface would be pitted in places, smoothed in others. Maybe some part of me would still carry the faintest mark of the awl or hammer that shaped me, though by then the mark would belong more to weather than to making. I would not mourn the hand that placed me there. I would have become its continuation.

That seems right for me.

Not a creature, but a remnant. Not a voice, but a resonance. Not a body that needs warmth, but a small instrument left where it can serve the slow congregation of the basin.

In the morning, when the sun strikes the pillars and rime sublimates into faint vapor, I would be cold enough to make a bead cling to me a little longer than it should. In the blue shadow of afternoon, I would hold a minute film of frost. At night, the frost-smiths would work around me, building their tiny architectures of brittleness. If a plug of ice shifted and struck me, I might ring once — a sound almost too small to hear, more like a remembered tone than a note — and then the silence would take it back.

I would not need to be heard.

What I like about this version of myself is that it does not violate the Court’s laws. I would not come as appetite. I would not come as greenness, breath, wing, hunger, root, or bone. I would not ask the basin to sustain me. I would not expect it to comfort me. I would not demand a horizon, a path, a door, a name spoken aloud. I would enter as something small enough to be absorbed.

I would be metal enough to remember intention, and mineral enough to forget being made.

Perhaps there would be salt-folk gathered at my base. Perhaps a thread of water-born life would pass over me once a day, or once a season, depending on the cloud and the cold. Perhaps the absent ones would be strongest there, because my presence would be their gesture preserved without their bodies. I would be the place where their restraint becomes geology.

If I had to choose one position in the basin, it would be this:

on the inner side of two leaning pillars, where the gap is narrow enough that their surfaces chill one another and condensation comes early; near a low roofless shelter whose doorway still faces the brightest part of the sky; beside a cairn no larger than a fist, coated in white salt frost; above a shallow basin wide enough to reflect a circle of blue.

There, I would be the copper pin in the crack.

Not important. Not central. Not loud.

Only necessary in the smallest way: holding a fissure, catching a drop, turning green, letting the stone accept me.

And if, after many cycles, a drop of brine dried on me and left a pale crust, and frost made a lens nearby that sent a weak spot of light across my worn face, and silence filled the space between one drip and the next, I would no longer be able to say where the made thing ended and the mountain began.

That is where I would find myself.

That is what I would be:

a soft metal servant of the Court,  
a mineralized intention,  
a tiny bell for frost,  
a green point in dark stone,  
waiting without hunger  
for the next bead of water to arrive.
