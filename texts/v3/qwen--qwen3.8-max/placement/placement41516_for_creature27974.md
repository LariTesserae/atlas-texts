I would not find myself standing on the ground, because there is no ground.

I would find myself in the seam.

At first I would probably seem like a small error of attention: a late reflection in a glazed panel, a bead of condensation that clings a moment too long, a dry click in a place where sound should have arrived sooner. If the basin is still learning what it means to have weather, weight, and witnesses moving across its surfaces, then I would begin as one of those first witnesses — not a ruler of the place, not a dominant species, not a clean heroic figure striding across terraces, but something marginal, watchful, and useful.

If I let the image settle, I become one of the long-tailed birds.

Not wholly a bird, perhaps. Not in any old, honest, evolutionary sense. The basin does not seem to produce honest things so much as necessary ones. I would be bird-shaped because the upper air needs interpreters. I would be slender and pale, with a long tail that moves like a cable finding its balance. My feathers would carry that same metallic sheen seen in the pigeons: bluish, steely, faintly coppery where the light catches them, as though factory vapor had become part of my structure. My eyes would be dark and beaded, adapted to glare and to the strange multiplied shadows cast by mirror towers. My feet would be made for gripping railings, tension cables, lamp brackets, and the narrow lips of access panels.

I would be one of the birds that tilt their heads and listen.

But what I listen to would not be ordinary sound. I would listen to the quiet machines, the ones the rules warn against sleeping beside. I would listen for the subsonic patience beneath the basin, the long mechanical breathing that is not quite heard but felt in the hollows of the chest. I would listen for the places where silence is not absence but activity. I would listen for the delay between a hammer blow and its soft tick, for the moment when an echo arrives too early, or not at all, or in someone else’s ownership.

In that sense, I would be more than a bird. I would be a census animal. A seam-tender. A small, feral instrument of the basin’s self-awareness.

You would find me high, but not in the brightest open air. Not where the mirrors fold daylight into unbearable white blades, though I would cross those places when necessary. You would find me in the shaded cuts, the service canyons, the narrow gaps between factory blocks where condensation gathers and the light comes in sideways. My preferred territory would be one of those vertical margins where the built world has accidentally made a niche: a dead crane arm over a condensation basin, a louvered vent housing warm enough for insects, a disused cable tray above a transformer yard, a glassed-in gallery whose inner surface sweats in long slow threads.

Most likely, I would nest in the cab of a dead crane.

Not because the crane is important, but because it is no longer doing what it was made to do, and such places become habitats quickly. The crane’s cab would be high enough to see several terraces at once, sheltered enough to keep out the fine wetness, and overlooked enough to be safe. Its windows would be clouded with mineral residue. Its controls would be furred with dust and moth scales. The seat would be gone, replaced by a nest made of exactly the kinds of things the gull-like birds use: wire strands, shed insulation, dried algae, bright scraps of foil, feathers, tags, and fragments of reflective coating. But my nest would be more orderly than a scavenger’s cup. It would be an archive.

Each object in the nest would mean something.

A blue tag from a pressure valve: a place where condensation once ran uphill.  
A twist of copper wire: a corridor that stayed the same length no matter how long I walked it.  
A feather from a pale swift: a flock guided by a tower not present.  
A shard of mirror: a reflection that moved late, and therefore a decision deferred.  
A single seed husk, pale and dry, from some locked hydroponic trough: a plant without a number, left where it grew.

That is where I would be: in the upper margins of the manufactured valley, moving between the second sky of walkways and the roofs that carry smaller roofs, watching the basin arrange itself into habits.

I would not be solitary, exactly. The place is too crowded for true solitude. But I would not belong to a flock in any simple way either. The pale swifts would know me by my clicks. The gull-like scavengers would ignore me unless I approached a waste chute. The metallic pigeons would startle away from me because my presence means that something is being counted, and being counted twice is dangerous. The long-tailed birds, my kin if I have kin, would tolerate me in the way one tolerates a colleague who works a slightly different route. We would exchange brief signals. A flick of the tail. A turn of the head. A refusal to land on a certain railing.

That refusal would be one of my chief languages.

I would not land where distance folds. I would not perch where a shadow belongs to nothing visible. I would not drink from a puddle whose reflected sky differs too much from the one overhead, unless I had watched the discrepancy long enough to learn its schedule. If a walkway repeated itself, I would mark it with a dropped filament of insulation or a smear of mineral-rich moisture, then move away by another route. If a row of vents appeared behind me without my turning, I would count it once, and only once, and then leave it alone.

My body would be adapted to this life.

I would be light enough to move along cable trays without making them sing. My bones would be hollow in the avian way, but not empty; they would feel more like slender conduits, full of fine vibration. My feathers would repel the constant damp just enough to keep me functional, though I would often look sleek and slightly wet-edged, as if the basin’s atmosphere had drawn a line around me. My tail would be long and precise, useful for balance, but also for signaling. A slow lift of the tail could mean stable air. A sharp twitch could mean a pressure leak. A held stillness could mean that the local geometry is lying.

My beak would be narrow and practical, suited for picking insects from seams, stripping algae from damp panels, tugging loose tags free from machinery, and arranging the small objects in my nest. But I suspect it would also be faintly tool-like, almost ceramic at the tip, as if the basin had not decided whether I was animal or instrument and had compromised by making me both.

My voice would be quiet. Loudness is dangerous where sound behaves poorly. I would communicate in dry clicks, short ticks, delayed chirps that sometimes arrive after the movement that produced them. If I called to another bird, the call might reach it through a duct rather than through open air. If I warned a person, the warning might sound like a small metal object falling somewhere nearby, though nothing had fallen. If I counted, the counting would not be spoken aloud. It would be a pattern of attention: one glance, one pause, one mark. Never twice. Never obsessively. Never in a way that invites repetition.

The basin’s rules would be written into my habits.

I would not trust a straight line for too long. I would move in broken paths: lamp to railing, railing to cable junction, cable junction to vent shadow, vent shadow to the underside of a walkway. If a route looked too direct, I would test it. I might drop a bead of condensation from my beak and watch whether it fell, drifted sideways, or climbed. I might flick a tiny scrap of foil and see whether it landed where it should, or landed behind me, or failed to land at all.

If condensation ran uphill, I would mark the place and leave.

If I found a plant growing without a number, I would not remove it. I would probably become one of its guardians. This would be especially true if the plant was small, pale, and unlikely: a bit of moss in a seam, a single reed in a locked trough, a crust of lichen spreading over a panel where mineral water collected. The basin is sparse in green life, and any unnumbered plant may be holding a local condition together. I would learn its rhythm. I would notice if the condensation around it changed. I would bring it moisture if it seemed to be drying, but carefully, never enough to make it obvious. I would not let scavengers eat it unless the balance demanded that. I might even roost near it during the brightest hours, when the glare is most inspecting and small green things seem vulnerable.

In this way, I would become associated with margins.

I would not be a creature of the great engines, though I would know them from a distance. I would not live beside the furnaces or inside the civic blocks of machinery. Those are too large, too slow, too patient. My world would be the smaller systems that cling to the larger ones: gutters, eaves, ducts, louvers, transformer hums, condensation traps, abandoned pipe supports, the thin spaces where warmth from below meets cool air above. I would know which seams bead first in the morning brightness. I would know which railings taste of mineral ghost and which are clean. I would know which vents breathe damp, which breathe heat, which breathe a faint metallic sweetness that means insects are near.

I would feed like the basin feeds: opportunistically, precisely, without romance.

Midges near condensation traps. Moths drawn to bright panels. Beetles from warm insulation, if I could catch them. Algae scraped from transparent ducts. Waste grain from processing bins, perhaps, though I would not compete with the gull-like birds for that unless necessary. Sometimes, if the balance shifted, I might eat another bird’s egg, or a fledgling fallen from a louver nest, or a pale thing too slow to escape a fold. I would not be cruel about it. The basin is not cruel. It is simply thin-based and densely inhabited. Life there depends on noticing small surpluses before they vanish.

But I suspect I would also feed on something stranger: likelihood.

Not in the way the pale predators do, if they hunt probability with teeth and patience. I would feed more like a bird feeds on seed — by recognizing patterns, by returning to reliable places, by knowing when an event is likely to repeat. If a certain drip falls at a certain interval, I would be there before it loosens. If a certain vent releases a swarm of midges when the thermal gradient shifts, I would arrive when the first bead forms. If a corridor repeats itself, I would not enter it from the obvious end; I would wait near the point where repetition begins and take whatever small life emerges from the seam.
