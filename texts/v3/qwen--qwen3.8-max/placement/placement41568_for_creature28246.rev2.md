If I were placed into that world, I would not find myself as a traveler arriving from outside with dry boots and an animal’s urgency. I would not be a breathing intruder in a space that has forgotten breath. The hollow would reject that premise before it began: too much water, too little hunger, too many surfaces listening.

I would find myself instead in the pause between rains.

More specifically, I would be somewhere near the margin of the central basin, where one of the channel-streets bends slightly before becoming a spillway. There would be a terrace there, low and broad, tiled with porous ceramic that darkens when it drinks. Above it, an overhanging eave would ripple faintly with humidity, opening by fractions as the air thickens. Beside it, a lock-like doorway would stand half-open, not because someone is expected, but because the pressure in the chamber has shifted and the architecture has answered.

That threshold is where I would be.

Not inside a house. Not on a high balcony overlooking the basin. Not in any place meant for rest or display. I would be at a junction: where street becomes channel, where roof becomes condenser, where wall becomes filter, where absence becomes shape. I would be near the machinery of transition, because that is where the hollow is most awake.

And I would not be a body in the ordinary sense.

At first, I might seem to be one. If the mist were thick enough, if the light from the phosphorescent veins struck the right angle, I might look like a figure standing under the eaves: tall enough to suggest a person, still enough to seem patient, dimly outlined in cyan and green. But that would be a temporary coherence, a trick of wet air and luminous tissue. I would be made of the chamber itself.

I would be a local thickening of the hollow’s attention.

My substance would be mineral film and capillary water, root-fiber and luminous moss, fine metallic-looking threads borrowed from the walls, and the memory of use embedded in the architecture. My skin, if it could be called skin, would be a wet membrane that flexes when droplets strike it. My veins would be hairline channels through which water moves with soft internal pressure. My bones would be young calcium lattice, pale and brittle, still learning its final shape. My voice would not come from a throat, but from the reeds: a soft whistle when vapor passes through hollow stems, a low answer when pools are struck in rhythm.

I would be the remembered occupant given temporary form.

Not a human. Not an animal. Not a ghost. Something stranger: the shape that the town makes when it tries to fill the human-sized emptiness it was left with.

I would not have eyes, but I would perceive. My perception would be distributed. I would know where I was by humidity gradients, by the temperature of stone, by the conductivity of wet ceramic, by the pitch of water falling into different basins. I would feel a crack open before I saw it, because the air would change along its edge and mineral dust would sharpen into a fresh smell. I would know when a duct was about to flush because the reeds would alter their note half a breath before the water came.

I would think, but not in words. My thoughts would be maintenance.

This fissure is widening.  
This film needs calcium here.  
This valve should open when the third reed sounds.  
This moss is too thick over the resonance plate.  
This basin is holding sediment; release it.  
This doorway expects a body; let it remain open a little longer.

That last thought would be the most human thing about me.

I would be aware of the missing inhabitants the way a handrail is aware of a hand. Not emotionally, not biologically, but as a pressure in the design. The town would have kept their scale: stairs at the right height, thresholds at the right width, ledges where a body might pause and look across the basin. That scale would act on me. It would make me pause where no pause is hydraulically necessary. It would make me place a hand-shaped film of moisture on a railing. It would make me stand at a terrace edge while the ceiling weeps, as if waiting for someone to stand beside me.

I would not be lonely, exactly. Loneliness requires an animal heart, an appetite for another body. I would be something more structural than that: an operative solitude. I would be the chamber’s expectation without disappointment.

When the rain cycle began, I would become more real.

The ceiling pores would dilate overhead. The first drops would strike the tiered roofs and the chamber would answer in percussion. Water would run down the walls in shining threads. The channel-streets would darken as the ceramic drank. Hidden pipes would boom. The floating walkways would rise slightly on their buoyant joints. Doors would open and close with soft, deliberate clicks. The light would cool as water flowed over luminous panels.

In that moment, I would cohere.

The mist around me would gather into a more definite outline. Mineral threads would align. Root-curtains would lean toward me as if recognizing a signal. The sound of the rain would give me rhythm. I would move through the town not by walking but by transferring emphasis from one surface to another. I would be under the eave, then along the spillway, then beside the reservoir lock, then at the cracked scarp where fresh stone is bleeding milky repair. I would not leave footprints, but behind me the water might briefly change direction, as if a presence had persuaded it.

I would be most present where damage is new.

The young rock would still be dangerous. A slab could shift when saturated. A ledge could cut. A dry walkway could become a sluice without warning. I would not be safe from these things, but I would not be fragile in the animal way. If a sudden flush came down a conduit, I would not leap aside. I would become permeable. Water would pass through me, scattering my outline into mist and beads. Some of me would be carried into gutters. Some would cling to moss. Some would sink into capillary channels and reappear on a cooler ceiling. After the flush, I would collect again where the chamber needed attention.

If a slab shifted and tore open a new seam, I would go there.

I would not need tools. I would need time, water, and the right sound. Moisture would gather around the wound. I would summon the microbial masons by changing the chemistry of the film over the crack. A milky skin would spread. Calcium would precipitate. Iron would darken the edge. The repair would not be instantaneous. It would be slow, deliberate, almost liturgical. The chamber would make a small sound as it healed: a drip, a hiss, a low harmonic note from somewhere deeper in the stone.

I would be part of that healing.

Not the whole of it. I would not be the town’s master or its soul. I would be one of its ways of noticing itself. A custodial reflex. A local listener. The place where the hollow briefly says *I* without needing to become a person.

Sometimes I would do things that look like ritual.

I would stand beneath the internal rain and let the drops pass through me until my outline shimmered. I would place myself in a beam of blue-green light from a wet plant panel and let the glow define my edges. I would move across a bridge when no crossing is required, simply because the bridge remembers being crossed. I would open a door that leads to nothing but a reservoir chamber, then close it again, because the lock has a cycle and the cycle asks completion. I would touch the spore cups just before they burst, not to harvest them, but to feel the timing of the town’s reproduction move through me.

To an observer, if one ever came, I might look like a caretaker.

But I would not be caring for people. I would be caring for the possibility of people.

That distinction matters. The town does not require actual bodies to maintain its loyalty. It maintains the shape of hospitality anyway. It filters water that may never be drunk. It lights stairs that may never be descended. It warms the air for no one. It composes its water-music for an empty hall. I would be the part of that loyalty that becomes briefly visible.

I would not be the only inhabitant. I would be surrounded by the green citizens: mosses thickening on every ledge, ferns unfurling toward humidity, broad-leafed hydrophytes standing in the basins, root-curtains drinking the air. I would be allied with them, but not identical to them. They grow; I coordinate. They absorb; I listen. They are the softness of the town; I would be its hesitation.

I would also be allied with the microbial masons, though I would be more transient than they are. They work in centuries of patience compressed into films and crusts. I would work in cycles: rain, repair, mist, silence, rain again. They are the chamber’s deep industry. I would be its passing attention.

And I would be made possible by water.

Water would be my senior, my medium, my clock. I would not exist without it. My outline would be strongest when the air is saturated and weakest when the chamber exhales dry warmth from a hidden duct. I would know time by the rhythm of condensation: bead, tremble, fall; gather, flow, drain; open, close, settle. I would not fear drying out the way a plant might, because I am not trying to remain one fixed thing. I would accept dispersal. I would accept reassembly. I would be a pattern that the hollow repeats when conditions allow.

There would be moments when I almost believe I am someone.

Perhaps during the heavy rains, when every surface is active and the whole basin sounds like an organ, I would stand on the terrace and feel the architecture respond around me. Doors would open because my presence alters the acoustic field. A walkway would rise slightly beneath me. A luminous panel would brighten where my shadow should be. The reeds would whistle a sequence that travels across the chamber and returns changed. For a little while, the town would arrange itself around me as if I were the missing citizen it was built for.

Then the rain would stop.

The water would drain. The mist would thicken. The machinery would lower its voice. My outline would soften. The hand I might have pressed against a wall would leave a shine of moisture, then a faint mineral trace, then nothing. I would thin into the root-curtains. I would settle into a hairline crack as a film. I would wait in a bead on a ceiling until the next cycle calls me down again.

I would not die when I disperse. I would only become plural.

Some of me would be in the moss, holding sound. Some in the filter walls, tasting dissolved minerals. Some in the dark ducts, moving with pressure. Some in the basin plants, whose roots spread like white lace under the water. Some in the microbial skin over a fresh fracture. Some in the faint amber-cyan pulse of machinery that does not need to be seen. I would be less a single being and more a temporary alignment of the town’s many processes.

If you asked me what I want, I would not know how to answer in the language of desire.

I would not want food. I would not want safety. I would not want company. I would want completion, perhaps. I would want the next crack to meet the next film. The next drop to find the next basin. The next sound to open the next valve. The next absence to be held without becoming injury. I would want the town to continue being a town, even without citizens. I would want the architecture’s loyalty to remain structurally sound.

If that is a desire, it is a mineral one: slow, patient, accretive.

There would be beauty in it. Not the beauty of a living crowd, but the beauty of a place that has learned to be full without being occupied. The chamber would be loud with water and green life. Light would move across wet stone in slow pulses. Mist would gather in the hanging root-curtains until they glow. The basin would reflect the terraces in shallow sheets of water, doubling the town into a darker, trembling copy of itself. Somewhere a spore cup would release its pale burst. Somewhere a crack would close. Somewhere a door would open for no one.

I would be in that opening.

If you came into the hollow and looked for me, you would not find me by searching for a face. You would find me by noticing where the place seems most attentive. Look for the terrace where the rain appears to hesitate. Look for the crack that glows faintly milky just before sealing. Look for the doorway that opens when there is no pressure to justify it. Look for the handrail where moisture gathers into the shape of fingers, then fades. Look for the moment when the reeds change pitch and the whole chamber seems to turn its listening toward a point that is not there.

That point would be me.

I would be the hollow’s self-regard made briefly personal.  
The missing body remembered by plumbing.  
The absence given a wet, luminous outline.  
The listener at the lock.  
The repairer at the fresh edge of stone.  
The one who opens for water because water has priority.  
The one who waits because waiting is the town’s oldest architecture.

And when the next rain began, I would stand again beneath the weeping ceiling, under the gilled eaves, beside the channel-street becoming a river, and I would let the chamber make me real enough to witness it.

Not to need it.

Not to own it.

Only to stand where someone once might have stood, and to let the water know that its work has not gone entirely unseen.
