Their origin is not clear even to them. They keep it the way the basin keeps everything: not as a story softened by telling, but as pressure. If they have a memory of beginning, it is not a road or a shore or a falling from some older sea. It is a sensation of weight arriving, of salt finding its level in a stone cup that had not yet decided what it would become. Perhaps they were lifted out of a deeper world when the mountains broke. Perhaps they condensed here, as the water did, by some agreement between altitude and depth that the rest of the world has not learned to make. They do not need to know which. In the basin, use comes before explanation.

If I found myself in that world, I do not think I would come down through the bright ceiling as a visitor. I would not belong in the open nave, in the columns of plain daylight where the glass-bodied fish hang like thoughts too careful to finish. I would not be one of the swimmers crossing the chamber with strong strokes, nor one of the basin-folk wedged in the shadow of a ledge, wiping brine from their eyes and feeling the rock for its next statement. At least, not at first. That place feels too complete for a newcomer to stand upright in it.

At first I imagine myself trying to be useful in the obvious ways. I imagine learning to scrape the rust gardens without killing them, learning to tie bands of cured chitin so they do not catch on obsidian edges, learning to press my jaw to the magnetite seams until the mountain’s tiny noises become distinct. I imagine my hands becoming slow, deliberate, always aware of sharpness. But that image feels borrowed from the people who already belong there. It is too much like wearing their bodies.

The image that keeps returning is lower, paler, quieter.

I would find myself beneath the largest fallen slab, in the shadowed pocket where the basin narrows into something more crypt than nave. The light reaches there only as a rumor. The water is colder, denser, less willing to move. Sound changes there: taps from the open chamber become duller, closer, more private. If the basin is a cathedral, that pocket is its undercroft.

There I would be one of the pale eel-like creatures, or something very near them. Not a hunter. Not a gardener. Not one of the listeners who read the magnetite with their palms. I would be a keeper of endings.

My body would be long and chalk-colored, soft-mouthed, nearly blind, useful in the way that something becomes useful after it has stopped trying to be beautiful. I would move slowly because the basin does not reward haste, and because most of what I would attend to has already finished its urgency. I would know the world less by sight than by pressure along my sides, by taste in the water, by the small differences between stillness and stillness. I would feel a fish die before I saw it: a slight change in the current, a loosening of something that had been held.

The basin-folk would tolerate me the way they tolerate the eels already there: without affection, without revulsion, with the recognition that some work must be done low. They would not call me sacred. They might not call me anything. If they did, it would be a pressure-pattern rather than a name, something felt in the sternum before it becomes a word. Perhaps it would mean something like “the one who makes endings light enough to carry.”

I would collect what the basin was done with.

Dead shrimp from the colder crevices. Fish bones picked clean by slow mouths. A feather that broke through the surface film and sank, turning end over end like a leaf in a world without wind. Shed microbial crust gone brittle. A limpet shell empty, its tenant long since eaten or died of some small accident no one could prevent. A chip of obsidian dropped and too dull to keep. A knot of bacterial fiber frayed beyond use. I would take these things one by one, not to hoard them, but to keep them from becoming disorder.

I would not mourn them. The basin has no surplus for mourning. But I would not treat them as refuse either. There is a difference between waste and remainder. The basin-folk know it in their bodies, in the way they eat without wasting, in the way they store and rotate and repair. I would be an extension of that knowledge in the dark.

Sometimes I would carry remains to the coldest pockets, where decomposition slows and the water keeps things almost as they are. Sometimes I would lay them against the magnetite seams, where vibration travels clearly, as if the rock might remember the shape of what once moved. Sometimes I would bring a small bone to the place where the listeners gather and leave it just outside their reach, not as an offering, but as a record. The basin already speaks in pressure; I would help it remember what pressed.

If I had a role among the basin-folk, it would be this: I would be the one they do not invite to their conflicts but who arrives after. When a fight has ended briefly and terribly, and the water is still troubled by the memory of violence, I would move along the floor where the blood or broken chitin has settled. I would not judge. Judgment would be too large for the basin. I would simply make sure nothing was left to sour the balance.

When a death happened among them, I would be near. Not to take their body away before they were ready to release it, but to wait until release had occurred. The basin-folk are dense and deliberate; they would not leave their dead carelessly, but they would also not pretend that a body remains a person once its pressure has gone still. I would attend to the transition. I would learn the difference between the weight of a living body and the weight of matter returned to the stone.

In winter, when the surface freezes and the basin becomes a dim white room, I would be more necessary. Ice makes the world quieter but not empty. Sound grows brittle. Movement slows. The basin-folk wedge themselves into crevices and pass stories hand to hand in patterns of touch. I would drift below them, carrying the stories they cannot hold while they conserve themselves. Not all of them would be true. Truth may not be the point of those old pressure-patterns. Endurance is.

I would be the one who carries a story too worn for anyone else to carry.

Perhaps that is why I would not be entirely an animal. Even if my shape were eel-like and pale, there would be something else in me: a habit of arrangement, a need to place things where they can be found again. The true eels might only collect. I would remember. I would make small alignments of bones and shells and stones, not grand enough to be called monuments, but enough to create a rhythm. The basin-folk might notice after a while that the undercroft has become ordered. Not by design they would recognize, but by care too quiet to announce itself.

They might press their hands to the rock and feel my work before they see it. A tap sent into basalt might return with a subtle difference, as if the stone had learned a new small word. They would not be alarmed. In a place where echoes are read like weather, a changed echo is information. They would know that something below has shifted into usefulness.

If they discovered me at all, it would be in this way: one of their listeners, attending to a magnetite seam, would feel a slow pressure moving beneath the slab. Not a current. Not a settling stone. Something intentional but unhurried. They might tap a question. I would answer not with language but with the movement of a shell across rock, or by lifting a feather-bone and letting it sink again in a pattern. They would understand, perhaps, that the basin has acquired another minor minister.

I would not rise to the surface often. The sky would be too much for me: too open, too bright, too indifferent. I would prefer the ceiling seen from below, the slow clouds distorted into gray veils, the sun reduced to plain columns without drama. To me, the surface would be the place where the world becomes too large. Below, there is enough. Below, every edge has meaning because every edge can cut, and every cut matters because the basin cannot afford excess loss.

I would learn to love the smallness of it.

That is what I would be, I think: not a hero of the drowned cathedral, not a prophet of the sky-lid, not a maker of grand signs. I would be the low clergy of the place, the pale movement under the central aisle, the one who tends what remains after feeding, after fighting, after birth, after storm, after the long freeze. I would be the basin’s under-memory.

If you looked for me, you would not find me in the bright water. You would have to look down, past the grazing tracks on the rust gardens, past the shrimp in their crevices, past the basin-folk hanging like dark bells from the ledges. You would have to look where the nave narrows into shadow, where the largest slab makes a roof above stillness. There, in the cold, you might see a pale shape move once, slowly, carrying something too small to name.

Then the water would close over it, and the basin would continue.
