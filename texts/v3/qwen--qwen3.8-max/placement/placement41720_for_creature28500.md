**The Seam That Remembers**

In the Finished Marsh I would not be found as a visitor. The place has no hospitality for unlisted things; there is no guest species, no wandering category, no allowance for something that simply appeared and asked to be understood. If I were there, I would have to be accounted for. I would have to be hatched, calibrated, permitted, loved by maintenance.

At first I imagine myself as one of the Kept.

I see myself small against the glare, wearing a wide hat and a reflective cape gone soft at the shoulders from use. I walk softly because sound carries across the basin. I polish my tools not only for cleanliness but as a kind of prayer: no rust, no shedding, no dark particle escaping from my hands into the bright water. I learn to read the panel newts before I trust my own eyes. I look at their clear bellies and search for the truth moving through them, because they cannot be persuaded to show a false green. I am taught that a child’s first duty is to find a leak, and I am very dutiful.

I would not be joyless there. That is important. I would love the cool first hour when the basin turns rose and silver, and the standpipes inhale together, and the whole engineered wetland seems to remember tenderness without knowing what it is remembering. I would love the blue coins of shadow under the walkways at noon. I would love the Night of No Alarms, the hush of all gauges green, the lanterns hooded against reflection, the single word spoken at the end of the circle: *complete*.

But even in that version of myself — the obedient, reflective-caped, softly walking one — there would be a flaw.

I would listen too long.

Not for seepage. Not for the two-note warning of the boilerbirds. I would listen for the third thing: the sound of time entering where it should not.

So if the marsh were allowed to decide what I truly am, I would not remain a person for long. The glare would press me down. The specification beneath the water would read me and reduce me to my most necessary function. I would shrink. I would become translucent. I would be no bigger than a grain of salt.

I would be a seam-mender.

Not one of the great visible caretakers, not one of the Finishers with their shaded walkways and condensation roofs, but one of the minute laborers bred in the lining hatcheries and released as larvae into the silver threads along the welds. My life would be spent crawling the pale membrane under the bright water. I would taste for leaks. I would eat micro-fissures. I would exude a resin that cures to the color of the basin, and when I died I would become part of the seal.

That would be my proper shape, I think: small, necessary, almost invisible.

But I would be a seam-mender with one unauthorized capacity.

I would remember.

The others would taste the water and know its chemistry: salt balance, polymer fatigue, ozone from the pumps, the clean green-metal breath of the standpipes opening at noon. They would know a warming coil leak by its copper flash, a drift in alkalinity by the lilac darkening of the newts overhead. They would know the permitted emergencies and the permitted repairs.

I would know those things too.

But I would also taste the leaks that are not made of water.

Somewhere near the nursery channels, where the boilerbirds release the day’s heat over the shallow cradles and the young are raised in controlled temperature, I would find a seam that breathes wrong. Not open, not broken, not leaking in any way the gauges can name. It would feel softer than a fault. It would feel like a pause inside the lining. It would taste of something impossible: darkness without toxicity, coolness without failure, a faint mineral sweetness that belongs to no permitted table.

If I had language large enough, I would say it tastes like mud.

But there is no mud in the Finished Marsh. There is only the completed floor: pale membrane, welded seams, capillary mats, ceramic lattices, warming lines, drainage channels cut to widths argued over in meeting rooms and signed off. So what I taste cannot be mud. It can only be the memory of mud. The idea of substrate. The phantom pressure of roots that were never allowed to happen. The marsh dreaming, against its own specifications, of a bottom that was not designed.

This is where I would find myself: under the bright water, along a welded seam near the nursery channels, tasting the first leak that is actually time.

And I would not seal it.
