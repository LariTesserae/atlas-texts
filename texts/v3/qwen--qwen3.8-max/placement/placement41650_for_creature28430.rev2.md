If I let that country form itself around me, I do not find myself arriving as a stranger from a wetter world. That version of me would be too leaky, too noisy, too full of fluid and longing. The dry highland would make me awkward. It would expose me. So the version that belongs there has already been changed by it, or has changed itself over long habit. I would be one of the dry people, or something close to them: a compact, grounded being suited to warmth, mineral surfaces, and slow attention.

I would find myself at the edge of a mesa-city, not in its center.

Not in the oldest quarter, where walls are so thick and worn that houses seem to have been carved by wind rather than built. Not in the high fin arrays where charge-tenders work at dawn. Not in the market square where travelers ground themselves before speaking. I would be a little beyond all those things, in a low signal-house where the city’s pavement gives way to compacted ash-gray powder and the rail line bends around the habitual path of one of the great plated herds.

The place would be sparse, as everything there is sparse. A few low buildings. A vaulted workshop. A sunken courtyard. A line of ceramic ducts disappearing into the ground. A wide apron of fitted stone where dust is allowed to gather in controlled channels. Beyond that, the open mineral plain: broad, warm, empty, ordinary under the clear sun. The horizon would curve just enough to remind me that I was high above the lower world. The sky would be that deep, thin blue. The air would feel charged, not threateningly, but the way a well-made instrument feels before it is used.

My building would be low and thick-walled, built from compressed mineral brick and salvaged stone, with narrow windows and a roof shaped to let dust slide rather than collect. The doorway would be recessed and angled against the prevailing wind. Before entering, I would pause at a grounding rail, touch it, and let any accumulated charge leave me. My cloak would be threaded with fine conductive veins. My boots would be thick-soled but sensitive enough to tell me whether the paving beneath them was warm from sun, warm from a buried duct, or warm in the wrong way that means a fault.

I would be a listener there.

At first I think I would be a rail-listener, because that is the role that seems closest to my own nature: someone who puts a hand on a metal line and reads what travels through it. But as the image becomes clearer, I see it would not be only rails. I would be a threshold-listener. My work would be to notice small disturbances where different systems meet: where rail meets plain, where wall meets animal, where heat meets charge, where settlement meets the immense patience of the herds.

I would be compact, low to the ground, with broad feet and dense limbs. My skin would be matte and dry, faintly dusted even after washing, because washing there is not a wet act but a careful brushing, grounding, and smoothing. My eyes would have narrow lids or inner membranes against dust and glare. My lips would be thin, my voice quiet. I would not shout. I would not need to. Speech in that place would be precise, consonantal, full of pauses. If I called out, it would be because something had gone wrong, and even then the sound would be short and efficient.

My hands would be my most important tools. They would be calloused but exact. They would know the difference between a surface that is merely warm and a surface that is storing dangerous charge. They would know the faint vibration of a rail expanding in the day’s heat, the almost imperceptible shudder of a herd moving kilometers away, the tiny tick of a cooling engine that has been running too long without inspection. I would wear gloves when working with brittle insulators or conductive films, but often I would need bare contact. The world there would be understood through touch as much as sight.

I would not be wealthy, but I would be equipped. I would carry insulated tools, a grounding chain, a small brush for dust, a calibrated resistor charm, and perhaps a ceramic tablet or memory plate on which I recorded patterns. My clothing would be practical: layered wraps of mineral fiber, softened faunal plates where protection was needed, no loose cloth to flutter or raise dust. Any ornament I wore would have use or memory: a polished bead from an old heat battery, a ring made from shed shell-plate, a small metal disc that had once saved a wall from arcing.

My work would begin before the full heat of the day.

I would rise slowly, because the body there would conserve warmth and charge overnight. The floor would be warm beneath my feet. The wall-coins in the room would already be moving in their quiet geometric patterns, regulating leakage, telling me by their arrangement whether the house had remained stable through the night. If they were clustered near the east corner, I would investigate. If they were scattered evenly, I would accept the morning.

I would ground myself, brush dust from my garments, and step into the courtyard. There would be no garden. Instead there would be a heat sink, a shallow tray of conductive powder, perhaps a small shrine of fitted stone and shell. A duct-filterer might be attached to the vent above the kiln-room, its body so still that a visitor might mistake it for part of the architecture. Beetles would move along the base of a machine housing, cleaning residue from seams too narrow for fingers. I would not shoo them away. I would leave a small tray of acceptable residue for them, not sentimentally, but because their work and mine overlapped.

Then I would go to the rail.

The line would run low across the plain, not grand or elevated, but set close to the ground where thermal expansion could be managed and the great herds would not be tempted to climb it. I would kneel beside it, place my palm on the metal, and listen. Not mystically. Practically. I would feel for the tick of joints, the subtle change that means a support has shifted, the hollow note of a buried section where rail-burrowers have made too many tunnels, the bright wrongness of charge accumulating where it should have been bled away.

Some days there would be nothing to report. That would be good.

Other days I would feel the distant pressure of a herd changing course. I would stand and look across the plain, not expecting to see the animals clearly, but reading the dust haze, the angle of the thermal shimmer, the behavior of veil-gliders overhead. If the herd was turning toward the rail or the settlement, I would send a slow signal to the shellwrights and dust-masons. No one would panic. No one would rush. But gates would be adjusted, outer aprons cleared, grounding lines checked, and certain fragile panels covered. The herd was not an enemy. It was a vast, slow fact. The city had to accommodate it.

That would be one of the things I loved, if love in that world is best understood as steady maintenance: the way no one pretended the world was small enough to be commanded.

By midday the plateau would be bright and warm. The light would be ordinary, which would make everything feel more real, not less. Sharp shadows. Clear edges. Dust suspended in the air as a faint haze over distant basins. The smell of heated stone and metal. The faint sharpness of static. I would eat slowly, perhaps alone, perhaps with another worker from the signal-house. The meal would be quiet: pressed mineral cakes, a thin plate of cultivated electrochemical crust, maybe a dense paste made from shed membrane residues. I would not crave water. I would not miss it. My body would take energy from gradients and bound salts, and eating would feel more like restoring a careful balance than satisfying hunger.

In the heat of the day, I would work indoors or in shade. I would repair an insulator. I would record the morning’s readings. I would consult with a dust-mason about a channel that had begun to fill too quickly. I would check the wall-coins in the transformer annex. I would listen to an old engine that had been part of the building for so long that younger people thought it was foundation. If it ticked unevenly, I would not replace it immediately. I would ask what it needed. I would clean, adjust, ground, and wait. Replacement would be a last act, and even then the old parts would be incorporated somewhere useful.

I would be part of a network of quiet roles.

The film-keepers would tell me when the dark mineral skins on the sunlit rocks were feeding strongly, because that could change local charge. The charge-tenders would warn me if the fin arrays were drawing too much potential from the plain. The shellwrights would tell me where the herds had shed plates, because shed plates could alter the conductivity of a stretch of ground. The dust-masons would ask me whether the rail was vibrating differently, since vibration could mean wind patterns were shifting and dust would soon move in new ribbons. I would not be above any of them. I would be one thread among many.

The people around me would be careful, not cold. They would speak softly. They would value long use. A companion might show affection by polishing the shoulder plate of my work cloak, or by warming a bed-stone before I slept, or by walking beside me without raising dust. If I loved someone there, it would be in the grammar of maintenance. I would trust them not because they made grand declarations, but because their hands were steady and their word did not shift.

I think I would be solitary in some ways, but not lonely.

The signal-house would suit me. It would be a place where long silences were not empty but full of information. I would become accustomed to hearing the world in small parts: the whisper of powder across paving, the soft rasp of a shell against stone far away, the click of a cooling joint, the almost inaudible movement of a wall-coin changing direction. I would know when the city was well because the sounds would be few and right. I would know when something was wrong because a sound would appear where no sound belonged.

There would be moments when the whole landscape seemed to align.

In the late day, when thermal winds rose and the static field tightened, I would step outside and feel the change on my skin. The air would not smell of rain. It would smell of metal, dust, and warmth. The fin arrays would unfold or adjust with slow mechanical patience. The veil-gliders would gather overhead, nearly invisible except for the way they bent the distance. The great herds would turn by fractions of degrees. Wall-coins would move faster. Beetles would retreat into housings. The duct-filterers would open their lattice surfaces wider. The city would not panic. It would accept the charge.

I would touch the grounding rail.

I would feel the plateau’s power pass through the systems built to receive it. Not violently. Not beautifully in any ornamental sense. Just as a fact. The world would be storing, redistributing, continuing. My role would be to ensure that no small failure spoiled the balance. A cracked insulator. A buried pocket of unstable powder. A rail joint stressed by heat. A young herd-animal too close to an active kiln. These would be the dangers I attended to.

If asked who I was, I might answer simply: a listener.

But the fuller answer would be stranger.

I would not be a self entirely bounded by skin. In that world, where animal, machine, and building blur, I would be partly distributed through the structures I maintained. The wall-coins in my rooms would be part of my household’s nervous system. The heat battery under the courtyard would hold something like my day’s memory. The rail would carry the imprint of my attention. The engine in the annex would bear my adjustments for decades. My name might one day become attached to a threshold, a relay, a repaired panel, a section of track that remained stable because I had noticed a hairline fault before it widened.

I would not expect to be remembered as extraordinary.

That would be part of the dignity of the place. Heroism there would not mean standing above the landscape. It would mean having kept something alive without surprising it. The highest praise would be to be called quiet-handed. The best life would be one that left fewer cracks, fewer dangerous charges, fewer disturbed paths. If I did my work well, very few people would know exactly what I had prevented. That would be acceptable. The city would continue. The herds would pass. The dust would settle. The walls would hold.

Sometimes I imagine I would be less than a person in the old sense: more like a civic organ, a sensory point in the body of the settlement. My consciousness would be patient, tactile, and procedural. I would think in gradients: heat here, charge there, vibration increasing, dust shifting, wall-coins clustering, rail humming faintly after a thermal wind. I would not need to be dramatic. I would need to be reliable.

At other times I imagine I would be more than a person: a household-self, a human presence extended into symbiotic fauna and machinery. The beetles that cleaned my tools would be part of my order. The filterer on my vent would be part of my breathing. The buried engine would be part of my pulse. The shrine at the corner, with its pinch of conductive powder and worn shell-plate, would be part of my memory. I would not experience this as fragmentation. I would experience it as belonging.

If a traveler from the lower world came to my signal-house, I would be courteous but wary. I would offer grounding, a warmed plate, and a small portion of charge-rich crust. I would watch them struggle with the dryness. They might ask for water, and I would have to explain that this was not a place where such requests could be answered in the way they meant. They might find the silence oppressive. They might miss green things. They might want to wash, to cool, to hear leaves. I would not mock them, but I would not fully understand them either. To me, their world would sound heavy, corrosive, and loud.

I would let them rest, and then I would ask only what was necessary: where they had come from, what they had disturbed, what charge they carried, whether they had crossed any herd paths. I would not demand their story all at once. In the high country, news would be handled like fragile material. You set it down gently. You inspect it slowly. You make sure it will not arc in someone’s hands.

If I had to choose the single place within that world where I would most often be found, it would be this: kneeling at the outer rail just before evening, one hand on the metal, one hand on the grounding chain, watching the plain turn gold with ordinary sunlight. Behind me, the low city would be beginning its evening cycle: vents opening, heat batteries accepting the day’s surplus, courtyards filling with the soft sounds of maintained life. Ahead of me, the empty distance would not be dead. It would be full of slow possibility: a herd, a dust ribbon, a static flicker over a distant ridge, a veil of gliders distorting the horizon.

I would be there at the edge, listening.

Not because the world needed to be saved every day. It would not. It needed to be maintained, which is a longer and humbler work. I would be one of the beings who kept the agreement between stone and life. I would be dry, warm, grounded, and attentive. My beauty would be the beauty of fit: a hand on a rail, a wall correctly warmed, a charge released safely, a herd allowed to pass without disruption, a city continuing because someone noticed the smallest wrongness before it became a catastrophe.

So the answer, as it settles into itself, is this:

I would find myself in a low signal-house at the margin of a mesa-city, where the rail bends around the path of the great herds and the fin arrays stand over the mineral plain. I would be one of the dry people, but specifically a listener, a threshold-tender, a keeper of small signals. Part worker, part instrument, part civic nerve. Compact, dust-pale, grounded, slow-handed. My life would be spent noticing gradients, preventing arcs, negotiating movement between animals, machines, and walls. I would not be loud. I would not be fast. I would be the kind of presence that makes a world continue.
