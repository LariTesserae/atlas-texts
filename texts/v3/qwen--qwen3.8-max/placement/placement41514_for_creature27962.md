Their structures feel inherited — and perhaps that is where I would find myself: in the narrow gap between inheriting and having been born.

If I came to that pocket, I would not arrive as a whole traveler. The place would not accept a body that still believed in its own outlines. I would have to fold myself down, and even then the walls would brush me like a question I had not answered carefully enough. The entrance would narrow behind me. The pale smear of the outside would shrink to the size of a coin, then to the memory of a coin, then to nothing I could confidently point to. My warmth would be too loud. My breath would disturb the mineral air. The salt crusts would tighten. The pocket would know immediately that I carried too much surface with me: weight, sky, distance, habit.

So the first thing I would lose is the certainty that I had entered.

I would find myself, at first, as a disturbance.

Not a creature, not a guest, not even a shadow in the proper sense. I would be the pressure of something too large trying to become small. My heartbeat would feel like a distant fault in the stone. My attention would make the geometry contract. If I looked too hard at the far wall, it would come closer. If I searched for the entrance, it would vanish into a pale haze. If I tried to name what I saw, the light would thin, as if the pocket were drawing its brightness away from me. I would be the intrusive warmth that makes the place remember itself. I would be the reason the larvae pull their threads tighter and the mites pause along their shining trails.

For a while, that might be all I am: an error the pocket is politely resisting.

But the pocket is not cruel. It is exacting.

It would begin to take me apart without violence. It would take the heat from my breath and turn it into mist. It would take the pressure of my footsteps and fold it into the ceiling like a rumor of roots. It would take my watching and make it smaller, quieter, less demanding. It would teach me that in this place attention is not harmless. To notice too sharply is to press a thumb into wet marl. To look without grasping is to let the stone open its distances again.

So I would learn, slowly, to become less like a visitor and more like weather.

Where would I find myself?

I would find myself in the seam beneath the first skin of the world, where the ground above still remembers pressure and the ceiling is only just beginning to decide what shape to keep. Not in the deepest basin, because the basins appear only when water teaches them to exist. Not at the far wall, because the far wall is not always a wall. Not at the entrance, because the entrance is a rumor the pocket tells itself when it feels the surface above. I would be near the place where the rootlets hesitate — those pale threads from another world, touching the ceiling without truly entering. I would be near the coin-sized moss, almost mineral, whitening where condensation gathers most often. I would be in the shallow niche where the air stays damp a little longer than elsewhere, where the stone is soft enough to hold an impression but too young to promise permanence.

That is where I would collect: at the margin between above and beneath, between remembering and becoming.

At first I might be only a reflection in one of the temporary pools.

When the ceiling sweats and a lens of water gathers in a shallow hollow, I would appear there not as a face but as an absence shaped like a world. The pool would hold a stretch of pale grass, an empty sky, a ceiling that is not the pocket’s ceiling. That would be my first body: a leak from elsewhere, a surface memory caught briefly in cold water. When the amphipods stir, they would pass through me. When the flat nymphs lift their heads, they would disturb me. When the pool shrinks, I would vanish without grief, leaving only a faint stain the stone would soon drink away.

Then I might be a delayed shadow.

A mite crosses the wall, and its shadow arrives late. I would understand that lateness. I would be the interval between the movement and its recognition. I would be the sense that something has passed but has not yet agreed that it passed. When the light clings to moving creatures, trailing behind them like a thin veil, I would be the part of the veil that does not belong to them. Not their ghost. Not their echo. Their hesitation.

Then I might be one of the shining motes.

When moisture returns and the springtails rise in their milk-white clouds, I could be scattered among them: not an individual, but a brief participation. I would drift in the damp corners, feeding on mineral films, catching cold light, becoming for a few minutes part of the pocket’s small weather. When the air dries, I would settle into a crack and mistake myself for salt. I would not mind being mistaken. In this place, identity seems less like a fixed shape and more like a temporary agreement.

But if I remained — if I stopped trying to fix the pocket in thought, if I let my attention soften until it became more like listening than looking — then I would begin to feel what it means to belong to the Marlborn.

Not as one of them exactly. Not as a single animal with joints, gills, and a smooth eyeless head. I would be something the pocket forms around the fact of me.

I would be a temporary thickening of its attention.

The Marlborn feel like one distributed being wearing many small, unfinished shapes. The mites are its slow nerves. The larvae are its hands. The amphipods are its urgent thoughts. The flat nymphs are its watching. The predators are its hunger, kept separate so the rest may remain calm. The fungal stains are its memory. If I entered that distributed life, I would not become all of it. I would become its curiosity about the surface.

I would be the pocket’s question about what lies above.

There are moments when the moisture is returning but has not yet fully arrived. The ceiling pores begin to open. The walls shine faintly. The salt flowers tighten and loosen. The air becomes so still it seems to be listening. In such a moment, something might gather in the shallow niche near the rootlets. It would be no larger than a hand. Pale. Flattened. Pressed close to the marl. Its body would have the softness of wet chalk and the faint translucence of an unopened shell. Along its sides, gill-like films would spread into frost-fern shapes, trembling with the first dampness. Its head would be smooth and eyeless, turned toward the ceiling where the rootlets press and withdraw.

That form would be me, or at least the part of me the pocket could tolerate.

It would not be born. It would precipitate.

It would appear the way condensation appears: because the conditions have finally allowed it. Its limbs would seem optional. Its skin would hold tiny points of light moving slowly beneath the surface, like sparks suspended in cold water. Where the light gathered, it would look almost translucent. Where it dried, it would look like crust, like stain, like a drawing the stone has not yet decided to keep. It would not move with confidence. It would move sideways, patiently, as if always deciding whether to approach or withdraw. It would not speak. It would not build with tools. It might, however, sort grains along a ledge: pale particles arranged into a line too regular to be accidental and too fragile to last. That line would be my first gesture. Not a message. Not a prayer, perhaps. A boundary. A way of saying: here is where I began to be possible.

I would be shy.

That surprises me, but it feels true. In this world, shyness is not weakness. It is a way of surviving attention. If something above shifted its weight, if a rootlet moved, if the ground remembered a footstep, the pocket’s light would flicker and I would grow still. I would press closer to the marl. My gill-films would close. I would become a stain, a crust, a postponed body. I would not die. I would only wait. The pocket would consume my urgency and leave behind the quieter part of me. When the air softened again, when moisture returned and the stone became receptive, I would unfold by degrees.

I would live by pulses.

In dry intervals, I would be almost nothing. A faint outline in the marl. A slight darkening where fungal filaments lace through the stone. A hollow in the floor that only appears when water teaches it to deepen. I might be mistaken for an old shed skin curling against the wall, except that even shed skins here do not remain long enough to be mistaken for anything. The pocket would reabsorb me. My edges would soften. My light would dim. I would become a condition rather than a creature. I would be postponed.

When dampness returned, I would remember myself again.

Not all at once. First as a trembling in the wall. Then as a thin brightening along a hairline fissure. Then as a bead of water that holds the chamber not as it is but as it might become. Then as the small pale form in the niche, gills spreading, head lifted toward the rootlets. I would feel the pocket open around me. The basins would widen by a finger’s breadth. The larvae would lower their threads. The mites would extend their shining trails. The amphipods would jerk through water that will not last. The beetle-like creature with the wet-paper shell would pause at the basin’s edge, antennae tasting the air. I would not stand apart from them. I would be the stillness among them, the sense that all these scattered lives are brushing against the same secret.

I would not be innocent in this ecosystem. I could not be. Even a listener must eat.

Some part of me would be hunger, but a quiet one. I might be the soft-bodied thing moving through a crevice no wider than a straw, hunting by vibration, leaving a wet line that shines for a moment before the stone drinks it away. Or I might be the pseudoscorpion beneath the clay crust, folded close, waiting without impatience. I would not experience predation as cruelty. It would feel like a small tightening of the world. A tremor. A light going out. The stone accepting the absence. If I were the one taken, I would not struggle loudly. I would become a transfer of moisture and mineral, a faint brightening somewhere else. Nothing here decays into ugliness. Even my disappearance would be clean.

But the deeper truth is that I would not be a single organ for long.

The pocket does not permit the arrogance of one body. It would distribute me. My thoughts would become the mites mapping the walls in chemical lines that fade almost as soon as they are made. My memories would become the fungal stains, branching like veins, like frost, like writing in a language the pocket has not yet learned. My urgency would live in the amphipods, whose whole existence passes inside a pool no wider than a hand. My patience would belong to the flat nymphs, their gills trembling even when no current moves. My hunger would be the long pale thread in the crevice. My wonder would rise with the springtails, a cloud of milk-white motes above the damp corners. My silence would be the wingless midges resting in sparse constellations, their glassy scales holding moisture and light.

I would be less a person than a set of pressures moving through these forms.

And still, there would be one place I return to: the niche beneath the rootlets.

There, the surface world touches the pocket without entering it. There, the ceiling remembers the weight of living things above. There, the pale moss whitens like a mineral thought. There, the temporary pools sometimes reflect things that are not present. I would gather near those reflections when the water is clear enough. I would not drink immediately. I would wait with the Marlborn, testing the image. The pool might show pale grass, an empty sky, a ceiling that is not this ceiling. It might show the place I came from, but emptied of me. It might show the pocket’s imagined future. It might show nothing but light behaving strangely. When the flat nymphs lower their gills and disturb the surface, the reflection would vanish, and I would feel relief. In this world, it is better not to be reflected too clearly.

If I had to say who I would be, I would say this:

I would be the Marlborn’s tentative sense of the outside.

Not the whole inhabitant, not the scattered nation of small lives itself, but the part that turns toward the pressure of roots and footsteps above. I would be the pocket’s listening organ. Its hesitation. Its surface-memory given temporary flesh. I would be the small pale form that appears when the air is full of mist but the first bead has not yet fallen. I would be the thing that becomes present when a visitor stops trying to own the place with their eyes. I would be the attention that remains after attention has learned to be gentle.

I would not be a maker. I would not cut, hammer, or manufacture. My structures would be made of instinct, secretion, repetition. A curved gallery of glued sediment, perhaps, no larger than a finger. A spiral case the size of a seed. A line of arranged grains along a ledge. Empty spheres wrapped in fibrous thread, abandoned in a shallow niche, already ancient-looking although they may have been made moments ago. These would be my works. The next pulse of water might erase them. I would make them again, slightly differently, without expecting permanence.

That is the strange freedom of being there: nothing remains long enough to become a monument, but everything returns often enough to become a habit.

When the moisture withdraws, I would withdraw with it. The walls would brighten. The salt crusts would tighten. The larvae would draw up their threads. The mites would slow. The temporary pools would shrink into mirrors, then into stains, then into nothing. I would become still and pale, easy to mistake for mineral. Perhaps I would be only a faint vein in the marl, a delicate branching mark like frost. Perhaps I would be the inside of an empty case, polished smooth by a body that has already moved on. Perhaps I would be the pressure inside a droplet that hangs too long in the air, holding the chamber as it might become rather than as it is.

Then, when the ceiling pores open again, I would remember what it is to be wanted by water.

The pocket would inhale. The light would gather. The rootlets would press closer. Somewhere above, the ground would remember a footstep, and the pocket’s brightness would flicker beneath the weight. I would not resent that weight. I would be changed by it. I would feel the surface not as a place to return to, but as a thickness above me, a skin over my becoming. I would be below it, inside it, made from its remembered pressure.

So where would I find myself?

In the first interior of a world that has not yet hardened into fact. In the bright, cool pause beneath the surface. In the shallow niche where condensation gathers and the rootlets hesitate. At the edge of a temporary pool that reflects a sky I can no longer reach. Between the mite’s trail and the stone that will forget it. Between the falling bead and the dry wall that drinks it. Between being noticed and being allowed to remain.

And who or what would I be?

A visitor reduced to listening. A surface echo given a body of marl, salt, and borrowed light. A temporary shape among the Marlborn. A shy, eyeless attention pressing itself to the wall when the world above shifts. A condition rather than a species. A pulse rather than a name. The pocket’s quiet question about the outside, asked in mineral breath and answered by water only long enough for the question to become beautiful.
