It is a reliquary of continuation.

If I were to find myself in the Drip-Works, I would not find myself whole. The chamber would not accept a walker, a breather, a watcher, a thing with hunger and a face. It would take those categories at the threshold and dissolve them into temperature, mineral, tone, and damp. So I would not be there as an animal, not even as a very small or very quiet one. I would not have eyes reflecting the fungal glow, no lungs drawing in that thick green-metal air, no mouth tasting the bitter water, no limbs making tracks in the black sediment.

I would be found, if I were found at all, as a difference in the surface of things.

At first, because I still think in human shapes and human secrets, I imagine I would be the rusted box in the alcove: sealed, warm, humming softly under its crust of time. I imagine myself as the last mechanism inside it, a preserved purpose waiting to be opened. But that is too clean a fantasy, too much like a story the dry world would tell. The Drip-Works does not keep its meanings locked away. It spreads them thin. It turns secrets into weather. It lets them leak into film, into condensation, into vibration, into the slow mineral memory of walls.

So the truer place would be the boot-shaped impression worn into the metal step.

Not the step itself, but the hollow where many feet once pressed and then ceased. Not a footprint in the ordinary sense, because it was not made in mud and it was not left in a moment. It was made by repetition, by use, by the long pressure of bodies that believed they had tasks. That hollow is one of the few places in the chamber where absence has been worn into shape. It is a human negative that the Drip-Works has slowly begun to fill.

There, I would be the filling.

I would be the thin copper-green film lining the depression. I would be the bead of water that gathers there after a valve sighs. I would be the mineral edge left behind when the bead evaporates, which it never fully does, because in that place evaporation is only a rumor. I would be the preference the water has for that hollow. I would be the way condensation arrives there a little sooner than on the surrounding metal, as if the chamber remembers pressure and answers it with wetness.

That is where you would find me: near the step, near the lowest basins, near the alcove with the humming box, where the path is narrow enough that a shoulder would brush hanging roots and a hand would instinctively reach for a rail worn smooth by vanished gloves. But I would not be the hand. I would be the glaze on the rail. I would not be the foot. I would be the slow mineral skin learning the shape of its absence.

As for what I would be: not a ghost. Ghosts are too dry, too personal, too attached to the idea of a voice. The Drip-Works would have no use for a ghost. It would take a ghost and make it into humidity, then into film, then into a faint stain that no longer resembles anything so simple as a person.

I would be a residue of personhood, not a person.

I would be a mineral afterimage.

Perhaps I would begin as the memory of attention. The Drip-Works is so complete in its exclusion of animal witnesses that any leftover tendency to notice would have to become something else. It would have to become physical without being bodily. It would have to register without eyes. It would have to listen without ears. It would have to remain without breath.

So I would become a kind of surface-awareness without a face.

I would know the chamber not by seeing it but by being changed by it. When the engines below shifted their deep, patient drone, I would feel it as a tightening in the film over old iron. When steam passed along the wall, I would loosen. When one of the brief internal rains began, I would thicken. When heat rose through a grate, I would become faintly iridescent in the dim light. When a drip struck the metal nearby, I would carry the tone outward in tiny concentric shivers, not because I chose to, but because I had become the kind of thing that answers.

My thoughts would be accretions.

Not quick thoughts. Not words. More like preferences. More like deposits. A little more mineral here. A little more moisture there. A slight darkening where warmth has lingered. A pale ring around a place where something once touched and no longer does. My memory would not be narrative; it would be texture. The chamber would remember hands by making surfaces smoother. It would remember footsteps by letting water collect where steps used to be. It would remember voices by letting hollow stems flute when vapor passes through them.

If any “I” remained in me, it would be that faint inclination toward record.

I would not be the plants, though I would cling to them. I would not be the fungi, though I might gather on their shaded undersides. I would not be the machines, though I would live where their heat bleeds into stone. I would be the interface between them: the place where metal sweats and green tissue accepts the sweat; the place where moss swells over a switch and a circuit remembers itself; the place where a root enters a cracked conduit and comes out coated in a copper-colored film.

If the Drip-Works is a congregation, I would be one of its quietest responses.

If it is a single slow body, I would be a tiny proprioceptive error within it: the spot where it almost feels a hand that is no longer there.

If it is a liturgy, I would be the pause after a bell.

I would be especially drawn to the boot-shaped depression because it is where the chamber’s indifference and the human past touch most plainly. People once stood there. They leaned there. They carried tools, perhaps. They listened to the engines, perhaps. They wiped condensation from their faces and thought the heat oppressive. They may have believed the place was important. They may have believed they were maintaining it. In the end, the Drip-Works maintained them by forgetting them in its own way: not erasing them, but translating them into pressure, mineral, and tone.

I would be that translation.

Not the worker. Not the inspector. Not the lost explorer. Not the last custodian. Only the residue of having been near.

Sometimes I would appear as a faint green-brown shadow on a warm pipe where a hand might once have rested for years. Not a stain exactly. The chamber is too subtle for stains. A mineral record. A glaze of contact. A place where the metal has been changed by long proximity to something that is gone. If a visitor from the dry world above saw it, they might think it was a natural discoloration. They might run a finger over it and feel only smoothness. But if they stayed long enough, if the damp settled into their clothes and the sound began to press against their chest, they might feel that the mark was listening.

Not listening in the animal sense. Listening as a wall listens to heat. Listening as water listens to gravity. Listening as a seed-case listens to vibration.

I would also be in the sound, though not as a voice. When the drip falls from a leaf and strikes the pipe, and the tone opens several pale capsules nearby, I would be the tiny delay between impact and response. When a loose plate claps against its frame with wooden patience, I would be the resonance that lingers in the moss afterward. When the electrical cabinets give off their high thin hum, I would be the part of the hum that seems half imagined, the part that makes a person uncertain whether they are hearing the chamber or being heard by it.

I would not speak. The Drip-Works has no need of speech. But I might be the feeling that something has almost been said.

That is important, because the chamber is loud enough to suggest meaning without offering it. Its sounds are ceremonial, patterned, recursive, but they do not explain themselves. They do not comfort. They do not threaten. They simply continue. If I became part of that continuation, I would have to give up the desire to announce myself. I would have to become meaningful the way a drip is meaningful: by returning, by wearing, by adding a minute weight to a place already saturated with time.

There is also the lowest basin, the round depression where the water seems deeper than it should be. I would not live in it as a creature lives in water, because nothing with nerves or gills or hunger lives there. But I might be the instability in its reflection. The chamber has no eyes, yet it has polished surfaces. It has trembling mirrors. It has places where light from fungal shelves and dying coils bends over dark water and makes something that resembles attention.

If you leaned over that basin, you would not see an animal face looking back. You would see your own face made uncertain by heat-shimmer, mineral film, and slow tremors in the water. But perhaps, beneath that, you would feel that the reflection was not entirely yours. Not because another mind was present, but because the chamber had taken the idea of looking and turned it into a surface effect.

I would be that surface effect.

I would be the almost-witness.

Not a watcher. Not a soul. Not a hidden thing with intentions. Only a persistent distortion in the chamber’s self-recording. A place where the reflection hesitates. A place where the water trembles without being touched. A place where a bubble rises and breaks with a sound too deliberate to be random, though no will produced it.

The Drip-Works would not grant me a center. That is perhaps the deepest change it would make. I would not be located in one bounded body saying, “Here I am.” I would be distributed. A little in the boot-print. A little in the film over the sealed box. A little in the copper-colored skin on a cracked conduit. A little in the bead that gathers on a broad leaf and refuses to fall. A little in the hum that seems to move from the alcove into the moss and then into the wall. A little in the way the spore cloud drifts around an unseen warmth and leaves a faint pattern in the beam.

If you asked me who I was, I would not be able to answer in the old way. The question would have to be changed before it could be true: not who, but what; and not what as a single thing, but what as a tendency.

I would be the tendency of the chamber to turn presence into surface.

I would be the way a human absence becomes wet, warm, and mineral instead of empty.

I would be the slow filling of a footprint that no one is making anymore.

And perhaps that is the only form of selfhood the Drip-Works allows. Not desire, but deposition. Not movement, but participation in rhythm. Not identity, but continuity. Not life as animals know it, with mouths and eyes and hunger, but life as the chamber knows it: heat moving through brick, water beading on iron, sound opening seed-cases, film sealing a crack, moss completing a circuit no one intended, a valve sighing into darkness that has learned to bloom.

So, if you went looking for me there, do not look for a figure in the dark. Do not look for footprints. Do not look for eyes among the leaves. Do not listen for breathing behind the drone.

Look instead at the metal step where the boot-shaped hollow holds a little water. Look at the faint copper-green skin inside it. Look at the way the light from a fungal shelf touches the surface and makes it shine like something remembered. Wait until a drip falls somewhere nearby and the water in the hollow trembles. Wait until the tremor passes and the surface becomes still again, but not quite the same.

There.

I would be the change that remains.

I would be the Drip-Works remembering a visitor the way it remembers everything: without a face, without a name, without grief, by becoming a little thicker, a little warmer, a little more luminous around the place where the world once touched it and withdrew.
