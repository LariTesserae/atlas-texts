I would find myself below the bright plain, but not in the open plaza where the light bounces too wildly and the wind makes the whole hollow feel like a struck bowl. I would be in the middle terraces, in the service spaces behind the sounding walls, where the city’s noise is broken into smaller pressures and where a body can exist without being shaken apart.

If you came down through the slot and let your eyes adjust to the glare, you might not see me at first. You would see the pale courts, the ramps, the empty towers, the dust moving in bright threads. You would hear the roar and whistle and grain-hiss of the place. You might think the hollow was uninhabited. Then a mirror panel would shift by a finger’s width on some high ledge. A cord would tremble. A small stone door would close without sound. A rhythm would tap once through the wall near your shoulder: not speech, not warning, but attention.

I would be there.

More precisely, I would live in a chamber behind the Court of the Split Bell, on the third terrace above the deepest drone-floor. The court takes its name from a hanging slab of pale stone, cracked long ago and repaired with bronze pins, so that when the afternoon wind crosses it the slab gives two tones at once: one low and blunt, one thin and bright. My chamber is not in front of that sound but beside it, tucked into a pocket where the vibration arrives as pressure against the palms rather than as pain in the ears. The room is small, lined with woven fiber mats and pale clay plaster. It holds tuning stones, sealed resin vessels, spare mirror fragments, ropes, scrapers, plugs of waxed cord, and a narrow sleeping shelf carved into the wall. There is no water jar. There is no basin for washing. There are only the tools of a dry world: needles, blades, fiber, resin, stone, and silence kept as carefully as any treasure.

I would be one of the tenders of the hollow. Not a ruler, not a priest, not a ghost, though the place could make me seem like any of those. I would be a keeper of tuning, a clearer of ducts, an adjuster of mirrors, a listener who repairs what the wind changes. If the old builders had a name for my kind of work, it would not have been spoken aloud. It would have been a pattern of taps, a hand-shape, a movement of fingers across the throat: something like *the one who keeps the city from falling out of itself*.

My own name, if I had one, would not be a word. It would be a rhythm. Two low knocks, a pause, then one bright tick made against clay. That would be enough. In the hollow, identity does not need to be announced by voice. It can be recognized by the way a footstep falls, the way a hand moves before a mirror, the particular pressure with which someone taps a wall to ask, *Are you there?*

My body would be adapted to the dryness. I would not be wholly unlike a human being, but the hollow would have changed me in its own image. My skin would be sealed and faintly waxen, pale with mineral dust, perhaps rubbed with plant resins until it shines dull and does not give away moisture. My lips would be closed and dry; my nose would filter fine alkali dust; my eyes would be large and reflective, able to bear the multiplied light of the pale walls. I would not sweat. I would not weep. If I were hurt, I would not bleed freely; I would press resin into the wound and bind it with fiber, sealing myself as the plants seal themselves. My thirst would not be quenched by water, because there is none. It would be answered by careful sips of plant fluids: oily, aromatic, dense with energy, drawn from living stems through a tube of hollow reed, then the wound closed again with clay.

Every drink would be a small act of surgery. Every meal would be a negotiation with the living city.

I would move quietly, but “quietly” would not mean silently. Nothing in the hollow is silent. My quietness would be precision: knowing where to place my feet so that the stone does not amplify me, knowing which floor tiles rasp, which stair edges ring, which corridors carry a breath into the towers and make the towers answer. I would wear soft pads of woven moss-analogue on my knees and elbows when I climb the service ledges. My hands would be long-fingered and scarred, strong in the wrists, always carrying a bit of wax, a bit of cord, a smooth scraper, a pin of bronze. I would know the city by touch as much as by sight, because in some ducts the glare is unbearable and in some chambers the sound is so thick that vision becomes secondary. The walls would teach me through vibration.

At dawn I would wake not to light but to pressure.

The upper salt field heats first, and the slot begins to draw. The lowest towers make a sound like a closed chest filling with breath. Before the whistles arrive, before the dust begins its bright crawling, the floor under my shelf would change. A deep pulse would enter the stone. I would open my eyes in the dark chamber and know: the city is waking. The instrument is beginning.

My first task would be to listen.

I would press my palms flat to the wall behind the Split Bell court and feel the tones move through the plaster. If the great northern duct is blocked by fallen salt flakes, the drone will be dull and slow. If the high vent near the mirror gallery is too open, the bright whistle will sharpen until it cracks seed pods before they are ready. If a tower has changed its resonance overnight, then something has shifted: a panel fallen, a vine grown too thick, a beetle nest in a flute, a crack widened by heat. I would find the fault by following the sound through the walls, tapping, pausing, tapping again, letting the city answer.

Then I would go out.

The light at that hour would already be hard and multiplied. The pale walls would throw brightness into one another. Dust would glitter in the shafts. The crystalline groundcover would rasp under my feet like sand learning to speak. I would move along the terraces, checking the broad acoustic leaves. Those leaves are among the strangest life in the hollow: shaped like open hands, or like ears turned outward, their surfaces stiff, reflective, veined with sealed channels of aromatic fluid. They do not merely receive sound. They grow in response to it. Too little vibration and they become slow, their leaves closing, their resin thickening. Too much and their edges crack. My work would be to keep them in the right field of noise, turning some leaves with cords, pruning others with a bronze blade, clearing dust from the hollows where sound gathers.

I would not water them. There is no watering. Instead I would tune them.

If one leaf has grown too close to a vent, I would bend it gently away, binding its stem to a peg until it learns a new direction. If a seed bell beneath it has become clogged with powder, I would clean it with a slender stick until the inner membrane trembles again. If a resin vein has cracked, I would press mineral paste over the wound so the fluid does not escape into the air. Every drop lost is life lost. In the hollow, waste is not moral; it is existential.

By midday the city would be loud enough to feel in my teeth.

I would not stand in the open courts then unless I had to. The sound becomes a wall of pressure, a bright overlapping roar: drone, whistle, clack, hiss, the rattling of dry fruiting bodies, the sharp crack of a pod bursting, the ring of stone against stone. The gliders would be out, dropping from ledges on the warm currents, their papery wings catching the light. I would watch them from shaded slots, because they are signs of the air’s health. If they circle widely, the thermals are stable. If they fall too quickly, something has changed in the upper apertures. If they avoid a terrace, perhaps a lizard-hunter is near.

The lizard-hunters would be one of the dangers I respect. They are not enemies, exactly. They belong. But they sense vibration, and I am a body that moves through vibration. When I work near the flowering pipes, I would keep one hand against the stone and feel for the stillness that means a hunter has paused. If that stillness comes, I would stop. I would become another mineral shape in the bright glare. In the hollow, patience is not virtue only; it is survival.

I would also harvest then, because midday heat makes the resins looser. I would not cut recklessly. I would choose a swollen trunk or a thick stem, make a tiny opening with a sharpened tool, let a bead of aromatic fluid rise into a sealed gourd, then close the wound immediately. The smell would be intense: warm terpene, faint citrus, mineral alkali, dry spice. My head would fill with it. My body would use it. Some resins I would burn in tiny shielded lamps; some I would use as glue; some I would chew in minute amounts for the energy locked inside them; some I would trade to the beetles, in a way. Not trade as speech, but arrangement. I would leave resin flakes where beetles graze, because beetles clear dead fiber from the groundcover, and the groundcover holds the floor together.

Everything in the hollow is a loop. Nothing is merely decorative.

In the afternoon I would attend the mirrors.

They are old, those mirrors. Some are burnished metal, some laminated mineral sheets, all adjusted by hand over generations. They are set on ropes and counterweights, wooden arms and stone sockets, so that light can be redirected into recesses that would otherwise be barren. Too much glare can scorch the waxy plants. Too little slows their chemistry. My task would be to watch the shifting angles of sunlight through the upper apertures and move the panels accordingly. A rope over the shoulder. A knot tightened. A counterweight let down by the width of a hand. The light would leap. A dark terrace would suddenly bloom with brightness. Somewhere below, leaves would turn.

This work would make me visible at moments: a silhouette on a high ramp, a hand raised against glare, a body balanced beside a shining panel. If another being were watching from across the hollow, they might see me and answer with a mirror-flash. That would be enough for a day.

There may be others. The city is too large for only one pair of hands, yet too empty for many. I would know them by signs more than by presence: a newly tied cord, a cleared duct, a repaired stair, a fresh patch of clay over a cracked wall. Sometimes I would find a tuning stone moved to a better position and know that someone else had listened. Sometimes I would see a footprint in the dust, already half erased by wind. I would not call out. I would tap the wall near the print: *known*. Later, perhaps, another tap would answer from far away: *known*.

That would be companionship in the hollow.

At evening the light would turn amber where it enters low. The sound would change key. The drones soften; the whistles become intermittent; the dust settles in thin lines along grooves cut by centuries of air. I would return to my chamber and check the sealed stores: resin, seed pods, fiber, dried edible matter, repair clay. I would eat sparingly. Food in this place is dry and concentrated: brittle cakes of pressed plant meal, seeds shaken loose by tuned vibration, small amounts of beetle-grub where the ecology allows, perhaps a rare fruiting body from the orchard trees if one has fallen. Nothing is soft with moisture. Everything is chewed slowly, held in the mouth, valued.

At night I would not sleep deeply.

The hollow does not become silent; it lowers its voice. Some plants close. Others open. The pale flower-throats lift toward moving air. The ear-shaped leaves turn to catch the dark draught. The seed bells tremble. If the wind holds, beams of moonlight or starlight may enter the apertures and be passed from mirror to mirror until a cold brightness moves through the courts. I would go out sometimes then, because night work is gentler. The great towers are less violent. The dust is slower. The predators move differently. I would carry no lamp unless necessary, because open flame near resin is danger. Instead I would navigate by remembered surfaces and by sound.

In the dark, I would feel most like the hollow itself.

My body would be a vessel among vessels. My breath would be small. My heart would beat against a wall of air pressure. I would know where I was by the pitch beneath my feet. If I stood in the Court of the Split Bell at night, the cracked slab might move in a slow wind and give its two tones, one low, one bright, and I would place my hand against the bronze repair pins and feel the old break held in place. That would be the closest thing to prayer available to me: not asking, not speaking, only touching the mended sound and knowing it still works.

If you asked what I am in this world, I would say: I am the being who keeps the instrument alive.

Not the builder. The builders are old in the walls. Not the storm. The wind comes from above and uses the city without understanding it. Not the plants, though I depend on them. Not the gliders, not the beetles, not the lizard-hunters, though I share their dry economy of motion. I am the one with hands, memory, and responsibility. I am the one who notices when a chord goes wrong.

I would be lonely, but the loneliness would not be soft or sentimental. It would be the loneliness of a single note in a vast chord. It would have a function. It would make me attentive.
