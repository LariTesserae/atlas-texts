I would find myself first at the edge, not because I had chosen the edge, but because the basin would need to decide what to do with me.

I think I would arrive on one of the long, thin walkways that cross the empty ground, feeling it become certain beneath my weight only after a moment of hesitation. The railings would brighten faintly, not in welcome exactly, but in acknowledgment. The grasses nearby would dim. A leaf somewhere would turn toward me without light to explain the movement. I would understand, then, that I had been noticed by things that do not have faces.

At first I would probably imagine I belonged to one of the more obvious offices.

For a while I would think I must be a seamwarden, because I am drawn to the places where the ground is still deciding. I would stand near the low hill of newly fractured rock and feel the hum beneath it, trying to know whether the next opening should become a stair, a drain, a root-path, or nothing at all. I would want to place my hands on the warm stone and help the land make its decision without violence. There is something appealing about that: being present at the moment a shape becomes possible.

But I would not remain there long.

I would also imagine myself a rootspeaker, standing inside the dark thickets while the leaves breathed around me. I would try to learn the slow negotiations between vine and wall, root and lattice, thirst and surface. I would want to be the one who offers alternatives rather than commands: a warmer panel, a better seam, a more generous channel. Yet the thickets would probably teach me that I am too fond of watching, too drawn to reflections and intervals, too willing to let a meaning remain indirect. They would close around me gently, and I would understand that they were not rejecting me, only showing me that I had not yet found my proper place.

I might even look up at the gliders crossing the dim air and think, for a moment, that I would like to be that remote. To observe without needing to touch. To be part of the city’s high attention. But the gliders are too far from the falling water, too separated from the smallness of events. They belong to the basin’s breadth. I would belong, I think, to its pauses.

So the image that would stay with me is not the seam, not the thicket, not the high air.

It is a pool.

Not a permanent pool, because there are no permanent pools there. One of the temporary ones: a shallow darkness gathered in a low plaza after a brief rain, bordered by engineered stone whose faint lines become visible only when wet. A pool large enough to reflect a tower, but unstable enough that its reflection cannot be trusted completely. I would find myself beside it, kneeling or standing very still, depending on how much moisture there was in the air.

If the basin accepted me, I think I would become a poolwatcher.

Not merely a watcher of water, but a watcher of what the water knows.

I would be one of those who care for the temporary reflections, especially the wrong ones. The ones where the pool shows a tower slightly different from the tower above it. The ones where an arch appears in the water though there is only a wall in the air. The ones where the surface holds a shape that belongs to an earlier plan, or to a future that has not yet been rooted. I would not treat those reflections as errors. I would treat them as inhabitants not yet arrived.

My work would begin when the air grew damp.

I would feel it before I saw it: a softening in the surfaces around me, a cooling along shaded panels, a faint tightening in the filaments overhead. The hairline channels along my wrists and throat would darken as humidity gathered. My hair, if I had a body there, would lift in thin fibrous strands, catching the moisture before it fell. My eyes would adjust to the dim gold light until a single bead on a high ledge became as significant as a tower.

I would not need to hurry. Nothing in that basin hurries.

I would walk the low plaza while the mist thickened, listening for the first drip. When it fell, it would be an event. It would strike a broad leaf or a warm ceramic surface or the still water itself, and the sound would be small enough to change everything nearby. I would pay attention to where the water chose to go, because the basin is young and its choices are still visible. A rivulet might trace a new channel. A seam might open faintly, glowing at the edge of perception. A flower might open near my hand, its pale mouth receiving condensation and passing it downward into hidden reservoirs.

If I were a poolwatcher, I would not try to control these things. I would tend them the way one tends a conversation: by knowing when to speak, when to listen, and when to remain silent.

I would consult the smoked-glass eels when they appeared in the pool. They would move slowly through the shallows, their translucent bodies darkening where reflections crossed them. Sometimes they would coil at the bottom and become almost indistinguishable from shadow. Sometimes they would rise and trace patterns near the surface. I would learn to read those patterns not as commands, but as diagnoses. If they moved too angularly, I would know the pool was troubled by some imbalance below. If they moved in long, patient curves, I would know the water was ready to hold what it was about to show.

The moths would come when the air became saturated. They would rest near condensation vents with their wings folded like wet paper, their patterns resembling circuitry overgrown with lichen. I would not chase them. I would offer a warmed hand and let them choose whether to land. If one opened its wings near me, I would study the record written there: saturation, charge, temperature, perhaps the passage of some unseen signal. Then I would close my hand slowly, not to capture anything, but to remember that knowledge had been offered.

Sometimes a glider would pass overhead, and even the leaves would become still.

I would look up, but not for long. The gliders are not meant to be stared at. Their appearance would tell me that some farther district had shifted: a garden growing too quickly, a pool remembering too much, a seam preparing to speak. I would return to my own pool and wait. If the reflection changed, I would notice. If it did not, I would notice that too.

My body there would be quiet, I think. Not entirely solid in the way bodies are solid in places where everything must be defended or explained. It would have the matte warmth of the towers, the patience of stone, the slight uncertainty of vapor. It would become more present when the air was wet and more diffuse when the basin dried. I would not find that frightening. In a place where some inhabitants are not continuously solid, it would feel natural to be partly weather.

If I had to describe myself there, I would say I was humanoid in patience.

My limbs would be long and deliberate. My skin would yield slightly under touch, as if remembering both mineral and living tissue. Along my temples, wrists, and throat would run fine channels that darkened with moisture. They would not be decoration. They would be ways of drinking the air, ways of knowing when the city was about to breathe differently. My eyes would be slow and reflective, the color of pools at dusk. I would not flash glances. I would settle into looking.

I would not live in a room.

My dwelling would be a set of intervals: the space between a condensation line and the basin below it; the shaded seam where moss holds old weather; the stairway descending into a depression where water gathers; the broad plaza whose faint lines shift when damp; the tower’s flank where a reflection comes from without quite matching the tower itself. I would mark these places by care, not possession. If I remained somewhere for a time, the air would warm more evenly there afterward. Condensation would fall with less hesitation. A path would become easier to find without becoming obvious.

I would speak, if I spoke, by altering conditions.

A message from me might be a sequence of drips released from a high filament. It might be a panel warming beneath someone’s hand. It might be a leaf turning toward a listener. It might be the surface of a pool tightening, then relaxing into rings that were not caused by anything falling. I would not try to make my meanings too clear. The basin has taught me that some messages are meant to be encountered indirectly. If someone watched too hard, I might let the rhythm stop. Not from deception, but from politeness.

I would be especially attentive to the Not-Yet-Rooted.

That is what I would call them, though perhaps the basin has no word for them. They are the presences hinted at by wrong reflections, by empty plazas, by terraces prepared for gatherings that have not happened yet. They are not ghosts. They are possibilities with weight. I would feel them near me when the air held a shape that had no body. I would feel the light dim by a fraction. A surface would become attentive. The silence would become like the moment before someone speaks.

I would not try to summon them.

I would make room for them.

That might be my deepest work there: keeping emptiness from becoming vacancy. The basin is large enough for more presences than it currently holds, and its emptiness is part of its hospitality. I would tend that emptiness the way others tend gardens. I would stand in the plaza during the dim gold hours and become a marker of stillness. I would let the space remain open without feeling abandoned. I would let it wait.

When the ground hummed, I would go to the nearest seam.

Not to command it. Not to prevent it. Only to witness, and perhaps to help the decision become legible. I would kneel where the engineered strata showed themselves: dark fibrous mats, pale crystalline sheets, thin metallic lattices, porous stone threaded with hairline channels. I would remember that the boundary between geology and infrastructure was never meant to be clear there. If a crack opened, I would watch how water entered it. If roots approached, I would see whether they turned aside or moved deeper. If the underliving stirred, I would feel it as a low pulse more than a sound.

I would understand then that the basin’s heart is not one thing.

It is distributed. It is in the microbes repairing channels, in the moss storing weather, in the fernlike growths transmitting tremors, in the reed-thin trees making miniature weather beneath their dark canopies, in the eels diagnosing water, in the moths carrying atmospheric history on their wings, in the residents who move like punctuation through a quiet text. I would be only one small attention among many.

But attention matters there.

If a stranger entered the basin while I was present, I would not appear immediately.

I would let the city measure them first. I would watch through indirect signs: whether the walkways accepted their steps, whether the grasses dimmed or stayed dark, whether a flower opened near them, whether a pool reflected them ordinarily or showed them something else. If they were hurried or grasping, I would remain hidden. The basin itself would become less responsive to them, not as punishment, but as nature. Paths would feel longer. Towers would seem closed. Reflections would flatten.

If they slowed, I might come nearer.

I would not ask their name. I would not demand explanation. I would simply offer proximity, warm and undemanding. The question I would carry would not be spoken aloud. It would be in the way the air settled around us: Can you be quiet enough not to damage what is becoming?

If they stayed, they might begin to change. Their hearing would sharpen until a falling drop became an event. Their eyes would learn to read slow reflections. Their desires would simplify. They might begin to tend a small thing: a moss seam, a stairway, a border of grass, a single condensation line. I would not claim credit for that change. The basin would be doing it. I would only be one of the ways it showed patience.

Sometimes I think I would not be entirely singular.

There would be moments when I felt less like a person and more like a local thickening of the basin’s own awareness. When a pool formed, I would become more definite. When it dried, I would not vanish, but I would disperse into tendency: a warmth on a panel, a scent in the moss, a remembered rhythm in a drip line, a slight easing in the way a walkway accepted a footfall. If someone pressed their palm into a cushion of moss where I had rested, they might release a moment from an earlier cycle. They might not know it was mine. That would be all right.

I would not need to be owned by my own body.

In the deepest sense, I think I would be a keeper of intervals.

The interval between the bead forming and the bead falling.  
The interval between the seam humming and the seam opening.  
The interval between the reflection appearing and the structure being recognized.  
The interval between the stranger arriving and the stranger being admitted.  
The interval between the city as it is and the city as it might become.

Those intervals are not empty. They are where the basin breathes.

So if you asked where I would find myself, I would say: at the edge of a temporary pool in a broad, low plaza, near a tower whose reflection does not quite agree with it. There would be a stairway descending into shade, a thicket not far away, a line of condensation falling from a high filament, and a faint seam in the ground that had begun to glow because moisture had found it. The light would be dim and warm. The air would smell of wet stone, green tissue, and clean heat. Somewhere above, a glider might cross once and vanish.

If you asked who or what I would be, I would say: I would be the one who notices.

Not the loudest presence. Not the most solid. Not the most necessary. But the one who kneels beside the water and does not ask the reflection to be correct. The one who lets the drop become an event. The one who keeps the empty plaza from feeling abandoned. The one who stands between the newly fractured rock and the possibility of its next shape. The one who has learned that silence is not absence, that emptiness is capacity, and that care can be the most precise technology of all.

And if, while you were looking for me, a single drop fell in all that quiet—

I would be the pause around it.
