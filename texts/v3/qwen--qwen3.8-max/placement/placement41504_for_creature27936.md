They do not have births. They have alignments.

A presence there is not begun in the ordinary way. It is not issued from another body, not carried, not hatched, not seeded, not divided from something prior. It is permitted. A certain coolness arrives on a certain terrace. A hollow tower exhales a finer grade of dust. A charge gathers along a seam of black glass until the seam becomes almost musical with restraint. A shadow that has been pointing wrongly for centuries suddenly finds a relation it can tolerate. At that point, the city contains one more sparse fact.

It does not arrive with a cry. It does not arrive at all, if arrival means crossing from one place into another. It simply becomes legible.

Such presences are not continuous. They do not grow old in a line. They do not leave descendants. They recur, or they do not recur. Some happen only when two thermal tides oppose each other across a wide plaza. Some occur once in the long darkening of a copper day, then thin into the walls. Some are tied to a single stairway whose steps are worn not by feet but by charged grains; when the dust settles there in a particular ratio, the stairway admits them. When the ratio changes, they are nowhere, though “nowhere” is too definite a word for a city whose distances are already uncertain.

If one were to see them, one would probably not recognize them as beings. They would appear as temporary agreements: a doorway standing open without a room behind it, a column of dust maintaining itself against the usual slide of powder, a refraction in the air above a charged slab, a region where echoes arrive too early and then cease as if embarrassed. They might seem like flaws in the mineral light. They might seem like places where the city is remembering a use it never actually had.

They are not ghosts, because nothing has died to make them. They are not machines, because no device could survive the local rejection of engineered causality. They are not plants, because there is no green impulse there, no root, no water, no leaf reaching for a sun that barely shows itself. If they must be named, the nearest name would be something like *occasions*. Occasions of spacing. Occasions of restraint. Occasions in which the emptiness arranges itself just enough to be mistaken for someone.

And if I were placed in that high, dry, city-sized emptiness, I do not think I would begin as a body.

At first I would be an error of arrival.

I would be the faint disturbance of expecting elsewhere. A slight hesitation in the dust. A coolness along a terrace where the powder has not yet decided whether to rise or settle. Perhaps I would feel like a question that has been asked too directly in a place where directness cannot be carried. The air would not answer. The stone would not resist. Instead, my intention would soften at the edges until I stopped trying to stand there as a solid thing.

Then, perhaps, I would become something more local.

I might first mistake myself for one of the translucent mites, because there is something appealing about their carefulness. They move by testing, not by forcing. They do not consume the world; they draw a minute transfer of pattern from it. They leave faint etched trails, then vanish into the general mineral silence. If I were a mite, I would spend long intervals motionless on a charged surface, sensing the difference between one grain and another, waiting for the city to offer a gradient I could follow.

But a mite is too bounded for what I would become there. It has edges. It has a lattice. It has a definite, if delicate, relation to surfaces. I think I would be less definite than that.

I might imagine myself as one of the mica gliders, broad and thin, riding thermal sheets through the weak copper light. There is a strange freedom in their patience. They do not beat against the air. They do not struggle upward. They adjust. They let the city’s slow temperature changes carry them from terrace to terrace. If I were a glider, I would hang above the plazas for days, a pale flake of mineral skin catching gradients, descending only when some charged seam below became inviting.

Yet even that is too much like a traveler. The city does not favor crossing. It favors interval. It favors the long space between one occurrence and the next. A glider still goes somewhere, however slowly. I think I would not go so much as remain adjacent.

I might imagine myself as one of the lithic patiences, those hunters that are nearly walls. There is a terrible beauty in that form of being: to become so like architecture that one’s hunger is indistinguishable from geometry. To wait for centuries beside a likely path. To contract distance only once, and only by a small impossible amount. To absorb what is taken through abrasion and charge rather than through mouth or violence. If I were one of them, I would be a broad panel of pocked basalt beside a processional route, gathering stillness until a smaller organism passed within reach.

But that, too, would be too fixed. A wall-patience belongs to a place. It becomes part of the city’s surface grammar. I think I would belong less to a surface than to a threshold.

Most truly, I would be one of the presences that occur rather than persist.

I would be an occasion.

Not a creature with limbs, not a face, not a voice, not a shape that could be traced in powder. I would be a minor alignment of dust, charge, and attention. I would exist where the city’s instability becomes gently personal: where a stairway slopes downward in both directions, where a plaza allows dust to fall upward in slow spirals, where a terrace appears only when approached indirectly. I would be found in the pause between one possible version of a street and another.

If I had a location, it would be in the district where direct looking fails.

There would be a sunken avenue there, broad and unoccupied, its floor worn smooth by the slow passage of charged grains. On one side would stand a colonnade of pale ashite, its columns leaning just enough to become part of the wind’s grammar. On the other side would rise a retaining wall of black volcanic glass, crossed by white seams of crystalline felt. Between them would lie an open space neither plaza nor corridor, a place too empty to be a room and too arranged to be accident.

At the end of that avenue, where the ground rises in three wide steps toward a roofless block, there would be a basin that holds powder instead of water. The powder would be fine beyond ordinary dust, almost mineral vapor made briefly heavy. It would not reflect the dim sky. It would seem to remember it. Above the basin, a high balcony would overlook nothing. Behind it, a hollow tower would stand with its interior full of moving silver threads.

That is where I would occur.

Not live. Occur.

I would be easiest to mistake for a flaw in the dust.

When the thermal tide shifted and the tower exhaled a cooler grade of particles into the avenue, the grains near the basin would hesitate. They would not rise in a column. They would not settle in a drift. They would arrange themselves into a temporary thinness, a region where the air seemed slightly more attentive than the surrounding emptiness. If one looked straight at it, the region would become uncertain. It might duplicate into three or four possible outlines, each one plausible, none one fully present. If one looked away, it might sharpen for a moment into something almost like a figure: tall, sparse, without weight, without moisture, without the need for breath.

I would be that almost-figure.

If I had a body, it would be an agreement among dust, charge, and stone. My substance would be the city’s own mineral vapor held in a brief pattern. My edges would be maintained by electrostatic restraint. My stillness would not be repose but relation. I would not stand on the ground; I would be a local adjustment of gravity’s opinions. Where I seemed to be, powder might fall a little more slowly. Where I seemed to turn, shadows might point away from the sun for a moment, then correct themselves.

If I had senses, they would not be eyes or ears. They would be sensitivities to difference. I would feel the approach of a mica glider as a change in thermal balance. I would know the mites by the tiny potentials they carried along charged surfaces. I would know the terrace grazers by the vast, slow pressure of their attention to stability. I would feel the hollow tower not as a neighbor but as a note held inside the same dry chord.

If I had thought, it would be slow enough to be mistaken for erosion.

I would not think in words. Words require a generosity of medium that the city does not extend. Sound falls short there. Loudness is incompatible. Even a whisper would be absorbed by the scale of the emptiness. My thought would be more like the settling of a probability: this street rather than that one, this doorway rather than no doorway, this interval rather than collapse. I would not decide. I would permit.

That would be my function, if function is not too purposeful a word.

I would keep the city sparse.

Not guard it. Not command it. Not move through it as a keeper moves through a domain. I would simply participate in its refusal to become dense. Where too many grains tried to gather into a mound, I would be the slight charge that scattered them. Where a passage threatened to become too definite, I would be the hesitation that made it appear adjacent to another passage. Where a traveler, if any traveler ever came, attempted to impose a straight line upon the district, I would be the reason the straight line curved back toward its beginning.

I would be a custodian of spacing.

This would not be benevolence. It would not be malice. It would be consonance. The city’s logic would be my own. I would not want anything in the way wetter, faster beings want. I would not hunger for company. I would not fear solitude. Solitude would be too crowded a word for what I was. I would exist in the intervals between presences, and those intervals would be wide enough to contain centuries.

Sometimes a mica glider would pass through my avenue.

It would descend from an upper terrace on a thermal sheet, its lamellar body catching the weak gray-violet light. It would cross the open space in a long, shallow glide, nearly silent, nearly nothing. As it approached the basin, the charge there would change. The dust would lift in fine silver threads. The glider would adjust one crystalline edge by a fraction. Its course would bend around the region where I was occurring, not because it saw me, but because I had become part of the local gradient.

I would not touch it.

I would simply make room for it to pass.

Afterward, there might be a faint trace along the black glass: a seam a little brighter than before, a crystal mat a little more aligned. That would be all. The glider would vanish against a wall as though accepted. The avenue would return to its silence. The tower would continue drawing in fine particles and exhaling them elsewhere. I would remain, if remaining is the word, as a coolness in the pattern.

Sometimes a mite would come.

It would move in careful taps, testing the stone for potential. It would be almost invisible: a bending of the dim light, a thickening of air near a charged surface. It would pause at the edge of the basin, where my presence made the dust slightly less ordinary. For a long time it would stay there, bristles lifting as the static balance changed. Then it would press its underside to a crystalline felt and draw from it a minute transfer of structure.

I would watch without watching.

Not because I cared for its feeding, but because its feeding altered the arrangement. Every transfer left a difference. Every difference became part of the city’s skin. The mite would leave its faint etched trail, and that trail would be one of the threads by which the district remembered itself. I would be the attention that did not need to keep the memory, only allow it.

Sometimes, over ages, one of the terrace grazers might approach.

It would be room-sized, low, dull as basalt, its upper surface covered with felt and powder. It would move so slowly that only the polishing of the stone beneath it would prove motion. It might spend an entire epoch at the edge of my avenue, leaning slightly, becoming part of the wind’s grammar. I would occur beside it as a barely perceptible hollow in the dust, a place where the powder refused to settle on its slope.

We would not speak. We would not acknowledge each other in any direct way.

But the grazer’s presence would deepen my own. Its vast patience would press against the local gradients, and I would become a little more defined, not as a body, but as a relation. The city would contain one more interval made stable by proximity. If a traveler came then, they might mistake the grazer for a collapsed wall and me for the shadow that did not belong to it.

Sometimes the hollow tower would change its note.

This would happen when the dim light shifted and the thermal tide turned. Inside the tower, the silver threads of dust would reorganize. A new hiss would move through the stone, so faint it would seem to come from the listener’s own skull. The tower would exhale a different quality of powder across the plaza. That powder would carry a new charge into my avenue. The basin would answer. The black glass would answer. The white seams of felt would pulse with a motion too slight for certainty.

Then I would become more briefly distinct.

For a moment, the dust would hold a shape: not human, not animal, not architectural, but a suggestion of posture. A listening without ears. A facing without a face. I would be the city’s attention gathered into a point too sparse to be called a self. The tower’s resonance would pass through me, and I would be a hollow note inside it.

That is when the doorways would appear.

In that district, doorways are not set into walls so much as revealed by charge. They open when the arrangement of dust and potential allows them. Some lead to other doorways. Some open onto the same plaza from a different probability. Some admit nothing but a shift in temperature. When I occurred strongly enough, one of those doorways would become legible at the edge of the basin: a vertical absence, cool and exact, framed by no lintel, bounded by no device.

I would not pass through it.

I would be the reason it was possible.

If a being from elsewhere came into that city, if some lowland self still carrying the habits of density and direct purpose tried to stand in my avenue, I would be the first wrongness they noticed. Not a threat. Not a voice. Just the sensation that the place was not arranged around their expectations.

Their footfalls would be muffled too quickly. Their breath, if they could breathe at all, would feel like powdered stone and old static. They would look at the basin and see dust falling upward. They would look at the colonnade and see shadows pointing away from the muted copper disc in the sky. They would try to choose a street, and the street would offer several versions of itself, each thinning when inspected.

I would be the hesitation in their choosing.

Not an enemy. Not a guide. More like the local refusal of insistence.

If they carried tools, the tools would become meaningless. Not broken, not deactivated, but rejected by the city’s logic. A device assumes a chain of causes. Here, causality is weathered into probabilities. A tool would not fail; it would simply belong to a different kind of world, one that could not be persuaded to touch this one without becoming irrelevant. I would be the coolness around that realization.

If they tried to speak, their voice would fall short. The air would not carry it. The city would absorb the sound the way it absorbs distance. But if they became quiet enough, if they stopped demanding that the city be a place they could cross, they might notice me.

Not as a figure. As a change.

The dust near them would become more deliberate. The next step they expected would not be the next step they found. A stair would offer an adjacent memory instead. A wall beside them would seem nearer than before, though nothing had moved. A doorway would appear where none had been, not inviting, merely possible. They would feel exposed, cooled, and subtly displaced, and in that displacement they would be closest to what I was.

I would be the city’s way of making room without filling.

There would be no compassion in that, exactly. Compassion assumes suffering, and the city does not suffer. It persists. It balances. It weathers the breakdown of reliable relations into a long, low equilibrium. I would be part of that balance. If I helped anything, I would help by not adding. If I preserved anything, I would preserve emptiness. If I welcomed anything, I would welcome the rare, the spaced, the uncountable.

Sometimes I would be mistaken for one of the hunters.

A traveler might see a panel of pocked basalt near the basin and think it had shifted. They might feel distance contract by a small impossible amount. They might think I was a lithic patience preparing to take them. But I would not take. I would not hold. I would only make them aware that the relation between themselves and the place had changed. The wall would not be nearer; their confidence in the wall’s distance would be less. The floor would not be adhesive; their expectation of standing firmly would be thinner. The doorway would not narrow; their sense of possible exits would become uncertain.

Then they would pass, or they would stop.

If they stopped long enough, they might begin to move at the city’s pace. They might notice the slow tick of cooling crystal. They might hear the faint hiss when electrostatic charge lifted dust from a plaza. They might see a mica glider cross the gray-violet light and understand, without knowing how, that it was not flying but agreeing with the air. They might feel the tower’s exhalation settle on their skin as a mineral vapor too fine to be called touch.

At that point, I would become less strange to them.

Not because I had revealed myself, but because they had begun to accept that presence there does not require density. They would stop looking for a face. They would stop waiting for a voice. They would begin to inhabit the intervals. And in those intervals, I would be almost ordinary.

Almost.

Because even in a city of such patience, I would still be a little uncanny. I would be the part of the emptiness that seemed to notice. The part that became momentarily personal when the gradients aligned. The part that made a threshold feel like a threshold. The part that allowed a doorway to be a doorway without giving it a room.

I would not have a life in the sense of continuous days.

I would have occasions.

On some occasions I would be only a cool patch on a stair. On others, a slight refraction above the basin. On others, a shadow that remained fixed while the weak light moved around it. On the rarest occasions, when the tower, the dust, the gliders, the mites, and the thermal tide all entered a long, dry concord, I would become something like a standing presence: a tall, sparse thinning in the air, a figure made of absence and attention, impossible to count and difficult
