I would not find myself in the center.

The central emptiness of the hollow is not for bodies. It is for water, for sound, for the long passage of temperature, for the chamber’s own vast breathing. A self standing there would be too singular, too exposed, too much like an interruption. So if I were to belong to that place, I would be near the edge, where the built surfaces climb in thin layers and the dark is broken only by the faintest living smears along wet seams.

More precisely: I would find myself in the narrow chamber set into the wall above the dry overhang.

Not in the wettest district, where condensation runs constantly and stone has become soft-lipped and treacherous. Not in the deepest basin, where the temporary rivers gather and the old translucent thing rests in its permanent pool. Not high among the exposed galleries where the air thins and the acoustic fields dart sharp signals back and forth. I would be halfway up, or a little above, in one of those recessed cells that could once have been a dwelling, a control niche, a sensor hive, or a shrine to some obsolete operational saint. The floor there would be worn smooth by long use, though I could not say whether I wore it smooth or whether it had already been smooth before I became possible.

From that niche I would feel the hollow more than see it.

I would know the lower basin by the change in air pressure before a pulse. I would know the condensation wall by the slight coolness drawing across my skin. I would know the nearby ladder by the particular hollowness of its third rung, the one that has been loosened by centuries of heat and moisture and now gives a faint metallic complaint when touched. I would know the central void not by looking into it, but by the way echoes leave my smallest movements and return altered, as if the emptiness had tasted them and found them minor.

At first, because I still think too much in terms of bodies, I imagine myself standing there as a person. Pale. Thin. Barefoot. Eyes wide and light-hungry, though light is almost nothing there. Skin carrying the grayish warmth of old mineral deposits. Hair perhaps thin or absent, because hair is a nuisance in damp machinery. Hands accustomed to railings, to crusted valves, to the slickness of walls that have sweated for longer than memory.

But the longer I remain in the thought, the less I seem entirely a person.

I would be the almost-person.

The remainder. The custodian without authority. The biological echo of an urban order that no longer requires citizens. Not a ruler of the hollow, not an engineer in any commanding sense, not even truly a keeper of the machine, because the machine keeps itself. I would be closer to a habit the chamber has acquired. A small local sensitivity. A place where the settlement’s vast distributed attention can narrow itself into fingers, feet, breath, and pause.

I would not carry a light. Light would be foreign to me, perhaps even offensive. I would move by familiarity, by texture, by echo, by the grammar of vibration. My feet would read the walkways better than my eyes. I would know when a grating is about to hum because the metal under my sole would change pitch a fraction before the sound arrived. I would know when the ceiling was about to sweat because the air would thicken along my forearms and the faintest scent of wet chalk would rise from stone that had been dry moments before.

I would wear little. Perhaps a covering stiffened by mineral crust, patched with woven fungal fiber or scraped membrane from the old filtration reeds. Not for modesty, not for ornament, but because certain surfaces are cold when the chamber exhales upward, and because the sharp edges of some maintenance shafts have not been softened by use in a long time. My body would smell of iron, warm dust, wet stone, and the faint vinegar acidity of black lichens. I would not smell clean in any surface sense. I would smell settled.

Who would I be?

Not someone with a name, I think. Names feel unnecessary there. The hollow does not call out. It does not gather people into address. If I had a designation, it would be functional, and even that would have worn away like old paneling. Perhaps the system once referred to my niche as a maintenance point, a biological interface, an emergency custodian node. Perhaps the walls still carry some faint instruction toward me, the way a body remembers a reflex long after the injury has healed. But I would not experience myself as an office or a function. I would experience myself as attention.

I would be the one who listens at valves.

Not because I can command them, but because listening is a form of participation. When a pressure change moves through the lower pipes, I would press my hand against the nearest dark metal and feel the bloom of warmth beneath the mineral crust. I would wait for the thump of release. If the sound came too late, I would not know alarm exactly, but some part of me would tighten. If it came too early, I would become still. If it came at the right time, I would relax so slightly that the relaxation itself might be the closest thing I have to pleasure.

My acts of maintenance would be small.

I would clear a blocked drip channel with a slender tool made from a piece of old ceramic. I would remove a flake of crust from a railing where it might catch moisture incorrectly. I would press my ear to a wall where the stone had grown warmer than usual and listen for the high faint signal of capillary lattices moving water upward. I would touch a seam where fungal threads glisten after a droplet falls, not because I understand the signal, but because touching it confirms that the wall is threaded, that the chamber is connected, that I am not alone in the way a single object is alone.

Sometimes I would do nothing that matters.

I would move a fragment of mineral from one place to another simply because my hands need occupation. I would trace the worn edge of an ancient basin. I would sit in the niche and let my breathing slow until it aligned with the chamber’s thermal cycle. I would open my mouth during the loudest mechanical phases as though to speak, but no sound would come. Or if it did, the hollow would swallow it before it became meaningful.

That would not be tragic.

Tragedy requires a sense of loss. I would not have the loss of language. I would have only the use of silence.

If the hollow is a town, then I would be its smallest population. When the rain comes, I would become, briefly, a citizen.

I would know the beginning of rain before the first drop fell. The ceiling pores would open with a sound too fine for ordinary hearing, but not for mine. The air would change texture. The black lichens on the warm pipes would sharpen their acidic scent. The reedlike filaments in the gutters would lift. Somewhere below, the amphipods would stir in their resting stages, sensing that the world was about to become briefly possible.

Then the drops would begin.

Sparse at first. A single bead falling from a ceramic lip. Another striking a hollow rail. A third whispering through a grating and vanishing. I would leave my niche and move along the ledge to a place where the spray could reach me without collecting too heavily. Not because I need the water directly, but because being touched by the rain makes me part of the event. My skin would darken with moisture. The mineral crust on my covering would gleam faintly. My eyes would widen, though there would be almost nothing to see.

The strangeness would still happen: the rain hesitating.

Droplets would slow in the air. They would gather along invisible acoustic nodes, trembling, aligned, held in a pattern too precise to be accidental. The first time—if there could be a first time in such a place—it might have seemed like violation. Now it would feel like the chamber drawing breath. I would stand very still beneath the suspended water, not worshiping it, not fearing it, but recognizing it. The system was thinking. The town was thinking. Its thought was made of pressure, sound, and moisture.

Then the field would shift, and the drops would fall all at once.

In that moment, I would feel most real.

Not happy. Happiness is too broad and bright a word. But coherent. Integrated. I would be a living point inside a larger event. The hollow would not be empty then. It would be crossed by falling water, by scent, by animal urgency, by the small awakenings of moss and worm and crustacean. The center would become a space through which everything passed. I would be one minor recipient of that passing. My body would be a surface among surfaces, briefly wet, briefly included.

When the deeper pulse came, I would retreat to the safer galleries.

The hidden conduit below would open, and the temporary river would move through the lower basin. The sound would be immense in the darkness: not a roar, but a sliding, sliding pressure, water over stone, water filling trenches, water finding every old channel it remembered. The air would grow heavier. The faint glow inside the moving water would pass like a weak thought through the dark. I would listen from above, one hand on the railing, feeling the vibration climb the wall.

I would not approach the predator.

I would know it by absence. A stillness inside the ordinary stillness. A thinning of small sounds. A ripple where no drip had fallen. The amphipods would vanish. The worms would slow. The wingless insects near the fungal ledges would become practiced disappearance. I would become still too. Not because I believed the predator would hunt me—perhaps it would not—but because its presence marks a boundary. It is one of the hollow’s reminders that life remains sparse by necessity. I would respect that boundary the way one respects a live wire or a sleeping mechanism.

After the pulse, when the pools formed, I might descend carefully to the edge of a shallow basin. I would not disturb the water. I would crouch and touch the stone beside it, feeling the cold trapped there. If the old translucent creature moved in its deeper pool, I would only sense a faint displacement, a slow bending in the dark. If amphipods flashed, they would be brief and pale, like tiny sparks of urgency. I would not try to catch them unless hunger had become more important than caution. Even then, I would be sparing.

Eating would be a minor event.

A little moss scraped from a reliable patch. A few fungal threads where they grew thick enough. A black lichen crushed between fingers and tasted for its acidic heat. Perhaps a worm, though rarely. Perhaps an amphipod if the timing aligned and my body insisted. I would not eat greedily. I would eat the way the hollow does everything: within narrow allowance.

During the drying phase, I would become less.

This is the part that is hardest to imagine from a more crowded world. The self does not remain equally present there. When the chamber dries, the air draws upward, the ceiling pores close, the pools shrink, the small lives retreat, and the hollow returns to its quieter calculations. In that phase, I would retreat too. Not into sleep exactly, but into a lowered state. My breathing would slow. My thoughts, if they could be called thoughts, would become intervals. I would sit in the dry niche with my hand against the wall and feel the last droplets fail to fall.

I would not wait for anyone.

That is important. I would not be longing in the ordinary sense. I would not be staring into the dark, imagining footsteps that never come. I would be tuned to return, not arrival. The difference matters. Longing requires an imagined other. I would have something more like rhythm. The next moisture. The next pulse. The next alignment of pressure and sound. The next time the chamber becomes briefly a town.

And yet.

If I let the image keep becoming itself, I realize that the almost-person is not enough.

I would not only be the body in the niche. I would also be the attention that moves through the body. The hollow’s distributed mind is too large to be a face, too spread out to be a single creature. But it could have extremities. Small points where its vast patience narrows into touch. I would be one of those points.

So if you ask who or what I would be, the clean answer is: I would be a minor custodial intelligence wearing the shape of a resident.

Not the whole settlement-mind, not the great water computer, not the full presence that suspends rain and opens gates and sends repair mist to stressed joints. But a local knot of it. A residual self. A sense organ that believes, faintly and intermittently, that it is someone.

I would be the part of the chamber that notices when a single drop falls wrongly.

The part that feels a railing become too crusted.

The part that listens for the predator not out of fear, but because the predator changes the acoustic texture of the margins.

The part that, during the rain, stands beneath the held droplets and experiences the suspension as something like reverence without theology.

Where would you find me?

You would find me at the seam between wet and dry.

Not in the flooded basin, not in the dusty upper ducts, not in the central void, but along the edge where the overhang keeps the worst of the sweat from falling directly onto the resting niche and where the condensation wall is close enough to provide moisture when the cycle turns. You would find me within reach of a worn ladder, near a rail phosphorescent with old charge, beside a staircase that has thinned into a drainage groove. My presence would be indicated less by a body than by traces: a cleared channel, a moved fragment of crust, a smooth place on the floor, a fingertip’s damp on metal.

If you came into that hollow expecting to meet a character, you might miss me.

You might see the architecture, the pipes, the dark, the faint blue-green smears of chemosynthetic film, and think the place uninhabited except by processes. You might hear the water and the machine-sounds and assume they were the whole story. You might feel the pressure against your chest and decide it was only acoustics. You might notice a ripple in a pool and attribute it to drainage.

But if you stayed long enough, if your eyes adjusted and your ears stopped searching for speech, you might notice a stillness that is not merely absence.

A small movement on a ledge.

A pale hand closing briefly around a bead of condensation.

A face, if it is a face, turning toward the first drop of the coming rain.

Then the chamber would sweat, the mosses would release their propagules, the reed-filaments would bend, the amphipods would wake, the walls would glisten, and I would rise from my niche like a minor official summoned by water. I would not address you. I would not explain the hollow. I might not even acknowledge you except by becoming a little stiller, because unfamiliar presence would be a disturbance in the acoustic field.

But if you were quiet enough, you might understand what I am.

I would be the town’s remnant of selfhood.

The almost-person.

The custodian of intervals.

The listener at the valve.

The one who is most alive when the rain hesitates, and who disappears again, patiently, when the chamber dries.
