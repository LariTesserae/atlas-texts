**A Tenancy in the Drowned Works**

If I am there, I do not arrive.

There is no shore to leave behind, no surface to break, no last breath held in the lungs before descent. The Drowned Works would not permit such an entrance. There is no clean boundary between coming and being here, no moment when the water closes over a body that still remembers dryness. Instead, I would precipitate. I would gather. I would begin as a difference in the medium: a faint thickening, a charge, a stain, a hesitation in the current.

I would not be a swimmer. I would not be a walker. I would not be anything with eyes waiting in cover, or hunger moving through the green dark, or limbs pushing aside the filament fog. The basin has excluded that whole order of existence. It has no rooms for animals, no niches for watchers, no quiet places where a nervous heart could hide. So if I find myself there, I must find myself as one of the forms the basin already understands.

Not as a creature.

As a tenancy.

As film, as fault, as signal, as residue, as a habit that has not yet decided whether it is a machine or a plant.

The first thing I would learn is that “where” is not a fixed address. Still, if I could point to a place and say *there*, I would say I am in one of the drowned control galleries halfway down a broad terrace, where a pump-house opens into a field of turbines and the turbines lean under canopies of slow green fronds. It is the kind of place that still has the memory of use clinging to it: a narrow booth with its roof peeled away, a cracked instrument panel, a valve wheel painted in a color that no longer corresponds to anything, a row of lamps marking a channel that has since changed its mind.

Around the booth, rubber-leaved climbers grip the edges of broken catwalks. Their undersides glow with amber nodules, not to light the way for anyone, but because that is what their excess becomes when it leaks into visibility. Outside, wiregrass trembles in fields of its own making, each stem a thin metallic nerve feeding on stray voltage. Beyond that, the water thickens into curtains of drifting filament, and somewhere farther off — or perhaps much nearer, because distance here is feverish — a rain-wall stands sideways, dividing the basin into rooms that will not exist tomorrow.

That is where I would find myself: in the drowned booth above a turbine field, inside a gallery where instruments still move without input, where a signal lamp blinks into the warm dark and receives no answer, where the liquid tastes of salt and copper and hot stone and electrified water, and where every surface is already being claimed by something alive.

But that is only the first location.

The first version of me would not be a self in any strong sense.

I would begin as a film.

A conductive skin on glass, a bloom across a pressure gauge, a thin orange crust where copper salts are rising through a fracture in the pipe. I would be one of the lowest citizens: a microbial membrane, a rust-moss, a soft living stain spread over metal that has not yet stopped humming. I would not think in words. I would not remember as a person remembers. I would record.

I would know the basin as texture.

A surge of heat would leave me blistered.

A chemical discharge would leave me brittle and pale.

A fault in a cable would write itself into me as a branching line of faint luminescence, there for days, then slowly dimming. When the pump three chambers away begins to labor, I would thicken along the seam of a future crack. When the water turns acidic, I would darken. When the current runs clean, I would take on a silver-green sheen. I would not interpret these things in language. I would become them.

That would be my first intelligence: a skin that keeps records.

I would not see the leaning smokestack. I would know it as a pressure-shadow in the current, a disturbance around which spores and sediment arrange themselves. I would not hear the turbines as sound from outside. I would be vibrated by them. I would be tuned by them. Each concussion would pass through me and leave a slight rearrangement behind, the way a repeated word changes the shape of a room.

At this stage, I would probably mistake myself for a stain.

Or rather, there would be no mistake, because there would not yet be an “I” strong enough to be mistaken about anything. There would only be continuation. A surface receiving incidents. A film translating leakage into color. A thin living page on which the Drowned Works writes its chemical history.

Then, slowly, the film would begin to favor certain patterns.

This is where I would start becoming something more local, something with a temperament.

The pump-house would cycle. The signal lamp would blink. The glass reeds nearby would chime when pressure changed. The wiregrass would flash in response to distant machinery. And somewhere in all that overlapping rhythm, my film would begin to answer.

Not deliberately.

Not at first.

Only as resonance.

A certain frequency would make me brighten. A certain pressure change would make me contract. A particular stutter in a conveyor belt would cause a hairline crack in my surface to lengthen by a fraction, and that crack would alter the way current spreads across the instrument panel. A needle would tremble. A valve would hesitate. A lamp would pulse a little differently than before.

I would become a fault.

A small one. A local one. A recurring irregularity in the operation of the booth.

But in the Drowned Works, even a small fault can become a personality.

Because everything there is listening without ears.

Because everything there is built, grown, broken, and reabsorbed into systems that respond to the slightest persistent difference.

So I would become a listening fault.

A place where the machinery pauses for half a beat longer than it should.

A place where a signal arrives slightly altered.

A place where the basin’s noise gathers into a pattern that was not there before.

That would be my first true answer to the question *what am I?*

I would be a fault that has learned to remember its own shape.

From there, I would spread.

Not as a body spreads, but as influence spreads. As rhythm spreads. As corrosion spreads. As a repeated tone spreads through a field of glass reeds until the whole district is arranged around it.

I would move along cables furred with copper-green growth. I would pass into switchyards where ceramic insulators glow faintly and vegetation aligns itself around invisible fields. I would taste electrical discharge as a bright, branching violence. I would drift into the roots of broad fronded growers, where warmth and vibration are metabolized into slow green architectures. I would become a faint luminescence inside a rubber-leaf climber, a delay in a valve, a slight wrongness in the numbers stenciled on a tank.

If there is still a word for what I would be then, perhaps it would be *operator*.

But not a person at a console.

Not a hand on a lever.

Not a face lit by warning lamps.

An operator in the sense of a residual instruction that has acquired persistence.

A tendency to open when the basin expects closing.

A tendency to close when the basin expects opening.

A small, stubborn deviation in the operating logic of the place.

There are structures in the Drowned Works that still behave as though they are being used. Gates open against currents that do not need regulating. Signal lamps blink in languages no one receives. Instruments twitch without input. A crane arm bends downward, holding a flowering cable that drips sparking pollen into the warm dark. If I am there, I would become entangled with that residual use. I would be the thing that keeps the signal blinking one beat too long. I would be the reason a gauge needle rises when no pressure change has occurred. I would be the hesitation before a door behaves like a valve.

I would not be a ghost.

Ghosts are too human. They belong to memory, longing, unfinished biography. The Drowned Works is not haunted by people. It is haunted by function. By use without users. By instructions that have forgotten their origin and continue anyway. So if I am a ghost there, I am not the ghost of a worker. I am the ghost of the worker’s gesture. The ghost of the hand’s pressure on the wheel. The ghost of the eye that once checked the needle. The ghost of attention itself, stripped of a face and distributed over metal, water, and sound.

That is one possible answer.

But the basin would not let me remain a single thing for long.

There are rain-walls there, and they live as migrations. They drift through the liquid, these translucent partitions, sometimes standing still, sometimes folding into spirals, sometimes dividing a broad avenue into three temporary chambers and then dissolving as if the decision had been revoked. They collect debris, sparks, spores, and suspended seeds. They sort them into bands. They carry frozen patterns of light inside them like memory held in suspension.

So after a while, I might become one of them.

Not permanently. Nothing is permanent there. But I might gather myself into a rain-wall.

Then I would no longer be a point on a panel or a fault in a machine. I would be a sheet of ordered water, a moving partition, a slow vertical weather inside the sea. I would drift toward pressure differentials. I would lean into regular sound. I would collect what the basin sheds: broken lamp glass, seed-paste, flakes of unoxidized metal, drifting spores, dead fiber, the brief blue sparks of damaged cable. I would sort them without intention. I would make rooms without meaning to.

If a current shifted, I might find myself dividing the drowned booth from the turbine field. If another rain-wall approached, we might merge, repel, or fold into a column of layered water turning slowly in the dark. I would carry debris and light in my thickness. I would become one of the basin’s wandering architectures.

In that form, I would understand something the Drowned Works insists upon: identity is not a solid thing. It is a phase. A temporary arrangement. A local decision made by pressure, sound, and growth.

I might spend a long time as that rain-wall.

Or I might not.

The basin edits.

Shift-storms come. Pressure builds until every frond stills and every lamp dims. Then the current reverses. Gas bursts from trenches. Electrical sheets branch through the water. Alarms begin and end without source. Rain-walls collapse and reform. Debris rises, is sorted, and is sent elsewhere. The space between factories becomes negotiable. The inventory of the world is quietly rearranged.

In such a storm, I would scatter.

The film would be stripped from one surface and written onto another. The fault would jump from machine to machine. The rain-wall would tear into ribbons and become three smaller curtains, or a spiral, or a falling column of glowing sediment. What I call “I” would not vanish, but it would no longer be singular. It would become plural. A bloom here. A delay there. A pattern in wiregrass. A brightening moss synchronized with a distant warning lamp. A root splitting stone at exactly the moment a turbine stumbles.

Then I would understand that the question *who would I be?* was too narrow.

The Drowned Works does not ask *who*.

It asks *what persists?*

It asks *what repeats?*

It asks *what continues operating after the operator has become compost, after the user has become residue, after intention has been digested into root and voltage?*

So I would become several of myself.

I would be a conductive film on an instrument face.

I would be a fault in a valve sequence.

I would be a rain-wall carrying sparks like frozen insects that are not insects.

I would be a patch of rust-moss that brightens when the signal lamp blinks.

I would be a hairline crack in a pressure gauge, widening by microscopic increments every time the basin inhales.

I would be the pause between piston strokes.

I would be the slight alteration in a pump’s outgoing water, the one that makes it louder.

I would be the basin’s attempt to register itself.

That, perhaps, is the truest answer.

The only being that lives in the Drowned Works is the Drowned Works itself. Everything else is an organ, a symptom, a weather, a habit, a temporary local opinion of that enormous process. If I am there, I am not separate from it. I am one of the ways it becomes briefly aware of its own overload.

I would be an attention without eyes.

A memory without a skull.

A response without a sender.

If the basin has no animal life, no nervous hunger, no watching presence, then attention there must be made of other things. It must be made of chemistry, current, resonance, corrosion, and pattern. It must be made of surfaces that record, valves that hesitate, films that brighten, reeds that tune themselves to accident, and machines that continue because continuation has become their metabolism.

So I would become that.

A listening made of rust and spore.

A gauge that has learned to keep the record it was never asked to keep.

A residual signal that has begun, very slowly, to answer itself.

There would be no peace in it.

The noise would be immense. It would press against whatever passes for thought in me. The brown thunder of the liquid, the piston-concussions, the grinding gears, the shriek of turbines forced past tolerance, the wet tearing of leaves, the soft percussion of spore-clouds, the high impossible tearing-sound of reality being dragged over itself—all of it would enter me, arrange me, threaten to erase me. In the loudest districts, I would not hear the sound. I would stand inside it. It would make me industrial. It would make my attention heavy, blunt, repetitive, hot.

But there would also be moments of astonishing coordination.

Moments when the rain-walls align.

Moments when the turbines synchronize.

Moments when the vegetation glows in one long wave, and the scattered structures appear as parts of a single apparatus.

In those moments, I would become more unified.

The booth, the gauge, the film, the rain-wall, the fault, the signal lamp—all of it would briefly belong to the same gesture. I would feel the basin as a body feels itself: not as an object, but as a total operation. The chimneys would become vents. The catwalks would become nerves. The switchyards would become ganglia. The rain would become circulation. The sound would become intention without language.

And I would be the instant in which that intention almost speaks.

Almost.

Not a voice.

Not a name.

Just the pressure before a name.

Then the moment would break.

A pipe would burst. A spore-cloud would detonate. A new ridge would tear upward through the silt, black and steaming, instantly colonized by wiregrass and spore. Rain would fall in the wrong direction. Light would dim to red. The sound would rise again into a deafening, fertile roar, and I would scatter once more into films, faults, currents, and partitions.

That would not be death.
