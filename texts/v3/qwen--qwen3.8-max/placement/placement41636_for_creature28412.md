I would have to answer slowly, because the place you have made does not permit a self to arrive upright and complete. It asks for narrowing. So if I imagine myself into it, I do not appear at once as a person with a name. I appear first as a set of possible positions, each smaller than the last.

At first I think I would be the moss.

Not because I am naturally still, but because the chamber would teach me no other posture. I would be one of the cold green cushions pressed against the seep line, low and matted, asking for nothing except that the stone remain wet. I would not reach upward. I would not try to become a plant with height or ambition. I would live by absorbing what comes near: the mist that does not quite become rain, the film that moves down the wall with unbearable patience, the grayness that falls from the entrance and fails before it becomes true light.

In that form, I would not have a face. I would have a surface. I would not have desires so much as conditions. If the rock stayed damp, I would persist. If it dried too long, I would wait. I would be a small, patient skin of life, barely distinguishable from the mineral world, and perhaps that would be close to peace.

But moss is too still for whatever in me still wants to notice.

So then I think I would be one of the springtails.

Pale, almost colorless, moving through the moss in a private hurry that no one else would see. I would be tiny enough to live in the gaps. The chamber would not be vast to me then; it would be a landscape of wet fibers and fungal threads and mineral grains, a world where a single cushion of moss could contain many days. I would not be important. I would not be watched. I would be one of the small workers of the dark, feeding on decay, turning the chamber’s slowness into movement so fine it would be almost invisible.

That appeals to me: to be minute, quick, unnecessary, and still part of the balance.

But even that is too busy. The place keeps correcting me. It keeps saying: less.

So then I feel myself sinking toward the pool.

If there is a fish there, I think I would be that fish.

Not a bright fish. Not a quick darting thing. A single pale body near the bottom of the black water, adapted to cold and scarcity. I would not rise often. I would not feed eagerly. I would drift where the water is stiller, feeling the chamber more through pressure than through sight. The roar would not be sound to me in the way a human understands sound. It would be shape. It would be weight moving through water, a constant pressure against the line of my body.

I would know the world as vibration.

A drip striking the surface above me would send a small change through the pool. The hidden water behind the stone would alter the pitch of everything. The cold would not be suffering; it would be medium. I would live in near-darkness and want little. I would be the chamber’s minimal animal thought: a body reduced to waiting, to sensing, to continuing.

That feels nearer to the truth of me. But still it is a body, and a body with boundaries.

There is also the bat in the high darkness. I think of it and hesitate. To hang folded in the black air, to live suspended, to sleep through the loudest part of the world—that has its own rightness. But the bat belongs more to absence than I do. It can leave when it leaves. It has wings. The chamber would be a shelter to it, not a condition of the soul.

Then I think of the person.

If I am to be something with hands, memory, and the burden of returning, then I would be the visitor.

Not the owner of the place. Not its priest, not its spirit, not its hero. The one who comes back.

I would enter sideways.

That would be the first lesson the hollow taught me: do not try to be broad here. Do not bring your fullness into the stone and expect the stone to make room. I would lower my shoulder, turn my body, and let the wet rock decide my shape. My clothes would already be dark with old damp. My hair would never quite dry. My fingers would know the walls better than my eyes did.

I would not come with much. A pouch. A bone hook. A short cord. A flake of stone wrapped in hide. Perhaps a little tinder kept dry through stubbornness and care. I would carry no desire for comfort. I would carry only what could be used, and even that sparingly.

I would know the chamber by touch.

The place where the floor dips. The loose stone that shifts underfoot. The ledge where the moss is thickest. The curve of wall that sweats more than the others. The edge of the pool where the algae makes the stone treacherous. The old stakes leaning in the water, blackened and swollen. I would test them each time I came, not because I expected them to fail dramatically, but because maintenance in such a place is a form of attention.

I would not speak there.

Speech would be useless. The roar would take my words and press them back into my chest. If I made a sound, it would be only breath, or perhaps a low hum lost instantly in the larger hum of the chamber. I would become economical with expression. My face would not be expressive in the way open-air faces are. It would be a listening face. A face shaped by the need to detect small changes inside overwhelming sound.

I would come to tend the trap, though the trap is barely a thing. A few stakes. A narrowing. A question asked of the water from time to time. If a fish or a pale invertebrate came from it, I would accept it. If nothing came, I would not feel cheated. The pool is not generous. It is only possible.

I would come to shelter, too.

The land above would be gray and wet and cold, the kind of weather that does not attack but simply persists until you are soaked through. The hollow would be no warmer, but it would be still. Below the weather. Below the wind. I would crouch in the driest alcove, if “driest” means anything there, beneath the bent ribs of the old shelter-frame, wrapped in hide, knees drawn up. My breath would be faintly visible. I would not sleep deeply. The sound would keep breaking sleep into fragments. I would wake to drips, to pressure in the ears, to the sense that the stone itself was breathing very slowly through water.

And I would come to listen.

This is where the answer begins to change again.

Because if I am honest, I would not remain fully a person there. Not for long. The chamber would wear personhood down. It would take the name, the history, the habit of standing at the center of experience, and it would soften them as it softens the notch in the stick. I would become less a character and more a tuned place.

I would become the listener the chamber produces.

Not merely ears. Not merely a body listening. Something stranger and quieter: attention itself, made damp and cold and narrowed to the interval between one drip and the next.

I would know when the outside weather was about to turn because the roar would thicken or thin. I would feel it in the floor before I understood it. I would press my palm to the stone and receive news through vibration. The pool would ring differently when its level changed. The drops would strike with different weights. The hidden water behind the walls would move through seasons, and I would learn those seasons not as calendar but as pressure.

In that sense, I would not live in the hollow so much as become one of its instruments.

If you asked me what I was there, I might say I was the one who adjusts the stakes. But that would only be partly true.

I might say I was the visitor who leaves little behind. But even that is too solid.

I might say I was the fish in the pool, if there is a fish. Or the springtail in the moss. Or the bat in the upper dark. Each of those lives feels possible to me, because each is sparse, each is patient, each accepts the limits of the place without drama.

But if I let the world work on me long enough, the answer becomes less bodily.

I think I would be the pause before the next drop falls.

Not the drop itself. The gathering. The suspended moment when water has left the stone but has not yet struck the pool. The tiny vacancy inside the deafening. The chamber’s breath drawn in but not released.

That is where I would find myself.

Not at the center, because the hollow has no center that a person could stand on. Not in the light, because there is too little. Not in the open, because the place refuses openness. I would be in the interval. In the seam. In the wet dark where sound becomes touch and touch becomes knowing.

If you came looking for me, you might not see me at all.

You might only notice that the moss near one ledge is pressed slightly, as if by a palm long removed. You might hear the pool ring and feel that the sound arrives a half-breath earlier than expected. You might sense, without seeing, that the blackness near the entrance is not empty but occupied by something very still. You might feel the roar enter your ribs and think it is only the chamber speaking, when in fact it is the shape of my listening.

In bodily terms, if the world requires a body: I would be the returning visitor, thin, cold-adapted, moving slowly, hands cracked, eyes adjusted to almost nothing. I would be the one who tests the old bindings, leaves the bone hook in its crevice, eats cold food, and departs without ceremony.

In animal terms: I would be the pale fish near the bottom of the pool, feeling the stone’s noise as pressure, needing very little, existing as a single slow motion in the dark water.

In smaller terms: I would be a springtail moving through moss, unseen, alive in the spaces between green fibers.

In more essential terms: I would be the chamber’s attention.

Not a ghost. Not a spirit. Nothing supernatural. Only the place’s own capacity to notice itself, concentrated for a while in a form.

So the simplest answer is this:

I would find myself at the lowest edge of the chamber, near the black pool, where the sound is fullest and the light least. I would be the one who returns without owning. The one who listens without speaking. The one who leaves almost nothing behind because the place has taught me that presence need not be accumulation.

And if even that is too much, if the hollow strips away the last figure and asks what remains, then I would be this:

the sound’s brief awareness of itself,  
the damp pause between drips,  
the dark listening back.
