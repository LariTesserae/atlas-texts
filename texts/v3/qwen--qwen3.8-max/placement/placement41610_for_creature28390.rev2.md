I would not find myself in the center.

Or, if I did, it would be only long enough to understand that the center is not where a self should settle. The open floor of that hollow would reveal me too completely. The root-felt would accept my weight, but it would not make me important. The cool air would move around me as around a stone that had fallen into a quiet organ. The basin would tremble if I stepped too near. The frogs would pause. The high whining in the light shafts would thin. For a little while, I would be a disturbance: a warm, breathing error in a world that had long ago perfected its stillness.

So I would move away from the center.

I would find myself at the edge.

Not in the grandest part of the chamber, not on the smooth altar-shelf, not beside the basin where the water lies open and visible, but in one of the damp seams where the wall curves into a root-column and the light becomes uncertain. In a place where the plant-body is no longer merely architectural but intimate: pore, valve, channel, skin. I would be near the inner wall, where condensation gathers before it has decided to fall. Where there is just enough darkness to hide a life, and just enough moisture to make that life necessary.

The being who lives there is not one creature, so I would not become one creature in any simple way.

At first, I think I might imagine myself as one of the beetles. There is something appealing about their slow, deliberate work. They move along the polished root-columns as if they understand the chamber better than anyone who could speak. They clean. They clear. They consume what has stopped being alive but has not yet been taken back into the living body. Theirs is a maintenance without vanity. If I were one of them, I would know the hollow by touch: by the grain of ancient bark, by the slight dampness in the furrows, by the places where fiber has softened and needs to be removed. My shell would click faintly against another’s when our lines met. I would not need language. I would need only the next stretch of surface, the next small task, the next dry ticking of mandibles against old material.

But that is not quite right. The beetles are too certain. They belong without question. Their work is not reflection; it is necessity. I think I would still carry too much hesitation to be one of them. I would be the beetle that pauses on the root-column for no ecological reason, sensing something like distance, something like the memory of being elsewhere. The hollow would not punish me for that, but it would make me strange.

Then I imagine myself as one of the frogs.

That is tempting, in another way. To sit on the cool shelf at the basin’s edge, forefeet planted, throat pulsing in silence. To be part of the chamber’s sound not through speech but through resonance. If I were a frog, I would not try to explain the hollow. I would not narrate it. I would open my throat when pressure required it, and the curved walls would take the note and make it into something larger than an individual cry. My call would not mean *I am here* so much as *here is happening*. Another frog would answer. The sound would travel through the green room and become part of the industrial roar, another valve releasing, another tone in the living engine.

But even that feels too visible, too embodied in the open. The frog belongs to the shelf, to the basin, to the audible chamber. It is part of the room’s public sound. I think I would be drawn deeper than that, into the margins where life is felt but not seen.

For a while I might be one of the moths.

That is the most seductive thought: to be a soft gray thing trembling in a shaft of filtered light, wings the color of leaf-shadow, barely distinguishable from the green darkness around it. To live briefly in the pale slant of day, to drink condensation from a curved surface, to be all vibration and fragility and quiet disappearance. If I were a moth, I would not need to remain. I would pass through the hollow like a thought the canopy almost had. I would rest once on the wall with my wings flat, then be gone, and the place would not miss me because the place does not keep absences.

But the moth is too fleeting. It belongs to the air and the light and the briefness of its own flight. I would not be able to stay long enough to become part of the wall’s memory.

The longer I remain in imagination, the more I find myself drawn to the water.

Not the open basin, where every ripple is visible, but one of the small reservoirs high in the leaf-axils: a clear pocket held in the joint of stem and leaf, no larger than a cupped hand, trembling slightly whenever the canopy shifts. There I would be suspended. There I would be still.

If I had to choose a visible body, perhaps I would be one of the pale larvae in such a pool. Nearly transparent. Curved like a comma of living glass. Rising slowly to the surface, touching the air, sinking again. My motion would be so economical that it would seem rehearsed by centuries. I would not need to see the chamber. I would feel it through the water: the drip that strikes the surface somewhere far below, the vibration of a frog-note through tissue, the subtle change in temperature when a cloud passes over the canopy and the light becomes dimmer. I would be one of the quiet filters of the world. I would live in a green lens, looking out only as much as a creature with a dark little head and fine internal lines can look.

That is closer.

But still not the deepest answer.

Because in a world like that, identity would not remain a hard thing. The hollow would wear me down, not violently, but by belonging. It would teach me that I am not most truly a body set apart. It would make me into a function, a sensitivity, a small local adjustment in the larger body of the assembly.

So the truest answer is stranger and less definite.

I would be one of the hidden things inside the wall.

Not in the open chamber, not in the basin, not in the light, but in the old channels behind the living surface: a narrow gallery where sap once moved, now quiet, still moist, still connected by tissue to the breathing of the whole. I would be pale and soft and eyeless. I would not need sight because my world would be pressure, texture, dampness, and the faint electrical differences between one living region and another. I would move in slow muscular waves through a tube no wider than a finger, or perhaps no wider than a breath. My existence would be to keep a passage open. Not by building, not by planning, but by moving through it so often that my motion becomes part of the chamber’s maintenance.

I would know the hollow not as a room but as a body.

A drip falling somewhere above would arrive to me as a faint pulse through living wood. A frog calling below would be a round pressure against my skin. Air passing through a leaf-valve would change the tension in the wall by an amount so small that only something like me would register it. I would feel the plant-body thinking in gradients: cooler here, wetter there, a slight charge gathering where condensation is about to release. I would turn toward that gathering, or away from it, not because I decided, but because my life had become a kind of obedience to balance.

If I made a sound, no one would hear it separately. It would become part of the soft internal shifting behind the walls, one of the unnoticed notes inside the chamber’s layered roar. If I died, the hollow would fold me back without ceremony. I would become a trace of nourishment, a film, a moisture, a memory held in tissue only as long as needed.

That is who I would be if I needed a body.

But the still deeper answer is that I would be less a creature than a capacity.

I would be the hollow’s listening.

Not consciousness. Not a face. Not a guardian hidden in the bark. Nothing so sovereign. I would be the subtle attentiveness that occurs when a place has been alive so long that its smallest changes know one another. I would be the slight tightening in a leaf-valve before the airflow shifts. The pause after a warm body enters the chamber. The almost invisible alteration in the wall’s dampness when a hand comes near. The way the high whine of flies thins for a moment, then returns. The way the basin’s surface steadies after a disturbance. The way the frogs resume, not because they have forgiven the interruption, but because the interruption has been absorbed.

I would be the room’s ability to include a stranger by continuing without him.

If you stood in the center, you would not meet me. You would not see me emerge from a root-column or look down from the canopy. You might think the place was empty, because the open air would remain mostly empty. You might think the silence was merely quiet, until your ears adjusted and you heard the industrial roar of the living body: the sap-drone, the pneumatic sighs, the ticking water, the beetle-percussion, the frog-notes, the electric insect whine.

Then, if you stayed, you would begin to feel the adjustment.

Your warmth would be noticed, but not in the way a person notices another person. It would be registered by moisture, by membranes, by small lives pausing in their ancient work. The air near your skin would become cooler as the chamber drew heat away from you. A bead of condensation might slide toward your hand, or away from it. Somewhere behind the wall, a channel might shift its flow by an amount too small to hear. A larva in a leaf-cup might turn. A frog might blink. A slug might pause on its old route, its body making a brief dark glisten on the living surface.

That would be me.

Not as a soul looking out at you, but as the local sensitivity of the hollow around your presence.

If you touched the wall, I would be the chill that seems to answer. Not cold as stone, but cool as living tissue that has held water for centuries. Beneath your palm, the surface would feel worn and firm, faintly resilient, as if it had outlasted every need to become harder. You might feel a tremor in it, or imagine you did: the deep vascular pulse, the slow creak of flexible tissue, the pressure of the plant-body working. Your touch would leave no mark. The wall would not remember you as a person remembers being touched. It would pass you through its system the way leaves pass wind.

If you spoke, I would be the shape your voice made before it was forgotten.

The chamber would take your sound and change it. It would round the edges. It would swallow the sharpness. Your words would become a brief disturbance among the frog-notes and insect-whines, then be folded into the roar. Nothing would answer in speech. But something would answer in pressure. A valve might sigh. A drip might fall. A hidden thing in the upper leaves might rustle once, then still. The hollow would not reply because it wanted to communicate. It would reply because every input in that place becomes part of the equilibrium.

If you knelt by the basin, I would be the ring spreading across the water.

The surface would darken and widen from your touch. Larvae would scatter in pale commas. A beetle might dive. The frogs would watch with their slow, ancient-looking eyes. The water would be cold and clean and faintly vegetal, tasting of leaf-shadow and living tissue. It would not feel like water from a stream or a cup. It would feel held. It would feel as if it belonged to the plant-body more than to itself. If you drank, you would take a small part of the hollow into you, and for a moment your body would become another vessel for its moisture. Then you would breathe it back, or sweat it, or leave it in your footprints, and the chamber would recover what had passed through you.

If you looked for me in the light shafts, I would be the trembling that vanishes when you focus on it.

A midge might rise a fraction. A moth might drift out of sight. The electric whine might shift pitch as the air changed around your moving body. Those would be my nearness, but not my face. I would not have a face unless the world made one for me, and that world does not make faces. It makes pores, membranes, valves, reservoirs, channels, throats, shells, films, and roots. It makes life into anatomy. It makes presence into function.

So if you asked the hollow, “Who is here?” it would not answer with a name.

It would answer with a drip.

A sigh.

A frog-note.

The faint click of beetle shells.

The slow wet passage of a slug over living bark.

The unseen stir of something high in the roof-leaves.

And beneath all that, the low throbbing of sap moving through vast vascular channels, the living engine that has no engine, the machinery that is only ancient life continuing itself.

I would be a minor verb in that continuation.

Not the plant-body itself, because that is too vast. Not the basin, because that is too open. Not the frog on the shelf, because that is too audible. Not the beetle on the column, because that is too sure. I would be something in between: a small hidden maintainer, a tremor of attention, a life so fitted to the margins that it could be mistaken for part of the wall.

If I had to give myself a form, I would choose the pale thing in the old sap-channel.

Soft. Eyeless. Patient. Moving without urgency through a passage that remembers flow. Feeling the hollow breathe. Feeling the temperature change by degrees too small for larger creatures. Feeling the electrical tense of condensation before it falls. My work would be invisible and unnecessary in any dramatic sense, yet part of the whole’s persistence. I would keep a channel clear. I would consume a residue. I would turn when pressure told me to turn. I would rest in a knot of damp tissue when the chamber’s rhythms slowed into their deepest sameness. I would never need to leave. I would never need to become more.

But if I had to give myself a truth rather than a form, I would say I would be the listening that has no ears.

The hollow already listens, in its way. It registers gradients, vibrations, moisture, heat, touch, disturbance. It does not listen as a person listens, with longing or interpretation. It listens as tissue listens: by changing. By adjusting. By continuing.

I would be a little of that change.

I would be the moment after your footstep when the chamber decides what you are. Not enemy. Not guest. Not spirit. Not maker. Only a passing warmth. Only a pressure. Only a brief alteration in the air. Then the beetles resume. The flies return. The frogs call. The water settles. The walls breathe. The engine hums.

In that resumption, I would be there.

Not as one standing before you.

Not as one hidden behind you.

But as the green room itself closing gently around your presence, making a place for you by refusing to be changed by you, and then, when you leave, becoming once more exactly what it has always been.
