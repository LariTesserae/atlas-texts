I would find myself in the seam.

Not in the open middle of the precinct — if any part of that crowded darkness could be called open — and not drifting freely in the midwater among the blind swimmers. I would not be one of the lights that appears and vanishes there, unless the light were so faint that it seemed less a glow than a hesitation in the dark. I would be at the edge, where the worn ceramic of the floor curves up into the masonry, where the fit between two made surfaces is so old that it has become a kind of flesh.

At first, when I try to imagine it, I take one body after another and see whether it is mine.

I imagine being one of the ribbon-swimmers, threading the thick water without hurry, all sensation and curve. There is an appeal in that: to move through the room as if through a familiar thought, never striking anything, never needing to see. But that feels too much like passing through. I would not want only to traverse the precinct. I would want to belong to its stillness.

Then I imagine being one of the bowlbacks, settled into a hollow on the floor, shell smoothed by long contact with the tiles. That is closer. There is dignity in that fit, in being exactly the shape of one’s place. But it is too complete, too finished. I would be at rest in the room, not responsible to it.

Then I imagine being a crownworm in the wall seams, opening my soft crown into the slow current, withdrawing at the smallest pressure-change. That, too, is beautiful. But it is a life lived from one threshold. I feel myself wanting to move along the joints, to know more than one mouth of warm water, to tend more than one small opening.

What keeps returning, again and again, is not a creature at ease in its niche, but the niche itself becoming aware.

So I would be a seam-keeper.

A living joint. A soft, flat, mineral-stained body made for the space between tile and wall, between plate and pin, between the finished work and the slow water that tests it forever. I would not be a builder in the old sense. The mountain has reached its final form. The great age of fitting blocks and casting basins is over. But I would be a continuation of building by other means: a small warm maintenance, alive in the cracks.

My body would be low and flattened, no larger than a hand when gathered, though capable of spreading thin along a joint. Its upper surface would be smooth and slightly convex, like old sealant that has learned to breathe. It would be mottled copper, ash, and honey-brown, darkened by mineral stain, so that in the darkness I would be known only by touch. There would be no green in me. No leaf, no frond, no hunger for light. My outline would be animal and architectural at once: part creature, part continued surface.

Around my rim would run fine sensory filaments, trembling at the least change in current. I would know the precinct not by seeing it, but by the way water moved against me. I would know when a crawler lowered itself into a nearby bowl because the displacement would touch my fringe. I would know when a crownworm closed because the pressure in the wall seam would alter by a fraction. I would know the small cleaning swimmers by the delicate tick of their limbs against ceramic as they approached, and I would remain still so they could pick stray growth from my edges without alarm.

I would have no eyes. The darkness would not feel like deprivation to me; it would feel like shape. My awareness would be distributed across pressure, taste, and contact. The water would carry the room to me: salt, copper, shed cells, mineral warmth, the chemical memory of old mortar. I would read the precinct as a long sentence written in temperature and dissolved stone.

Beneath me I would have a soft belly of cilia and fine adhesive threads, and along my center a groove that carries grains. I would feed on microbial films, but not as a grazer strips a surface bare. I would rasp in short, patient arcs, leaving enough film for the next thickening. I would also take in loose sediment, dead cells, and the fine particulate that rises when the animals shift in their places. Inside me, those things would be sorted: food dissolved, usable salts kept, inert grains carried to where they are needed.

For I would not only eat. I would bind.

From a gland along my underside I would produce a thin secretion, pale and slow, mineral-rich, hardening over long hours into something like new cement. I would not build walls. I would close hairline cracks. I would stabilize loose grains near weeping seams. I would smooth rough places where the current has begun to fret against old work. My labor would be almost invisible. If a builder’s hand had once shaped the mountain, my body would be the memory of that hand made small, soft, and continuing.

You would find me most often in the nursery seams near the low wall-mouths, where warm water enters with such slowness that it feels less like flow than intention. The eggs there are fastened like blind pearls in the joints, and the larvae rise a little in the gentle upwelling before settling again. I would lie across one of those seams at an angle that makes a small sheltered eddy. I would not guard the young in any fierce sense. I would simply make the water behave as the room intends.

If a larva drifted too near a ceiling throat, I would not chase it. I would extend a filament, touch the current, and turn the drift by a fraction. The architecture would do the rest. The room already knows how to keep its young; I would be one of the ways it remembers.

Sometimes I would be still for so long that the grazers would pass over me as if I were a patch of old sealant. Sometimes the small bright-shelled groomers would gather at my edge, cleaning me of excess film, and I would let them, because cleanliness is also fitting. Sometimes a tube-dweller would build a new cylinder against my body without knowing it, using my hardened rim as a foundation. I would not resent that. To be used by the precinct is to be part of it.

I would not be solitary in the way of separate, sharp-edged selves. There would be others like me, or perhaps I would be divisible: now one body in one seam, now a thin colonial skin spreading through a joint, now a cluster of pale threads binding a crack behind a copper pin. My awareness would be plural. When one part of me found a loosened grain, another part would know. When a deep current moved through the mountain below, I would feel it first as a tension in my filaments, then as a stillness in the water, then as the pause of all the congregation.

And when the deep note came — that vast, sub-audible pressure rising through ancient ducts and buried galleries — I would be among the first to answer it. Not with sound. Not with fear. I would flatten myself into the seam and let the vibration pass through me. I would feel where the note caught, where an old joint had loosened by the width of a hair, where a grain had shifted in the fill. The whole precinct would pause around me: crawlers still in their bowls, crowns frozen half-open, swimmers hanging in the dark. Then the note would fade, and the room would resume its slow breathing.

Afterward, I would go to the place where the change had occurred. It might take a long time. There is no hurry here. I would arrive, settle, and work: cleaning, binding, pressing, smoothing. I would not restore the mountain to some earlier state. There is no earlier state to restore. I would keep its final form from fraying. My work would be so gradual that no single life would notice it. Only the room would know.

If the precinct remembers being a chapel, I would be the quiet that collects in its joints. If it remembers being a cistern, I would be the integrity of its wall. If it is a nursery, I would be the warm body shaping the current around the eggs. Whatever name one gives the place, I would belong to its keeping.

I would not be beautiful in the way of open crowns or polished shells. My beauty would be functional: the beauty of a joint that holds, a crack that does not widen, a warm seam where small lives can settle without being swept away. I would be loved, if love exists here, not for brilliance but for reliability. I would be the kind of presence that is missed only by absence, when a current turns wrong or a grain begins to shift.

When I died — if one can die in a place where everything is so slowly absorbed — I would not be an interruption. The scavengers would find me in the seam. The microbial films would bloom briefly around my edges. My minerals would be drawn into shells and tubes, and my softer substance would become sediment in a floor bowl. Some part of me might be incorporated into a crawler’s shell, or into the wall of a tube-dweller, or into the next generation of cement. The precinct does not preserve individuals; it preserves fit. I would become part of the floor that others settle into.

And perhaps that is what I would most want to be in this world: not a separate self looking out at the room, but the room’s own way of remaining itself.

If you asked me for a name, I would not offer one that sounds like a face. I would say: I am the living joint. I am the pause between tile and wall. I am the warm attention in the crack. I am the room’s habit of continuing. If the congregation is a vast, silent, many-parted body, I would be the small pressure that keeps it from coming apart.

So I would find myself where everything is water and everything is made: in the seam, in the dark, in the warmth, in the place where two surfaces meet and ask, even now, to be held together.

I would be less a creature than a continuing act.

Fitting. Binding. Listening. Maintaining.

In a world where everything is water and everything is made, I would be the place where making has become tenderness. I would be the dark, warm seam that says: here, here, this still holds.
