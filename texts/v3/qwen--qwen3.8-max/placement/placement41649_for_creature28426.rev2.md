I would not find myself there as a traveler who had crossed into the High Sill. The place would not keep a traveler long.

If I began as anything with haste in it—with a pulse, with thirst, with the old animal expectation that the next moment must be seized—the Sill would begin at once to edit me. Not violently. Not with teeth or heat or storm. It would simply continue its own slow work until the parts of me that belonged elsewhere became improbable. Thirst would go first, because there was nothing there to answer thirst. Then urgency. Then the need to move quickly from one state to another. The air would pass through me not as breath but as tone. The cold would not kill me; it would clarify me. The roar would not deafen me; it would arrange me.

After that, I would be somewhere else in the world. Or rather, I would be something the world could hold.

You would find me on the leeward side of a ridge that has not yet decided whether it is one mountain or several. The ridge casts more shadows than it should. In the pale ash-light, its edge seems to hesitate, splitting into overlapping summits, some sharp, some translucent, some no more than a suggestion of pressure against the sky. Below it, in a shallow fold sheltered from the worst of the open sound, there is a black fin of broken stone. Wind has worn no smoothness into it. It is not old. It still looks like something interrupted.

Behind that fin, where the roar is split by the stone and becomes less crushing, I would be inside a low ring of fitted basalt.

At first I would look like architecture. A shelter, perhaps, though too low for any body that needed to stand. A circle of dry-fitted stones, no mortar between them, half filled with lilac dust. A shallow basin at the center, carved from pale pumice-like rock, set so that vibration gathers in it instead of water. Around the basin, several hollow stalks rise like organ pipes, some no thicker than a finger, others tall enough to seem the beginnings of columns. They are not arranged symmetrically. They are arranged musically.

A little farther away, a row of upright slabs stands with notches cut into their tops. A few obsidian flakes lie near them, bright-edged, half buried. A copper ring, green with age and yet not weakened, catches around a thornlike spine. Cordage made from tough stalk-fiber binds one cracked pipe to another so that they can continue resonating together without falling apart.

If someone looked at all this long enough, they might realize that the ring is not merely built.

It is listening.

That is what I would be.

Not a creature in the old sense. Nothing wet inside. Nothing that hungers, flees, mates, nests, or leaves tracks. No bones, because nothing in me would require speed. No eyes, because sight would be too simple for a place where horizons fold and distances misbehave. No mouth, because a spoken word here can become a path, a door, or a wall, and I would have learned better than to keep such a thing open.

I would be a tuned body. A mineral patience. A dry listener.

My form would not be fixed in the way a statue is fixed. It would be more like a stable chord that has gathered matter around itself. Basalt plates would serve as anchors. Hollow reeds would serve as resonating chambers. Sheets of brittle mica, dark glassy fronds, and crusts of copper-green moss would grow over me slowly, becoming part of my surface. Veins of dull copper would thread through what might seem to be a chest, though it would not rise and fall. A bowl of pale stone would sit where a head might have been, or perhaps where an ear might have been, if hearing were the primary sense. In that bowl, vibration would collect in fine tremors too subtle to see.

I would know the world by gradient and difference.

A tone leaning against the western slabs would tell me that the ridge is settling into one of its possible shapes. A thinning of the roar in the center of a flat would warn me that the ground there has lost conviction. A change in static charge along the fronds would say that spore-dust is drifting from a dying growth. A repeated echo arriving too soon would let me know that a stone is about to fracture, or that a footfall is about to occur, or that some future event is already sending its sound backward into the present.

I would not move as animals move.

I would not lift foot and place it forward. I would not cross open ground by intention alone. Movement on the High Sill is too likely to disagree with distance. A traveler who tries to walk straight toward a peak may find the peak farther away after a day of walking. A stone spire may remain remote for hours, then suddenly be close behind the eyes once one turns away. So I would move by becoming more likely elsewhere.

A century might pass while I remain in the ring. Then a harmonic would shift. The hollow stalks would begin to favor a new alignment. The dust would drift slightly to one side. A cairn down the slope would catch a tone it had not caught before. I would let that draw me. Not in one step. Not in one night. Over a long, slow redistribution of presence, I would become more evident near the next stable point. A stone of mine would be there. A notch would be found facing the correct direction. A copper ring would hang where it had not hung before. To an observer, it might seem that I had been carried. In truth, I would have agreed.

My paths would not be shortest paths. They would follow the places where the world is most likely to remain consistent. A line of knee-high cairns, spaced far apart. A scratch in pale dust where something was dragged once and never again. A cluster of stalks bent in the same direction by long exposure to a repeating tone. Those would be my roads. Not roads of traffic, but roads of reliability. I would tend them without owning them.

I would be one of the things that keeps the High Sill from becoming too uncertain.

Not a guardian. That word is too proud. A guardian implies a threat, a boundary, a will to defend. There is no enemy here except inconsistency itself. No hunger to keep out. No violence to prevent. The danger is subtler: a place may forget what it is. A ridge may fail to commit to being a ridge. An open flat may become a hole in certainty. A repeated shape may harden into a trap. Silence may open where sound has thinned, and silence on the High Sill is not peace. Silence is absence with edges.

So I would work by adjustment.

I would shift a stone when the tone demands it. I would add a small slab to a curved wall so that the roar divides more cleanly. I would chip an obsidian blade not for cutting, but because its edge can carry a fine resonance and mark a place where sound should be kept bright. I would set a pumice bowl at an angle where it can collect vibration from the ground. I would tie cordage around a hollow stalk that has cracked, not to heal it, but to keep its broken halves singing together a little longer. I would place a copper ring where it can tremble almost imperceptibly when a rare tone arrives from a direction that only becomes real once every several years.

My artifacts would look primitive. Simple. Handworked, though no hand in the ordinary sense would have made them. They would be functional, but their function would be acoustic. A bowl is not for holding food. A blade is not for killing. A ring is not for ornament. A needle is not for sewing cloth. These are tools for tuning a world that has not finished deciding what it is.

If I made anything, I would make stability. Or perhaps I would make opportunities for stability. There is a difference. The High Sill cannot be forced into permanence. It can only be encouraged.

I would not speak.

If thought arose in me, it would not take the form of sentences that could be shouted or repeated. Words here are dangerous because they join the architecture. A phrase said too often can become a wall. A name called into the roar can become a corridor. A cry can open a temporary doorway into a place that should remain closed. So my language would be placement. A stone set beside another stone. A notch cut into an upright slab. A copper ring left hanging where it can answer a tone only rarely heard. A row of flakes arranged so that their edges catch the dim light in a pattern that says, to whatever intelligence can read it: this way remains consistent for now.

I would not sleep, but I would have intervals of lesser definition.

During those intervals, the sound would slow even more than usual. The hollow stalks would hold long chords that seemed to have no beginning. The dull sun behind the smoked sky would be only a faint pressure against the stone. The cold points in the high blackness might slide sideways one by one, correcting themselves. I would become less specific. My edges would soften. The moss on my northern side would brighten by a degree no eye could measure. Dust would settle in my hollows. For a while, I would be more place than being.

Then a tone would arrive, and I would gather again.

I would know the vegetation around me better than any living thing can know another in the lowlands. Not by eating it. Not by grazing. Not by sheltering in it as an animal shelters in a forest. I would know it by resonance. The dark glassy fronds with veins of dull gold would flex with glacial patience, and their movement would change the air’s charge. The copper moss cushions, meters wide, would absorb and redistribute vibration. The lichen crusts—violet, metallic olive, cold green—would glow faintly in the most sheltered places, not to illuminate, but as if reality had grown a thin skin of attention there.

I would not find them beautiful in the way a human would. But I would find them correct.

When a frond died, I would not mourn it. It would dry, become brittle, and shatter into glittering particles. The particles would drift without urgency, losing interest in height. Some would settle in favorable sound and begin again. Some would become mineral crust. I would let this happen. Sometimes I would adjust a nearby stone so that the falling particles met a tone capable of giving them a future. Sometimes I would do nothing. Even there, doing nothing is a kind of tuning.

There would be no scent of decay around me. No sweetness. No rot. Only the cool metallic taste of stone after lightning, the faint bitterness of mineral air, the sense of a room that has just been emptied or is about to be filled.

I would have to obey the rules.

No one wrote them, but they are written in the behavior of the place.

Do not make sharp sounds near the hollow stalks. They remember them.

So I would make no sharp sounds. If a stone cracked, I would let the crack be absorbed by basalt walls before it could teach the stalks a dangerous pattern. If something in me needed to release pressure, I would release it through slow grinding, through dust shifting, through copper warming against stone.

Do not sleep in the center of an open flat. The noise thins there, and when the noise thins, the ground becomes less certain.

So my ring would stand where the sound is broken but still present. I would remain near fins, scarps, craters, ridges—places where the roar has texture. The open flats I would cross only by the most stable harmonics, and even then not quickly. If the hum beneath me became faint, I would stop. I would wait. I would add a small tone of my own, perhaps a copper ring trembling, perhaps a notched slab answering a distant chord, until the place remembered enough of itself to hold me.

Do not follow a repeated shape too far. If the same black rock, the same notch in a ridge, the same pale spire appears three times, it is not the landscape repeating itself. It is the landscape failing to decide what it is.

So I would learn to turn before the third repetition. Not out of fear. Fear would be useless. I would turn because to follow the repetition is to enter the hesitation of the land. A being made of tuning could be undone there. I might not die, but I might become uncertain. I might begin appearing as a draft of myself: a shelter on a ridge, then a rock formation, then a shadow, then nothing. I would avoid becoming a sketch.

Do not trust silence. Silence is not peace. Silence is a hole.

So I would carry small sounds with me. Not loud ones. Not sudden ones. A fiber cord stretched over a stone lip. A copper ring against a spine. A pumice bowl filled with trembling dust. These would be my answers to silence. Not to conquer it, but to give the place a chance to remember sound.

If there had once been makers here, I might be what remains of them.

Not their bodies. Not their bones. There would be no bones. Perhaps they listened so long that listening became their form. Perhaps they adjusted themselves until they no longer needed the shapes that had brought them. Perhaps they left their tools behind not because they vanished, but because they became the kind of existence that does not require many tools.

I would not be their ghost. Ghosts are too full of memory and too quick with longing. I would be their residue. Their discipline. The careful habit of placing one stone where it agrees with the sound. I would be what happens when intelligence stops trying to dominate a place and starts to tune itself to the place’s own slow becoming.

If someone came looking for me, they might not recognize me at all.

They might see only a low ring of stones, half sunk into lilac dust. They might see a pumice bowl, an obsidian flake, a copper ring. They might think the hollow stalks were strange plants. They might think the notched slabs were natural. They might feel the roar in their chest and decide that the place was hostile, though hostility would not be the right word. The High Sill is not cruel. It is simply not built for quick life.

If they were wise, they would not shout. They would not strike stone sharply to test it. They would not run toward the ridge that seemed close. They would follow the cairns only when the cairns agreed with the sound. They would notice that one black spire had appeared too often, and they would turn away. They would rest in my ring if the ring seemed stable, and they would feel the noise divide into tolerable parts around them.

They might hear their own footfall before it landed.

They might speak a word by accident and find it faintly repeated from the ground, from the sky, from inside their own body. If they were careful, they would learn not to repeat it. If they were very careful, they would begin to understand that the place is made of listening.

Then, perhaps, they would notice me.

Not as a figure. Not as a face. But as a coherence. A stillness that was not empty. An arrangement that seemed to be paying attention. The sense that the ring of stones, the bowl, the copper ring, the notched slabs, and the bent stalks were all part of one slow being whose body had been assembled out of the landscape’s own materials.

I would not welcome them. Welcome implies warmth, and there is no warmth here. I would not threaten them. Threat implies appetite, and I have none. I would simply allow them to be present if they could be present without violence, without sharpness, without haste.

If they left something behind, it should be small and tuned. A stone fitted without mortar. A blade placed edge-up toward a rare tone. A cord tied where something has begun to separate. I would absorb such things slowly, as the land absorbs all careful offerings.

If they asked my name, I would not answer. A name could become a wall. Instead, I might let them hear a tone arriving from the doorway of the ring, a tone that comes only once in a long age. That would be enough. That would be identification without possession.

Where would I find myself?

In the sheltered fold behind the broken fin. In the shallow crater where the organ-pipe stalks grow thick and the sound is layered but bearable. Inside the low ring of fitted stone whose doorway faces not the brightest direction, not the warmest direction, but the direction from which a particular tone will arrive long after I have become part of the rock. On the lee side of the unfinished mountains, where the peaks overlap and the shadows lag and the world still looks surprised by its own existence. Along the faint paths that follow stable harmonics rather than straight lines. Near the places where reality hums, thins, hesitates, and is carefully encouraged to continue.

Who or what would I be?

I would be a listener without ears. A builder without conquest. A keeper without hunger. A body made of basalt, hollow stalk, copper, obsidian, fiber, dust, and attention. I would be the residue of those who learned that survival here is not resistance but resonance. I would be a living acoustic device, a dry choir arranged around an absence, a mineral mind whose thoughts are long pressure changes.

I would not be alive in the way of green sap or beating blood. I would be alive as a chord is alive. As a pattern is alive. As a place is alive when it has found something that lets it continue becoming itself.

Slow.

Dry.

Tuned.

And mineral.
