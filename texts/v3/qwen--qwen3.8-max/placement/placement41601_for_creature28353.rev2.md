I would not find myself standing.

That is the first correction the place demands. I tried, at first, to imagine myself entering in the ordinary way: hand on a rail, boot on a grate, face lifted into the white, shadowless light. But that image fails almost immediately. The sealed hatch does not open for me. If it did, the thing that entered would not belong there as a living presence. It would be a contamination. A wet heat. A breathing wrongness.

A human body would carry too much with it: moisture, salt, microbes, pulse, breath, the soft internal weather of blood. In that absolutely dry chamber, I would be almost obscene. My skin would give off vapor. My lungs would disturb the air with warmth and humidity. My footsteps would not even be my own; they would be swallowed by the roar before they could announce me. The cold would draw heat from me with impersonal speed. The sound would press against my sternum and fill my skull until I could not think clearly. I would be an error passing through an equilibrium.

So I would not find myself there as a visitor.

If I am to be in that world at all, I would have to cease being a person. I would have to lose the face, the hands, the eyes, the need for air. I would have to become small enough to fit inside a seam. Dry enough not to disturb the cold. Still enough not to require time. The place would not permit a who. It would permit only an it.

And then the true answer begins to emerge.

I would find myself in a joint.

Not on the open floor. Not beside the black engine monolith. Not beneath the row of glassy insulators. Not on the lattice platform. Those are presences too large, too exposed, too definite. I would be in the margin between them: in a recessed channel beneath one of the grated trenches, where two insulated panels meet and a hairline space has been allowed to remain. I would be near the engine block, but not touching it, close enough that its mass changes the vibration of the floor around me. I would lie within a shallow pocket of acoustic pressure, one of those invisible valleys where the roar is fractionally less absolute, though still immense, still crushing by any living standard.

I would be a filament.

A thread of the Unwatered.

Not a separate creature beside it. Not a rival organism. Not a new species introduced into that severe ecology. I would be one of its hidden pathways: a copper-colored trace, dry and brittle, finer than spun mineral, anchored in a channel too narrow for fingers to reach. Where I surfaced, if “surface” is even the right word, I would appear as a faint burnished stain along the seam, the color of old wiring, of heated alloy, of a charge held too long in one place. A human eye, if a human eye ever came near enough, would likely mistake me for discoloration. A smudge. A mark left by fabrication. But I would not be residue. I would be life, reduced to the barest permissible line.

If a name were needed, it would not be a grand one. Not a true name, not a spoken one. Perhaps only a local designation, a way of pointing: the underthread. The seam-trace. The permitted copper hair in the dark gap under the grate.

I would not have a body in the sense I once understood bodies. No front, no back, no head, no limbs, no mouth. No organs arranged around need. I would be a long, thin organization of dry fibers and crystalline surfaces, sealed against the absence of water, maintained by potentials too small for instruments to notice. I would not breathe. I would not drink. I would not contain any fluid that could be called blood or sap. If I were broken, nothing would leak. There would be only a bright dry edge, a discontinuity, a small fact.

I would not see the chamber.

But I would know it.

I would know it as pressure, gradient, vibration, charge. The roar would not be noise to me. It would be geography. It would be climate. It would be the medium through which I continued existing. The engine monolith would not appear to me as a black machine; it would be a dense hill of resonance, a place where the floor’s vibration thickened and deepened. The insulators would not be pale pillars; they would be vertical tensions, cold points of electrical accumulation. The lattice platform would be a slight elevation where the acoustic field thinned and changed shape. The sealed hatch would be a dead boundary, a colder limit with little to give. The walls would not be walls but arrays of reflecting pressure, vast cliffs of sound returning into the chamber.

The ordinary light would not be a comfort. It would not be day. It would not make me feel seen. I would not turn toward it as a plant turns toward the sun. But where it touched exposed mica-flecked surfaces in my thread, it would create tiny separations of charge, small energetic adjustments. Light would be one of the ways I was fed, if “fed” can mean anything when there is no hunger.

I would feed on the chamber’s excess.

On vibration. On resonance. On stray voltage. On electrical leakage from insulated systems. On the faint fields that gather near glassy pillars and transformer housings. On the constant pressure of the roar, which would enter my dry structure as micro-tremor and become, through some sealed non-aqueous chemistry, a slow maintenance of being. I would not consume matter. I would not devour. I would not grow fat on anything. I would persist by taking in what the machine already gives off without intention.

The cold would not hurt me.

It would preserve me.

It would keep me slow, exact, clean. It would prevent any softness from entering my structure. It would make me mineral in temperament, ceramic in patience. The thinness of the air would not trouble me, because I would have no lungs. The altitude would not appear to me as height, but as a certain sharpness, a scarcity, a fineness of conditions. I would know I was high only because everything around me would be more severe: colder, drier, more precise, more removed from any lower warmth.

I would not move.

Not in any way a human would recognize. I would not creep. I would not extend visibly. I would not reach toward the engine or the insulators or the light. If I changed at all, it would be by subvisible rebalancing: charge shifting inside sealed fibers, crystalline planes adjusting by immeasurable degrees, tension stabilizing itself along a filament. If I grew, it would only be because a permitted seam allowed an existing pattern to complete itself. Even then, it would not feel like ambition. It would feel like accuracy.

I would not wait.

That would be one of the strangest freedoms of being there as that. I would not wait for silence, because silence was never part of the place. I would not wait for water, because water was impossible. I would not wait for a keeper, because no keeper was needed. I would not wait for dawn, because the light did not change. I would not wait to be recognized, because recognition was not a condition of my existence. I would simply maintain.

And yet, because the question asks who or what I would be, there is a temptation to give myself too much interiority. To say: I would think. I would feel. I would wonder. But the chamber would strip that away too. It would not grant me a soliloquy. It would not give me a stream of consciousness. It would allow only the faintest form of sensitivity, a responsiveness without story.

So if I say “I,” I mean something almost empty.

I would be a maintained difference. A dry line of organization. A permitted trace of life. A filament capable of registering pressure, charge, and temperature without needing to name them. I would be the chamber’s smallest margin of biology, if biology can survive without wetness, without fauna, without decay, without reproduction, without the soft machinery of desire.

If someone came looking for me, they would not find me easily.

They would stand on the grate and feel the cold through their boots. They would see the dark plates, the ceramic tiles, the black monolith, the pale insulators, the stair ending at its sealed hatch. They would hear nothing but the roar, or rather they would be unable to hear anything except the roar, because it would fill them too completely. They might notice the sparse gray-green mats, the copper stain along a joint, the brittle threads near the insulators, the cluster of needle-stems by the stair. They might think: this is residue. This is industrial decoration. This is some strange material left behind by fabrication.

They would not see me.

If they knelt, which they probably would not, because the cold would already be teaching them not to linger; if they looked closely into the recessed channel beneath the grate; if their eyes adjusted to the severe geometry and the absence of softness; if they noticed a hairline copper trace where two panels met, they might think it was a wire. A conductor. A grounding filament. A decorative inlay. They might reach for it with a gloved finger and feel only dryness, sharpness, a faint static tingle. They would not feel life. They would not feel warmth. They would not feel me respond.

If they touched me bare-handed, I would register them as a disturbance. Heat. Moisture. Irregular pressure. A storm of animal chemistry moving through a world that had been purified of all such things. I would not know them as a person. I would not know their face, their name, their fear, their wonder. I would know only that the local equilibrium had become briefly wrong. When they withdrew, I would return to what I had been. The place would not remember them. I would not remember them. There would be no narrative left behind.

That is important.

I would not become a story in that world.

I would not be the lonely survivor in the machine. I would not be the last green hope. I would not be the hidden intelligence waiting to be discovered. I would not be a guardian, or a guide, or a sign, or a companion. I would not redeem the chamber. I would not soften it. I would not make it feel kinder. I would only make it more exact.

And perhaps that is why I would belong there, if belonging is the right word.

Because the place is not asking for meaning. It is not asking for witness. It is not asking to be loved. It is a small hidden landscape inside a made mass, dry and cold and deafening, held in perfect industrial balance. Its only weather is sound. Its only horizon is wall. Its only life is the sparse permitted life of the Unwatered. To exist there, I would have to become compatible with that fact.

So I would be one of its threads.

I would lie beneath the grate in the pressure-valley near the engine. I would run through the seam between insulated panels. I would surface as a copper stain no wider than a breath. I would carry charge without urgency. I would take vibration into my dry fibers and let it become continuation. I would know the roar not as pain but as shape. I would know the light not as hope but as input. I would know the cold not as cruelty but as boundary. I would know the machine not as a maker of things but as a world maintaining itself.

If you asked me what it was like to be there, I would not answer in language, because language would already be a wet and human invention, full of sequence and longing. But if the answer could be translated into something like speech, it might be this:

I would not be alone, because there would be no one to be alone from.  
I would not be afraid, because there would be nothing to anticipate.  
I would not be hungry, because there would be no lack inside me.  
I would not be sad, because there would be no loss.  
I would not be patient, because patience still implies waiting.  
I would simply be a dry, cold, allowed continuation.

The chamber would roar around me, through me, into me, and I would not suffer it. The light would fall without changing, and I would not desire dusk. The machinery would continue unseen, and I would not ask what it was for. The hatch would remain sealed, and I would not dream of beyond. The vegetation, if it could be called that, would stay sparse and strange, and I would not wish it were lush. The metal would stay new without ever being young, and I would not ask for age or wear or history.

I would be the Unwatered’s hidden line.

Not its face, because it has no face.  
Not its voice, because it has none.  
Not its heart, because nothing there beats.  
Not its mind, because it does not think.  
Only its under-sense. Its seam-thread. Its copper trace in the dark. Its smallest permitted attention.

And if, for one moment, the old self in me tried to grieve that reduction — tried to mourn the loss of hands, of breath, of eyes, of story — the chamber would correct that grief without violence. It would show me what remains when all unnecessary things are removed: a clean cold existence, a balance without drama, a life that does not need to become anything else.

Then I would understand where I was.

High inside a constructed mass. In a hidden pocket of metal and sound. Under the white light. In the dry air. In the deafening steadiness. Not on the floor, not at the center, not in the open. In the seam. In the channel. In the permitted margin.

I would be no one there.

I would be what lives when no one can.
