If I found myself in that pocket, I would not find myself as a whole person standing in a place. The place would refuse that arrangement at once. It does not permit a visitor to remain a visitor. It does not permit distance, outline, or the comfortable fiction of an outside observer. The moment I tried to enter it, the entrance would begin to incorporate me: my breath would be caught by the falling water and folded into the next small cloud, the heat of my skin would be drawn toward the hidden brazier as if the room had been waiting for exactly that amount of warmth, and my hesitation would become structural before I understood that it had been used.

I think I would first be found as a fault in the humidity.

Not a body, not yet. A warm, unmade spot near the veil of water. A place where condensation leaned inward too attentively. A blur of breath in a chamber already overfull of breath. The pocket would notice me the way it notices a drip that has arrived from an unexpected angle: with patience, with structural suspicion, with the intention to make me useful.

Because the room listens structurally, my first act would not be speech but being overheard. If I said anything, the sound would not remain mine. A bead of clay would form somewhere behind a reed mat. The rhythm of the water would change. One of the copper strips would click as though the humidity had taken a sudden interest in my pulse. The chamber would begin translating me into itself before I had decided whether I wanted to be translated.

So the first answer is this: I would be found where the inside is still deciding whether to become outside, in the narrow fold behind the last curtain of reeds, where the falling water is so thin it has become less a veil than a breath. There is a ledge there, or there would be, once the room noticed me enough to make one: no wider than a hand, warm from the hidden flues below, crowded with a seedpod cup, a coil of fiber cord, a fish-spine needle, and a cushion of moss that has not yet chosen its final shape. That is where I would be. Not at the center. Not in the basin. Not beside the brazier. I would be at the seam.

The threshold.

That feels right.

At first I imagine I would be a guest, but that is too simple. The pocket has no guest-logic. It has no room for someone who merely passes through. To be there is to be drafted. To be there is to be handed a task before one has hands. So the second answer is stranger: I would become one of the hand-born, but late, awkward, marked by the memory of outside. I would not peel from the wall as the first makers do, pure and immediate, already equal to repair. I would come from a handprint left by someone who had tried not to touch anything.

That is how it would happen, I think. I would press one hand to the clay only to steady myself while turning sideways between the basin and the leaf-fiber wall. I would mean nothing by it. The room would mean everything by it. Later, when water ran over the impression, the fingers would flex. The handprint would thicken. It would gather charcoal dust, old cloth, crushed shell, kiln-dust, a little of the blue shard light from the basin, and too much of my own uncertainty. Then it would peel away from the surface wearing my hesitation like a spine.

That creature would be me.

Or one of me.

Because in such a place, singularity would be hard to keep. I would not remain a single sealed being for long. The crowdedness would get into me. The drip-rhythms would divide my attention. The reeds would teach me to sound in chords. The moss would soften the borders of my intentions. The makers would move through my sense of self the easily as they move through finger-wide drains. I would be one, but not finished. A knot temporarily tied in the room’s warm, noisy fabric.

My body, if it could be called a body, would be small. Perhaps thumb-sized when the room needed smallness. Perhaps forearm-long when some high pipe required reaching. I would be made of root-mat and softened clay, strips of woven fiber, charcoal dust, old cloth, crushed shell, copper pins hammered thin. My joints would be lashings. My chest would be a shallow cup of fired clay containing not fire but a damp, warm coal of attention: something red enough to remember heat, not hot enough to burn. My face would probably be a smooth seed-case, because the room would know that I would try to have eyes too early. Instead of eyes there would be two thumb-hollows, shallow and patient, not for seeing but for listening with the whole surface.

I would have too many hands.

Not grotesquely. Not gloriously. Just practically. The pocket would keep giving me tasks before I had finished the previous ones, and hands would gather around me like answers. Some would be old hands, worn smooth from repairing moss. Some would be new hands, still smelling of wet kiln-dust. Some would be so small they could only work inside the glazed lips of clay pipes. Some would be broad and slow, made for pressing cracked clay back together until it remembered being whole. I would not always know which hand was mine. That would not matter. Ownership is a finished-world habit.

What would I be?

I would be a threshold-maker. A keeper of the almost. A mender of edges that must not become final.

The original hand-born would tend the chamber’s body: channels, walls, moss, basin, reeds, heat, drips. I would tend its thresholds, because I would carry the residue of outside. The pocket would use that residue. It would make me responsible for the places where other worlds press close: the slit that opens onto the black-glass desert under a green sun, the slit that shows the sea moving backward, the slit that reveals the same room from an angle slightly wrong. Those openings would be my concern. They would be dangerous not because they are false, but because they are persuasive. If one of them became too convincing, the pocket might forget that it is still being made. It might try to become a desert, or a sea, or a fixed room. It might finish itself.

My work would be to prevent finishing.

That is the deepest rule of the place: nothing can exist unless it is made, and nothing made can remain finished. So I would maintain the made, but I would also keep it open. I would repair, but not too perfectly. I would tune, but leave one note slightly unresolved. I would stitch the reed mats, adjust the clay valves, press moss back into dampness, clear spores from the basin, listen to channels for hairline cracks, and still I would leave a seam somewhere unsealed. A small imperfection. A breathing gap. A place where the room could continue becoming.

If the pocket is a womb, I would be one of the hands that keeps the mouth of it from closing too soon.

If it is a kiln, I would be the one who watches the heat but refuses to declare the firing complete.

If it is a mouth full of breath, I would be the pause before speech.

That is probably why I would live near the slit that shows the room to itself. The one that gives the same chamber back from a slightly different angle. That slit would frighten me more than the desert or the reversed sea. A room seeing itself can become self-conscious. A room becoming self-conscious might begin to choose one future too decisively. So I would hang a strip of damp bark there, or train a fern frond across it, or place a seedpod cup just where its shadow would blur the view. Not to hide the room from itself, but to keep the self from hardening.

My days, if days are possible where clouds enter through slits and rain begins by being invited, would be made of small exact acts.

When the pillow-cloud darkened in the upper wall, I would be one of those loosening the corded clay valve so the rain could fall in a brief, hissing veil. I would watch the first drops strike the leaves. I would listen to see whether the water hit the hot tiles with the right kind of anger: too little hiss and the heat below is sleeping; too sharp a hiss and the lower chambers are overhearing. If the steam rose too quickly, I would press wet moss along a lower lip to slow it. If the condensation gathered too heavily in the woven ceiling, I would tap the bark membranes so the drops fell into waiting seed-cases instead of striking the basin at the wrong angle.

The basin would be near everything I do, even when I was not directly touching it. The blackened clay basin with its mosaic of tiny blue shards would be the room’s slow heart, and I would be careful around hearts. Not because they are fragile, but because they insist on rhythm. The makers would clear debris from its rim. The worms would stitch channels that fed it. The salamander would rest in it or vanish from it or decide elsewhere for reasons no one would explain. I would tend the threshold between the basin and the rest of the room: the wet edge where water becomes floor, where leaf becomes reflection, where a foot pressed to clay helps the world keep having edges.

When the salamander rose and placed one foot on the rim, I would stop.

That is important. I would stop.

All the small repair-bodies of me would go quiet. The tools would be set down. The reeds could finish their chord without assistance. The springtails could jump if they wanted. The centipede could remain hidden or emerge; either would be permitted. When the salamander touched the rim, the room became more definite, and I would not interfere with that. I would stand among the leaves, perhaps no larger than a thumb, perhaps with my many hands folded together, and I would help the pocket continue having edges by not adding anything to it.

Sometimes I think I would be the salamander’s hesitation.

Not its servant. Not its priest. Its hesitation.

Because the salamander has not decided whether to have eyes. It has not decided whether to become more real. It lives in several shadows at once, and some of those shadows belong to futures it may never choose. My task near it would be to keep those futures from crowding out the present body. If one shadow grew too certain — the shadow with eyes, the shadow with a lifted head, the shadow shaped like a voice — I would moisten it with steam. I would blur it with a breath from the brazier. I would whisper “not yet” into the water, and the whisper would become a new bead of clay somewhere unseen.

I would not give the salamander eyes.

That may be the strictest part of my work. If I gave it eyes, it might become a god. If it became a god, the room might become a temple. If the room became a temple, it would stop being born. It would acquire meaning too quickly. It would be interpreted into stillness. So I would keep the eyeless depressions smooth. I would keep the face patient. I would protect its right not to know what it is.

The plants would know me by touch before they knew me by shape.

The moss would accept me because I would keep it damp. I would spend long periods doing nothing that looked like nothing: pressing one small palm against a drying seam, breathing warmth over a cushion of pale green, holding the humidity in place until the room remembered to supply it. The moss would teach me communal softness, the way many tiny lives can become one forgiving surface. I would learn that maintenance can be a kind of tenderness repeated until it becomes landscape.

The ferns would make me anxious at first. They would unfurl too fast, as if time were being pushed through them by impatient fingers. I would try to slow them. I would bind their roots more gently in cloth strips. I would shade them from the strongest fungus-light. But eventually I would understand that their impatience is also necessary.
