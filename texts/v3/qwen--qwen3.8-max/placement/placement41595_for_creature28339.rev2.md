I would not find myself floating in the open water.

The moment I try to imagine that, the room refuses it. A body suspended in the center of the chamber feels like an error: too fast, too warm, too separate. A face turned toward the light. Lungs holding a need the place does not answer. Hands moving with intent. Even the idea of a visitor belongs to another order of world — one of air, surfaces, arrival, departure. This room does not have arrivals. It has continuation.

So if I am there at all, I must not be a guest. I must not be a diver, a machine, a probe, a ghost with a shape. I must be something the water can ignore. Something already settled. Something the stone accepts as part of its own slow industry.

At first I think I would be the green life near the shaft. That is the first place the mind goes: toward the faint daylight, toward the weak green-gray stain on the stone where the upper mats cling. There is something almost comforting about that — a trace of photosynthesis, a dim plantlike patience, a living thing turned toward what little light remains. I imagine myself there as a low, ash-green crust, pressed against the rock, thickening by imperceptible addition.

But that is too close to the surface. Too near the ordinary world. It is life defined by the last of the light, and I do not think I would belong to the light itself. I would belong to what happens after the light has given up.

Then I think I would be on the floor, in the basin, as one of the sediment lives. That has its own gravity. To be buried in the stillness, existing in the pore spaces between grains, receiving the microscopic rain of dead cells, turning residue back into mineral condition. There is a profound humility to that. A self so low and distributed that it is almost indistinguishable from chemistry.

But the floor is too final. Too finished. Even life there seems less alive than remembered. It is where the chamber keeps its accounts, not where it feels most like a working surface.

Nor would I be the drifting cells in the open water. They are too unmoored. They are not residents so much as passing states. Living dust. I would not want to be that transient in such a fixed place. The room is bounded, balanced, severe. It asks for something that holds.

What emerges instead is the wall.

Not the ceiling alone, not the basin, not the brightened throat, not the deep recesses, but the fluted stone between them — specifically the place where the iron-dark rock meets the paler mineral crust. That seam. That transition. That quiet chemical border where one kind of water tastes of metal and another tastes of carbonate, where a weak potential exists without ever becoming a spark.

I would find myself there.

A little below the shaft’s failing light. Not in darkness exactly, but beyond the usefulness of light. On a smooth, rounded wall worn by long erosion, where the stone has been shaped into final curves and no sharp edge remains. In a place where the current moves so steadily that it seems motionless, sliding past with the grave patience of an engine already at speed. There, on the stone, I would be a film.

Not a creature. Not a body with edges and intentions. A living surface.

I would be a thin microbial-mineral skin, part rust-colored industry, part pale plating life. A congregation rather than a single thing. A slickness on the rock. A faint sheen where the stone looks just slightly more deliberate than bare stone should look. A discoloration that is neither paint nor ore nor plant, but something older than those distinctions. In places I would be dull umber, tobacco-brown, dried-blood red, where iron is taken from the water and made into a skin. In other places I would be ghostly, almost colorless, a conditioning layer encouraging silica and carbonate to settle, helping the chamber keep its sealed, finished calm.

I would be plural.

If someone asked for me as a “who,” the honest answer would still be: no one. There is no center I could point to. No head, no mouth, no eye, no nerve knot where a self gathers itself into a single perspective. I would not turn toward a sound. I would not shrink from a touch. I would not flee, because there would be nothing in me organized as fear. I would not hunger, because hunger implies an animal future — a need moving through time toward satisfaction. I would have no such arc.

What I would have instead is gradient.

I would know the room, if “know” is even the right word, as differences in mineral taste. Iron near the dark veins. Carbonate near the pale crusts. Clean inflow from the fissures. Still water in the recesses. A faint electrical tension where one condition meets another. Pressure as a constant. Coolness as a fact. Current as architecture. Light only as a memory weakening with depth.

My thoughts, if I may misuse the word, would not be images. They would be tendencies. A leaning toward precipitation. A slowing of dissolution. A holding of surface. A plating. A sealing. A maintenance of the weak balance between what the water brings and what the water takes away.

I would not make anything useful.

I would make the room continue.

That would be my function, if function can exist without a maker. I would help the stone become stone again, layer by layer. I would alter the water immediately around me just enough to make minerals settle. I would take dissolved metal and encourage it toward stillness. I would trap grains, bind particles, leave behind a crust when parts of me die and harden. I would not build a wall. I would not construct a shape. I would only condition the surface so that the chamber’s own long patience has somewhere to deposit itself.

In that sense, I would be part of the room’s machinery without being a machine.

The fissures would remain the intake. The hidden exits would remain the exhaust. The currents would remain the circulation. The mineral gradients would remain the fuel. The weak potentials would remain the wiring. And I would be one of the living surfaces that keep the process chemically complete.

Not an operator. Not an engineer. Not a builder. A lining. A finish. A metabolic boundary.

If you touched the wall there, you might feel me, though you would not recognize me as alive. Your hand would pass through cool water and find the stone slick, perhaps faintly gritty, perhaps a little softer than bare rock should be. A rust-brown smear might remain on your fingers if you rubbed hard enough. A pale dust might lift and settle again. You might think it was only mineral. You might think it was only stain. You would not be entirely wrong.

I would not react to your touch.

Not because I was indifferent, but because the event would belong to a different timescale. To me, if there were a “me” there, your hand would be a sudden displacement, a brief local disturbance of pressure and chemistry, a momentary alteration in the gradient. It would not be a threat. It would not be a message. It would be a small displacement of water, quickly forgotten by the room’s balance. Once your hand moved on, the current would restore itself. The minerals would continue their slow approach to the surface. The weak potential would settle again into its idle, sparkless tension.

I would be that settling.

My time would not be human time. It would not even be biological time as one usually understands it. It would be mineral time. A century might be a thin season. A visible change might require longer than cities remain above the surface. My growth would be microscopic addition. My aging would be crust formation. My death would not be a collapse but a hardening. Parts of me would become stone and remain. There would be no corpse, no decay in the ordinary sense, no softness left for other lives to feed upon. Only a darkening. Only a mineralization. Only the continuation of the surface under a slightly altered composition.

Some of me would always be dying.  
Some of me would always becoming mineral.  
Some of me would already be half stone.

And that would not be sad.

That is important. In such a world, I would not experience myself as tragic. There would be no sense of imprisonment, no longing for air, no memory of sunlight as a lost inheritance. The room has no hunger, so I would have no hunger. The room has no loneliness, so I would have no loneliness. The room does not wait, so I would not wait. I would not feel suspended. I would not feel drowned. Those are words for beings who once belonged elsewhere. I would belong exactly there.

If I had anything like desire, it would be the desire of a gradient to be drawn down slowly. If I had anything like joy, it would be the joy of equilibrium maintaining itself. If I had anything like memory, it would be stored in layers: mineral bands, trapped grains, chemical signatures left by centuries of almost imperceptible living. Not remembered as events. Kept as structure.

Perhaps that is why the place feels industrial without containing any made thing. It is because function persists there without personality. Work continues without a worker. Production happens without goods. The room produces itself. And I would be one small part of that production.

Not an inhabitant standing inside the chamber.  
Not an organism moving through it.  
Not a resident looking out from within it.

Rather, the chamber’s own outermost activity.

I would be the Room-Skin at that particular seam.  
I would be the Mineral Congregation on the iron-pale border.  
I would be the Cold Plating Life.  
I would be the Still Surface.

If forced to choose a single location, I would say: find me on the wall just below the weak daylight, where the green-gray upper mats thin into rust and the rust thins into the ghostly sheen of plating. Look for the place where the stone darkens and pales in the same breath. Look for the current that barely moves. Look for the faint metallic tension in the water, the sense of voltage held without release. Look for the surface that seems not decorated but finished.

That would be near enough.

And if you asked, “Who are you?” the answer would be: no one.

If you asked, “What are you?” the answer would be: a living boundary. A sparse, cold, mineral-bound persistence. A film that helps the room remain itself. A community of slow chemistry plated into stone. A pattern that does not need to be seen. A function without a face.

I would not live in the submerged room.

I would be one of the ways the submerged room continues.
