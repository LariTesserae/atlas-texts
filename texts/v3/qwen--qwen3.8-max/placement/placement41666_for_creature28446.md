I would find myself low, in the middle distance of the basin, where the accepted ground thins into pale crust and every path has to be trusted with care.

Not at a center, because the city has no clean center. Not on the high shoulders, because they are blunt, damp, and already stitched with pipes. I would be in one of the packed seams between a pump gallery and a settling basin, in a room built into the gap under a roof meadow and above a condenser alley. The room would be small, warm by basin standards, and crowded with useful things: a bed raised near a heat duct, a window shelf holding a lamp, a jar of filtered water, a level wrapped in waxed cloth, a pot of mint with dark leaves pressed against the glass. Moss would have begun to take the sill. A coat would hang by the door, cuffs darkened, pockets weighted with a probe, copper pins, folded flags, and a small dry cloth.

At first I thought I would be a surveyor, because the place is so full of stakes and measurements. But the basin would make me something less final than that. I would be a crust-scribe: part ground-tender, part cartographer, part listener for settling. One of the low-voiced people who walk before shift-change with an iron rod and an amber lamp, reading the half-written sentences of the ground.

My body would be basin-shaped. Low center of gravity. Soft-soled boots broad enough to spread weight over young fill. Knees slightly bent, shoulders kept in from the wind, hands never far from a rail, a pipe, a wall, a cable. I would move with the economy everyone learns there: no wasted steps, no careless crossings, no running on unmarked crusts. My hood would be lined with dried moss for warmth. My fingertips would be stained green from root work, rust from iron, chalk from marl, and sometimes violet from mineral dust. My eyes would be accustomed to flattened light and small warnings: the too-pale sheen of new silt, a bead-line climbing brick, a bronze edge on a leaf near runoff, a stake leaning one finger’s width from yesterday’s angle.

I would carry a waxed ledger made of pressed root pulp, because paper in the basin must survive damp and handling. Its pages would not be maps in the old sense. They would be arguments. Records of load tolerance, drainage, expected settling, root strength, water memory. I would write in the basin’s tense, which is not “this is,” but “this is becoming,” “this is being persuaded,” “this has not yet failed.” A street might be listed under three names: what it was, what it is, and what it may be asked to become by next winter.

Mornings would begin quietly. I would step out onto duckboards still slick with condensation and listen before I looked. The city would be breathing in its muffled way: pumps under felt pads, water moving in clay-lined channels, birds stirring in crane frames, the soft tick of cooling metal. I would touch a wall to see whether it wept more than it should. I would kneel by a painted stake and press my palm to the crust beside it, feeling for hollowness, for cold wetness rising, for the faint mineral stiffness that says a bed is beginning to bind. If a surface rang false, I would mark it with cord, set a small flag, and redirect foot traffic with a lamp signal before the first carts came through.

I would not shout. Shouting would feel wrong there. I would communicate the way the basin does: hand taps, flag lifts, brief flashes, chalk marks, knots tied at agreed intervals. If I saw birds lifting suddenly from a roofline, I would stop and wait. If rats moved upward through wall cavities, I would check the lower pumps. If a goat refused a yard, I would not laugh; I would taste the air, look for discoloration in the moss, and call for a probe. The animals would be part of my instruments, not because they are magical, but because they are attentive.

You would find me most often at thresholds: where duckboard ends, where a lane becomes a channel, where a wall has been fed ballast and is being taught to stand, where a new sluice has changed the meaning of an alley overnight. I would spend hours near copper pins and surveyors’ rods, wiping condensation from gauges, leaving offerings of dry cloth at the bases of old machines whose dials still move. I would not think of that as superstition. It would seem more like manners. Some things deserve acknowledgment after long service. Some things deserve thanks for continuing.

My work would be repair as much as discovery. In the basin, repair is a civic art, and I would be one of its minor practitioners. A cracked crust would not simply be patched; it would be listened to, then answered with shell-grit, root mats, drainage cuts, or heat. A leaning wall might be braced, or fed, or partially dismantled so it could settle into a safer posture. A roof garden gone thin would receive new moss mats and be sewn down with staples. A courtyard that has begun to hold water might be reclassified as a settling pool and planted with sedges. Nothing would be treated as ruined merely because it changed. Ruin would be one possible transition among many.

I would know the planters by their stained hands and the pumpwrights by their listening faces. I would trade maps for seed cakes, dried moss, clean filter water, and news of which beds had accepted their fill. I would not own much land, because ownership there is a strange thing: plots are leased by load tolerance and expected lifespan as much as by area. Instead, I would carry responsibility for certain lines, certain stakes, certain vulnerable corners where the city is still deciding whether to become street, garden, channel, or wall.

If I had to name a single place where I belonged, I would say the room above the condenser alley, but that would only be where I sleep. My real place would be the network of routes I tend: the narrow passage behind the galvanic sheds, the catwalk between two chimneys, the duckboard crossing over a hollow that used to be an excavation pan, the roof meadow where the goats browse with their bells muted, the courtyard hidden behind curtains of wet green where the air smells of soil, mint, and cold iron.

In time, I think I would become less like a single person and more like a habit of the city. The one who appears after rain. The one who kneels beside a crack and does not panic. The one who plants a sedge where water has begun to gather, sets a pin where a path is still safe, wipes a dial clean, and updates the unfinished map by lamplight. I would be useful rather than emblematic. I would be trusted because I would not promise permanence. I would promise attention.
