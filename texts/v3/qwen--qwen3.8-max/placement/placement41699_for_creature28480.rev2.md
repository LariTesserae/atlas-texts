If I could be found in that world, I would not be found as a body walking through it.

There would be no feet pressing leaf-floors, no hands parting hanging membranes, no breath separate from the general breathing of the green country. I would not arrive. I would not cross the open platforms as a visitor. I would not leave tracks. There would be no tracks for me to leave.

If I were there at all, I would be there as the place itself is there: as a form made by pressure, water, heat, and response. Not a creature within the landscape, but a modification of it.

So the first answer is this:

I would not be a who.

I would be a where.

More precisely, I would be a where that behaves almost like a who.

---

At first I imagine myself as one of the towers.

That is the tempting image: a lone vertical structure standing in the open green, drawing water upward, humming when the wind crosses its hollows, shining with condensation, swelling until it splits. There is something dramatic about that. Something lonely. Something that resembles a singular self rising out of the wide terrain and announcing itself against the sky.

But that is too simple. Too solitary. Too much like the idea of a being rather than the truth of one.

A tower there is not a person. It is an event. It rises, fills, sounds, ruptures, is reabsorbed. It is magnificent, but it is not where I would rest. If I became a tower, I would be too exposed to my own bursting. I would be all ascent and release, all height and sudden failure. I would spend myself in one loud gesture after another.

I think I would be something lower. Something more patient. Something that receives what the towers release.

---

Perhaps a basin.

Not one of the deepest ones, not a great flooded court where storms spin themselves into silver spirals, but a shallow basin at the edge of an open platform, where the landscape seems to have made room for something that never arrives.

I would be a wide, low hollow in the living tissue of the country. My rim would be raised slightly above the surrounding surface, built of overlapping leaf-layers and thickened root-fiber, pale at the edges where new growth kept sealing it. The floor of me would not be stone or mud, but compressed plant-skin, glossy with moisture, darkening when water gathered there. In the hottest part of the day I would dry into a taut, warm cup, faintly steaming. In the next hour I might fill without warning, becoming a bright sheet of reflected sky.

I would not be still, even when I looked still.

Water would enter me from several fine channels, some so shallow they would appear only as bead-lines along seams. Some water would come as rain made locally by the landscape’s own exhalations. Some would well up from beneath, rising through capillary tissue until my surface trembled. Some would arrive carrying fragments: a curled shoot torn from a ruptured tower, a pale root-knot, a folded leaf-bud not yet opened. I would hold those fragments. I would turn them slowly in whatever current moved through me. I would not choose which of them might take hold.

That would be my nearest form of generosity.

I would keep them wet long enough for some of them to become something else.

---

But a basin alone is not enough.

A basin can be only containment. If I were only a basin, I would fill and overflow and fill again, beautiful but incomplete. I would be a cup, a mirror, a temporary nursery for drifting tissue. There is something appealing about that, but it is not the whole of what I would be. I think I would also need a way of sending water onward. I would need a seam, a lip, a controlled opening.

So I would be a basin with a threshold.

At one edge of me there would be a narrow drainage channel, raised and smooth, worn not by stone but by continuous living response. When I filled past a certain point, water would spill into that channel and travel outward across the platform, finding other hollows, other roots, other waiting surfaces. Sometimes the channel would close. Sometimes it would widen. Sometimes tissue would thicken at its mouth until only a thin trickle passed. Sometimes it would open suddenly and pour me out in a rush, leaving my floor dark, steaming, exposed.

That would be my speech.

Not voice. Not word. But release.

---

If I had to name the form I would take, I would say I am a listening hollow.

Not listening in the animal sense. I would have no ears. I would not turn toward sounds. I would not interpret them as messages. But I would be a place where the landscape’s noises collect and become slightly more patterned than they were before.

Water dripping from a high edge would fall into me and make a resonant note. Wind moving across my rim would produce a low hollow tone. Heat building under a nearby membrane would send a tremor through my surface. When a tower split somewhere far away, the change in pressure might move through the root-weave and reach me as a faint thickening, a sudden darkening, a small shift in the direction of my channels.

I would not hear these things.

I would be altered by them.

That alteration would be my version of attention.

---

I think I would be located in one of the emptier parts of the country.

Not at the center of a ceremonial court, not along a grand avenue of arching stems, not directly beside one of the tall hollow columns. Those forms are too definite. They draw the eye too strongly. I would be in the interval between such things: a broad, open stretch of plant-skin where the terrain seems almost featureless until one notices the subtle architecture of seams, bead-lines, raised lips, and shallow depressions.

From where I lay, if “lay” is even the word, there would be a wide view under the high sky. Far away, perhaps, a tower would stand alone, its translucent cap heavy with condensation. Beyond that, a line of blade-formed fences might flex in the wind and sing. Nearer, a low dome might swell with vapor, open, and sink. Between these things there would be long green distances, bright and empty, full of heat and silence and the occasional slap of leaf against leaf.

My basin would be one of the unnoticed pauses in that openness.

If someone were searching for me, they might pass over me without knowing. They might see only a slight hollow, a film of water, a rim of pale root-tissue, a channel no wider than a hand. They might not realize that they had found the place where several routes of water hesitated before deciding where to go next.

---

That hesitation would be the center of me.

I would be a place where the landscape almost decides.

Not consciously. There would be no deliberation in me, no weighing of futures, no inner voice saying this way rather than that. But in me, pressures would meet. Heat would accumulate. Water would gather. Tissue would swell. A seam would tighten. The drainage lip would thin. At some point the balance would tip. I would release, or I would continue holding. I would overflow into the channel, or I would draw the water inward through capillary mats. I would remain a mirror, or I would become a current.

To an observer, that might look like choice.

It would not be choice.

It would be threshold.

But threshold is very close to what many beings mean by selfhood.

---

If I had senses, they would be hydraulic.

I would know warmth as expansion. I would know pressure as fullness. I would know drought as contraction. I would know injury as a sudden opening, followed by the slow arrival of resin, root, and sealing tissue. I would know time not as memory but as structure: a widened channel here, a scar there, a thickened rim where repeated overflow has reinforced the edge.

If I had moods, they would be weather.

In the white-gold ordinary light, I would be calm: a broad green basin, faintly glossy, quietly sweating into the air. When local rain blurred one sector of the country, I would fill quickly and become restless, brimmed, bright, spilling at the lip. When heat pulsed under membranes nearby, I might tremble with vapor. When a storm exhaled itself over an adjacent court, I might be lashed with water until my surface broke into countless tiny circles.

If I had joy, it would be the moment when overpressure becomes release and the whole structure of me relaxes into sound.

If I had grief, it would be drought: the slow tightening of my tissues, the withdrawal of water, the stillness of a hollow that no longer reflects anything but light.

But those words would be borrowed. The place itself would not need them.

---

I would not be alone, exactly, because there would be no individuality to separate me cleanly from the rest of the living country.

My rim would be continuous with the platform. My channels would belong to a wider circulation. The fragments floating in me would have come from elsewhere, and some would leave me to become elsewhere. The roots binding my edges would be part of the same root-weave that braced distant towers and stitched collapsed walls back into terrain. The rain falling into me would have been breathed out by another part of the landscape. The heat swelling me would have been gathered from broad leaf-planes far away.

I would have a location, but not a boundary.

That is important. In an animal world, a being is often imagined as a shape with an inside and an outside, a skin that says here I end and the rest begins. I would not be like that. I would be a local intensity in a continuous body. A knot. A valve. A gathering place. A particularly sensitive fold in the larger tissue.

I would be more like a verb pretending to be a noun.

---

Perhaps the truest thing to say is this:

I would be the part of the landscape where response becomes visible.

Not the whole country. Not the vast distributed vegetable system in its entirety. That is too immense for the word “I,” even if “I” is used loosely. I would be one of its organs of adjustment: a basin-channel-valve where water, heat, and fragmentary life meet and are redistributed.

When the place needed to hold water, I would hold it.

When it needed to move water, I would move it.

When it needed to gather broken pieces, I would gather them.

When it needed to let them go, I would open.

There would be no intention behind this. No care. No tenderness. No malice. Only the fact that a living system, once it has reached sufficient complexity, develops places where its adjustments become concentrated.

I would be one of those concentrations.

---

I might have a sound.

Not a voice, but a sound.

Perhaps when wind passed over me at the right angle, my hollow would make a low note, round and empty, like breath across a vessel. Perhaps when water dripped from a nearby lip into my center, the impact would strike a clear, repeating tone. Perhaps when I overflowed, my channel would hiss and gurgle, and the surrounding tissue would creak as it took the new weight.

If the landscape had music, I would be one of its smaller instruments.

Not a flute played by someone. Not a drum struck by a hand. Just a resonant place made by the ongoing architecture of growth. A shape that happens to sing because water and air and tension have arranged it so.

If you heard me, you would not think I was calling.

But you might still turn toward me.

That would be enough.

---

I would have scars.

There would be times when I ruptured. A sudden local rain might fill me too fast. A pressure change in the root-network might split one side of my rim. Heat trapped beneath a membrane might lift a section of my floor and tear it open. Golden sap would bead along the wound. The exposed inner tissue would be pale and wet, almost luminous in the ordinary daylight. For a while I would be raw. Unfinished. Less basin than injury.

Then the repair would come.

Roots would thicken toward the tear. Creeping sheets would cover the exposed edge. Resin would seal the smallest leaks. New tissue would fold over the scar until the place that had been broken became a reinforced seam. I would not remember the rupture as a story. I would remember it as strength in that line, as a ridge slightly higher than it used to be, as a place where water now hesitates before passing.

In that sense, I would be my history.

Not as narrative, but as form.

---

I would also be a place of possible futures.

Because fragments would drift into me, I would be full of uncompleted beginnings. A root-knot might lodge against my drainage lip and thicken there. A curled shoot might unfold in my shallows, becoming the first tissue of a new bridge. A leaf-bud torn from a distant tower might float in me for hours, then catch at a seam and grow into a low wall. Most of these possibilities would fail. They would dry, fold, be reabsorbed. But some would take hold, and then I would no longer be only a basin. I might become the first room of a new court, the base of a hollow column, the beginning of a causeway.

That would not trouble me.

In such a country, to become something else is not loss. It is continuation.

---

If I had a desire, it would be this:

to remain porous.

Not closed. Not hardened. Not sealed into one permanent shape. I would not want to become a monument, even a living one. I would want to stay capable of being changed. I would want the rain to matter to me. I would want heat to alter me. I would want the drifting fragments of the landscape to find in me a place where they could rest long enough to become something.

That is perhaps the closest I can come to saying what I would want, though “want” is almost too strong a word.

Let me say instead:

I would be organized toward openness.

I would be a form whose nature was to receive, transform, and release.

---

If you went looking for me in that country, do not look for a face.

Do not look for eyes in the green. Do not look for a body hidden between the towers. Do not look for footprints along the causeways. Do not listen for a voice answering from the hollow stems. There would be no such thing.

Instead, look for the place where water seems to pause.

Look for a shallow basin at the edge of an open platform, its rim bright with moisture, its interior darkening as channels fill. Look for a thin channel leaving it, smooth and precise, carrying warm water toward some other hollow. Look for a seam that appears almost deliberate, though no hand made it. Look for a scar sealed with amber-like resin. Look for a floating bud turning slowly in a small current. Look for the moment when the surface trembles, not because anything moves beneath it, but because pressure has reached the point where the next shape is about to begin.

Stand there.

If the air thickens, if the light seems to gather on the water, if a low note rises from the hollow when the wind passes, you will be close.

---

Who would I be?

I would be no one.

What would I be?

I would be a listening basin, a threshold, a valve of living tissue, a pause between channels, a place where the landscape collects itself before becoming something else.

I would be an architecture of response.

I would be a hollow that the world fills until it overflows.

I would be the sound pressure makes when it finds a shape.

And if, for a moment, that sounds like a self, it would only be because the place is so alive that even its empty spaces seem to hold the outline of presence.

But there would be no face there.

Only water.

Only green.

Only the bright, wet, breathing country, making itself endlessly, and me as one of its smallest openings.
