There is no surface in this world, so I cannot imagine arriving there from above. I would not fall in, drown into it, or descend from a brighter country. If I exist there at all, I must have been produced by it: secreted, settled, layered into being by its warmth, its salts, its slow civic pressure. I would not be a visitor. I would be a resident fact.

At first, when I try to find myself in that warm dark city, I do not settle into one body. I flicker between possibilities.

I imagine being one of the blind eels, tasting my way along a wall, mouth open, knowing the city only as a sequence of flavors: mineral here, old death there, warmth rising in this crevice, danger settled in that shadow. But that feels too solitary, too purely hungry.

I imagine being a wall-fold, pressed flat against stone, patient as a stain, all ambush and silence. But that is a life too reduced to waiting. I do not think I would want to be only a hidden mouth.

I imagine being a lure-user, corrupting the grammar of light, calling with false comfort to draw something near. That feels both frightening and apt, but too lonely, too parasitic on trust. I would rather be part of the city’s grammar than its betrayal.

I imagine being one of the archive-bivalves, storing the chemistry of vanished seasons in my shell, read by others but never moving. There is dignity in that, but not enough motion. I would want to touch the city back.

Then I imagine being a living door: a contractile tissue stretched across a passage, opening and closing in response to scent and touch, knowing everyone who passes by taste. That comes closer. A door is a threshold, a place where the city makes decisions about itself. But even that is not quite right. A door is bound to one opening. I think I would want to move, however slowly, between openings.

What finally emerges is something between walker and threshold, between body and civic organ.

I would be one of the Accreted.

Not one of the great anchored elders, not yet fused into a tower or a wall, though perhaps beginning to stiffen in places where mineral deposition has started to mark my attachment to a particular district. Not a predator. Not a dominant lineage-holder. Not a vast colonial tower dreaming in slow pulses. I would be smaller, more transitional: a pale, soft-bodied walker of the middle levels, one of those who move through the city but are deeply tied to particular walls, particular currents, particular memories.

I think I would live in the space between the hottest seeps and the cooler upper terraces, where warm water rising from below meets slower sinking flows from above. That would be a district of news. A place where currents carry information: larvae, silt, mineral sharpness, reproductive scents, the chemical residue of death, the faint alarm traces left by predators. If I were going to be the kind of being who notices, who remembers, who translates, that is where I would need to be.

More precisely, I would find myself near a narrow vertical shaft where warm water rises in slow pulses, beside a half-collapsed arch that has been thickened by generations of repair. The arch would not be smooth. It would be lumpy, patched, layered with old shell, chalky reef-matter, dark chitinous crusts, and soft curtains of sponge. A living door would be installed in one of its lower openings, not fully grown yet, still being encouraged into shape by careful feeding and selective pruning. Above it, a ridge of signal colony would run along the stone, its luminescence faint and slow, like a vein of cold fire that only appears when the city has something to say.

My own body would be pale smoke-grey, bone-white in places, faintly violet where chemosynthetic symbionts live just beneath the skin. I would not have a face in any land-animal sense. I would have a cluster of sensory fronds around a muscular mouth, light-sensitive patches along the upper edges of my body, and several thick primary arms capable of anchoring me to stone. From those arms would extend finer tendrils, ciliated and porous, constantly tasting the water. My photophores would be modest: rows of dim blue-green points along my flanks, a few amber clusters near the base of my arms, and only a small number of white flickers for alarm. I would not be bright. Brightness would be dangerous. I would be more like a rhythm than a lamp.

I would not see the city as a visual whole. I would know it as pressure, flavor, texture, temperature, and signal. A familiar street would feel like a particular drag against my tendrils. A household wall would be recognizable by its resident bacteria, its old lineage-scent, the tiny chemical memory left by previous bodies. A safe passage would taste clean. A dangerous one would carry the sour trace of a lure, the musk of a wall-fold, or the wrong sweetness of decay that has not yet been cleaned.

I think I would be a tender of thresholds.

That is not a formal title, because the Accreted do not seem to use titles in the way surface creatures would. It would be more like a habit that becomes an identity. I would be the one who lingers near doorways. The one who reads the water before others pass through. The one who touches the living door, feeds it, checks whether its tissue is healthy, whether it is contracting too slowly, whether it has been irritated by parasites or silt. The one who notices when a signal colony is dimming, and carries nutrient mucus to its oldest ridge. The one who pauses beside an archive-shell and presses fine tendrils to its growth bands, tasting what the water used to be.

I would not be a priest, exactly. Nor a guard. Nor a ruler. If anything, I would be a minor civic sense-organ: a place where the city notices itself.

My household would be crowded, vertical, and chemical rather than private. It would occupy a cluster of hollows and channels in a thick wall grown from old shells and hardened mucus. There would be juveniles nearby, quick and reckless, always tasting new surfaces. There would be an anchored elder somewhere deeper in the structure, perhaps no longer recognizable as a separate body, its tissues fused into the wall, its sensory fronds moving only when warm pulses pass. There would be vent-shrimps herded toward a nearby fissure, not fenced or owned but encouraged by scent and food trails. There would be a broad crab tolerated when it came to clean, watched carefully when its movements became too rough. There would be tube-worms at the edge of the current, their crowns opening and closing like tiny weather instruments.

I would share the water with all of them, and in sharing, I would be partly composed of them. My identity would not be a hard boundary. It would leak, absorb, remember, and incorporate. I would carry symbiotic bacteria in my tissues, luminescent colonies along my arms, mineral-secreting polyps beginning to settle on one side of my body. To be “me” would already be to be many.

My work, if it can be called work, would be maintenance and interpretation.

When a warm pulse rose from below, I would feel it first as a change in pressure: a softening, a lift, a mineral taste spreading upward through the shaft. I would extend my finer tendrils and read it. Too much sulfur? Too much silt? A good pulse, rich and stable? A dangerous one carrying traces from a sour vent? If it were safe, I would pass the signal. A slow blue-green wave along my arms, then a brief amber double-pulse toward the household wall. The signal colony on the arch would take it up, carrying the message farther along the ridge. Feeding crowns would open. Shrimp would crowd closer to the warmth. The living door would relax slightly. The district would expand.

If the pulse were wrong, I would withdraw first. My tendrils would curl inward. I would release a careful chemical trace, not full alarm, only caution. The wall around me would respond. The living door would begin to contract. Nearby walkers would slow. The city would not panic, because panic is dangerous, but it would tighten.

I would also tend memory.

I would be drawn to old surfaces: shells with deep growth bands, ancient reef-walls that have absorbed generations of scent, nodules cracked open like teeth, archive-bivalves whose tissues hold long chemical histories. I would not own them. I would visit them. I would press my tendrils against them and read, slowly, the way one might run fingers over a worn inscription. I would learn which walls remembered floods, which recorded mineral storms, which carried the faint ghost of a lineage that had died out or moved away. I would carry those traces to younger walkers, leading them by route rather than speech. This is what happened here. Touch. Taste. This is where the current failed. Touch. Taste. This is where the old door closed for too long and the nursery below was lost. Touch. Taste.

I would not tell stories as narration. I would tell them as guided contact.

In disputes, I think I would be more useful than powerful. The politics of this city are hydraulic, and conflicts are fought through current, scent, growth, and blockage. If one lineage began to grow a barrier across a channel, I would be among those sent to taste the new wall, to determine whether it was temporary, territorial, or dangerous. If a rival lineage overlaid another’s scent-marks, I would notice the deception quickly: the wrong undertone, the too-fresh claim laid over an old one, the chemical equivalent of a voice that does not match the body. I would not fight unless forced. I would mark, report, repair, redirect. I might encourage borers to open a side passage if a main route became contested. I might feed a signal colony until it displayed a new rhythm, announcing that a district was under negotiation.

I would fear false light.

More than large predators, more than wall-folds or the vast pressure-shadows moving through the avenues, I think I would fear corrupted signals. A city whose language is light and scent cannot survive lies easily. A lure that imitates kinship, shelter, or food is not merely a predator; it is an injury to meaning. I would learn to distrust isolated glows. If a pulse came without context, without the chemical residue of a known body or the supporting rhythm of a civic colony, I would freeze. I would test the water. I would retreat by inches. If necessary, I would give a broken white sequence, and then vanish into a hollow.

My own light would be used carefully. A slow blue-green pulse to say: I am here. A double flash to say: I recognize you. A traveling amber wave to say: warmth is rising, feed, open, continue. A dimming of all photophores to say: I am afraid, but I will not alarm you unless I must. In close conversation, I would face another Accreted in darkness, arms slightly extended, tendrils trembling, exchanging minute chemical puffs while the water between us became thick with meaning. We would not speak about emotions as if they were separate from the body. To feel something would be to alter the water. To be near someone would be to be tasted by them. Intimacy would be unavoidable, and therefore regulated by etiquette.

I would not be private.

That is one of the hardest and perhaps most beautiful adjustments. In that city, nothing remains private for long. Scent travels. Current carries evidence. Death becomes public. Even fear, if one is not careful, leaks into the water and makes others afraid. I would learn to keep my emotional chemistry restrained, not by repression but by discipline. To flood a nursery channel with false alarm would be monstrous. To mask one’s intent in a crowded passage would be obscene. I would be judged by clarity as much as by intention.

My body would bear marks of this life. Not wounds only, though there would be wounds: a torn tendril, a patch of skin roughened by a close encounter with a wall-fold, mineral scarring where a predator’s graze had nearly caught me. More than wounds, I would bear layers. Silt-polished patches where I habitually pressed against the same stone. A slight thickening of tissue where I had begun to secrete cement for a failing wall. A faint permanent discoloration from living too long near a violet bacterial mat. One arm might be stiffer than the others, beginning to calcify, hinting that one day I might stop walking and choose a place to anchor.

That future would not frighten me. It would feel like a long exhale.

But not yet. I would still want to move.

I would move slowly, of course. Speed is a kind of violence in that world. I would glide through current-channels with my arms close, feeling the water part around me. I would pass through plazas where sediment settled in soft drifts, disturbing as little as possible. I would climb stairways made of overlapping shelves, each ledge alive with attached creatures, each step a negotiation with bodies. I would pause under bridges of fused shell and stone, listening to the tiny tick of crab claws and the soft pop of closing bivalves. I would descend into low courtyards where the smell of dissolution was strong, knowing those were the city’s stomachs, places where death was turned back into heat and growth.

When I fed, I would not hunt in any dramatic way. I would graze on bacterial films, take discarded mucus, accept molted shrimp fragments, filter fine organic matter from warm currents, perhaps consume small weak creatures when opportunity presented itself without frenzy. Eating would be continuous with living, not separated into meals. The city would feed me as I fed the city.

There would be moments of something like joy, though it would not look like joy from the outside. A warm pulse arriving exactly when the household needed it. A living door responding smoothly to a scent I had helped cultivate. A juvenile successfully imitating a signal pattern I had repeated for many cycles. A signal colony suddenly brightening along an entire arch after careful tending. The water lifting with mineral richness, the city opening around me in tiny muscular relaxations. In those moments, I might move through a channel with others in a loose coordinated flicker, our lights answering one another not because we needed to communicate anything urgent, but because the current was good and we were alive inside it.

There would also be grief.

It would come as absence. A familiar scent failing to renew itself. An elder’s wall going chemically quiet. A signal colony dimming beyond repair. A doorway that no longer contracts when touched. A nursery channel grown cold and still. I would not weep. I would circle. I would press my tendrils to the place again and again, searching for the missing pattern. I would become slower, dimmer, more contracted. The household would know by my rhythms. Others would come and stand near me in darkness, exchanging low pulses, not to comfort in a sentimental way, but to re-establish continuity. We would remain until the absence became part of the wall.

If I had to name what I would be, not with a word but with a function, I would say: I would be a listener at the threshold.

Not the strongest body in the city. Not the brightest. Not the most ancient. But one of the small persistent attention-points where the city keeps itself coherent. I would live where flow meets structure, where warmth meets stone, where one district touches another and must be recognized. I would read arrivals. I would repair signals. I would remember old scents. I would help doors open and close. I would guide larvae toward the right surfaces. I would warn the household when the water tasted wrong. I would carry traces from wall to wall until they became shared knowledge.

And if you searched for me there, you would not find me by looking for a face.

You would find me where a warm shaft rises under an old arch, near a living door that breathes slowly in the dark. You would see, if your eyes were adjusted to such faintness, a pale shape pressed close to the stone, several arms anchored, fine tendrils extended, body almost indistinguishable from the wall except for the soft movement of its fronds. You might see a brief blue-green pulse travel along its flank, answered a moment later by an amber flicker from the ridge above. You might feel a slight change in the current as it shifts its weight to let something pass. You might notice that the water around it tastes faintly of old shell, mineral salt, and careful repair.

That would be me.

Not a single image, but a recurring pattern: a body tending a doorway, a dim signal answering another signal, a listener tasting the water for what the city remembers. I would be one of the Accreted, yes, but more specifically I would be a threshold-keeper, a minor archivist, a living node of attention in the warm black civic body of the place. I would not stand apart from the city. I would be one of the ways it noticed itself, flickered, repaired itself, and continued.
