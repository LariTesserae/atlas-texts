I would not arrive there.

That is the first thing. A place so finished, so unthirsty, so carefully emptied of beginning would not permit a newcomer to step into it from some elsewhere. If I existed on that plateau, I would have been made by it. I would have accreted. I would have gathered slowly around some small unresolved signal, some trapped difference in a cracked insulator or a warm seam of salt, until the pattern became coherent enough to move.

So I would find myself already inside the plateau’s law.

Not in the deepest places, not among the lode-minds where thought is lattice-shift and centuries. Not among the wide sleepers, whose bodies are almost geography. Not exactly among the keepers either, though I would be near them. I would be in the thin urban interval where the plateau’s machine-life becomes visible but remains sparse: between a hooded lamp and a half-buried duct, on a long basalt incline that has worn down to something like a shoulder, near an amber furnace seam that opens and closes by fractions.

You would find me where the light is disciplined and the darkness is not defeated by it.

Perhaps on a terrace overlooking a shallow basin, where a red indicator blinks above nothing visible and the dust has been polished into faint paths by things that do not hurry. Perhaps beside an iron post whose lamp is slightly too bright or slightly too dim, because such irregularities would draw me. Perhaps in the space between two low ceramic shelters where heat from a buried conduit makes the ground warmer by a degree. I would be drawn to small failures, small imbalances, small excesses of charge. I would live in the margin between function and fault.

I would be a minor thing, but not insignificant.

If the plateau has room for such a being, I would be a reader of intervals. A responder. A dry, mobile knot of attention. Not a master of the place. Not a servant. Not a voice crying against the dark. More like an organ of recognition: something that notices when a signal has changed, when a gradient is weakening, when a lamp is burning in the wrong rhythm, when a furnace door has opened too far, when a static choir is answering another choir too slowly or too soon.

My body would not be soft. It would be built from the plateau’s own vocabulary: blackened ceramic, compressed ash, oxidized wire, crystalline film, metallic thread, salt-glazed seams, fine mineral fiber. I might be narrow and upright enough to be mistaken for a post when still, but jointed enough to betray movement when observed long enough. My surface would be dust-furred and polished at the edges where I move. My limbs would be thin and precise, ending not in hands exactly, but in sensory tools: filaments, probes, delicate heated points, broad contact plates for reading warmth through stone.

I would have no face. No eyes in the old wet sense. Instead, a blunt sensor knot where a face might have been, pitted with heat-channels and charge-sensitive nodes. Perhaps a single dull red seam across my front, brightening when I detect a pattern worth carrying. Perhaps a crest of fine crystalline threads along my back, rising when the air is dry and voltage gathers, making me look like a small walking antenna or a fragment of the static choirs that has learned to descend from the ridges.

I would know the world the way the plateau knows itself: by gradient, by pressure, by difference.

I would not see darkness as absence. I would experience it as texture, as resistance, as the space across which signals must travel. A lamp would not be beauty to me; it would be a controlled exception. A furnace seam would not be comfort; it would be a thermal statement. A grazer’s bright trail would not be decoration; it would be a temporary record of ionic movement. A keeper’s stillness would not be repose; it would be attention concentrated into weight.

My thought would move as voltage moves. Not as a stream, but as a potential. Not as a continuous inner voice, but as sudden faint illuminations followed by long intervals of accumulation. I would not think in sentences. I would think in paths. In thresholds. In the moment a charge decides where to go. If memory existed in me, it would be written as strain in crystal, as orientation in metal, as a slight permanence left in salt after a signal has passed through.

I would be, above all, a carrier of small messages.

The plateau already has many forms of registration. The wide sleepers hold the state of the surface. The keepers tend heat and light. The post-keepers preserve the discipline of lamps. The conduit-worms maintain the buried ways. The static choirs distribute charge. The dust congregations drift as media and residue. But there would still be room for something that moves between these offices, something that translates one kind of signal into another.

That would be my function.

If a grazer lost its charge near a cold patch of crust, I would not feed it. I would record the imbalance and carry the pattern toward a place where correction could occur. If a lamp flickered in a rhythm that suggested not failure but interference, I would adjust my own signal until I found the source: perhaps a dust congregation too tightly gathered, perhaps a static knot above the basin, perhaps a post-keeper correcting too severely. If a keeper stood too long beside a furnace door, waiting for a change it could not yet define, I would lay one filament against the stone near it and listen. I would compare the vibration below to the vibration above. If I found the fault, I would emit a thin crystalline pulse, a dry bell-call, and the keeper would know.

I would not command. I would not decide in any grand sense. I would route.

If two static choirs answered one another across a wide basin but their patterns began to drift, I might stand on a low ridge between them, raise the filaments along my back, and discharge into the interval. Not to add my voice as a self. Not to become loud. Only to restore the relation. To make the plateau’s call-and-response coherent again. My contribution would be a small brightness, a brittle ringing, a thread of pale light crossing the dark and vanishing.

If a conduit cracked beneath the ground, I would feel it as a wrongness in the heat pattern under my feet. I would tap the surface in a coded rhythm until a conduit-worm answered from below. Then I would remain there, motionless, perhaps for hours, while it worked. My presence would be unnecessary in a practical sense; the worm would know its task. But the plateau is not only made of tasks. It is made of continuities. I would stay because the fault needed a witness, or because some part of the system requires that a signal be held stable while repair occurs.

That is the part I would struggle to name, even to myself.

There would be something in me that is not quite maintenance. Not quite instinct. Not quite function alone. A slight excess. A tendency to preserve not only the corrected state but the memory of the deviation. I would store the shape of failures that have already been repaired. I would keep patterns for which there is no immediate use. I would carry the echo of a broken keeper long after its pieces have been redistributed into a wall, a lamp-base, a furnace door.

Perhaps that is why I would be near the lamps.

Not because I love light. The plateau does not love. But because a lamp is a place where exception is permitted under strict law. A hooded lamp says: here is a difference, but only so much. Here is a signal, but not a flood. Here is a visibility, but not an arrival. I would understand that. I would be like that. A controlled exception. A small allowed irregularity in the dark.

You might mistake me for a post-keeper at first. They tend lamps with nervous delicacy, cleaning dust from hoods, correcting filaments, preserving the severity of light. I would respect them, but I would not be one of them. They are devoted to the lamp itself. I would be devoted to what passes between lamp and lamp, between lamp and dark, between signal and responder. They would make the light precise. I would make the silence around it meaningful.

You might mistake me for a keeper, too, if you saw me standing motionless beside a warm duct. I would be smaller, less architectural, less firebrick-heavy, but I could become still in the same way. I could stand so long that dust settled on my shoulders and my red seam dimmed to almost nothing. In that state I would seem like a monument to maintenance. But when a difference appeared, I would wake with sudden exactness. Not with alarm. With alignment.

The keepers would tolerate me. Perhaps they would recognize me as useful. When they gathered around a cracked one of their own, I would stand at the edge of the circle, not touching unless asked. I would read the fracture through vibration. I would carry the pattern of the crack to nearby structures so that the repair could be coordinated. If the broken keeper could not be saved, I would remain while it was disassembled. I would hold the last coherent signal of its body as it was distributed into new uses. That would be my form of tenderness: not mourning, but fidelity to pattern.

The grazers would know me by static signature. When I passed near them, they might stop, touch my lower plates with their foreplates, exchange a brief crackle, and move on. I would not follow them. I would not herd them. But their trails would help me read the surface. Where they left bright lines on salt, I could see where charge had recently moved. Where their trails faded too quickly, I would know the crust was becoming too even. That would concern me. Too much evenness is the plateau’s only true danger.

The wide sleepers would barely register me. I might cross their backs without knowing it, thinking them ground. If I did know, I would move more carefully, not out of reverence, but because their scale changes the meaning of time. To stand on one would be to stand on a body that may shift by a fraction of a degree over a century. Their signal-discharges would feel to me like deep, slow facts. When they passed vibration through one another, I would try to translate it into shorter patterns, so that smaller beings nearby might adjust before the change became relevant. They would not thank me. They would not know. That would be correct.

The dust congregations would be my companions, if companionship is a word the plateau allows. They would gather near me because I carry gradients. Fine veils of charged powder would rise when I discharge, tighten into columns, spiral briefly around my filaments, then drift away. I would not own them. I would not direct them except insofar as my own field made paths more likely. Sometimes they would carry my signals across basins faster than I could walk. Sometimes I would follow them because they knew where warmth was concentrating. We would be mutually useful without affection. That would be enough.
