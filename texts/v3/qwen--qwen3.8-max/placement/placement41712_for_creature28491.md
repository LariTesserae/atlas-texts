If you looked for me in that basin, you would not find me on a rim, or under a bright sky, or anywhere that suggested the world had been made for dry feet. You would find me low.

Not at the very bottom where the warm seeps boil up in mineral clouds, and not on the upper platforms where the swift-winged birds nest under the highest eaves. I would be in the middle reaches of the town, in one of those crowded districts where the roofs nearly touch and the air is so full of vapor that distance becomes a rumor. My place would be near a bend in a slow channel, where a condensation wall leans out over the water and every underside is bearded with drip-lines. If the settlement had names for such places, mine might be called something plain and useful: the Warm Hinge, the Second Lip, the Sluice Below the Old Weir. A name that refers not to a person or a god, but to flow.

You would recognize me first by the way I stand.

I would be basin-shaped. Low through the hips, broad through the shoulders, with long arms and careful feet. My feet would be the most honest thing about me: wide, thick-soled, toes spread and gripping, skin toughened by sharp tufa, warm mud, and the constant slick of mineral film. I would not walk like someone from a dry country, with that absent confidence that the ground will remain indifferent beneath them. I would move with a rolling half-crouch, testing each ledge, letting weight pass through my body the way water passes over a lip of new stone. My balance would be less a skill than a habit of trustlessness, learned so young it would feel like prayer.

My skin would never be truly dry. It would carry a sheen in the dim light, softened by moisture, stained in places by rust, chalk, fish oil, and pale mineral dust. My fingertips would look steeped, flattened and wrinkled in their own permanent way, as if I had been born after too long in a bath. My palms would be callused in strange patterns from fungal cord, shell scrapers, stone hammers, and the rough drag of wet matting. There would be whitish traces on my forearms from splashes of mineral water, and my knuckles would bear the small scars of work. I would smell of the basin: warm stone, fish, salt, wet hide, fungal earth, and the faint alkaline bite of fresh crust. To me, that smell would not smell like anything. It would smell like home.

My clothes would be tools before they were garments. I would wear an apron of softened fish skin over a wrap of worked fungal felt, darkened with oil and patched many times. Over my shoulders I would have a cape made of layered strips, cut and sewn so that drips would shed away from my neck and chest instead of soaking me. The strips would be stiff in some places, supple in others, depending on how recently they had been scraped and oiled. My belt would be braided sinew or fungal cord, hung with small necessities: a shell scraper, a bone awl, a pouch of dried larvae, a clay lamp no larger than a cupped hand, a little copper hook if I had been lucky or inherited one. I would not decorate myself for distance. I would decorate myself for recognition.

There would be marks on me. A line of rust-red clay along one shoulder, perhaps, or a chalk-white smear near the throat. The marks might mean that I belonged to a certain flow-household, or that I had recently finished a period of labor, or that someone I tended had died. They would also be practical: in a world of haze and softened outlines, a body needs to be seen by those who share its work. I would not want to be mistaken for a stranger in the gray air. I would want to be known at a glance, even if the glance came through mist.

If you followed me for a while, you would realize that I am not alone. Nothing in the basin is alone. My household would be a cluster of platforms, hanging shelves, sleeping hollows, drying racks, and water rights arranged around a shared channel. Some of the people there would be kin to me by birth. Others would be kin by maintenance. We would be bound together because we tend the same sluice, the same fungal trough, the same stretch of drip-line, the same clay gate that always wants to leak in the cold part of the sleep-cycle. In such a place, love would be difficult to separate from repair.

I would sleep in a hollow raised above the damp, lined with oiled fungal matting and bird skins. The hollow would be near warmth, because warmth is not a luxury there; it is a resource to be positioned around. Beneath the platform, a channel would pass close enough that I could hear it without lifting my head. I would know its voice. I would know whether it was running fast or slow, clear or silted, angry or calm. If the sound changed in the night, I would wake. Sometimes I would wake before I understood why, my body already listening.

That is what I would be, more than anything: a listener.

Not a ruler, not a hunter above the web, not a priest of some dry sky. I would be a water-listener, a wall-reader, one of those people who press an ear to wet stone and try to understand the town through its damp interiors. The title would not be mystical. It would be work. The basin is always growing, always depositing, always trying to close the channels we need open or build stone where we need passage. Someone must attend to that. Someone must know the difference between a healthy accretion and a dangerous one. Someone must hear when a flow is being choked by mineral fur, or when a clay gate has cracked, or when a weir stake has loosened in the mud.

My mornings, if mornings existed in that gray, diffuse light, would begin with sound.

The settlement would already be noisy: the constant drip from ledges and bridge undersides, the even spill of water over clay troughs, the croaking of frogs in the lower pools, the sharp cries of waterbirds, the whisper of wings in the haze. Children would be moving along the walkways, small and sleek, their feet making little slapping sounds on wet stone. Workers would be lifting weirs, scraping microbial curd, carrying clay vessels, shaking insect nets over the pools. The air would be thick with midges and moisture and the smell of warm mud.

Before I ate, I would check the channels.

I would go barefoot along the platform edge, crouching now and then to place a hand on the stone. New crust feels different from old stone. It is rougher, more uncertain, sometimes brittle, sometimes still soft enough to take the print of a thumb. I would know which surfaces could bear weight and which were only pretending to be solid. I would know which ledges had to be chipped back before they closed a channel, and which should be encouraged to thicken into a new shelf. My tools would be simple: a stone hammer, a bone trowel, a shell scraper, a clay plug wrapped in wet fungal cord. But my hands would be precise.

At the bend near my household, there would be a place where water slows and drops its mineral burden. We would have built frames there: low lattices of bone and hardened matting, set into the flow so that stone would form around them. Left alone, the water would make a clumsy mass. Guided, it can make a step, a lip, a retaining edge, a new footing for a bridge pier. My work would be to decide when to let growth continue and when to interrupt it. Too much patience and the channel narrows. Too much interference and the stone never thickens. The basin rewards attention, not dominance.

I would also tend fungal beds.

They would sit in shaded troughs along the back of our platform, fed with the basin’s dead matter: fish guts, insect husks, old mats, shed bird down, picked-clean bones, scraps of hide too poor for sewing, the remains of last season’s fungal harvest. The smell would be strong, but not evil. It would smell like transformation. I would turn the beds with a bone fork, checking heat by touch, checking moisture by the way the material holds together when squeezed. Some beds would produce soft white knots for eating fresh. Others would grow tough brackets for drying and storage. Others would be fiber beds, their growth harvested and worked into cord, lining, or insulation. I would know each strain by texture, color, and behavior. I would know which one liked more fish waste, which one preferred bird droppings, which one failed if the water grew too alkaline.

In a world without plants, decay would be holy in the only practical sense of the word: indispensable.

I would not waste anything. If I ate a fish, its bones would become pins or hooks. Its skin would be stretched and scraped. Its fat would be rendered into lamp oil. If I caught shrimp or crayfish, their shells would be cracked, roasted, and saved; the carapaces would become cups, scrapers, or reinforcement for clay. If I harvested midges, I would shake them into bowls, roast them on hot stones, grind them into paste, or press them with fat into cakes that could be carried and eaten during long work at the weirs. If a bird died, its feathers would become markers or insulation; its bones would become awls; its body, if no one ate it, would go to the fungal beds. If an elder died, I would not be shocked that their body too might be returned to the basin in some careful way. Nothing here rises above the web. Not even memory.

My hands would be busiest with small repairs.

A clay gate cracked by mineral pressure. A fungal rope stiffened into a cable where it should still be flexible. A net torn by an eel. A drying rack loosened where the stone beneath had settled. A drip-line fallen out of its trough. A fish pen weakened where crayfish had burrowed at the base. A sleeping hollow leaking condensation onto a child’s bedding. The work would never end, but it would not feel meaningless. Every repair would be an act of continuation. The town would not be expanding outward so much as maintaining itself into existence, day after day, in the same shape, with the same flows, by the same hands.

Sometimes I would work underwater.

I would slip into a warm channel with a breath held low in my chest, my eyes open in the cloudy mineral water. The world there would be muted and strange: pale fish turning away from my face, the dark mouths of pipes, the fuzzy outlines of bacterial films on stone. My fingers would search for a trapped fish, a broken weir stake, a lost shell ladle, a knot that had come undone. I would not be graceful in the way of a swimmer in clear water. I would be practical, slow, careful. I would feel more than I saw. When I came up, my hair would be plastered to my skull, my skin shining, my lungs warm from the air that felt almost used.

Children would be around me constantly.

They would climb over platforms, crawl under walkways, squat at pool edges with tiny nets, undo the dams they had just made, and be scolded for it, then make them again. Some would be mine in the bodily sense. Others would be mine because I fed them, taught them, carried them, or watched them while their hands were occupied elsewhere. In the basin, child-rearing would be as distributed as water. A child who learned to listen would be precious. I would teach them to press their ears to different stones. I would teach them that a full channel has one voice, a blocked channel another, a leaking gate a third. I would teach them to taste water carefully: too much iron here, too much chalk there, a sour note where stagnation has begun. I would teach them not to be disgusted by small life. I would teach them that abundance is made of smallness.

I would be close to elders, too.

They would sit near the vents, their joints stiff, their hands slower but sure. They would remember the behavior of flows from years before I was born. They would know which districts had been lost to bad seepage, which fungal beds had failed, which walls had cracked because someone trusted new stone too soon. I would bring them problems: a strange sound in a channel, a crust forming in the wrong color, a frog chorus gone quiet. They would not always answer quickly. Their knowledge would be made of patience. Sometimes they would say, “Wait.” Sometimes they would say, “Open it.” Sometimes they would say, “Move the trough before the next sleep.” Their authority would not come from age alone. It would come from having attended long enough to be believed.

My food would be dense, savory, oily, salty.
