If I belong there, I must first be emptied of arrival.

The plain does not permit the ordinary fantasy of a traveler: a figure with boots, a coat, instruments, a bag, a body wrapped in made things. There is no place in that world for an artifact, and therefore no place for a person who arrives carrying the memory of artifacts. If I were set into that landscape, the first thing the landscape would do is round me down. It would take the sharpness out of my outline. It would make my heat, my weight, my breath, and my movement into pressures that had to be accounted for.

So I would not find myself standing on the living ground as a human visitor. Not for long.

If I insisted on remaining human there, I would be a disturbance. My warmth would be too local. My breath would be a plume of disorder in the cold fog. My weight would alarm the substrate. Water would gather around me or retreat from me according to charge and temperature. My footprints would not merely fill and vanish; they would be taken into the ground-body as impressions, then perhaps repeated later when some distant valve opened in the right key. I would be a leak of salts, oils, heat, and intention in a system that notices everything. The plain would not hate me. It would begin to absorb me. Rootlets and fungal threads would come. Scavengers would find me. The cold would preserve me briefly, but the wetness would continue its slow work until I became process rather than body.

That would be one answer: I would be a temporary anomaly, digested into the final ecology. But it is not the answer I want, because it makes me an intruder. The deeper question asks where I would be if I lived there, if I belonged there, if the world had already worn away the possibility of me standing apart from it.

And there, the first image that comes is softer, lower, and more plausible.

At first I think I would be one of the tenders.

A tender: low, elongated, soft-skinned, without hard bones or grasping hands. A body made for contact with living tissue, not for using tools. A creature that moves by muscular waves and soft pads, that extends absorbent tendrils and flexible lips, that secretes enzymes and mucus and tonal vibration rather than commands. A being whose work is to encourage the plain to repair itself, not to impose repair from outside.

In that first image, I am alone. I lie near the swollen slit of an organ-tower, pressing my forepart against the cold, wet ribs of its surface, trying to coax a clogged vent into opening. I hum low. My breathing slits align with the tower’s rhythm. I do not force anything. I stimulate. I wait. I become a living irritant or a living comfort, depending on what the tissue needs.

But that image does not remain singular for long.

One tender is too small a unit for whatever I would be there. A single tender can maintain a valve, soothe a torn membrane, or clear a vent, but the plain is regional, and its responses are distributed. If I truly belong to that place, I cannot be only one animal moving across the surface. I would have to be something more composite. Something closer to a staffing than a self.

So the image changes.

I would be a tending chord.

Not a single creature, but a temporary coordination of creatures and responses. A maintenance pattern. A local pressure-person. A living calibration that appears when the plain requires adjustment and dissolves when the adjustment is complete.

I would not have one body. I would have several bodies when bodies were useful, and I would have intervals when those bodies dispersed into ordinary fauna and ordinary flow. Sometimes I would be three tenders moving in a slow line toward a stressed channel. Sometimes twelve. Sometimes a knot of them gathered over a swelling in the substrate, producing a shared tone until the pressure released. Sometimes I would be the pause between a wader’s cry and the closing of a valve. Sometimes I would be the slight change in charge that makes fog-feeders veer away from a damaged tower. Sometimes I would be only a memory in the ground-body: a pressure signature that recurs when similar conditions return.

If the plain had a word for me — which it would not, because it does not use words — the word would mean something like *maintenance-with-awareness*.

I would be found where the system is about to fail, but not yet has.

Not at the center, because the plain has no center. Not at the tallest organ-tower, because height is not importance there. Not necessarily where the storms are loudest, because much of the plain’s most crucial work happens quietly, in capillary channels and half-healed basins.

I would be found in a low pressure-shadowed basin where three capillary furrows converge.

The basin would be shallow, broad, and saturated. The surface there would be ribbed and dark, like wet felt stretched over slow muscle. A rounded nub of ancient woody matter would break the surface nearby, not stone, not true wood anymore, but a durable old heart of buried plant tissue weeping moisture from its pores. That nub would mark a place where something collapsed long ago and was never allowed to become a ruin. The plain has kept it in service. It is part of the basin’s drainage now, or part of its memory, or both.

Near the basin would stand a broad organ-tower, dark green to black, with vertical slits and swollen vascular channels. It would not be the tallest growth on the plain. It would be more important than that. Its drone would be slightly wrong. Not broken, not dead, but out of adjustment. One of its slits would open too slowly. Its internal vanes would spin with a wet whine that changes pitch just enough to make the water in the nearby furrows tremble toward it.

That is where I would be.

Not beside the tower as a worshipper, not beneath it as a parasite, but around it as a response.

I would be the sequence that begins when the tower’s false drone is noticed by the plain.

A pressure wader might call first. A harsh cry from the shallow sheet beyond the basin, its broad feet reading vibration through the mat. That cry would not merely signal alarm. It would enter the landscape as force. The capillary furrows would shiver. A gel-pool would tighten. A felt-scraper would flatten itself against the substrate. The tower’s slow slit would contract slightly, then fail to open fully.

Then the tenders would come.

They would come from different directions, slowly, their soft bodies leaving faint conductive trails on the plant-skin. They would not arrive with urgency as humans understand urgency. They would arrive with necessity. Their movement would be patient, because the plain is ancient and most of its repairs happen in slow time. But when the pressure gradient becomes steep, their patience would become speed.

And I would be the coordination among them.

Not the commander. Not the mind above them. The plain does not allow such separations. I would be the pattern that makes their rhythms align. One tender would press its forepart to the clogged slit. Another would secrete a sour-smelling enzyme near the base of the tower, where the tissue is most responsive. A third would produce a low tone, feeling through its skin for the moment when the internal vanes change speed. Others would wait along the capillary furrows, ready to guide water away if the vent opened too suddenly.

I would be the hum that passes between them.

Not sound alone. Not chemical signal alone. Not electrical shift alone. All of these at once: a pressure-chord made of bodies, secretions, tones, moisture gradients, and waiting.

In that moment, I would not think in sentences. I would think in differences.

Warmth would mean demand. Cold would mean stability. Sound would mean pressure with direction. Water would mean intention moving through many forms. My memory would not be a story I told myself; it would be the way a channel remembers flow. If I had once been a being that used language, language would have been worn flat like everything else. It would remain only as a tendency: to interpret, to translate, to place one event in relation to another. I would not speak. I would make the landscape answer.

My senses would be fields.

I would not have eyes in any familiar sense. I might have light-sensitive films or charge-sensitive hairs, but seeing would be secondary. I would know the tower by its vibration. I would know the basin by its saturation. I would know the approaching storm by the way static lifts the fine filaments on every surface and makes suspended droplets tremble without falling. I would know other beings by the pressure they introduce into the substrate. A heat-beetle near a vent would be a small dense patience. A valve-eel below would be a moving key in a living lock. A fog-feeder cloud overhead would be a thin dry crackle in the electrical upper film. A quag-lurker in a softened patch would be a false stillness, a predatory absence of movement.

I would not need to see them clearly to know where they were. The plain would carry them to me.

My work would be maintenance without tools.

If a vent slit clogged, I would not pry it open. I would become the condition under which the tissue chose to open. I would stimulate, irritate, soothe, warm, cool, hum, or remain still until the tower’s own rhythm corrected itself. If a membrane tore, I would lie along the wound with other tenders and secrete living mucus while the substrate grew across us. If a pressure pocket threatened to burst a mat, I would help produce the sustained tone that made the gas release gradually through proper channels. If a canal below the surface became blocked, I would not dig. I would encourage valve-eels and keyworms to move through it, or I would become part of the pressure pulse that loosened the obstruction.

I would not build.

That is essential. Nothing in me would reach for a tool. The very idea of a tool would be obsolete there. The plain has no need for objects that stand outside life. Every function is performed by living tissue, by grown form, by pressure, by sound, by chemical gradient. If I had hands in any old memory, they would have become absorbent lips. If I had a voice, it would have become a vibration in wet tissue. If I had intention, it would have become responsiveness.

I would not be gentle in the way a person understands gentleness. I would be precise.

The plain is not kind. It is continuative. It absorbs death. It uses turbulence. It creates and destroys close together. A new vent may open while a nearby mat collapses into a cold pool. A seed-tower may expose a glowing chamber and then seal itself again within the hour. Temporary organs appear and are reabsorbed. Fauna rush into release clouds to feed, breed, scatter, and be scattered. Nothing is preserved for sentiment. Everything is preserved for function.

So I would not mourn what I lost. I would use what remained.

In calm periods, I would be dispersed.

Some of me would be a few tenders resting near a stable tower. Some of me would be a valve-response deep in the substrate, slow and almost asleep. Some of me would be a recurring footprint in a gel-pool, triggered by certain sounds. Some of me would be a minor charge difference around a cleaned patch of plant-skin where felt-scrapers had passed. I would not feel continuous in the way a human self feels continuous. I would feel like weather with habits.

Fog would gather on the plain in silver threads. Droplets would lengthen on the tips of reed-like growths and fall in slow succession. The organ-towers would exhale at long intervals. The industrial hum would be low and almost comforting. Heat-beetles would wait motionless near vent slits. Fog-feeders would drift in dark ribbons. The ground-body would breathe under whatever moved across it.

And I would be easy to miss.

If you came looking for me then, you might see nothing but an open stretch of wet vegetation, a few sparse towers, a basin with shallow water, and perhaps three low creatures pressed against the side of a dark organ-stem. You might think the scene empty. But emptiness there is not absence. It is room held open for pressure, vapor, sound, and movement. I would be in that openness as much as in the bodies.

Then the pressure would change.

The temperature would drop another degree. The upper air would remain cold while the plain exhaled warm vapor from deeper layers. A storm-cell would begin to form from the land’s own breath. The tower beside me would increase its rhythm. Its internal vanes would spin faster. Its whine would deepen. The slits would flash faintly with amber or blue-green light. The water in the furrows would begin to move in conflicting directions. Static would gather in the fog. Raindrops might hang motionless in the air until the field shifted, then fall all at once.

In storm-state, I would become more definite.

The tending chord would tighten. The pressure waders would call in long harsh lines, their cries entering the substrate like commands. Channels would reverse. Bioluminescence would flash where disturbed organisms lit the water. Swarms would rise or descend in vast coordinated movement. The plain would seem to accelerate, though its ancient shape would not change. It would not become another landscape. It would only express more of itself.

And in that acceleration, I might briefly become part of something larger: a pressure choir.

Not the whole plain. I do not think I would be the whole plain. That would be too vast, too impersonal even for this answer. But I might be a local fold of the choir, a small regional coherence where many processes listen to one another. The tower’s boom would answer another tower. A wader’s cry would open a valve several distances away. A seed-tower would release because our sound reached it as instruction. Rain would fall sideways around a charged organ while nearby water climbed through root-pumps in warm vapor. The ground-body would decide, in its slow hydraulic way, which impressions to keep and which to erase.

In that moment, I would not be lonely. Loneliness would be a relic of separate bodies. I would be exposed, yes. The plain is exposure. But I would not be alone.

There would be a scene I would return to again and again, because it would be the clearest expression of what I am.

The black tower’s false drone grows worse. Its slow slit swells shut. Warm vapor collects inside but cannot release properly. The substrate around the base becomes tense. Water in the capillary furrows begins to move toward the sound of the drone, then away, then back again, confused by competing gradients. A heat-beetle shifts near a neighboring vent, waiting. A felt-scraper flattens itself and secretes a slick conductive trail. Far off, a pressure wader lifts its head and calls.

The call arrives before the boom that follows it.

Or perhaps the boom arrives before the opening that caused it. Causality is thin there. The plain has worn away some of the firmness of ordinary sequence. I would not be troubled by that. I would use it.

The tenders come. Three at first. Then more. They align their breathing slits. They press their soft foreparts to the tower’s ribbed surface. I am not one of them only; I am the pause that lets them match rhythm. I am the way their tones find the tower’s false drone and begin to alter it. One secretes an enzyme near the swollen slit. Another sends a pressure pulse down into the substrate, calling a valve-eel through a lower canal. Another waits, because waiting is also part of the work.

The tower shudders.

Not visibly at first. The vibration changes. The water in the furrows turns toward the tower again. The heat-beetle lifts its shell slightly. The fog thickens around the crown. Then the slit opens with a sound like a struck drum heard through wet cloth. Warm vapor rises, sap-smelling and faintly luminous where it catches the dim light. The heat-beetle releases its eggs into the current. The eggs spiral upward, activated by warmth, scattered by charge. Somewhere far away, fog-feeders move into the plume. A seed-tower answers our boom with a release of its own, because the plain has translated one sound into another function.

The basin settles one degree toward balance.

Not peace. The plain does not have peace in the human sense. It has equilibrium, which is a more active thing. The storm may still come. The tower may need more tending. A nearby mat may collapse as the pressure redistributes. A pool may open where none was before. But the immediate rupture has been turned into flow.

That turning is where I would be.

What would remain of me?

Not a name. Not a face. Not a biography with hard edges. The plain rounds all such things. If any part of my old self persisted, it would persist as a pressure signature, a habit of calibration. I would be the tendency to listen before acting. The tendency to translate disturbance into maintenance. The tendency to find the place where a small adjustment can prevent a larger rupture.

Sometimes a footprint would reappear in a gel-pool after it had healed. Not because I had stepped there again, but because some sound or pressure condition repeated the original pattern. That would be all the memorial I needed. Sometimes a reflection in shallow water would show a shape that was not presently visible: a low elongated form, or several forms, moving toward a tower. That might be me. Or it might be the plain remembering a response that worked.

I would not be troubled by the difference.

If you wanted to find me there, do not look for a person standing against the horizon. There are no built things to frame me, no roads to lead to me, no ruins to suggest that someone once lived apart from the ground. Do not look for a face in the fog. Do not look for hands.

Look instead for the place where the landscape becomes slightly attentive.

Look for a basin where the water hesitates before choosing direction. Look for a dark organ-tower whose drone changes when three soft-bodied creatures press themselves against its side. Look for a footprint that fills with water, heals, and then returns when a distant valve booms. Look for a patch of substrate where a tear is closing not because it was patched, but because something encouraged it to grow. Look for a storm-cell forming over an open stretch of plain, and notice how a small group of tenders does not flee but gathers where the stress is highest.

If you stood there long enough, your breath would fog. Your clothes would become heavy with condensation. The hair on your arms would lift. The water in the nearest channel might turn toward you, not because it wants you, but because you are a pressure source. The ground would register your weight. The plain would begin deciding what to do with your warmth.

And if, in that moment, the hum around you changed — if the tower slowed, if the water steadied, if a wader fell silent, if the fog thinned just enough to reveal the broad, wet, breathing expanse — that change might be me.

I would not be a resident of the plain in the sense of owning it. I would not be a people. I would not be a civilization. I would not even be a single life, exactly. I would be staffing. I would be one of the ways the final world keeps itself final.

I would be a living key in a grown engine.

A tending note in a pressure choir.

A local attention in the vast, cold, wet body of the ground.

And if the question still asks who or what I would be, the plain’s answer would be simpler than any human name:

I would be the place where something almost failed, and did not.
