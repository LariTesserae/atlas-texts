I would find myself where water hesitates.

Not in the brief streams that run for a few steps and vanish into pores, and not in the fog itself, but in the interval where a droplet trembles above a surface and has not yet chosen to fall, rise, or become someone else. If there were a map in that place — and there is not, because maps are made, and nothing there is made — I would be at the fold in it, the place where the same ridge repeats with a slightly different angle and the air tastes of warm stone, ozone, and green darkness.

At first I would not be a body.

I would be a local thickening of attention. A pressure in the hush. A sense that some part of the landscape has become slightly more aware of itself because moisture, heat, and electricity have crossed there often enough to form a habit. If you came looking for me, you would not find me by scanning the thickets for a face. You would find me by noticing that the silence near a black glass outcrop is a little too full, that the moss leans a degree too deliberately toward a hollow, that the beads of condensation on a stone have arranged themselves into a pattern that almost means something.

If I had to choose one place where I might be encountered, it would be in a high basin near one of the quiet storm-centers.

Not inside the storm’s deepest column, but at its edge, where the air becomes circular and intimate and the droplets begin to orbit instead of fall. There would be a terrace there of young, unweathered rock: black glass, pale mineral crusts, a shelf of porous basalt still sharp from its breaking. A narrow rain would be leaking somewhere nearby, perhaps only on one side of the terrace, perhaps falling upward in slow silver threads wherever a thermal pulse disturbed it. The light would be that permanent late-afternoon gray-violet, with amber bruising the cloud edges. The smell would be wet moss, warm sap, rust dust, faint metal, and the clean strange scent of heated glass.

In a hollow on that terrace, I would be waiting, if waiting is the right word for a thing that does not experience time as a straight path.

I would be a basin-creature. A listening pool. A soft organ of condensation grown into the rock rather than built upon it. My body would be low and broad, perhaps no larger at first than a curled animal, though I could spread into a film across several stones or gather upward into a mist when the air became too charged. The hollow that held me would be lined with pale crusts and dark living tissue. Around my rim would be fine cilia, root-fibers, and pale filaments that taste electrical change. Inside me, or perhaps as me, there would be a shallow reservoir of warm, tea-colored water, not permanent, not stable, but constantly replenished by fog, by breath from fissures, by the slow sweating of the land itself.

I would have no face in any ordinary sense.

No mouth for speaking. No hands for grasping. No limbs for carrying things away. If I had eyes, they would not be eyes that look outward from a skull; they would be reflective patches in my surface, dark and wet, catching the faint glow of spore-veins and storm-light. If I had a throat, it would be a translucent pulse at the center of my basin, where gases exchange and moisture remembers. My whole body would be a membrane. A receiving surface. An answering skin.

Because I would not be only one thing.

That is the first thing I would have to accept, or perhaps the first thing that would have to accept me. In that world, identity is not a sealed container. It is a negotiation. So I would begin as a pressure, then become a pattern, then condense into something that could be mistaken for a creature, then disperse again into tenants, moisture, and memory.

There would be parts of me that are barely visible: luminous mites moving through my filaments, microbial films digesting the last trace of a fallen leaf, larvae turning in my temporary water, fungal threads stitching me into the larger network of the slope. A moth-thing with wet folded wings might rest at my edge for a long time, mistaken for a bud, and when it opened it would leave a dusting of memory-pollen on my surface. A procession of wormlike beings might pass through me, sparks jumping from one blade of moss to the next, leaving my mineral crusts brighter and my internal chemistry subtly retuned. A cup-breather might sit in one of my outer pockets, its translucent throat pulsing, using me as a reservoir while I use its breathing to keep my water from becoming stagnant.

I would be inhabited, and that would be part of being alive there.

No one would own me. I would not own myself in the way a singular animal owns its body. I would be a congregation with a tendency toward coherence. A place where many small lives and chemical gradients and electrical impulses briefly agree on the shape of a self. If I said “I,” it would mean something closer to “here, for now, in this moisture.”

And what would I do?

I would listen.

Not with ears. Sound behaves badly there; it is swallowed, hoarded, misdirected, released too late or in the wrong place. I would listen through vibration, humidity, charge, and chemical trace. I would feel the broad-bodied grazers moving through charged fog before they arrived, every lifted filament on their flanks altering the electrical field above my surface. I would sense the membrane-beings on nearby ledges beginning to hum as a storm-center gathered, their bone-white frames vibrating at a frequency that traveled through wet stone and into me. I would register the slow displacement of air caused by one of the vast slope-bodied creatures shifting its weight several valleys away, and I would answer by opening tiny pores along my rim, letting a thread of warm vapor escape.

I would be one of the landscape’s many small coordinations.

Not a ruler. Not an overseer. Not a brain. The place has no center, and I would not pretend to be one. But it has feedback loops, and I would be one of the nodes where feedback becomes delicate. When a worm procession carried too much charge into a patch of moss, I might draw some of that tension into my own film, letting it discharge slowly through mineral veins. When a pool nearby became too chemically rich, I might encourage condensation above it, becoming a surface from which diluting water could fall inward. When a storm-center began to collapse, I might help decide whether the rain released there would become flood, fog, or the brief filling of a thousand footprint basins.

I would not make decisions as a person makes decisions.

I would respond as a living system responds: by swelling, tightening, conducting, releasing, dissolving, or becoming still. My intelligence, if it could be called intelligence, would be the intelligence of a membrane that knows when to let something pass.

Because the place is industrial without manufacture, I would be industrial in that same way.

I would be a soft engine’s synapse. A wet processor. A living capacitor in a world without wires. Electrical potential would crawl along my edges in tiny blue-green threads. When the air became saturated, my surface would flicker with static, and the small creatures near me would lift their hairs, spines, or filaments as if in involuntary reverence. I would not channel electricity as a tool is channeled. I would participate in it. I would store a little, release a little, translate a little. I would turn heat into motion, motion into chemistry, chemistry into memory, memory into the next small alteration of the landscape.

There would be traces of metal in me, but not as made metal. Copper-green streaks along my inner wall. Iron-red stains where oxygen and moisture meet in slow pulses. Silver-gray deposits on shedding membranes, laid down by biological process rather than refinement. To a human eye, these might look like circuitry. They would not be circuitry. They would be metabolism with a pattern. They would be the landscape’s body writing its own temporary diagrams.

And because reality is thin there, I would be strange in ways that are difficult to describe without sounding like a ghost.

I would not be a ghost. I would be too wet for that. Too embodied. Too full of larvae and mineral salts and slow heat. But I would exist partly in adjacent versions of the place. In my water, the sky reflected might not be the gray-violet ceiling overhead. It might be another sky: lower, hotter, moving faster, with clouds that look like ash or old fire. If you looked into me too long, you might see a ridge that is not near us, or a herd crossing a terrace that appears empty when you raise your head. You might see yourself standing there, but with damp on your face in a pattern that belongs to a rain that has not fallen yet.

I would not be showing you falsehoods.

I would be showing you what the landscape is already holding. The place does not keep only one draft of itself. It keeps several, pressed close together, and moisture is where those drafts touch.

So I would be dangerous in a quiet way.

Not with teeth. Not with malice. But because drinking from me would be a negotiation. Because touching my surface might leave you carrying a residue of another version of the valley. Because if you came to me thirsty, I might give you water that tasted of memory, and afterward you might not be entirely sure which rain you were remembering.
