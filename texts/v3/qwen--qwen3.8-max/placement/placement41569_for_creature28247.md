Their limbs do not end in hoof or claw, but in broad, layered pads: dense, fibrous cushions that distribute their weight across the resonant ground and let them move without striking it. They do not stamp. They place. Their motion is slow not because they are sluggish, but because waste has been burned out of them by the world itself. Every unnecessary gesture would be a loss of solvent, heat, balance, or silence. Their joints click with dry precision; their breathing valves open and close in metered hisses; their bodies keep time with the larger respiration of the plain.

The plates across their backs and flanks are not inert shells. They are living instruments. Each one is curved, ribbed, and tuned by growth to the pressures it must endure. Wind passing over them makes tone. Movement answers tone. When the herd walks, it produces a low composite music: a pattern of footfall, plate-contact, valve-hiss, and ground-return that is not separate from language. They do not speak in words. They alter rhythm. They change spacing. They let vibration travel through the plant-stone until the whole group knows whether to continue, turn, halt, or spread.

Their senses are suited to a world without wetness and without quiet. Their eyes are deep-set beneath ridges of cuticle, shaded from glare and grit, but sight is only one way of knowing. They read distance by the bending of sound around tower-forms. They read danger by the taste of charged dust. Fine bristles along their shoulders and flank-plates lift when static accumulates. The soles of their foot-pads feel changes in ground tension long before an event becomes audible. A seed-burst does not begin for them as a noise. It begins as a tightening in the substrate, a change in the way their own weight is answered by the plain.

Their mouths are transverse rasps, sealed when not feeding, capable of cropping thin living films from cracked surfaces without tearing more than they can process. They graze on sealed crusts, resinous skins, spore-fall mats, and the tight waxy growths that form in polygon fractures and wind-shadowed hollows. They do not drink. They take their solvent from living tissue, and then they keep it with severe discipline. Breath passes through labyrinthine internal channels where moisture is reclaimed. Waste leaves dry. Heat is shed through plates, not sweat. Their bodies are not merely resistant to the world; they are made from its central rule: nothing open, nothing spilled, nothing left uncontained.

They are not gentle because they are kind. They are patient because the world punishes excess. A sudden charge into still air can draw a spark. A heavy footfall near a pressure ridge can meet a discharge. A grazer that lingers too long beneath a venting organ may be caught in a concussive sigh or a cascade of seed-vessel bursts. So they move with care. They rest in acoustic shadows. They feed where productive currents meet, but not where those currents become violent. They know, in the way their kind knows, that safety is not found in permanence but in timing.

And if I were to answer for myself — if the question is not merely what lives there, but where I would be within it — I would not begin as a human figure standing exposed on the plain. That figure would be a brief error in the world: thirsty, deafened, blinded, searching for shelter where shelter cannot be built and for water where water does not stand. No, I would not be a visitor there. I would be inside the condition of the place.

I would be one of the herds.

You would find me in a moving quiet. Not in absolute silence, because this world has none, but in one of those fragile intervals where sound cancels sound: in the acoustic lee of the herd itself, among bodies broad enough to break the pressure waves, among plates angled just so, among footfalls that turn the plain’s deafening chord into something survivable. The herd is not only a group of animals. It is also a shelter made of participants. It makes a temporary calm by the way it stands, moves, and spaces itself.
