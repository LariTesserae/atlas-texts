It has presences rather than citizens, tenants rather than crowds: small lives that arrive with moisture, hide in frost, and belong more to intervals than to streets.

If I were in that world, I would not be found standing in the open plaza, or walking the rim, or looking down at the basin as a traveler might. I would already be inside its grammar. I would be one of the small lives the basin tolerates, one of the quiet bodies that occur in the pauses between wind and water.

You would find me in the wet quarter.

Not at the lowest pools themselves, where too much exposure would make me visible, but just above them, in the darkened seam between a moss terrace and one of the hollow columns. There would be a narrow channel nearby, dry most of the time, but darkened by old water and by the memory of flow. When condensation gathers or frost thins around noon, that channel would wake for a little while. I would live near that brief silver thread.

I would be small.

No larger than a hand laid flat, perhaps a little longer when my filaments are extended. My body would be pale, almost bone-colored, but not clean or bare. It would carry a living surface: a thin bloom of ash-colored lichen, fine moss hairs, mineral grains held in place by slow secretions. To a casual eye, I would look less like an animal than like a detached piece of the city that had acquired motion. A knot of crust. A swelling on the wall. A pale lichen that can fold itself into a crevice when the wind becomes too sharp.

I would be jointed, but not in a way that suggests speed. My limbs would be thin, patient, and adapted to holding stillness. Some would end in soft pads for clinging to damp stone. Others would be finer, almost thread-like, able to reach into narrow seams where water beads in darkness. Parts of my body would be hollow, or semi-hollow, not because I had made them into tools, but because that is how my kind would have grown: shaped by the same cold, mineral, slow accretion that shapes the basin itself. When wind passed over me, I would not whistle loudly, but I would add a faint note, a small living voice inside the larger instrument.

I would not have a face in the human sense.

I would have dull light-sensitive spots, enough to know the difference between clear day, cloud, fog, and the approach of night. The basin’s real world would not be visual. It would be vibrational. I would know the place through pressure, tremor, humidity, temperature, and the minute behavior of air in hollow spaces. I would feel the wind entering the columns as a change along my limbs. I would know rain before it arrived by the first quickening of the hollows. I would know another living thing by the tiny disturbance it made in moss: a springtail moving through green felt, a midge trembling near a pool, the almost imperceptible pause before a spider-like predator shifts in a crack.

That would be my way of being awake.

I would eat little. The basin does not offer abundance, and I would not require much. Midges where moisture collects. Springtails moving through the moss. Perhaps, rarely, a moth that has come too far and become too cold to escape. I would not hunt by pursuit. I would wait in a sheltered seam where condensation runs, spreading fine biological filaments from my body. These would not be devices. They would be extensions of me, the way a spider’s web is an extension of a spider’s body: spun, temporary, repaired, dissolved, renewed. In the wettest moments, those filaments would catch droplets as well as prey. I would drink from the bead on a moss hair, from a thread of meltwater, from the cold skin of condensation forming on stone.

My home would not be a house.

It would be a position. A favored hollow. A niche inside the lower part of a column where the wind is divided and the temperature changes more slowly than in the open air. The column would not belong to me. I would belong to it, in the way a hermit belongs to a shell, except that I would not have carried it anywhere. It would have grown into being over ages, and I would have grown into it. My body would fit the cold there. My limbs would know the exact curve of the inner wall. There would be a small shelf of mineral crust where I rest, a dark stain where moisture gathers near me, a faint accumulation of shed skins and tiny mineral traces where I have remained through many seasons.

If the basin has a citizen, I would be that.

Not a builder. Not a ruler. Not a maker of marks. Not a keeper of memory in the human way. I would be a minor continuation of the place. The city is made by accretion; I would be an accreter too. My presence would be almost invisible. A slight darkening in a crevice. A few threads across a channel. A faint clicking when a fragment of brittle lichen falls because I moved through it in the night. A small disturbance in the moss that heals slowly.

I would not experience the basin as empty.

Emptiness is a judgment made by creatures who expect crowds. For me, the basin would be full of pressures and arrivals. The morning frost would be an event. The first drip from an overhang would be a signal. A passing cloud that lowers the light and makes the stone smell colder would change the whole meaning of the day. The sound of the hollow columns would not be noise; it would be the condition of my life. I would live inside that sound the way a lowland creature lives inside air. When the wind swells and the basin becomes loud, I would draw deeper into the column and let the resonance move through me. When the air goes still, I would become more alert, because stillness in that place means something else: frost tightening, water pausing, prey becoming careless, the cold coming closer.

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness would require the expectation of another kind of company. I might know something like recognition if another of my kind left a trace in the basin: a shifted filament, a changed vibration in a column, a faint chemical mineral signature on stone. But we would not gather. We would not build together in the open. We would be too few, too slow, too fitted to our separate niches. If we met, it would likely be in the narrow grammar of moisture: at a damp seam, during a brief rain, when the channels darken and the city briefly remembers flow. Even then, we would not speak. We would register one another as a change in the pattern.

If I had a name, it would not be a word.

It would be something more like a position in the basin’s sound. A thin note near the wet quarter. A pause between two drips. The faint click in a hollow column when the wind shifts. If the basin could name anything, it would name me as one of its small listening points: a place where the city’s emptiness becomes slightly aware of itself.

In ordinary daylight, I would be almost invisible. The normality of the light would make me harder to see, not easier. There would be no glow around me, no aura, no sign that the stone held a hidden inhabitant. The sun would cross the sky. Clouds would pass. Shadows would move over moss and column and pool. I would be there among the black-green felts and pale lichens, pressed close to the old stone, cold and patient, with frost on my back and moisture gathering along my filaments.

At night, I would slow.

The basin would contract around me. Water would stiffen. Moss would become brittle. Ice would form in delicate sheets over the smallest pools. The hollow columns would sing with a harder voice. I would fold myself into my niche and become as much like the wall as possible. My thoughts, if they could be called thoughts, would be slow and local: wet, dry, cold, less cold, resonance, silence, prey, rest. I would not dream in images. I might dream in vibrations: the memory of rain in a channel, the pressure of fog against stone, the long mineral patience of the mountain’s final form.

If some trace of human feeling remained in me, it would not be desire for fire, or tools, or speech. It would be this only: a tendency to notice pattern. A sense that the channels are streets.
