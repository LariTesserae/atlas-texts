The old there is not kept because it is old. It is kept because it is known, and because the known is what can be repaired.

If I were to find myself in that high bowl, I think I would at first mistake my own place. I would be drawn to the water plaza, to the low parapets and the darkened stone lips, because that is where the town’s meaning is most visible. I would want to stand near the basins and be useful there. I would imagine myself as a basin-keeper, dipping the marked stave, reading the level, cleaning the mineral crust from the mouths of cisterns. There is something calming about such work: a clear quantity, a public trust, a task that can be done the same way each day without becoming less necessary.

But I would not last long in that first imagining.

I would be too attentive in the wrong way. I would check too often. I would worry over the water, lean too close to it, try to anticipate what it had not yet decided to do. The town would not approve of that. The basins do not need anxiety. They need routine. They need someone who can look at a level and accept it, who can record the fact without turning the fact into fear. I would have to learn that caring, in that place, is not the same as hovering.

So I would move upward in my mind, toward the residential terraces, and for a while I would imagine myself among the roof-walkers. That life has its own attraction: the open sky, the pale slabs, the gutters running thin after fog. I can see myself with a soft broom, brushing dust toward the channels, clearing grit from corners where the wind has left it. I can see myself on a ladder at dawn, checking an overlapping tile after a night of cold air. It is honest work. It is work with immediate results.

But I think I would be too restless for it. Roof-walkers must be patient with weather. They must not resent the fog for coming thinly, or the wind for carrying dust, or the sun for drying what was only just damp. They must serve the catchment without wanting it to be more generous. I would have to learn not to look at the sky as if I were asking something of it. The town does not ask. It receives.

Then I would imagine the fog-readers, standing at the rim walk with their hands on the parapet, knowing by some long practice how the cloud will enter the bowl. I would admire them most, I think. They would seem to me the most complete expression of the place: quiet, observant, unhurried, their knowledge so integrated into habit that it would not sound like knowledge at all. But I would not be one of them, not at first. That kind of authority is not claimed. It is grown into, like lichen on stone. It belongs to those who have stood there long enough to be trusted by the town’s silence. I would not yet know which wall darkens first, which gutter breathes first, which terrace remembers the fog longest. I would only be guessing, however beautifully.

No. If the town took me in and assigned me a place according to my nature, I think it would put me lower, in the dim passages near the water ward, where the channels run under stone and the town’s smallest failures can be heard before they become losses.

I would become a listener for leaks.

That is not a grand title. It might not even be a title at all. If anyone spoke of me, they might say only, “They are good at finding where the sound changes,” or “They know which wall is damp before the stone shows it.” I would live near the lower-middle terraces, not far from the covered walk that connects the basin court to the first stair. My room would be small, cool, and almost empty: a folded sleeping mat, a wool blanket, a jar in a wall niche, a low bench, a shutter that closes tight against the night air. There would be a narrow window, but it would not be there for the view. It would be there to let a little dry air move when the room must be cleared of closeness.

My clothes would become like everyone else’s: ash-gray wool, repaired in squares, soft at the cuffs. I would keep a small tool roll at my belt. A wooden scraper. A bronze hook no longer than a finger. A strip of felt. A short cord. A bit of chalk or pale clay for temporary marking. Nothing decorative. Nothing that would catch on a doorway or ring against stone. My sandals would be soft-soled, and I would learn to walk so that my arrival was announced by no more than a slight change in the air.

My work would begin before light.

While the basin was still dark and the roofs were only beginning to collect themselves into shapes, I would go out with a shaded lamp. Not because I needed much light, but because the town’s keepers must know where one another are without calling out. I would pass through the water ward and lower myself into the service spaces behind the cisterns, where the stone is older than polish and the air smells of damp mineral and old clay. I would set the lamp down where it would not glare. Then I would listen.

At first, anyone hearing the town in that way would think it silent. But it is not silent. It is full of small separate sounds, each with its own interval and intention. A drop striking a stone lip. A whisper of water moving through a covered channel. The faint hollow note of a cistern that is fuller than it was the night before. The almost inaudible tick of a wooden cover settling as the cold releases it. Most of these sounds are right. They belong to the town’s ordinary breathing. They mean that the pattern is holding.

But some sounds are wrong.

A leak has a different grammar. It is not always louder than the proper sounds; often it is softer. It is a hesitation where there should be continuity. A double drip where there should be one. A faint hiss against stone that should not be wet. A spreading quietness where moisture is taking a path no one gave it. To find it, one must not strain too hard. Straining makes the ear greedy. The town would teach me to listen the way the stone itself receives fog: without effort, but with complete exposure.

I would kneel beside a cistern door and place my ear near the joint. I would press my palm flat against a wall and wait for coolness to become dampness. I would follow the sound along the underside of a stair, along the back of a trough, along a channel that ran beneath paving stones worn smooth by centuries of careful feet. Sometimes the fault would be obvious: a blocked downspout whispering with frustration, a cracked tile letting water stray into a dry joint. Sometimes it would be subtler: a hairline passage opened by cold, a root too ambitious in some forgotten corner, a settling block that had changed the angle of a gutter by less than a finger’s width.

I would not repair everything myself. The town knows better than to let one person become too many things. If the fault was stone, I would mark it and call a mender. If it was clay or mortar, I would call the one whose hands knew linings. If it was wood, I would fetch the person who understood hinges and covers. My task would be to find the place where the town had begun to forget itself, and then to hand that knowledge to the hands that could make it remember.

That last part would be the hardest for me, I think. I would want to fix everything at once. I would want to be the one who restores the pattern completely, who stands back and sees the result immediately. But the town does not work that way. It does not reward dramatic repair. It rewards the small, correct next action. If a step is cracked, the step is replaced. If a gutter wears, the gutter is re-lined. If a leak is found, the leak is stopped. Nothing becomes an occasion. Nothing becomes a story told too loudly.

So I would learn to be content with finding.

I would learn, too, to be quiet about finding. In the bowl, sound carries. A voice raised in triumph would be a kind of damage. If I discovered a fault, I would not announce it across the court. I would go to the right person and speak in the tone one uses for necessary things. “There is a wetness under the third landing.” “The south channel is whispering where it should not.” “The cover on the lower jar has begun to cup the air wrongly.” The words would be plain. The meaning would be enough.

I would become accustomed to being unnoticed.

That would be strange for me at first. I think I would carry some old habit of wanting to be seen while working, of wanting the effort to be recognized. The town would wear that out of me, not by punishment but by indifference. There, work is not performance. A clean gutter is not praised. A level basin is not admired. The success of the town is that nothing happens. No wall falls. No water is lost beyond knowing. No change accumulates into rupture. The keepers move through their days and leave behind only continuation.

I would begin to understand that this is not emptiness. It is a kind of fullness kept in check.

My days would have a rhythm. Before light: listening. After the first gray brightening: checking the places where night had left moisture. When the sun came: watching how stone dried, because uneven drying can reveal what even darkness hides. In the late morning: walking the covered passages, the undersides of stairs, the backs of troughs, the corners where shadow holds. At midday: resting in shade, because hurry at that altitude is foolish. In the afternoon: helping where help is needed, carrying a tool, holding a rope, sweeping dust from a repair site, fetching a replacement slab if my shoulders are strong enough. In the evening: returning to my room, cleaning my tools, eating simply, drinking carefully.

I would drink differently there. I do not mean only sparingly, though that too. I mean I would drink with attention. I would know, in the act of lifting a cup, how many surfaces had gathered that water, how many gutters had directed it, how many hands had kept it clean. I would not gulp. I would not waste. Even thirst would become a form of respect.

My body would change in small ways. My hands would dry and harden. My lips would crack until I learned to keep them covered. My breathing would slow. My footsteps would shorten. My eyes would become used to following fine lines: a seam in stone, a bead of moisture, a faint discoloration along a wall. I would stop thinking of silence as absence and begin to hear it as a texture. The town’s silence would not be blank. It would be composed of many small permitted sounds: drip, breath, wool brushing stone, the soft closing of wood, the distant cry of a bird crossing the bowl without leaving a mark on it.

I would also be given unused rooms to tend.

This part of my life there would come later, perhaps after I had learned to listen well enough to be trusted with absence. Someone would show me a row of chambers on a middle terrace, their doors low, their shutters closed, their thresholds worn by feet that no longer come. “These need air,” they would say. “Not too much. Not at the wrong hour.” And I would understand. The rooms are not dead. They are held in readiness. They are part of the town’s capacity, and capacity must be maintained even when it is not being used.

I would open them in the dry part of the morning. I would lift the shutters just enough to let the cool air move. I would sweep dust from floors that seem already clean. I would check hinges, corners, ceilings, and the small stone niches where jars once stood or might stand again. I would not rearrange anything. I would not make the rooms more comfortable, more modern, more suited to my own taste. I would keep them as they are, because their shape is a promise: that if someone returns, the town will still know how to receive them.
