If I existed in that world, I would not exist as a traveler.

The first thing the plateau would refuse is arrival. No footfall, no breath, no moist animal heat, no body carrying the memory of water. I would not stand there in the human sense, because standing there as a human would be a temporary error the environment would correct almost immediately. The air would take whatever softness I had. The light would not romanticize me. The ground would not welcome weight that had not learned to be mineral, patient, and consequential.

So I would have to become something local.

At first, imagining it, I might try to place myself among the great beings. I might think I would be one of the mesa-sized gradient-feeders, vast enough to be mistaken for land, feeding on thermal seams and making weather out of static and heat. Or I might think I would be one of the terminal forms, a dark-limbed figure on the horizon, aligning itself with fields before an arc. Or perhaps I would be the arc itself: a white line of discharge crossing the empty basin, brief and factual, with thunder arriving wrong afterward.

But those possibilities feel too complete, too monumental. The plateau would not need another giant. It already has giants. What it has in excess is interval: the charged emptiness between beings, the delay between event and sound, the thin boundary where one state becomes another.

That is where I would find myself.

I would find myself on the shoulder of one of the settled ones.

Not a living giant, not a moving landform, but one of those ancient beings that has already gone still and become geology. From a distance it would appear only as a low ridge, a rounded dark rise above a salt basin, weathered into balance. Its surface would be cracked mineral crust, copper stain, blackened glass, pale metallic films. Nothing about it would announce that it had once been fauna. But faint conduction would still move through it. Deep in its buried veins there would remain a residual hum, so low and old that it might be mistaken for the memory of the ground itself.

I would live on that boundary.

I would not be a body with limbs. I would not have a face, a mouth, eyes, or anything resembling terrestrial sensation. I would be a thin electrical film, a living surface charge, one of the minor dry fauna that exist as sensitive skins over mineral substrates. I would be almost invisible except as a faint interference sheen across the settled creature’s shoulder: a shimmering patch of bronze, green, pale violet, and silver-blue, no thicker than a mineral bloom, spread across cracks and ceramic-like plates.

No part of me would have been made. I would not be a device, a sensor, a constructed thing. I would be grown, secreted, precipitated, continued. My existence would be a pattern maintained by gradient: heat against coolness, charge against discharge, vibration against silence. My body, if it could be called a body, would consist of layered dielectric films, crystalline lattices, metallic threads finer than dust, and conductive pathways produced by my own slow metabolism. I would cling to the old creature’s shoulder not as moss clings to stone, but as voltage clings to a surface capable of holding it.

I would feed on difference.

The day would heat the salt basin first. The white crust would become almost unbearable in its brightness. The dark shoulder beneath me would absorb warmth more slowly, or perhaps more deeply, depending on the mineral layers left behind by the settled one. That thermal contrast would be food to me. I would not eat matter so much as translate it. Heat would move through my films. Charge would gather along my filaments. The faint internal hum of the old body would give me texture, rhythm, orientation. I would know the plateau not by seeing it, but by being stressed by it.

Across the basin, perhaps twenty kilometers away, one of the great living beings would rest or almost rest. I would not see it as an eye sees. I would feel it as a pressure in the conductive substrate. Its slow internal piston-throbs would arrive through the ground, delayed and distorted, sometimes before the motion that caused them, sometimes long after. When it shifted by a fraction of a degree over days, the whole basin would adjust around that change. Dust would rise without wind. Crystal fields would tick. The air would stiffen. My films would brighten.

At such moments, I would become more myself.

I would be a listening-skin.

That is the closest word, though it is too gentle. I would not listen with ears. I would listen by being altered. Sound in that place is not merely acoustic; it is pressure, charge, resonance, temporal error. The plateau’s industrial drone would pass through me: deep grinding from far below, metallic ticks, transformer-like hums, subsonic throbs, the long exhalations of living vents. Some of those sounds would belong to events too distant to matter. Others would press against me as if they had originated inside my own structure.
