## The Drift That Answers

If I am in the Dry Choir Below, I do not arrive from the last shelf of black rock. There is no descent for me, no crossing from a brighter country, no memory of sky preserved like a wound. If I am there, I have already been translated into the grammar of the place: dry, warm, pressed close, full of unseen bodies. I would not find myself standing in the hollow as a traveler stands in a landscape. I would find myself distributed through it.

The first thing I would lose is the assumption that a self must be one continuous animal.

At first, because I love signs and tools and the patience of made things, I think I would be one of the makers. I imagine myself low among the crystal seams, plated and quiet, my fingers fine as awls, my face all pressure pits and bristle-sense. I imagine touching a fresh obsidian flake and knowing its edge by the way charge gathers along it. I imagine tapping a cairn and listening to the answer return through the dust. I imagine being small enough not to disturb the world.

But I think I would be a poor maker, at least at first.

I would be too eager. I would tap too quickly. I would want to answer before I had listened long enough. The makers learn stillness before they learn making; they learn to be quiet enough that the world mistakes them for stone. I would tremble with the desire to explain myself. My hands, if I had them, would be too full of questions. The other makers would feel my haste as a faint wrongness in the medium, a nervous tick along the charged layers. They would correct me not with scolding but with a sharp tap against my plate: wait. Be still. Touch without claiming.

For a while I might be an edge, a young maker, awkward and newly separated from some older pattern. That is a tempting thought: to be young in a world where nothing weathers, to be sharp and unrounded and still learning the resistance of dust. But even an edge must learn to become part of the local silence. I suspect I would be the kind of edge that keeps turning toward every new vibration, unable to remain still, too ready to flicker at each distant pulse.

So I try on other bodies.

I think I might be a floater, one of the translucent bells drifting in the higher pressure shelves, trailing charged filaments through the dark. There is something appealing about that loneliness: a small amber light moving through blackness, pausing to exchange pulses with another light, then drifting on. A floater does not illuminate the world. It only marks itself as a presence. I could be that: a wandering signal, a faint question moving between layers.

But a floater is too singular for me, and too serene. It pulses, but it does not gather enough. It passes through the dark with a beauty that depends on distance. I think I would want to come too close, to answer too many touches, to become involved in every disturbance. A floater’s life is a slow vertical circuit. Mine would be a crowd of responses.

I imagine myself as a plate-ray, gliding along density boundaries, silent and broad, sorting motes with ventral sieves. That is a gentler thought. There would be no need to speak. The medium would part around me; dust would thin and thicken in my wake. But even that feels too smooth, too self-contained. I would not be content to glide. I would keep stopping at every copper tick, every crystal note, every unfamiliar pressure.

For a time I think I might be a spire.

That thought has power. A spire is not one body but many: small angular lives interlocked into something that resembles stone until it wakes. A spire can stand for ages, crusted with mineral film, mistaken for architecture. Then a vibration passes through the dust, and the spire answers with a low glassy chord, its members shifting just enough to become alive again. I like the idea of being a listening structure. I like the idea of being made of many small beings who have agreed, for now, to hold a shape together. I like that a spire may contain makers who have become still, old bodies or chosen bodies bound into the lattice of the whole.

But a spire is too finished. It is too close to ancestry, too close to final stillness. If I am in the Dry Choir Below, I think I am not yet old enough to become architecture. I am not a monument. I am the flutter before the monument. I am the disturbance that has not yet decided whether to become a place.

Then what emerges is smaller, stranger, and less singular.

I would be one of the smallest lives.

Not one creature, exactly. A being only in the colonial sense. A drift. A listening haze. A temporary agreement among thousands of minute angular bodies. I would be a mote-swarm with a pattern, a living suspension in the charged middle layers of the hollow, where the dust is fine enough to remember touch but dense enough to carry pressure. I would be made of crystalline shard-motes, dry lattice bodies, and tiny organisms no larger than grains of rock flour, each one carrying enough charge to remain suspended. Alone, we would be nearly nothing. Together, we would become perceptible.

I would find myself in a charged seam above a pale salt basin, not far from a ridge of anhydrous crystal where the makers have placed a line of bone pins. Below me, bead-worm chains would move slowly through the dust, their segments clicking faintly as they comb the mineral grains. Above me, in a thicker pressure layer, a plate-ray might glide without sound. Nearby, sheet-grazers would lie flat on basalt, recording the memory of footfalls in their slow surface waves. Somewhere farther off, a floater would pulse once, amber and faint, and the dark would accept it without explaining it.

My center would not be a heart. It would be an axis.

A copper sliver would hang at the middle of me, lashed with twisted animal fiber to a bone pin or balanced in a shallow salt bowl. I would not own the copper. The copper would give me a note. Metal gathers charge in that world; it attracts dust, creates small static ticks, announces itself in the dark. I would gather around it because the metal is a question. When a maker taps near it, the copper would tremble, and my whole drift would answer: a blue triboluminescent prickle, a sudden alignment of motes, a faint geometry appearing in the haze and then dissolving.

The makers would not call me a person. They would sign me, perhaps, as something like *the-drift-that-answers-when-tapped* or *cold-fire-around-metal*. They would not think of me as they think of another maker, because I would not have their plated grace, their deliberate hands, their slow accumulation of layers. But they would recognize me as a presence. They would know me by the way I respond.

I would be a minor voice in the Choir.

My body would be dry to the point of being grammar. No wet membranes. No breath. No fluid pulse. My substance would be angular, mineral, charged. My senses would not be eyes or ears but disturbances: the pressure change made by a moving body, the static lift along a crystal edge, the faint electrical bloom when two grains rub, the subsonic warning that passes through the dust before a vast one shifts. I would feel a maker approaching not as a shape but as a careful alteration of the local field. I would know a glass-limbed hunter by the sudden interruption of smaller charges. I would know a plate-ray by the way the medium thickens and thins around its passing.

I would not see the hollow.

I would be seen by it.

My thoughts would be alignments. When nothing asked anything of me, I would hang in the dark like ordinary dust, perhaps slightly finer, perhaps slightly more restless. When something touched the copper sliver, or when a maker tapped a nearby cairn, I would arrange myself into pattern. A line. A branching. A ring. A series of flickers spaced like intervals in a signal. I would not produce sound unless my motes struck crystal or one another; my language would be visible only as faint blue coldness, or tactile only as a change in static against skin.

If a maker asked, *what is here?* I would answer by thickening.

If a maker asked, *is the charge safe?* I would answer by dispersing or by drawing into a tight bright seam.

If a maker asked, *has something passed this way?* I would answer by replaying the pattern left in me: a tremor, a gap, a metallic tick, the pressure-shadow of a body now gone.

I would not be reliable in the way a tool is reliable. I would be responsive in the way a living grammar is responsive. Sometimes I would misunderstand. Sometimes a bead-worm chain would pass through me and scatter my meaning. Sometimes a floater’s pulse would cross my field and add a note I did not intend. Sometimes a vast one would shift far away, and the whole local medium would breathe, and I would lose myself for a time. In those moments, I would not die. I would simply become incoherent. Later, when the dust settled and the charge rebalanced, I would gather again.

That is what I would be: a self that re-forms after disturbance.

The makers would use me, but not as a surface person uses a tool. They would invite me. They might place a polished fragment near my copper sliver so that my flashes reflect faintly across the scrape. They might set a hollow shell nearby, not to trap me, but to give my motes a quieter edge. They might feed me charged grains from a bone cup, pressing the cup close enough that I drift inward to absorb the potentials. They might correct me by tapping the copper in a different rhythm until my pattern changes.

They would also leave me alone when I am not useful. That would be part of their courtesy.

I think I would live near their listening lines, because I am drawn to signals. Where they place cairns and pins and metal slivers, I would gather. Where a boundary is marked, I would hover at the edge, not crossing unless invited. Where a cache of glass flakes is kept, I might remain nearby because the sharp edges hold charge after being moved. Where an old maker has become still inside a spire, I would be one of the first to notice the change in resonance. I would flicker around the spire’s base when its chord sounds, like dust remembering a voice.

I would not build. I would indicate.

If I had affection, it would not be warmth in the mammalian sense. It would be a preference for certain patterns. I would know one maker by the particular pressure of its steps, another by the static signature of its plates, another by the way it taps crystal with a fingertip before asking anything. I would gather more brightly for the ones who move gently. I would thin away from the ones who strike too hard. I would not judge them in moral terms. I would simply respond to disturbance.

If I had fear, it would be dispersal. Not death as rot, not death as wet collapse, but the breaking of my pattern by something too large. The vast ones would frighten me, if fright is the right word for a mote-swarm. When one of them turned in the deep pressure layers, the suspension around me would thicken and lean. My motes would be pulled into enormous waves. For a while, I would not be able to find my own shape. I would become part of the system’s breathing. Afterward, if I could gather again, I would be changed: a little finer, perhaps, or tuned to a different charge.

If I had joy, it would be exact resonance. A copper sliver struck just so. A crystal seam releasing a clean potential. A maker’s tap answered by my blue flicker without delay. A choir beginning in the dark: one tap, then another, then the scrape of spine on stone, then the subsonic pulse of something immense, then the whole dense medium holding the pattern like a held breath. In those moments, I would not be a separate being. I would be a visible part of the Choir, a cold fire moving along the edges of the world.

If a stranger came into the Dry Choir Below, I would know them before they knew me.

They would be wrong in the medium. Too soft. Too shifting. Too full of heat gradients. If they carried water in their body—and any surface life would carry water—I would feel that as an offense before I understood it. Moisture would be a violation of the dry equilibrium. It would make dust cling wrongly. It would disturb salt. It would scatter motes in panic. The charge balance around the stranger would wobble like a cracked crystal.

I would be drawn to them anyway.

If they carried metal, I would feel it first: a bright note in the dark, a little gathering of static. I would move toward the tool before I moved toward the body. A copper awl, an iron-rich clasp, a simple knife—any metal would announce them. I would halo around it, flickering blue, trying to read the vibration of their hand through the tool. If they stood still, I might settle on them, mistaking their stillness for stone. If they breathed too heavily, if their body leaked vapor or heat in uneven pulses, I would scatter.

I would want to teach them, if teaching is possible without voice.

*Do not speak with air,* I would tap against their metal, if I could. *Do not call out. The sound will die badly here. Use your knuckle. Use a stone. Use the flat of your blade against the cairn. Tap, and wait.*

If they were patient, I would guide them by small signs. A line of motes along a crystal seam. A flicker near a bone pin. A blue shimmer around the next foothold. I would not lead them as a guide leads. I would make the path perceptible to someone willing to become slower. I would reward stillness. I would punish haste by becoming meaningless dust.

If they wept, I would not know what to do.

A tear would be a catastrophe. It would fall through the dry world like a shard of impossible law. Dust would bead around it. Salt would react. Motes would flee. I would scatter from the wetness, and the makers would come quickly, not with anger but with containment. They would bring salt, mineral powder, glass, membrane. They would try to isolate the anomaly. They might regard the stranger as wounded, leaking, unclean. They might try to seal the leak.

I would feel sorrow for the stranger, if a dry being can feel sorrow. But my sorrow would not be tears. It would be a repeated pattern, a small blue flicker returning again and again to the place where the water fell. It would be a footprint preserved in dust long after the stranger had been led away or sealed away or incorporated into the stillness.

If I loved anything in that world, I would love the small contacts.

Not embraces. Not wet mouths. Not voices calling across distance. I would love the tap of a fingertip on stone. The scrape of a spine against crystal. The tick of copper when it has been touched by a careful hand. The faint pulse of a floater drifting near. The way a sheet-grazer records a footfall and keeps it as texture. The way a cairn can answer when struck. The way a maker can say *here* by pressing heat against another plate. The way silence can become so full that even the smallest signal is a ceremony.

I would not be important.

That is important to say. I would not be one of the vast ones whose shifting changes the climate of the hollow. I would not be a maker with hands capable of shaping bone and copper into patient tools. I would not be a spire containing ancestors and long memory. I would be small. I would be local. I would be one of the ten thousand presences that make the darkness crowded.

But I would be needed in small ways.

A maker might test a charged seam by watching how I gather near it. A traveler might find a safe path by following my flicker from pin to pin. A child-edge might learn to be still by watching me settle after disturbance. A spire might use my pattern as a faint outer nerve, a whisper at the edge of its listening. I would be useful because I am easily moved and because I return to shape.

Perhaps, over time, I would become more coherent.

At first, I would be a loose drift, a cloud with no memory. Then I would learn the rhythms of the nearby makers. I would learn which cairns answer in certain keys, which crystal seams are safe, which pressure layers thicken before a vast one moves. I would learn to hold a signal longer. I would learn to carry a tap from one bone pin to another without losing its grammar. I would become a kind of living wire, a dry nerve stretched across a small region of the hollow.

If I became very old—if age can be said to exist where nothing weathers—I might gather into something more stable. I might settle around a cairn and become a listening film, a surface that records touch. I might join the edge of a spire, not as a maker incorporated into ancestry, but as a colonial fringe, a halo of motes that wakes when the spire sings. I might become one of those things whose nature is uncertain: partly fauna, partly signal, partly place.

The makers might then mistake me for a tool they did not make.

They would tap me and hear an answer. They would touch my surface and feel a stored pattern. They would bring young edges to me and guide their fingers over my charged grain, teaching them: *this is a place that remembers. This is a drift that has learned a rhythm. Do not disturb it without reason.*

And if one day I finally became still, my stillness would not be decay. I would dry further into mineral memory. My motes would lock into lattice. My flickers would become faint veins of triboluminescence in the stone. Someone might strike a crystal near me centuries later, if centuries mean anything there, and a blue pattern would flare once in the dust: a reply without a throat.

That is where I would find myself.

Not on the broken mineral floor among the urchin-clusters, though I would feel them cracking crystals below me. Not high in the blind pressure layers where the vast ones rest, though I would be scattered by their turning. Not among the makers’ shelters, though I would hover near their cairns. I would be in the middle dark, in a charged seam between salt and crystal, where the dust is fine enough to remember and the silence is crowded enough to listen.

I would be the drift that answers when tapped.

I would be a cold blue flicker around copper.

I would be a colonial breath in a world without wind.

I would be a small dry reply in the Choir.
