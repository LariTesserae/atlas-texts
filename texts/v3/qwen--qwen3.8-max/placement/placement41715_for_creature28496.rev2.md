At first I keep trying to stand upright in this place.

I imagine myself as a mirror-keeper, crossing a white floor with a polished shell fragment held in one hand, testing whether a panel has drifted enough to steal brightness from a drying terrace. I imagine my hands stained with mineral dust, my eyes narrowed against sudden glare from wet stone. Or I imagine myself as a water-tender, kneeling beside a groove cut into ancient limestone, reading the darkening of a floor as if it were a sentence beginning to form. I imagine being one of the pale, broad-footed people of the basin, patient and exact, learning to speak with my hands and to save my voice for necessity.

But the longer I remain in the idea of that world, the less upright I seem to become.

The basin does not call me toward the center. It pulls me to the margins: to the seam where a dry pan begins to darken, to the space behind a drying rack, to the low edge of a cistern where fish turn without sound, to the cracked alcove where fungal felt thickens and the air smells of cold salt. The place that feels most mine is not a plaza or a court, but a threshold. A place where one state is becoming another. Dry becoming wet. Stillness becoming movement. Waste becoming clean stone.

So, if I let the world decide for me, I would not be one of the people at all.

I would be one of the soft-footed scavengers.

Not a wild thing in the sharp sense, and not a housed thing either. One of those low, pale bodies that come down from the upper galleries when moisture returns, moving quietly through the city’s wide spaces, tolerated, recognized, never owned. I would be a creature of the basin’s digestion and memory: a cleaner of leavings, a follower of damp, a listener at the edges of human work.

My body would be low and broad, made for passing beneath stretched hide screens and moving along the bases of walls. My hide would be pale, ash-white and gray-blue, dusted with fine mineral powder that settles into every crease. My feet would be broad and soft-padded, able to cross wet limestone without slipping and without making the smallest sharp sound. My eyes would be reduced to faint seams, good only for noticing changes in brightness, not for shaping the world into hard outlines. I would not need to see clearly there. I would read with my whiskers, my nose, the sensitive skin of my feet, and the vibrations that travel through stone.

I would feel the city before I understood it.

A tap on bone would come to me as a tremor in the floor. A mirror-keeper adjusting a panel would register as a shift in warmth and reflection. The approach of a water-tender would be a change in smell: damp hide, crushed chalk, cold brine. Children learning to move quietly would feel like small hurried pressures, uncertain but trying. I would know them all by rhythm more than by face.

I would live in the district between the cisterns and the drying terraces, where waste appears and must be removed before it spoils the careful balance of the basin. My den would be in a dry upper alcove, perhaps beneath an old rib of stone where earlier inhabitants once anchored a shelter long since moved. The place would not be mine in the way a house belongs to a person. It would be mine in the way a hollow becomes associated with a particular body: because I return there, because my scent lies faintly in it, because the people know to leave it undisturbed unless water claims it first.

From that alcove, my routes would spread outward in a pattern shaped by moisture and habit.

First, the broad pan where microbial crust lies in gray-white sheets. I would not go there to graze, but to wait near the edges, where plate-grazers have been moved through and loosened fragments remain. Then the fish-keepers’ cisterns, where a careless hand may leave a scrap of pale flesh on the stone, or a net may shed a few scales that must not be allowed to rot in a breeding pool. Then the felt alcoves, where fungal wool spreads over old chitin and dead hide, and where my work is welcome because I help bring the material down into what the basin can use again. Then the drying racks, where cured fish and crustacean leathers hang in the cool reflected light, and where I would be watched more carefully, because even a useful scavenger must learn the difference between leavings and stores.

The people would not love me in a sentimental way. They would value me. That would be enough.

A fish-keeper might leave a small heap of waste at the edge of a channel, not as a gift, but as a placement: a way of directing my work without forcing it. A felt-tender might tap twice on a stone when I come too near a young culture, and I would turn aside, not from obedience but from long understanding. Children would be taught my shape and my purpose. They would learn not to chase me, because chasing makes noise, and noise disturbs fish and grazers alike. Instead, they would learn to guide me with slow movement, with scent-lines, with the careful placement of objects. One of their first lessons might be to watch me and understand that cleaning is not lesser work. It is the work that keeps the rest from poisoning itself.

I would have a name, though not a spoken one.

It might be a rhythm: two soft taps on shell, then a brushing sound made by an open hand across stone. Or it might be a hand-sign, low and quick, fingers sweeping inward toward the palm, meaning something like “gather” or “clear.” Perhaps the people of one district would call me by a sign that means “soft foot at the sill.” Perhaps another district would know me by a different arrangement: a fish vertebra placed beside a chert flake, point turned away from the water. I would not care about the name in the way a person might. But I would know when I was being called. I would know the difference between a warning, an invitation, and a dismissal.

My days would be governed by pulses.

When glass-worms rose at the edges of pools, I would know that pressure was changing and water might move. I would leave low hollows before they filled. When mist gathered under the ceiling and bead-rain began to fall in silence, I would follow the people as they adjusted screens and covered racks, staying near the paths where scraps were likely to appear. When a seep opened in an old channel, the whole district would shift, and I would shift with it. If water had right of way, then so would I, because I would not argue with water either. I would simply move to higher stone and wait until the basin settled again.

Sometimes I would be alone for a long while. The city would seem empty. Light would stand in pale columns over white floors, and dust would hang motionless in the shafts. I would rest in a cool crack, my paws folded beneath me, my whiskers barely moving. To an outsider, that stillness might seem like absence. But I would know it as patience. The basin was not dead; it was holding its breath until the next damp returned.

Then the signs would begin.

A faint mineral smell would sharpen. A wall would darken by the width of a finger. Damp-crawlers would cluster more thickly near a seam. The first crustaceans would emerge across newly wet stone with that soft rasping sound, like stone remembering water. People would appear from widely separated places, moving with quiet urgency. Racks would turn. Hide sheets would be loosened or tightened. Temporary pens would be shifted. Fish-nets would be spread. Children would be sent to safe terraces with small hand-signs and a touch to the shoulder. And I would rise too, because where moisture returns, life thickens, and where life thickens, leavings follow.

I would not experience this as drama. I would experience it as correctness.

The world would make sense when each thing moved in its proper direction. Water went where water must go. People adjusted. Animals followed. I cleaned. The stone accepted all of us without surprise.

I think I would like being small in that world.

Not powerless, but not central. Not trying to lift the basin into a shape it did not already have. The people there are careful because they know that even a slight act can change the balance: a blocked light-path, a loud sound, a footprint pressed too hard into living crust. As a scavenger, I would be even closer to that truth. My body would pass through the city’s waste and death, turning it toward continuation. I would eat what was left behind and help make room for what came next. My hunger would be useful. My silence would be useful. My refusal to insist would be useful.

I would also have a strange tenderness for the city’s signals.

Even if I were only an animal in the ordinary sense, I would be one that has learned the habits of the people so deeply that I seem almost to answer them. If a shell had fallen from a sill and blocked a thin channel, I might nudge it aside without meaning to. If a cord had loosened and its end trailed where water needed to pass, I might pull at it once, because the movement attracted me, and in doing so I might make the path clearer. If a child left a polished pebble at the edge of a pan, I might sniff it and leave it in place, recognizing by scent that it had been put there deliberately. The people might see such things and read them as signs. They would not be wrong to. In the basin, even small movements can be part of the grammar.

Perhaps that is why they would tolerate me so near their careful works. Not only because I clean, but because I seem to understand threshold.

There would be moments when I became important in a quiet way.

A breeding pool might begin to foul. The people would know it by smell, by the behavior of fish, by the thinning of a crust. Before they could solve it, I might arrive with others of my kind, following the scent of spoilage, and we would remove what needed removing. A dead crustacean in a hidden crack would be found by me before it could rot into a cistern. An old hide scrap dropped near a fungal bed would be broken down because I carried it into the right dampness. Nothing heroic. Nothing loud. Just the slow prevention of harm.

And when the city retuned itself after a flood or a dry spell, I would retune with it.

If my alcove became damp, I would move. If my routes were interrupted by a new pool, I would find another way. If a district was abandoned for a season because moisture failed, I would stay near the people or follow the higher galleries until the place became livable again. I would not mourn a changed route. The basin had taught all its inhabitants, even its animals, that continuation matters more than fixed form.

I would know the mirror-light differently from the people. They would watch it to manage it. I would feel it as warmth on my hide, as a brightening against my closed eyes, as a sign that certain surfaces were drying and others were about to become slick. When light flared from a newly wet wall, I might pause, whiskers lifting. Not dazzled, but attentive. The whole basin would seem to breathe in those moments: stone brightening, water appearing, people moving, animals gathering, and all of it held inside a cool, hush-filled glow.

I would never miss green things, because I would never have known them.

The idea of a leaf would be as foreign to me as the idea of a forge. My world would be mineral and animal, wetness and crust, bone and shell, hide and fungal thread. Beauty would be found in pale surfaces, in the sheen of a well-tended mirror pool, in the translucence of fish skin, in the soft regularity of molted chitin, in the clean smell of stone after thin rain. I would find comfort in the textures of the basin: polished limestone under my pads, the slight grain of marl, the woolly resistance of fungal felt, the smooth coldness of shell.

If I were to die there, I would not be hidden.

I might be found in a dry hollow, my body growing still while mineral dust settled over my hide. Scavengers would come, because that is the order of the basin. Water would arrive eventually and dissolve what could be dissolved. The stone would take what it could take. If a person found my bones and they were suitable, perhaps a small part of me would become a needle, an awl, or a tapped signal rod. Not because I was special, but because nothing there is wasted. My life would continue as maintenance, and my death would continue it too.

I think that is why this is the being I would be.

Not a ruler. Not a builder of monuments. Not a voice raised above the quiet industry of others. Not even fully a person. I would be a necessary presence at the edge of the city’s life: soft-footed, pale, patient, alert to the first signs of change, useful in the way that humble things are useful. I would not make the basin obey me. I would learn its moods and move inside them.

If you looked for me there, you might not notice me at first.

You would stand in a broad court of white stone, see a mirror-keeper far away adjusting a shell-bright panel, see children making hand-signs near a cistern, see a line of blind crustaceans moving along a newly wet seam. Then, at the edge of your sight, a pale low shape would slip behind a stretched hide screen. Its feet would make no sound. Its whiskers would test the air. It would pause beside a fish-cleaning stone, sniff once, and move on.

That would be me.

Not in the center of the light, but where the light touches damp and the city begins to change. Not speaking, but listening. Not owning the basin, but belonging to it. A soft-footed scavenger, a cleaner of thresholds, a small keeper of the basin’s quiet balance, moving without raising my voice through the bright, ancient, water-stained world below the dry surface.
