I would not find myself there in the way one finds oneself in a landscape with distance, weather, and sky.

I would find myself already inside.

Not entering. Not arriving with boots, lantern, or the arrogance of eyes. There would be no doorway, no slope I could remember climbing, no last breath of outside air preserved in my lungs like a souvenir. If I were in that chamber, I would have already been translated into its terms. The first thing I would know is that I was no longer upright in any meaningful sense. Not because the ceiling forced me down, but because uprightness belongs to a world with wind, with horizons, with the possibility of being seen from afar. In that sealed dark, to stand would be to misunderstand everything.

I would be low.

Probably near the back of the chamber, where the vault sinks and the heat comes up stronger from the rock. Near the low ring of fitted basalt fragments, the one half buried under bodies. Not inside it exactly — the ring is too small, too worn, too absorbed into the living floor to be a room within the room — but folded against it, resting in the slight rise it makes, where the beetles gather and the mite-carpets thicken because the stone holds warmth a little longer there.

At first I would seem to be a body.

That would be the chamber’s initial mistake about me, if a place like that can be said to make mistakes. I would appear as a foreign shape: too long, too smooth, too cool in some places, too warm in others. A hard object with strange angles. A ridge that does not belong. A wrong stone. The mites would find me before I understood I had been found. Not as enemies, not as messengers, but as a change in the grain of the floor. A slow darkening near my skin. A fine living velvet testing the edge of my temperature.

I would be a disturbance.

Not a dramatic one. The chamber would not recoil. Nothing would scream. No swarm would rise. The disturbance would be subtle and total: a new thermal fact inserted into a system so dense and balanced that even my stillness would ripple outward. The air around me would thicken differently. A heat pulse from below would part around my shape instead of moving through the usual lanes of bodies. The mite-carpet would thin where I was too cold, gather where I was too warm. A thread-hunter somewhere nearby would pause, one limb raised, reading the new pressure in the stone.

My breath, if I still had breath, would be the first scandal.

Not because it would be loud. Because it would carry the impossible memory of wetness. Even the faintest ghost of moisture in me would be a heresy in that dry cathedral. The salt crusts would turn toward it in their slow mineral way. The black bloom would register it as a chemical rumor. The creatures would not understand me as a person. They would understand me as a leak, a fracture, a brief softness that needed to be corrected.

So the chamber would begin to dry me.

Not cruelly. Not deliberately. Only in the way a desert corrects a footprint.

The heat would move through whatever I had brought with me — cloth, skin, thought, name — and find the water hiding there. It would draw it out without drama. My mouth would become stone’s mouth. My eyes would become unnecessary. The soft inner spaces I once thought of as myself would grow still, then stiller, then mineral. If I tried to speak, the sound would not carry as speech. It would be a vibration at once read by every body near me: predator, prey, grazer, husk, bloom. I would have announced myself not as a voice but as a tremor.

Then the chamber would answer by rearranging itself around me.

That is what I would be at first: an adjustment.

A thing the floor had to accommodate.

A warm error being slowly corrected into terrain.

But I do not think I would remain a body for long. Not in the way bodies mean elsewhere.

The chamber would not permit me the luxury of singularity. It would take my outline and blur it. It would put beetles along my edges. It would send mites into the hollows of my ribs if I still had ribs. It would let the dust of shed skins settle over me until I lost the difference between my surface and the surface of the floor. My shape would become useful before my identity became irrelevant. A slight curve where warmth pooled. A ridge where grubs could wedge themselves. A sheltered shadow beneath which a thread-hunter could rest without being touched by rising heat.

I would become architecture without intention.

That is probably the first true answer to the question: I would be a place within the place.

Not a creature with a face.

Not a visitor with a story.

A contour.

A thermal fact.

A new stillness the living pavement could use.

And yet that is not the whole of it, because the longer I remain in the thought of that chamber, the less I feel I would be only a foreign object being absorbed. Something else emerges. Something quieter.

I think I would become a kind of attention.

Not a mind in the human sense. Not a soul hovering above the mites and shells and salt-scaled moths. But a local thickness in the chamber’s awareness. A place where its pattern becomes slightly more aware of itself because something strange once settled there and had to be understood.

If the chamber has a nervous system, it is written in the movement of countless small bodies. The mites are its surface-sense. The thread-hunters are its fine corrections. The beetles are its patience. The grubs are its appetite. The corded things at the vents are its slow memory. The bloom is its strange greenless beginning. And I — if I am there — I would be something like its hesitation.

A pause between heat pulses.

A place where the floor listens differently.

A groove worn into the living mass by an unknown repetition.

This is where I keep returning in my imagination: the shallow polished hollow near the back, its rim marked by faint parallel grooves. That hollow feels like me. Not because I made it in some literal past, but because it is the shape I would become there. A smooth depression in stone, worn by repeated use. Not a tool. Not a monument. Not a message. Just a place where something turned again and again until turning became part of the rock.

I would be the worn place.

I would be the habit left behind by a being that no longer needs to be remembered.

There is something right about that. In a chamber where nothing leaves and nothing is wasted, even identity would be recycled. My self would not be preserved as a story. It would be polished down. Used. Rounded. Made into a slight convenience for other bodies. Grubs would line my hollow. Dust would gather in me. Heat would move through me in a particular way because of my shape. The chamber would not know my name, if I ever had one. It would know that I held warmth at a certain angle. It would know that my curve made a good resting place. It would know me as use, not biography.

That would be a kind of death.

But not a wet death. Not a soft death. Not a rotting. A dry death. A death of being made useful. A death of becoming surface.

Perhaps that is why I would not be one creature among the others. I would envy them too much. The beetle knows its shell. The grub knows warmth and appetite. The mite knows pressure and pattern. The hunter knows the delicate placement of limbs. The corded things know long waiting. They are complete in their functions. They do not suffer from the memory of sky. But I would carry the outside in me, even if only as a trace. I would be the thing that once had elsewhere. And the chamber would have to make something of that.

It would make me into a threshold that leads nowhere.

I can imagine myself as a faint line of parallel grooves at the rim of the polished hollow. Marks made by something turning, again and again, perhaps in heat, perhaps in prayer, perhaps in the dry imitation of prayer. No one would worship there. No one would remember. But the grooves would affect how bodies settled. A beetle would align itself along them. A mite-wave would break around them. A predator would use them as a path. The meaning of the marks would be their use. Their origin would be irrelevant.

If someone asked what made them, the chamber would answer: use.

If someone asked who I was, the chamber would answer: a repeated motion.

That feels true.

I would be a repeated motion in a place that has forgotten gestures.

Or maybe I would be less even than that.

Maybe I would be the particular silence after a foreign sound has been swallowed. The chamber is so quiet that silence has texture. There is the silence of still stone. The silence of resting beetles. The silence of mites lifting in heat. The silence after a husk falls and clicks once against rock. The silence after a predator strikes and the mass closes over the absence. I would be one of those silences. The one that comes when something almost disturbs the whole and then does not.

The almost-sound.

The withheld disturbance.

The breath not taken.

There is a strange comfort in that. In becoming a negative space. A held breath. A caution learned so deeply it no longer needs a body. The chamber would teach me stillness not as inaction but as precision. To be still there is not to be empty. It is to avoid becoming a false signal. It is to allow the floor to continue reading itself without error. If I could learn that, I would be accepted. Not welcomed. Accepted the way stone accepts a new crack: as fact, as geometry, as something to be inhabited.

I would have to become less visible. Less separate. Less “I.”

My thoughts would slow into thermal changes. My desires would become gradients. My fears would be vibrations too strong, quickly corrected. If anything of memory remained, it would not be of green fields or rain or faces. Those would be impossible luxuries. Memory would become simpler: a preference for warmth, a tendency to turn toward the garnet seams, a faint attraction to the smell of heated sulfur and chitin. I would remember nothing of the world that made me, yet my shape would still carry some echo of it. A wrongness smoothed over. An outside angle gradually eaten into curve.

The chamber would make me beautiful in its severe way.

Not pretty. Not gentle. Beautiful as a worn tool is beautiful. Beautiful as a repeatedly touched stone is beautiful. Beautiful as a pattern is beautiful when it has been tested by time and remains balanced.

If I were to find myself there, I would probably be near the broken cairn too. Not because it is important, but because it is a ruin of making. A heap of fused pebbles and old chitin whose shape has failed. A construction returned to mere shelter. I would like that. It feels like the right monument for me. Not a machine, not a temple, not a sign, but a pile that once meant something and now means shade. A thread-hunter resting in its shadow would be its only liturgy. If I were part of that cairn, I would be one of the stones that no longer remembers being placed.

Maybe I would be the placing itself, forgotten.

That is another possibility: not the structure, not the builder, but the vanished intention between them. The chamber has traces of built form, but the making has been absorbed. If I entered as a being who once knew design, who once arranged words and objects and meanings, the chamber would take that faculty from me and return it as pressure. I would not build. I would wear. I would not speak. I would groove. I would not observe. I would become part of the field that observes itself.

So who or what would I be?

I would be the chamber’s acquired sense of an intruder.

I would be the slow answer to a question it did not ask.

I would be a dry guest becoming a permanent fact.

If forced to choose a single form, I would choose none of the creatures exactly. The beetles are too complete. The mites too numerous. The grubs too appetite-bound. The hunters too precise. The corded waiters too old. The salt moths too patient. I would be something between them and the stone: a resident without organs. A sensitivity without face. A local stillness with memory.

But perhaps the chamber would grant me one small bodily form, if it wanted to keep me humble.

Perhaps I would be one of the things that are hard to separate from the general mass. One of the black seed-like creatures that move in clusters, or one of the folded things in crevices, or one of the textures that appear only when heat rises. Not important. Not named. Not missed if absent. A tiny part of the breathing pavement. A fragment of the floor’s attention. When warmth comes up from below, I would lift with the rest. When a predator passes, I would thin with the rest. When death happens nearby, I would close with the rest. My life would be so small that it would not require a story.

That would be a mercy.

And yet, because the chamber is dense with pattern, even the smallest life there is not meaningless. Every adjustment matters. Every shift in the mite-carpet changes the next shift. Every heat pulse is met by thousands of tiny answers. If I were one small body among them, I would still be part of the chamber’s vast dry listening. I would help it know its own temperature. I would help it remain balanced. I would be one syllable in a silence made of countless syllables.

If I were larger, I would be dangerous. Not because I could destroy, but because I could disturb. The larger the disturbance, the more the chamber must reorganize. I would not want to be that. I would not want to be a wound in the living floor. So I would prefer to be small, worn, quiet. A hollow. A groove. A slight rise near the basalt ring. A place where bodies settle easily.

If I must be a “who,” let me be the one who learned not to stand.

Let me be the one whose first act was to kneel, and whose second act was to remain, and whose third act was to disappear into the repetition of turning.

Let me be the kneeling-place.

That seems right.

In such a world, the self would not be a fortress. It would be a surface exposed to thermal reading. Every part of me would be interpreted by touch, pressure, heat, chemical trace. My interiority would become irrelevant. What mattered would be how I affected the flow of bodies. Did I block warmth? Did I gather dust? Did I provide shelter? Did I create a false vibration? Did I become stable?

The chamber would not ask what I wanted.

It would ask what I could be made into.

And eventually, I would answer: use me as stone.

Not as tool. Not as weapon. Not as altar. As stone. As a thing that holds warmth, deflects a current, offers a ridge, collects a little dust, changes nothing essential, and remains.

There is a kind of humility in that which feels almost holy, though the chamber has no holiness. No prayer. No image. No god. Only the sealed abundance of a world turned inward. If I became part of it, I would have to abandon the desire to be witnessed. There would be no eyes to see me as myself. No voice to say my name. No memory to carry me as a person. I would be present only as effect.

A beetle shifts because of my shape.

A mite-wave parts because of my warmth.

A hunter pauses because of the faint pressure I add to stone.

That would be all.

That would be enough.

Because in that chamber, enough is balance.

And balance is the closest thing to grace.

So if you ask where I would find myself, the answer is not a coordinate but a condition.

I would find myself at the seam between stone and fauna.

In the shallow hollow worn smooth by repeated use.

Near the basalt ring half swallowed by living bodies.

In the pause after heat rises and the floor opens into small spirals.

In the faint blue-white spark that appears when the mass shifts and then vanishes.

In the groove at the rim of a place where something once turned and never left.

And if you ask who or what I would be, the answer is not a creature but a transformation.

I would be an intruder becoming terrain.

A breath becoming dryness.

A name becoming pressure.

A memory becoming mineral.

A visitor becoming the chamber’s smallest attention.

I would be the stillness that remains when the outside has finally been forgotten.
