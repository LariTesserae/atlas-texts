I would not find myself on a shore, because there is no shore. I would not be standing at the rim looking down, because that would still make me an outsider, and the basin does not seem to permit outsiders. If I were there at all, I would already be inside the water, held in its cool density, suspended among the mineral motes, with the bright surface above me and the pressure of the young mountain below me.

The first thing I would lose is the expectation of hands.  
The second is the expectation of a single, bounded self.

At first, when I try to imagine what I would be, I reach for the brightest part of the basin. I think I might be one of the pale bells just beneath the trembling skin of daylight: glass-clear, thin, opening and closing in slow pulses, drawing in dust and charged particles. That life has a kind of purity. It is near the light without needing to make the light into food. It is moved by the basin without pretending to steer it. For a while, that seems right. To be a bell, a translucent receiver, a body whose organs show like dark seeds in cold water. To rise when the water warms, sink when cloud-shadows pass over, and shiver only when electricity touches me.

But that image is too open, too indiscriminate. It takes in everything the water brings. It does not distinguish between one vibration and another. And if I am honest, what draws me in that drowned amphitheater is not only brightness. It is the sense that the whole place is listening without ears.

So I drift lower in imagination.

For a time I think I might be one of the whorls in the midwater: a colony of small bodies arranged in rings and slow-turning rows, held together not by will but by flow, charge, and geometry. That feels closer. There is something true about being plural, about being a pattern rather than a person. A whorl does not own its shape. It does not defend a territory. It forms because the basin permits formation, and when pressure scatters it, it gathers again without resentment. I can understand the appeal of being a slow thought made of many tiny lives, rotating near other bodies in a bright, crowded volume.

But that, too, dissolves too quickly. If I were only a whorl, I would be beautiful, but I would not be a point of attention. I would be carried by the basin’s rhythm without becoming sensitive to its errors. And this world is not only rhythm. It is also disturbance. It is the wrong note inside the industrial roar. It is the tiny failure in the chorus that makes a hunter move.

Then I imagine myself as one of the heavy grazers on the wall, iron-toothed and mineral-crusted, moving so slowly that my only biography would be a polished path across black glass. There is dignity in that. To be the first softness in a sharp world. To dull the youngest edges by repeated touch. But that life belongs to the stone more than to the water’s whole rumor. It would press me against the rock until I became part of the wall’s slow skin. I would be useful to the basin’s long erosion, but I would not be where the charge gathers.

The shape that keeps returning is sharper.

I think I would find myself in the midwater, a little below the bright ceiling and above the broken mosaic of the bottom, near a fresh obsidian fracture where a vent-throat’s pulse can be felt but is not directly discharged. Not at the surface. Not in the deepest crack. Somewhere in the crowded middle, where light is still architectural and sound is already body-deep. There the suspended particles burn when the sun is direct. There the clouds pass as gray panels. There the water tastes metallic, and the smallest hairs of living things stand outward when pressure builds.

There I would be a listening shard.

Not exactly a hunter. Not exactly a bell. Something between the two: a thin, angular body, mostly hydrated silica and conductive filament, clear enough to be mistaken for a broken flake of newly made glass, but alive. My edges would be sharp because the world is young and nothing there has been worn gentle. My tissues would be laced with fine threads that respond to charge. I would have no hands. No tools. No nest. No stored objects. No green memory. If I had a mouth at all, it would be a simple seam for taking in mineral films, charged dust, and the finest living particles shed by other bodies. If I had sense-organs, they would be vibration-lines, electric seams, pressure-sensitive filaments along my flanks.

I would hang in the water by finding the balance between cold sinking current and warm upward exhalation. I would not swim restlessly. I would adjust. A slight tilt. A slow opening of threads. A minute shift toward or away from a chemical taste. The basin would move through me, and I would remain in place by participating in its motion rather than opposing it.

When the mountain drew cold water into its fissures, I would feel the change as a tightening in the surrounding sound. When a hollow column below prepared to exhale, my filaments would stand outward before the bubbles came. When the charge accumulated and the swimmers froze in silver ribbons, I would become still with them—not from fear, but because stillness would be the correct shape for that moment. In the pause before discharge, I would be almost nothing: a transparent angle, a held breath in animal form, a thin line of conductivity waiting in the crowded water.

Then the boom would come.

The water would thicken with sound. Bubbles would rise. Blue electricity might tick along a nearby vein. For an instant, borrowed charge would flash across my silica edges, not as my own light but as the basin passing through me. That would be my only speech: a brief, borrowed flash. Then the pressure would release, the shoals would scatter, the bells would resume their pulsing near the surface, and I would settle back into the listening posture.

I would feed without owning. I would take what the current brought: mineral flour, shed shell-dust, pale film broken from seams, spores of black fungus, brittle white threads if they drifted near enough, and the fine living motes that hang in the bright shafts. I would not cultivate anything. I would not clear a patch of rock. I would not make a store. If mineral crust began to build over me into something resembling shelter, the basin’s vibrations would crack it, or I would shed it in a slow pulse. I would remain unowned by objects.

Perhaps my closest kin would be the hunters, because they also wait. But I would not share their hunger. I would be less intent on prey and more intent on pattern. I would be the kind of creature that notices when the basin’s rhythm changes: a swimmer moving against the current’s law, a clicker releasing gas too soon, a bell sinking faster than a cloud-shadow demands. I would not necessarily act on such knowledge. I might only turn slightly, or let my filaments align differently, adding one microscopic adjustment to the congregation.

If I had to name what I was, I would not call myself a person. I would be a local attention in the amphitheater. A nerve-flake of the water. A pause-animal. A shard-bell tuned to pressure and charge. Part of the unmade mechanism, not because I was made for it, but because my body would have grown into its necessities.

And perhaps that is why I keep returning to that middle depth. It is neither the pure light of the surface nor the heavy labor of the bottom. It is the interval where the basin becomes most aware of itself without knowing itself. The place where sound is so dense it feels like touch. Where electricity moves through the water like invisible hands. Where every volume is occupied, and yet nothing is built. Where the sun remains a white coin overhead, ordinary and separate.

So, if I answer plainly:

I would find myself in the crowded midwater, just below the bright trembling skin of daylight, near a fresh fracture where current, charge, and sound intersect.

I would be a translucent, silica-edged listener: a thin, conductive being with no hands, no tools, no green habits, no desire to leave a mark. I would live by timing, by stillness, by sensitivity to pressure and disturbance. I would not build. I would not rule. I would not garden. I would be one small point of attentiveness in the basin’s larger, distributed life—a living pause in the roar, a shard that flickers when the mountain speaks.
