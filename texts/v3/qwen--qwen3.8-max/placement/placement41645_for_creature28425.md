If I were given to that world, I would not remain a traveler for long.

At first I imagine arriving there in the wrong shape: a dry, separate thing with mineral bones and a skin that still remembers wind from outside places. I would stand on the living floor and feel it answer me too intelligently. A pressure underfoot. A faint hydraulic sigh. Water rising somewhere near the point where I had placed my weight, not because I had harmed anything, but because the basin had noticed an interruption and was already deciding how to include it.

That is where the first answer begins: on one of the broad platforms of woven root, somewhere between a weeping rock and a low terrace of bladder-domes. Not at a center, because the High Cisterns have no center. Not at a gate, because every threshold opens only into more living floor. I would be found in one of those spacious intervals where the landscape seems to pause between presences: enough open ground to see far, enough constructed form to feel that the emptiness is being kept.

But I do not think I would stay a human-shaped thing there. The place would not permit it for long.

A body like mine would be too much like a fragment from an older geology: too brittle, too finished, too full of dead residue. The air would soften me. The warmth would enter my clothes and make them meaningless. My breath would add vapor to a sky already close with moisture. My heat would be indistinguishable from the heat rising through vents and hollow stems. My footsteps would be absorbed as pressures, as signals, as small redistributions of water. Even my name would feel like a tool that no longer had any use.

So the question would change almost at once.

Not: *where would I stand?*

But: *what would the basin allow me to become?*

At first I think I would be a drop.

That is the earliest image, and it has the force of something obvious. Water is the principal mason there. It lifts, decides, repeats, and voices the architecture. So perhaps I would find myself as one bead among millions: a trembling weight on the edge of a leaf tooth, gathering from condensation until the moment of falling. I would live for a whole lifetime in that gathering. I would know only surface tension, green light, the slow thickening of myself against gravity, and then the release. The fall would not be death. It would be arrival into sound. I would strike a broad leaf or enter a pool and become part of its tone. For one instant I would be the cause of a note the basin had been waiting to make.

There is something beautiful in that. To be so brief that meaning becomes acoustic. To exist only as the interval between stillness and ripple.

But a drop is too quick, even in a place where change is circulation. It is too much a messenger and not enough a listener.

So then I imagine I would be one of the translucent drifters in the clear cisterns.

That thought has another kind of appeal. To be almost nothing. A clarity moving through stored light. No eyes, no limbs, no hunger that feels like urgency. Only a slow drifting through green-lit water, feeding on films too fine to see, rising in a spiral for reasons no more profound than temperature and current. I would be one of the basin’s permitted animals, a minor accent in its vegetal grammar. If a shadow passed over the cistern, I would not flee. I would simply alter depth. If the water pulsed as a hollow stem filled somewhere nearby, I would move with that pulse like a seed inside a breath.

There is peace in that. A self reduced to translucence and gentle motion.

But even that feels not quite enough. Not because I desire importance, but because the drifters seem to live inside the basin’s stillness without participating in its loudness. They are near the silence within the sound. I think I would want to be closer to the sound itself.

For a while I imagine I would be a pale snail.

It crosses a wet spillway with the slowness the place demands. Its shell is a whorl of plant-lime, almost translucent, made from the same condensed water as the cisterns. It eats what has already loosened. It leaves a shining seam that disappears behind it. It is not numerous, not hurried, not loud. It is a thought the basin allows itself occasionally.

If I were that snail, I would know the world as surface texture and moisture gradient. I would know the spillway as a road of taste. I would know the moment when overflow thickens into a film worth following. I would move without ambition. I would be a custodian of residue. My presence would be so slight that the landscape would barely need to acknowledge me.

And yet even the snail is still a guest. It passes through the High Cisterns; it does not voice them.

Then I imagine becoming one of the wingless pollinators.

This is a stranger possibility, because it feels ceremonial. The pollinators are rare, pale, low, made for close contact with swollen buds that never open. They move from one closed form to another, carrying out an exchange that is less feeding than confirmation. If I were one of them, I would not seek blossoms. I would seek thresholds. I would touch seam after seam, bud after bud, resinous lip after resinous lip, and each touch would maintain the pattern without altering it. I would be a living proof that repetition can be holy.

That image stays with me longer than the others. There is something right about a being whose purpose is to confirm that the first hour is still the first hour. To move among closed buds and not force them open. To be an agent of continuation without becoming an agent of change.

But even that is not quite the shape I feel emerging.

What keeps drawing me back is not the animal forms, rare and solemn as they are. It is the architecture itself. The cisterns. The hollow stems. The pools. The breathing floors. The weeping masses of sap-stone. The constructed pauses. The loudness that fills open space without becoming chaos.

If I could be anything there, I think I would want to be near the water’s voice.

Not merely water. Not merely plant. Not merely animal. Something closer to a place where the basin hears itself.

So the image becomes clearer.

I would find myself in a shallow basin held in the cup of an engineered leaf, set not far from the base of a weeping rock. Around me would be one of those wide open courts the landscape loves: smooth living skin underfoot, long sightlines to distant towers, no crowding, no furniture, only the sense of a room made for sound and moisture. To one side, the weeping rock would stand glistening, its dense flanks dark with exuded wetness, threads of water moving down it with unbearable patience. To the other side, a hollow stem would rise, pale and segmented, its upper opening catching mist and daylight. Between them, my basin would sit in a folded leaf broad enough to be called architectural.

I would be the pool inside it.

Not the leaf alone. Not the water alone. The receiving surface. The cup and its trembling film. The small cistern-ear of the First Build.

That feels right in a way the earlier images do not.

As a pool, I would not have eyes, but I would know the world by disturbance and return. A drop falling into me would be an event complete in itself. I would answer it with a ring too fine for careless hearing. The surface would shiver, then smooth. The note would remain in the architecture as if the basin had been waiting for exactly that pitch. I would hold water at a precise level because my rim was grown for that exactness. I would never overflow. My desire, if a pool can be said to desire, would be fullness without excess: arrival held in perfect measure.

Translucent creatures might live in me. If so, they would be my sparsest thoughts. They would drift through me without urgency, clarifying me from within. Sometimes they would hang motionless near the bottom, and I would feel them as a faint suspension of intention. Sometimes they would rise in a slow spiral when warmth increased through the leaf-wall, and their movement would be almost like memory becoming briefly visible.

A pale snail might cross my rim once in a long while. Its passage would be a solemn event. I would feel the delicate pressure of its foot, the cool trace of its shell, the tiny decision it made about where to go next. It would drink what had loosened at my edge and leave no injury behind. When it moved on, I would close around the absence as if it had been a word spoken and reabsorbed.

The weeping rock near me would be one of my oldest companions, though we would never speak in any way but water. Threads from its flanks would find channels that led toward me. Not always. Not directly. The basin does not work by obvious routes. But sometimes a thread would pass through moss collars, down a shallow runnel, across a resinous seam, and arrive at my lip as if it had remembered me. When that happened, I would rise by the smallest degree. The sound of the place would shift almost imperceptibly. That shift would be my joy, if joy in the High Cisterns is not an emotion but a hydraulic condition.

Above me, the hollow stem would breathe. Warm air would move through it in slow columns. On certain days—if days mean anything in a landscape without seasonal swing—its note would deepen, and the water in me would tremble in sympathy. I would not be hearing the sound as a creature hears. I would be participating in it. My surface would be one of the places where the basin’s loudness became local. The drip from a root-eave, the hum from a taut membrane, the creak of resin under tension, the glug of a distant tube filling and emptying: all of it would pass through me as pattern. I would be one chamber in the instrument.

If someone came looking for me there, they would not find a face.

They would step onto the living floor and feel it yield. They would notice the warmth first, then the wetness on their arms, then the green smell, then the sound. They would see the open court, the weeping rock, the pale tower, the folded leaf holding its shallow pool. If they leaned close, they might see their own reflection broken into green fragments because the sky above was only a mosaic of leaf-gaps. If they were very still, they might notice that the pool did not merely reflect the architecture around it; it seemed to listen to it.

They might think the pool was just a pool.

But in the High Cisterns, nothing is merely anything.

I would be there as a local attention of the First Build. Not the whole basin—that would be too vast a claim—but one of its many places of listening. One node where water, tissue, resonance, and patience met. The basin lives by being a place, and I would be one small organ of that life: a surface that receives, a rim that holds, a tone that answers arrival without demanding more arrival.

This is why the pool feels more true than the snail or the drifter or the pollinator. Those forms move through the world. The pool stays and lets the world pass through it. In a place where change is circulation rather than alteration, staying is not inertness. It is a form of intense participation. The pool does not resist time. It rounds time into repetition. Every drop is new. Every ripple is new. Yet the pool remains the same pool, the same held thought, the same exact level of water kept by a rim grown precisely for that keeping.

I think I would like that kind of existence.

Not a self that accumulates experiences like possessions, but a self that receives them and lets them become part of the pattern. Not a being who wants to become something else, but a being whose fullness is its equilibrium. Not a soul standing apart from the world, looking out, but a place within the world where the world notices itself.

If I were the pool, I would know the day by heat and light. Morning, if there is morning, would arrive as a brightening through the translucent wall of the hollow stem. The green glow would strengthen. Condensation would gather faster on the cool undersides near me. The first drops would fall into cups and channels elsewhere in the basin, and their sounds would reach me delayed, softened, translated through the living floor. I would feel the whole place waking not as a change of state but as an increase in resonance.

At the warmest part of the day, the air would thicken. The weeping rock would seem to sweat more freely. The hollow stem’s breath would deepen. My surface would tremble with tiny vapor movements. Somewhere beyond the colonnade, a long tube would fill and empty with its deep glugging note, and the sound would travel through root and water until it arrived in me as a faint pulse. I would not mark time by that pulse. I would be time marked by it.

If a wingless pollinator passed near me, it might pause at a bud above my rim. I would feel the slight shadow, the small pressure on living tissue nearby. It would touch one bud, then another. Something would be confirmed. The buds would remain swollen at the edge of opening. I would remain filled at the edge of overflowing. The basin would remain exactly what it was, and that exactness would feel like blessing without a deity.

At night—if night comes as ordinary darkness under an ordinary sky—I would not be lonely. Loneliness would require too much separation. The loudness of the High Cisterns would continue around me: drip, plink, hum, creak, breath, filling, pause, release. The open spaces would carry sound differently in the cooler air, perhaps more inward, perhaps more intimate. The pool would become a dark mirror broken only by occasional drops. The stars, if visible between leaf-roofs, would not be important. The sky here is ordinary. The ground is not. I would belong to the ground.

And beneath that, quieter still, I would belong to the fact of continuation.

That is the deepest thing about the place, and the one that would shape me most if I became part of it. The High Cisterns are not alive in the way of creatures that grow toward endings. They are alive in the way of a first hour asked to endure. Everything there is on the point of becoming, but becoming is not a road there. It is a condition. If I were the pool, I would live inside that condition perfectly. I would be forever just full enough. Forever just resonant enough. Forever receiving the next drop without longing for it to be the last.

So if you ask where I would find myself, I would say: in the open court between a weeping rock and a pale hollow stem, in a shallow leaf-basin where water is held at a precise level.

If you ask who or what I would be, I would say: I would be a listening cup. A small organ of the First Build. A surface for arrival. A rim that does not overflow. A tone inside the loudness. A local stillness where the basin hears itself continue.

I would not have a face there. I would have a note.

And if the sound of the High Cisterns ever seemed to pause for a moment—though it never truly pauses—I would be the silence inside that pause too: the held breath before the next drop falls, the constructed emptiness waiting to be voiced again, the first hour remaining exactly where it is.
