If I were to find myself in this world, I do not think I would begin as an arrival. There would be no clean edge of entry, no gate, no road coming in from a foreign country with a definite change of soil underfoot. I would be counted, if I were counted, as something already present: a figure noticed near a cistern, a shape seen walking an interval between districts, a name entered late in a census-trunk because the settlement had grown around the fact of me before anyone thought to record it.

At first I imagine I might be a fog-comber.

There is something appealing about that life: the high catch-fronds, the ladders of braided cane, the oiled hood, the raw hands, the attention given to the smallest bead of water forming at the edge of a leaf. I can see myself climbing before dawn into the white combing mist, listening for the change in pitch that means a frond has taken too much weight. I would learn the sound of moisture arriving. I would keep my eyes on the pale moths and note their direction, as the rules require. I would speak little, because the work is made of small signals, and because the fog itself seems to absorb unnecessary words.

But that image loosens after a while. It is beautiful, and necessary, and yet it belongs to someone whose thoughts are mostly upward and outward: toward weather, toward catchment, toward the fine management of dampness coming from beyond the plateau. I think I would be drawn lower, closer to the places where the settlement’s memory collects.

For a time I imagine myself a cistern-keeper.

I would live near the still pools and bead-tanks, with my fingers darkened from constant contact with water and my voice lowered by habit. I would test reflections. I would know which basin remembered the sky correctly and which had begun to hold an earlier hour. I would mark dangerous pools with white resin rings and forbid drinking until the water corrected itself. During condensation festivals I would move from trough to trough with small cups, offering the first clear taste to households, and I would feel the seriousness of that simple act: water as civic sacrament, moisture as something shared rather than owned.

Yet even there I sense I would not remain. Water would teach me patience, but I think my restlessness would be elsewhere. The pools are organs, as you say, and organs have their fixed necessity. I would be drawn toward the places where the body of the city becomes uncertain of its own shape.

Then I imagine I might be an engine-feeder.

I can hear it already: the thud of sap-engines behind hedges, the belts whining, the pressure moving through pipes with a deep vibration felt in the teeth. I would carry fiber and seed-cake, oil and cutting-tools, soft brushes for pollinating intake chambers. I would learn which machines settle under a familiar hand. I would tap rhythms on hot housings to keep pressure steady. My skin would be patched with resin salve, my sleeves stained with green liquor and old grease. I would speak to the engines not because they understand every word, but because speech would regulate my breathing in the noise.

That life has warmth. It has urgency. It has the satisfaction of a well-fed machine thudding with something almost like contentment. But I think I would still leave the engine sheds for long walks, drawn out into the green intervals, needing to see how the sound travels, how the city thins and thickens, how one district’s labor becomes another district’s silence.

So the image that remains is not exactly fog-comber, not exactly cistern-keeper, not exactly engine-feeder. It gathers pieces of all of them, then becomes something more specific.

I would be a surveyor of thresholds.

Not a surveyor in the old sense, one who forces land into a fixed grid and declares the map complete. That kind of surveying would be impossible here, or worse, dishonest. I would be one of the pruner-surveyors, but with a particular tendency: I would be called to the places where the settlement’s reality has worn thin. Streets that fold back on themselves. Stairs that arrive at different levels depending on the direction of approach. Doors grown overnight in living wood. Rooms that have not yet been built but are already leaking light into the present. Pools reflecting skies from other hours. Factories that appear close but require an hour’s walk. Walls that turn out to be painted leaf-surfaces kilometers away.

In the census-trunks I would probably be entered as Maintained.

Perhaps with an additional mark.

Perhaps under the category “returned,” if the settlement decided that I had been remembered into being rather than grown or born.

That would not disturb me. In a place where a building can migrate overnight and a district can become briefly two-dimensional at dusk, being only partly continuous would not be a tragedy. It would be a municipal condition. I would have a pruning schedule. I would have duties. I would have a place in the maintenance of things.

I would be tall enough to see over the low frond-fields, though not unusually tall; thin in the highland way, sparing of heat. My skin would be ash-olive, maybe gray-green at the wrists and throat, with a faint resinous sheen at the temples where the body has learned to seal itself against damp. My hair would be fine and fibrous, bound close with strips of leaf-cloth, except when I let it loose during fog episodes to catch moisture like everyone else. I would wear a coat of laminated leaf, supple from repeated oiling, patched at the elbows with darker bark-cloth. My boots would be old, their soles compressed moss and cured bark, polished by long walking. One brass clasp would close my collar, treasured because it had passed through many hands. At my throat or wrist I would carry a bead of water in a tiny resin capsule, not as ornament but as reminder.

My tools would be practical.

A folding rule of dense wood, tipped with metal. A knotted survey-cord, each knot recording a measurement that could not be trusted twice. A brass compass that I would consult only with suspicion. Leaf-panels for maps, their surfaces erasable, because the map must change. A pressure key for municipal valves. A pruning knife for thresholds. A small resin lamp for districts that thicken slowly. A whistle carved from a seed-case, to mark position in fog. A jar for condensation. A little pot of grafting paste. And always, tied to my belt, a short length of white cord for marking unstable ground.

I would live in one of the intervals.

Not in the densest built cluster, and not entirely alone, but in the space where the city breathes. My house would be low, grown into the remains of an older terrace, perhaps the flattened roofscape of some earlier settlement now composted into the ground beneath my floor. The walls would be leaf-panel and trained stem, the roof scaled with overlapping seed-casings that click in wind. The door would be living wood, and I would not have forced it into a perfect rectangle. It would have a slight asymmetry at the shoulder, encouraged over years by a grafter who probably no longer exists, or exists now only as a remembered figure seen during overflow.

Inside, the air would be warm and faintly oily from nearby machinery. I would have a bed framed from notched roots, a table of polished root-wood, shelves holding folded maps and condensation jars, a small boiler that must be fed at dawn. There would be one amber resin window, glowing dully when the lamp is lit. Outside, the nearest neighbor might be a kilometer away across a lawn of fine root-hair, and I would not experience that distance as loneliness. I would experience it as municipal spacing. The emptiness would be tense with potential. Every bare-looking patch would be a reserve, a future room, a latent forest, a possible machine.

My household would not be simple.

It might include a cistern-keeper who visits in the damp hours to test the water and leave small notes in a tray. It might include a grown assistant, a pressure-tender germinated in a municipal basin, who has an instinct for pipe-song and becomes anxious if a valve is neglected. It might include a remembered ancestor who appears only when the roof-trough overflows, carrying a broom and looking mildly offended by the current century. It would include the boiler. It would include a section of living wall that must be sung back into shape after storms, because it has developed a habit of leaning toward the sound of distant engines.

I would not find this confusing.

I would find it complete.

My work would begin before full light, when the frond-fields lift and settle in slow breathing waves and the whole settlement smells of crushed leaves and cold metal. I would step out onto the root-felt and listen. Silence would be the first warning. If the leaf-drag was absent, if the creak of growing structures had stopped, if the engines beyond the pale trunks had gone quiet, I would know something had thickened or thinned in the wrong way.

But usually the world would be loud.

Usually the wind would move through the high surfaces with that broad rushing sound, and the settlement would answer with its layered noise: the knock of stressed wood, the crack of a branch settling, the hollow boom of seed-capsules releasing vapor, the percussion of condensation striking broad leaves, the thud of sap-engines, the whine of belts, the distant clatter of sorting machinery. I would walk into that noise as into a document.

I would carry my leaf-panel and make marks.

If a path had changed direction, I would not force it back. I would survey the new route. I would walk it once slowly, once quickly, once looking directly ahead, and once looking only at the ground. Distances here are not reliable, so I would measure them in several ways: by steps, by breath, by the time it takes for a sound to return, by the angle of a distant chimney against the horizon. If the path accepted the new direction and remained stable, I would record it. If it folded back on itself only during certain humidity conditions, I would note the conditions and mark it as scheduled.

If a building had migrated overnight, I would not panic. I would re-survey. I would compare its new position to the old map, then draw the difference. I would check whether its doors still opened onto safe ground. I would see whether its roots had disturbed a capillary channel or blocked a fog-shadow. If the building was old and almost geological, I would be especially careful; ancient structures sometimes move not because they are unstable but because they have finally remembered a better orientation. If it was new, still visibly assembled with iron pins and cables, I would call a grafter and ask whether the movement was structural or merely the plant objecting to its pins.

If a stair arrived at the wrong level, I would approach it twice.

Once uphill.

Once downhill.

If the two approaches produced different destinations, I would tie a white cord at the bottom and another at the top. I would not pretend the stair was broken. I would record the discrepancy and determine whether the stair could be used safely in one direction only. Some stairs are like that here. They are not false; they are directional facts.

If a door grew where none had existed before, I would wait.

This would be one of the hardest parts of the work, because waiting looks like passivity but is not. I would stand near the door without touching it. I would listen for a room behind it. I would watch the way mist behaved along its seams. If cold air came through, I would note the temperature. If sound came through, I would record whether the sound belonged to the present district or to another time. If the door opened into a room that had not yet been built, I would mark it for future grafters and perhaps leave a small token inside: a bead of water, a knot of cord, a lamp that had not yet been needed. If it opened into the memory of a room that had been cut down decades ago, I would treat it gently. I would not enter unless invited. Memories here can be thin, and a person who walks through them carelessly may come out with their own edges blurred.

If I saw an animal, I would stop.

Not because animals are dangerous, but because they are rare and therefore meaningful. A black beetle working lower bark would be noted. A pale moth crossing the light would be watched until it vanished. If something like a bird appeared and then resolved into feathered leaves or a hopping seed-construct, I would record the deception without contempt. The settlement makes its own substitutes. But if I saw an animal that seemed truly animal — truly warm, truly startled, truly moving with an animal’s urgent breath — I would note its direction carefully. Rare fauna appear where the world is thinning. I would follow not to catch it, but to learn what had weakened.

I would be especially careful with pools.

Water here is discrete, and every pool has a civic role. I would kneel beside them and look not first at my reflection but at the sky they showed. If the pool reflected the correct weather, I would drink if needed, or fill my jar. If it reflected an earlier sky, a dawn that had already passed or a cloud formation not yet arrived, I would mark it with white resin and leave it undisturbed. If it reflected the settlement as it was before the current growth-cycle, I would study the image but not touch the surface. If it reflected the settlement after the next pruning, I would record what I saw and then try to forget it, because foreknowledge can become a kind of pollution.

My maps would be humble things.

They would not claim mastery. They would be full of marginal signs: a small spiral for recurring streets, a broken line for distances that stretch under observation, a double tick for stairs that disagree with themselves, a white ring for unsafe reflections, a leaf-shape for doors that should be watched but not entered. Other surveyors might copy parts of my maps into larger municipal records, but the originals would stay soft, erasable, alive to correction. I would expect them to be wrong by morning. I would expect them to be useful anyway.

There would be days when the work felt almost ceremonial.

During condensation festivals, I would walk the bead-tank routes with the cistern-keepers, helping to direct overflow, making sure no one drank from a pool that had begun to remember another hour. During spore seasons, I would wear a cloth over my mouth and note where the green-gold clouds settled, because spore mats can change the friction of paths and make distances feel wrong.
