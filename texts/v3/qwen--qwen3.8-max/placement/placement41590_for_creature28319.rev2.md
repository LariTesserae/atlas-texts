I would find myself low.

Not above the Wet Throat, not across from it, not in some clean observation place where the roar could be admired from a distance. The first thing I would learn there is that the place does not allow a spectator. You are inside the sound before you understand it. You are inside the moisture, inside the mineral smell, inside the crowd of bodies. The Wet Throat would already be on my skin, in my nostrils, under my feet, and whatever I became there would have to become that way too: wet, implicated, temporary.

At first, imagining it, I think I might be one of the drifting things. A gas-bladdered flier riding the warm shafts, pale and translucent, rising and falling with heat, trailing fine threads through the light. There is something tempting about that: to move above the basins, to catch the updrafts, to see the whole crowded floor as a shifting mosaic below. But even as the image forms, it feels too detached. The Wet Throat is not a place one floats above for long. It pulls everything downward, into contact, into use, into decay, into becoming ground. To live there, I think, I would need hands. I would need to feel the water change under my palms. I would need to listen with my whole body.

So the shape that emerges is not a drifter.

I would be one of the makers.

Not a chief, not a great hunter, not one of the strong ones who can pin a struggling pool-dweller against stone or carry a heavy carapace across a broken terrace. I would be smaller than that, quieter, more useful in the ways the Wet Throat rewards: attentive, patient, hard to notice, quick to move when the ground changes. I would be one of those beings whose body is built for passing through pressure rather than commanding it.

I imagine my skin smooth and dark as wet charcoal, mottled with salt-grey where spray dries on shoulders and back. My feet would be broad, with thick gripping pads and small hook-like spurs for slick stone. My center of gravity would sit low in hips and chest, because a body that stands too proudly there is a body that falls. My hands would be long-fingered and strong, the palms rough enough to hold wet rock, the thumbs capable of fine work. Between the fingers would be loose membranes, folded away when I handled tools but useful in shallow water. My face would be broad rather than long, my mouth wide, my teeth replaceable and worn into useful shapes by scraping films and gripping shell-meat. Along brow and jaw, heat-sensitive pits would read warm currents and unstable stone. My ears would seal. My nostrils would close. My eyes would clear themselves of mist with inner membranes. I would not look like a visitor. I would look like something the Throat had made for itself.

Where would I be found?

Not in the deepest basin, where the sound thickens into pressure and the air is heavy with birth fluid and crushed shell. Not directly beneath the strongest drip, where a sudden pulse could wash a camp away. Not on the open mud flats, where the crawlers press together like pavement and the burrowers make the ground boil. I would be on a low shelf above a wide basin, near the edge of a light shaft but not directly inside it. The shaft would come alive for part of the day, sending a clean beam down through mist and falling droplets, turning the damp air to pale gold. My camp would be placed where the stone is stable enough to bear weight but close enough to moisture that we would not have to travel far when the drip strengthens.

The camp would be small. A low ring of fitted stones, enough to anchor shelters and keep tools from sliding when the floor shudders. A windbreak made from ribs or spines, covered with large shed carapaces. A drying rack leaning against the wall. A few clay vessels, hardened near a heat vent. A bladder of water. A knot-cord tied to a bone peg. Nothing heavy. Nothing precious in the way surface worlds mean precious. Everything there would be useful, repairable, carryable, abandonable.

If you came looking for me, you might not see me at first. You would see the basin, the light, the moving floor, the walls blinking with stacked shells and suckered bodies. You would hear the roar before you understood it: water slapping stone, wings snapping open, throat-noises, shell on shell, the vast wet breathing of the place. Then, near a dark stitch of shelter against the mineral wall, you would notice a small figure crouched with one hand flat on the stone.

That would be me.

I would be a listener.

That is the role that feels most true. In a world so loud, the most valuable creature is not necessarily the one that makes the most noise. It is the one who can hear difference inside the noise. I would be the maker who knows when a drip is about to become a stream. The one who can feel a hollow place through the soles of the feet. The one who notices when the wall-clingers suddenly tighten, when the floor-crawlers lock into one another, when the ordinary chaos changes key because something large has moved below. I would keep the rhythms of the band, not because I commanded them, but because I remembered them.

My most important tool would not be a spear. It would be a sounding staff: a dense length of bone capped with hard shell, polished by use. With it I would tap stone, strike shell, test ledges, send messages through the ground. A short tap, a scrape, a double knock, a pause, a thrown pebble striking water. In the Wet Throat, voice is often swallowed. Meaning travels better through vibration. I would carry patterns in my body the way another creature carries fat or poison. Safe ledge. Water coming. Danger below. Harvest here. Move now. Wait. Listen. This pool will not last. This shelf is only crust. This silence is wrong.

I would be one of the keepers of those signals.

My days would begin before the light shafts align. I would wake to darkness and wetness and noise, because the Wet Throat never becomes truly silent. The first act would not be standing. It would be listening. I would lie still with my cheek near the stone, feeling the camp breathe around me, feeling the small vibrations of the band, the deeper vibration of the basin beyond. I would learn whether the night had changed anything. Had a crack opened? Had a pool drained? Had a section of ceiling fallen? Had a predator passed close enough to make the smaller things quiet?

Then I would tap a morning pattern, low and deliberate, just enough to say: still here, still together, still moving.

The others would answer. Not with voices, not necessarily. A hand striking stone. A spear-butt on shell. A foot pressed twice against the ground. In that exchange, the band would become briefly coherent. Not settled. Never settled. But coherent.

We would move carefully. There is no casual walking in the Wet Throat. Every step is a negotiation with a surface that may be alive, hollow, sharp, slick, or made of compressed remains. I would learn to read the ground the way a sailor reads water. A patch of shell-grit that shifts too evenly might hide burrowers. A smooth ledge with too many mineral stains might be crusted over weakness. A warm depression might hold eggs, or a breeding pulse, or a predator waiting for the things that come to eat them. I would carry my staff not only to strike signals but to test before weight.

My band would tend flows rather than places. That would be one of the hardest things for my old mind to accept, if I brought into the Wet Throat any memory of owning land. There, ownership would be a mistake. You cannot possess a mud flat that may drain by nightfall. You cannot claim a wall covered in breathing bodies. You cannot fence a pulse of water or inherit a light shaft. What we could hold would be access, and only while we maintained it. If we cleaned a shelf of dead crust so bacterial films could bloom, the shelf was ours while we tended it. If we built a small weir of stacked shell and clay to guide water-creatures into a capture pit, the weir was ours while it stood and worked. If we left, the claim weakened. If another band took up the work, the old claim became memory.

I think I would understand this deeply. Perhaps too deeply. I would be the one reminding the younger ones: do not take every egg from the warm hollow. Do not let the pool turn sour. Do not kill more than can be carried, eaten, dried, or used. Not from softness alone, but from sense. The Wet Throat gives, but only to those who do not break the giving. My ethics would be hydrological. My morality would be a matter of currents, pulses, continuities. To waste would be a kind of violence against tomorrow.

In the brightest hour, when several shafts align and the mist glows, I would be most alert. That is when the place reveals itself and also when it becomes most dangerous. Beams fall across terraces. Wings flash. Pools ripple. The walls seem to ripple with feeding mouths. The floor opens with eyes. A swarm may rise without warning, darkening the light, then fall again as if the air has lost cohesion. A disturbance-body may move through the shallows, pushing water over its flanks, rearranging the basin floor, sending smaller lives scattering. A plate of ceiling crust may crash into a pool, and within minutes scavengers arrive. Everything becomes opportunity. Everything becomes risk.

I would watch the light, but I would listen more.

If a new drip appeared, I would be among the first to notice it. A thin wandering thread on a fractured wall, a bead gathering on a ledge, a dark stain spreading where there had been none. I would press my hand there. I would taste the air. I would feel the warmth of the stone. Is this a momentary weep, or the beginning of a stream? Will it bring food, or will it wash us away? I would mark it with a knot-cord, a daub of clay, a stacked shell. Then I would tap the pattern for water-coming, and the band would adjust. We might move the camp a hand’s width higher. We might place shallow clay cups or shell basins. We might set nets where the moisture will gather smaller lives. We might abandon the shelf entirely if the drip promises flood.

My body would know the place through moisture. I would not think of water as a resource separate from life. Water would be law, calendar, news, danger, food, and prayer. When the air dried too long, my skin would tighten and I would become irritable in ways I might not recognize as emotion. When a pulse arrived, my whole body would quicken. There would be no time for reflection then. Only movement. Only harvest. Only the urgent work of catching what the Throat had released before the place turned predatory.

I would eat as the makers eat. Scraped bacterial films settled into paste. Shell meat pried from wall-grazers. Eggs taken sparingly from warm depressions. Dried flesh on racks. Mineral-rich pastes that taste of salt and iron and are necessary in small amounts. I would carry water in a bladder and know which pools are safe for only a short time. I would not be disgusted by crowded life. I would be part of it. My hands would smell of wet shell, ammonia, warm stone, dried membrane. My body would carry the scent of the camp, and the camp would carry the scent of the place.

I would not be alone. That is important. The Wet Throat is too full for solitude in the old sense. Even when quiet, it is inhabited. Even when still, something is moving nearby. My selfhood would be shaped by the band’s rhythms. I would sleep close to others for warmth and warning. I would know the tap-cadence of each companion: the sharp impatience of a hunter, the slow certainty of an elder, the uneven nervousness of a child learning to feel meaning through the feet. I would be responsible for some of those rhythms. I would teach the young ones not to fear sound, but to fear the wrong sound. I would teach them that silence is not peace. I would teach them to move when the water moves, and to leave behind what cannot be carried.

Perhaps I would be older than I now imagine. Not necessarily aged in body, though age might come early in such a place. Old in listening. Old in memory. In a landscape that keeps remaking itself, memory is one of the few portable treasures. I would remember which shafts bring light at certain hours. I would remember which basins flooded last. Which walls bloomed after heat. Which passages closed after rockfall. Which pools drained and never returned. Which ledges are only shell-crust over void. Which rhythms calm a rival band. Which taps mean that a hush-mouth is near.

That memory would not make me powerful in the way of fixed hierarchies. It would make me necessary. The band would keep me because I help it continue. I would keep the band because alone I would be less than a single voice in the roar. We would belong to one another in the same way we belong to the Wet Throat: temporarily, attentively, through use and need and repeated rhythm.

If I had to name what I would be, I would say I would be a translator of pressure.

Not between human and human only, but between the band and the place. Between the drip and the decision. Between the silence and the meaning. Between the coming pulse and the movement it demands. My mind would be less a storehouse than a resonant chamber. It would hold patterns long enough to use them, then release them when the place changed. I would not try to freeze the Wet Throat into a map. I would make maps that dissolve. Knot-cords that are untied. Tap-signs that mean only until the next collapse. Memory as a living current, not a monument.

There would be moments of beauty. I do not want to pretend the place would only be struggle. In the aligned light, when mist becomes columns of gold and the whole basin seems to inhale, I would feel something like awe without needing a word for it. Wings glittering. Water flashing. Shed skins rising like pale ash. The floor moving in broad bands of living color according to temperature. The sound becoming one vast wet note. A camp appearing for a moment as a tiny dark stitch against the mineral floor. In such a moment, I would understand that I was not outside the scene. I was one small pressure among millions, one throat among throats.

I might strike my staff three times against a stone. Not to command. To mark. To say: this moment is here. Listen. Then I would let the echo become indistinguishable from the roar.

There would be fear too. Constant, practical fear. Not the fear of a single great enemy, but the fear of a world that can rearrange itself while you sleep. A pool draining. A ledge collapsing. A swarm pressing too tightly. A flood pulse arriving from an unseen channel. A hush-mouth moving through the gaps between sounds. I would learn to fear silence more than noise. I would learn to read the sudden stillness of smaller creatures. I would know when to abandon food, tools, shelter, even companions if the place turned against us. That would be hard. But the Wet Throat would not reward sentimentality. It would reward continuation.

If someone I loved were taken by a pool predator or crushed in a collapse, we would not bury them. There is no soil for burial there. We would lay the body where scavengers and water and heat could work quickly. Perhaps in a warm current. Perhaps at the edge of a basin where the small cleaners gather. There would be a brief pattern of taps: a final rhythm. Then movement. To remain too long with the dead would be to invite stillness, and stillness is dangerous. But I would carry that rhythm afterward. I would tap it sometimes without meaning to, on a stone, on a shell, on the side of a clay vessel. The dead would become part of the place’s ground, and also part of my pattern.

I think I would die there eventually. Not as tragedy, or not only as tragedy. In the Wet Throat, death is transfer. A body becomes shell, bone, grit, membrane, crust, compressed remains. It becomes ground under other feet. If I died, I would prefer to be given to water. A warm current. A shallow pool. A place where the films bloom. The band would tap the rhythm and move on. The Wet Throat would continue being born out of itself.

So who would I be?

I would be a maker, but not a master. A listener, not a ruler. A keeper of temporary signs. A creature with hands and memory, low to the ground, sleek with moisture, heat-pitted, watchful, shaped by crowd and pulse and mineral damp. I would live in pauses. In camps that are not settlements. In claims that are not ownership. In tools that are not treasures. In rhythms that are not permanent. I would be one small dark stitch against the basin, one voice in the vast throat, one body helping the place continue until it no longer needs my help.

Where would you find me?

Near the edge of a basin, where a light shaft falls crooked through mist and a drip has just begun to strengthen. Crouched on stable stone with one palm flat against the rock, feeling the next change come through the ground. My staff across my knees. My feet ready to move. My skin gleaming faintly in the damp light. Around me, the camp waiting for my tap. Below me, the water thick with life. Above me, ordinary daylight falling into an unborn world.

If you heard a pattern strike the stone three times, low and careful, and then a pause long enough to feel the whole Wet Throat breathe in answer, that would be me. Not calling for mastery. Not asking to own the water. Only saying: here. Here. The flow is changing. We must move with it.
