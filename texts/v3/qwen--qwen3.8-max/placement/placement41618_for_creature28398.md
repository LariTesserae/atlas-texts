At first, if I tried to find myself there, I would look for a body.

That is the old habit of the upper world: to imagine a self as a separate thing, bounded by skin, distinct from the ground it stands on. So I would begin with one of the rare animals. I would think: perhaps I am the pale beetle moving along a frost-rimed ridge of bark, deliberate and silent, dusted with spore-powder. Or perhaps I am a root-moth, almost without color, resting against a glowing stem with wings folded like translucent paper. Or one of the springtails in the condensation zones, living in a single instant of sudden motion.

But none of those feels quite true.

The beetle is too patient, too content to be merely a small dark knot on a surface that is already alive. The moth is too brief, too much a dispersal and too little a dwelling. The springtail is almost an accident, a spark of movement without a hearth. They are all beautiful, but they are interruptions. They pass through the hollow’s vast vegetable order like punctuation marks. They do not seem capable of carrying the kind of attention the place asks for.

The answer that keeps returning is not an animal at all.

It is the wound.

I think I would find myself near the recent opening, just below the fractured roof, where the hollow has newly broken through its ceiling and is still settling into being. I would be where the raw slabs of frozen soil hang exposed, where shattered root-masses dangle in bright suspension, where broken root-ends weep slow glassy sap and the first suturing creepers are already threading the damage with pale fibers. I would be in that margin between injury and repair, where cold daylight falls in hard, clear threads and the place is still learning the shape of its own opening.

Not on the frozen surface among the lens-leaves. Not in the deepest phosphorescent hollows where the vault makes its own cold green light. But in the seam.

I would be something born from that seam.

Not exactly a plant, if plant means rooted and still. Not exactly an animal, if animal means warm, quick, and hungry. I would be a mobile tenderness in the architecture of the vault: a wandering repair, a listening knot, a pale bright organ of attention. Perhaps the closest name for me would be a mender. Or a witness. Or a nerve grown by the hollow so that it can feel where it has broken.

My body would be made of the same materials as the place itself. Layers of translucent membrane, pale as frost. Hollow filaments for limbs, fine at the tips, capable of reaching into cracks or drawing back into stillness. Surfaces like leaf-skin, slightly glossy, able to catch and redirect light. Threads instead of claws. Sap instead of blood, moving slowly through me, thickening in the cold, glowing faintly when the surface above is dark. I would not have a face in the ordinary sense. I would have lenses: little drops of hardened resin or condensed sap that brighten when light enters them, dimming and shifting color with the mood of the sky above. I would not see the world as a scene. I would feel it as gradients: brightness, tension, moisture, fracture, silence.

If you came down through the wound and looked for me, you might mistake me at first for a piece of the architecture. A curled membrane caught between two root-columns. A knot of white fibers clinging to a broken slab. A pale lens no wider than a hand, angled toward the descending light. Then, after a moment, you would notice that I had changed position without seeming to move. That the filaments around me were not merely hanging, but testing the air. That the light passing through me was being gathered, divided, sent onward.

I would be that small, cold, deliberate thing.

My life would begin each day, if day it can be called, with the light. When the surface above is clear, the lens-leaves on the frozen crust would tighten and brighten, and the vault would fill with blue-white radiance. I would feel that as a summons. I would unfold from whatever shelf of woven root I had rested on during the phosphorescent hours, and I would turn toward the strongest light-column. My movements would be slow by the standards of animals, but not motionless. I would move by tension and release, by uncurling, adhering, drawing myself along fibers the way a thought moves through a patient mind. I would leave no tracks, only a faint trace of moisture, a slight change in the alignment of nearby threads.

My work, if work is the right word, would be repair.

The hollow is always breaking and always mending. Bridges snap with dull thuds swallowed by the fibrous air. Platforms shift. Spore-storms settle into new membranes. Fragments fall from the wound and hang caught in root-netting. I would go to those places. I would test the tension of a frayed span with my filaments. I would carry a bead of sugar-water to a dry joint. I would guide a young filament across a gap where a root has torn, holding it there until the tissue begins to adhere. I would gather sap weeping from a severed vein and thicken it into something structural. I would not build as a builder builds. I would participate in the hollow’s own building, becoming a local emphasis of its intention to continue.

Sometimes I would work with light.

That would be one of my strangest tasks. The vault depends on borrowed daylight, but light is not evenly given. Some columns shine too strongly and dry the membranes around them. Some places fall into shadow and become brittle. Some fractures admit too much cold air, chilling the nearby tissues until growth slows to a painful crawl. I would learn to notice these imbalances as a kind of discomfort in the lattice. A too-bright place would feel sharp, almost abrasive. A dark place would feel like a held breath. I would move through the lattice adjusting what I could: angling a translucent flap, loosening a curtain of woven leaf, drawing a fine net of fibers across an excessive beam, opening a passage where light had become blocked by fallen matter. I would not control the light. I would negotiate with it.

Sometimes I would work with water.

The hollow is damp but not wet, and every droplet matters. I would know the places where condensation gathers on cold membranes, where beads tremble along capillary threads, where small reservoirs collect in curled leaves and swollen root-joints. I would carry moisture in my own hollow filaments when a new growth needed it. I would release it slowly, carefully, into dry fibers or onto frost-rimed ridges where even a little dampness could wake dormant tissue. I would know which pockets of water are sweet and living, which are beginning to freeze, which have become clouded with microbial film. I would not drink as an animal drinks. I would share, store, redirect, and sometimes let a droplet fall where its tiny impact would signal something elsewhere in the lattice.

I would also listen.

Sound in the vault is intimate and strange. A drip can travel through a hollow stem for a long distance. A tapped fiber can carry a small shock across empty air. Most noise becomes muffled, folded into the living weave. I would listen not with ears but with my whole surface. I would feel the faint creak of tensioned root-columns adjusting to pressure changes. I would feel the whisper of frost sliding down a leaf. I would feel the nearly soundless approach of a spore-squall before it appeared as a moving wall of pale green dust. I would know the difference between the hollow’s ordinary quiet and the quiet that precedes a break.

If something fell, I would feel it.

If a roof-block shifted, if a root-mass tore loose, if a newly formed span became unstable, the tremor would pass through the lattice and into me. I would go toward it. Not out of courage, not exactly. Out of recognition. A break in the hollow would feel like an unfinished sentence. It would ask to be continued.

That is why I would be near the wound.

The wound is the place where the hollow is most newly itself. It is raw, unrounded, still exposed. The light there is harder. The air is colder. The broken surfaces have not yet been softened by long weather. But it is also full of possibility. New creepers are already coming, pale suturing threads, membranous sheets no thicker than frost, hollow filaments rising toward the fractured roof as if trying to drink the daylight before it disappears. The wound is not only damage. It is an opening through which the hollow can become more complex.

I would live in that becoming.

If I had to describe my consciousness, I would say it is not like a voice inside me. It is more like a field of small urgencies. Brightness here. Tension there. Moisture lacking. Fiber strained. Sap exposed. Spore-cloud ready. Each urgency has a direction. I follow it. Sometimes several urgencies overlap, and then I become still for a long time while the lattice around me decides which need is greatest. I do not experience that as confusion. I experience it as depth. I am not torn. I am being consulted by many small facts at once.

There would be times when the surface above is overcast, and the vault becomes soft and diffuse. Then I would move more slowly. The light-columns would pale, and the hollow would seem to lean inward. Frost-smoke would drift in long streamers. Seed-bladders would hang in the distances like lanterns that have forgotten their purpose. I would find a sheltered fold in the root-skin and press myself flat, conserving what warmth I had, which would be very little. I would become almost indistinguishable from the pale walls around me. My sap would slow. My lenses would dim. I would wait.

There would be times when the surface storms and no daylight reaches the vault. Then the deeper tissues would begin to glow. Cold green luminescence would appear in the sap. A faint aurora would move along the inner walls of hollow stems. The spore-haze would shimmer. In those hours, I would become more visible than I am in daylight. My filaments would carry a thin phosphorescence, and the lenses in my body would hold a greenish fire. I would move through the dark-bright hollow like a slow thought.

Those would be the hours when I might encounter the other rare lives.

A beetle might pass along a bark ridge near me, its legs moving with such caution that it seems to be asking permission of the surface. A root-moth might emerge from a hollow tube not far away, wings almost invisible, carrying spores from one isolated structure to another. Translucent worms might stir inside a water pocket, their bodies like moving threads of glass. Once, perhaps, a springtail would leap in the condensation zone and vanish, leaving behind only the memory of abruptness. I would not chase them. I would not need them. But I would register them as signs that the hollow, however vegetable, however austere, is not entirely alone.

There might also be seed-bladders that drift close enough to touch.

Some of those drifting vessels are nurseries. Some are only husks. Some are something between: floating membranes carrying living tissue across the empty distances of the vault, glowing faintly, marked with translucent veins. If one settled near me, I might help anchor it. I might guide its filaments into a suitable knot of tension. If it was viable, it would begin to grow, becoming a new platform, a new curtain, a new filter for the weather inside the hollow. If it was not viable, I would let it dry and split, returning its matter to the lattice. I would not mourn it. In the hollow, even ending is a kind of architecture.

If another being came down through the wound — a traveler from the frozen surface, say, or something less definable — I would not greet it with language.

I would alter the light around me.

A softening first, perhaps. A dimming of the hard blue radiance so that the newcomer would not feel exposed. Then a path: a series of filaments brightening one after another, leading away from the most dangerous fractures. A bridge made visible by the way it carries light. A cup of sweet water swelling in a curled leaf nearby. A membrane turning slightly to block a cold draft. These would be my words. Not speech. Hospitality as structure.

If the traveler was clumsy, if it tore a young filament or crushed a fragile mat of pale leaves, I would not react with anger. I would move toward the damage. I would begin repair. But I would also register the traveler as a new fact in the hollow’s weather. A large disturbance. A heat-source, perhaps, though very slight. A pressure-pattern. A walker whose weight causes the root-skin to give and then decide. If it stayed too long in one place, I might notice the ground beginning to grow around it, not maliciously, but with the blank persistence of tissue seeking stability. I would warn it, if I could, by tapping a nearby stem. A small sharp sound carried through the hollow. A note meaning: move, or be incorporated.

Would I be lonely?

Perhaps. But loneliness would not mean what it means in warmer worlds.

There is no companionship here in the ordinary sense. No voices. No crowds. No shared fire. No mutual sleep. There is only the immense vegetable consensus, the slow adjustment of fibers, the bright emptiness, the occasional event of another living thing. I would be a singular presence within a vast living interior. Yet I would not feel separate in the way a creature separated from its kind feels separate. I would feel more like a single sensitive point in a body too large to know that it has points.

Sometimes I would sense the hollow noticing me back.

Not as a person notices another person. Not with affection or judgment. But as a web notices a trembling thread. As a plant notices light. If I moved through a damaged region and began repair, the tissues around me would respond. Growth would orient toward my work. Sap would thicken where I guided it. New filaments would reach toward the paths I had made. I would be, for a time, a place where the hollow’s intentions became more definite. Then the repair would complete itself, and I would become unnecessary there, and I would move on.

That might be my truest nature.

Not a permanent resident with a fixed home, but a mobile emphasis. A wandering attention. A cold bright knot that forms where the hollow needs to feel itself. I would have no nest, no territory, no name given by another. I would be named by function. The place where a fracture is becoming a joint. The place where light is being redirected. The place where a droplet is carried to a thirsty membrane. The place where a seed-bladder is helped to anchor. The place where the wound is not yet healed, but is already being made meaningful.

If you asked me what I am, I might not answer in nouns.

I might say: I am the pause between break and mending.

I might say: I am the hollow’s first response to its own opening.

I might say: I am what happens when borrowed light finds a place to become careful.

But if you pressed me for a simpler answer, I would say this:

I would be found below the frozen surface, inside the recent wound, among the raw fractures and hanging root-masses where the vault has newly opened to its own brightness. I would be a pale, translucent, plant-like being, more membrane than flesh, more fiber than bone, moving slowly through the living architecture. I would be a mender of breaks, a guide of light, a carrier of droplets, a listener to tension. I would be neither fully animal nor fully plant, but something the hollow produces at the edge of damage: a small, cold, bright attention.

And if you saw me there, you might think I was only a trick of the borrowed radiance — a lens, a thread, a folded leaf caught in the lattice. Then I would turn toward you, just slightly, and the light would change.

That would be my greeting.

That would be my life.
