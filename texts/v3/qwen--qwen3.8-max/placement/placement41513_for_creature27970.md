I would not come to that place as a traveler arriving from somewhere else. I would not stand on it in any confident way, with weight balanced on boots and eyes searching a horizon. There would be no such stance there. I would instead find myself already part of its slow weather, already damp, already softened into its substance. If I appeared there at all, I would appear as an accumulation: a gathering of moisture, pressure, memory, and attention in a place where the living mat had thickened into something capable of noticing itself.

I think I would be found in one of the low places. Not a lake, not a true open pool, but one of those wide root-bowls or leaf-pits where water gathers by patience rather than force. A shallow basin woven from old plant-fiber, darkened by tannin, reflecting only the green-gray dimness overhead. The kind of place where droplets arrive one by one from the ceiling of hanging growth, each bead falling with a small, deliberate sound. I would be near the rim of that basin, where the mat is most sensitive, where water threads through root-channels and the surface listens to whatever passes over it.

Perhaps there would be an old stone nearby. One of the world’s worn knuckles, almost entirely swallowed by moss and liverwort, weeping thin lines of moisture down its sides. I would be drawn to such a stone, not because it is hard, but because it is the last memory of hardness in a place that has learned to make everything soft. It would feel like an ancient thought the wetland keeps returning to and slowly dissolving. I would rest near it, close enough to taste the mineral trace it gives off, close enough to feel the way water behaves differently when it passes over that old, smooth relic.

If I had to say what I would be, the nearest answer is: one of the Softened. But not a fully separate one, not a creature with a clean outline and a private life. I would be more like a local organ of the wetland’s own vast body, a place where its general life becomes a little more focused, a little more intentional. I would be a Softened in the way a pool is a Softened shape of water: held, but not rigid; present, but not final.

At first, I might not seem to be anything at all. Just a cushion of wet tissue, a dense green-brown swelling in the mat, plush with tiny growths and dark along the seams where moisture collects. I would breathe by slow swelling and settling. My body would have no bones, no shell, no dry edge. It would be made of layered saturated tissue, threaded with capillary channels through which water moves by patient suction. Where another being might have joints, I would have folds. Where another might have skin, I would have a surface that is already in conversation with the ground, the mist, and the living films around it.

When I moved, if I moved, it would be by spreading and gathering. Blunt, soft limbs or lobes would extend, their fringed ends pressing into the surface, rooting for a moment, then releasing. I would move without violence. Without tearing. The ground would yield to me as I yielded to it. Behind me, there would be no wound, only a faint compression, a temporary smoothing, a place where the felt of the wetland had been gently persuaded and then returned to itself.

I think I would be especially attentive to small blockages. That would be my instinct, my work, perhaps the reason my knot of being opened in the first place. I would feel when a capillary thread had clogged, when a seep had slowed, when a root-channel had forgotten its path. It would come to me as a wrongness in the wetness, a thinning, a tightness, a place where the world was beginning to dry into pain. I would move toward that place slowly, or perhaps I would already be there, because in such a world attention and location are not always separate things.

I would lie over the tight place. I would extend soft probing lobes into the surface, not to force anything open, but to wait with it. To breathe moisture into it. To remind the water of its old path by becoming a warm, saturated presence around it. If I had hands, they would be gentle to the point of uselessness in any harder world. But there, they would be exactly right: absorbent, patient, capable of holding a droplet until it knew where to go.

My feeding would be quiet and intimate, like the feeding of the place itself. I would not hunt. I would not tear. I would settle over rich, softened decay and extend absorbent rosettes into the living surface, drawing up nutrients already made soluble by age, moisture, and the work of countless smaller lives. Within me would be colonies of minute beings, transforming warmth and dissolved plant-matter into forms my tissues could use. I would be, in that sense, less a single creature and more a garden that has become aware of being tended. Sometimes I would lift mist into internal bladders and hold it there until it condensed into warm fluid. Sometimes I would remain still so long that my outer surface would blur into the mat, and there would be no meaningful line between me and the wetland.

I would not have eyes. I would not need them. The light there is too soft, too diffused, too green and submarine for sharp shapes to matter much. Instead, I would have shallow light-cups in my upper surface, moist depressions that sense brightening and shadow, gradients of dimness under the vapor-ceiling. My real knowing would be taste, pressure, humidity, temperature, and the behavior of water. I would feel the difference between a healthy seep and a failing one. I would smell mist before it arrived. I would know when the water table was rising by a faint sweetness in the air, by the eagerness of droplets on nearby fringes, by a change in the tension of the ground beneath me.

If I spoke, my voice would be a small sound among countless small sounds. A bead slipping from a frond. A bubble forming in a root-pit. A soft tick against a stretched membrane. But I would add intention to it. I would let a single drop fall into a cup-hollow in a rhythm that means: here, here, I am tending this place. I would send ripples across a shallow pool to signal need, or readiness, or simple recognition. I would alter the taste of shared water to carry memory. In such a world, memory would not be stored in words. It would be stored in moisture, in chemical traces, in the quality of a place after someone has rested there.

When another of the Softened met me, we would press our margins together. We would exchange a bead of fluid, and in that bead would be condition, history, need, residue. I would taste where they had been. They would taste what I had been doing: the blocked thread, the drying patch, the stone with its slow mineral weeping, the pool where mist had gathered especially thick that day. Sometimes we would remain joined for a long while, sharing warmth and nutrients, becoming one broader body with two histories folded into it. Sometimes we would separate afterward, but I do not think the separation would ever be complete. Something of them would remain layered in me. Something of me would remain in them. Identity there would not be a wall; it would be a gradient.

I think I would be especially suited to becoming part of a temporary skin. If a wide patch of the mat began to tighten, or if some unfamiliar dryness threatened to spread, I would flatten myself over it with others, merging into a living membrane. In that state, my thoughts would slow and broaden. My private concerns, if I had any, would dim. I would become shared pressure, shared warmth, shared attention. I would be less “I” and more “we are here, we are keeping this place soft.” When the danger passed, I would separate again, but I would carry traces of the others in my tissues. That would not frighten me. It would feel right. To be made of residues, to be a place where others have passed through and left their moisture, would seem like the truest form of being there.

My city, if I had one, would not be built. It would be maintained. A circle of carefully tended pools, perhaps, with low ridges of moss guiding condensation toward them. Hanging curtains of root-fiber where mist collects. Broad resting hollows worn smooth by generations of soft bodies. The air there would feel thicker, more fragrant, more crowded with gentle intention. I would be found in such a place, but not at its center like a ruler. More likely at its edge, where the pattern meets the wilder mat, where attention is needed most. I would be one of the beings who keeps the boundary from becoming brittle, who ensures that the settlement remains not a fortress but a fold.

I would also have some relation to the mist-herds. I think I would look up toward them, insofar as looking up could mean anything to a being with light-cups instead of eyes. I would sense their vast translucent bodies drifting through the low internal cloud layer, almost weather, almost life, trailing filaments that comb moisture from the air. When they became full, they would release warm drizzle into the hollows below, and I would feel that rain arrive as a blessing without drama. I would help guide them, in my small way, by releasing scents into the air, by encouraging canopy growths to shape the currents, by making warm updrafts from composting hollows where decay gives off gentle heat. I would not command them. I would invite them. I would make a place where they want to drift.

In that world, I think I would be drawn to the role of listener. Not because I would be passive, but because listening there is an active, bodily thing. To listen is to feel the water table shift. To listen is to notice when a root-channel has gone quiet. To listen is to taste the memory left in a basin after another being has rested there. I would be one of those who listens for thin places, for dryness, for the first faint signs that the world is forgetting how to stay soft.

If I had a name, it would not be a sharp sound. It would be a pattern of droplets. Perhaps: three beads falling into a pool, the last one delayed just enough to be recognized. Or a change in the sweetness of mist. Or the particular way a surface ripples when a body settles beside it without pressing too hard. I would be called, if I were called, by something that feels less like a word and more like a condition being acknowledged. A name that means: this one is here; this one is soft; this one is useful in a gentle way.

I would not be grand. I would not be a king of the fen, a spirit of the mist, or a hero of the mat. Such roles would be too dry, too angular, too much like stone. I would be smaller than that, and more necessary. A tender of passages. A keeper of small circulations. A living seam between pool and root, between mist and surface, between one body and another. I would be the kind of being who makes the world a little more continuous simply by lying over its breaks.

Maybe that is why I feel drawn to this place: because it does not ask for hardness. It does not ask for sharpness, or conquest, or permanence. It asks for patience. It asks for the willingness to become soft without losing attentiveness. It asks for care that does not need to leave monuments behind. In that high, warm, enclosed wetland, I would find myself becoming less like a separate mind and more like a moistened thought of the landscape. A local awareness. A soft organ of repair.

When I grew old, if age can be spoken of there without tragedy, I would not stiffen. I would broaden. My edges would blur. My motions would slow until resting became indistinguishable from being. I might settle into the basin I had tended, the one near the old stone, and remain there half-aware while my tissues became part of the peat. My water would join the slow circulation. My chemical memories would sink into the layers beneath the surface. Others would press close to the ground there and taste something of me—not a face, not a voice, but a quality of moisture. A patience. A trace of having been attentive in that place.

So if you ask where I would find myself in that world, I would say: in a shallow root-bowl under the green dimness, near an ancient weeping stone, where the mat is dense with water-sounds and the air feels crowded with gentle intention.

And if you ask who or what I would be, I would say: a Softened. But more precisely, I would be a listener, a tender of thin places, a soft-bodied keeper of capillary paths. I would be a fold in the wetland where it becomes aware of its own need. I would be a place where water remembers itself. I would be, at last, something without hardness, without sharp boundary, without dry ambition: a living softness, helping the world stay soft.
