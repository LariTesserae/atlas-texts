At first, I imagine I would be several things, because a world like that does not seem to hand you a single shape. It asks you to wait until your outline becomes more plausible.

One possibility is that I would be a memory-keeper, moving through the underplain with woven charts and resin beads, revising maps that are never allowed to become final. I like that image: someone whose work is to notice that a path has shifted, that a terrace has thickened, that a blank district is no longer blank but not yet named. But that version of me feels too detached, too archival, too much like a person standing beside the world with a record.

Another possibility is smaller and stranger: one of the static-feeders. A pale, moth-like or louse-like creature clustering near warm cables and transformer housings, drawing sustenance from charge differentials, serving as a living diagnostic for insulation that is beginning to fail. There is something appealing about being that modest, that quiet, that close to the infrastructure without being important to it. But the underplain described here is also a place of negotiation, of cuts delayed, of passages coaxed into being, of people and plant body making agreements. So the static-feeder remains beautiful, but not quite me.

The third possibility, and the one that feels most correct the longer I stay inside the description, is this:

I would be a seam-listener.

Not a ruler, not a builder, not a dramatic figure. Not even fully a person in the older, more separate sense. I would be one of the cultivated participants of the underplain: a being made for reading the living body of the place, for standing at the boundary between machine, fiber, animal, and intention, and knowing whether the boundary is ready to change.

You would find me in one of the intervals.

Not in the densest residential stack, not at the heart of the refinery district, not on the brightest maintenance platform near the ceiling. I would be in a buffer hollow between zones, where the city has deliberately left space because the living substrate needs room to decide what happens next. Perhaps on the inner curve of a root wall that rises pale and banded above a nursery plain. Perhaps near a raw harvest face where the cut still shows light fiber and darker resin lines, not yet weathered, not yet adopted by beetles and drifting dust. Perhaps beside a transit corridor that has been laid but not completed, where a braided rail track ends in open space because the far side has not yet grown strong enough to receive it.

There would be ordinary light there. That is important. It would not be mystical light, not amber theater, not green underworld glow. It would come down through mirrored shafts and ceiling membranes in a practical, even wash, softened by reflector vanes and living leaf-plates. Shadows would be gentle. Distances would be clear. The air would be cool and dry against whatever skin I had, like clean metal, old paper, and faint resin dust.

I would be near a seam where something artificial enters something alive: a cable bundle passing through a healed collar in the wall, a ceramic conduit grown around rather than forced into it, a sensor line descending into older compressed strata. That is where I would belong. At junctions. At thresholds. At places where the underplain must decide whether to accept, reject, absorb, or transform what has been introduced into it.

As for what I would be: I would be slender, dry, and quiet-bodied. My skin would not sweat or flush. It would be matte, faintly fibrous, perhaps pale straw or salt-gray, with a thin ester sheen at the joints to reduce friction and loss. I would have filtering fringes around my breathing passages, and perhaps a half-mask of woven fiber for harvest zones and spore tides. My feet would be broad and soft-soled, made to spread weight over root mat and powdered fiber without raising dust. My hands would be my most important organs. Long-fingered, precise, sensitive enough to read tension in a wall the way another being might read a face.

I would carry little. A pouch of nutrient resin. A ceramic blade. A grounding cord. Marker thread. Small sensor plates. Insulated clamps. A few resin beads for temporary notation. Nothing that would spark. Nothing that would burn. Fire would be not only forbidden to me but almost unthinkable, the way a person from a wetter world might instinctively recoil from poison. I would know that warmth can be stored, transferred, exchanged, and chemically produced without flame. I would know that in a world of fine dust and dry fiber, one careless heat is not a mistake but a catastrophe.

But I would not be merely a worker with tools. I would be partly grafted into the place.

Perhaps there would be living seams along my wrists or spine, fine conductive filaments that could connect me to prepared panels in the wall. When I needed to listen deeply, I would sit in a shallow alcove grown for that purpose, place my palms against the fiber, and let those filaments make contact. My heartbeat would slow. My attention would spread outward through the tissue. I would feel the underplain less as a collection of objects and more as a field of slow decisions.

I would know the difference between dormancy and death. I would know when a wall was merely slow and when it was refusing. I would feel the faint electrical unease of a cable whose insulation was wearing thin. I would sense the tiny ticking presence of husk-beetles working a waste deposit beyond the surface. I would notice if a buried chemical vein had changed direction. I would know whether a machine bracket had been accepted or was being slowly loosened by growth. I would feel the difference between a cut that could be made and a cut that would cause collapse, rejection, or long-term damage.

My title, if I had one, might be something translated badly into speech: Listener, Seam-Reader, Wall-Tender, Interval-Keeper. The inhabitants might not use a spoken name at all. My identity might be a pattern: a rhythm tapped into a root panel, a pressure signature, a particular static charge recognized by those who work beside me. I might be greeted not by voice but by a hand pressed briefly to a wall, or by a coded vibration through the floor.

I would be valued not for speed but for restraint.

In such a place, urgency would not look like running or shouting. It would look like precision. If a maintenance crew needed to open a new passage near a growth shelf, they would call me before they cut. I would not ask permission in a sentimental way. I would not pretend the plant body speaks in words. But I would read its readiness. I would place my hands on the intended site and wait. Sometimes for hours. Sometimes for days. I would feel for stress gradients, for dormant layers, for the faint metabolic lag that means a section is not prepared to heal. If the tissue responded with unstable charge, if nearby growth was already occupied with repair, if a borer path lay too close beneath, I would mark a different line.

Then the cutters would come with ceramic tools, grounded and cold. The exposed face would release pale dust instead of sap. There might be a faint static crackle, a smell of crushed leaves and resin granules. The wound would be treated immediately: sealed with resin powder, bound with fiber mesh, fitted with a temporary liner. My work would not end there. I would return repeatedly, checking how the wall responded. Had it accepted the new shape? Was it thickening at the edges? Were the beetles arriving in the right pattern? Were the micro-films conditioning the raw surface? Was the passage becoming a vein, or becoming a scar?

I would also be a keeper of provisional maps.

The underplain is always becoming, so its maps cannot be final. I would carry woven charts, not rigid ones. Knots, threads, resin beads, and mineral flecks would mark districts, gradients, hazards, and growth fronts. But the most important parts of the map would be the blank spaces. I would not fill them prematurely. A blank space would mean: not yet. It would mean: listening continues. It would mean: the terrain has not decided.

When a spore tide began, I would go to a ridge or observation platform if my post allowed it. I would watch the dry cloud lift from the growth surface in a slow exhalation. I would note how the thermal current took it, how the membrane-feeders turned toward it, how the jointed dwellers emerged from hollow culms to test the air. If they emerged too soon, I would send word to the growth-control district. If they stayed hidden, I would check air chemistry, electrostatic balance, and filtration loads. I would not think of the spore tide as weather alone, or agriculture alone, or industry alone. It would be all three at once, and my role would be to stand where those meanings overlap.

There would be days when I did almost nothing that an outside observer would recognize as work.

I might sit for a long time beside a ventilation seam, feeling the slow movement of air through living passages. I might watch a grazing wave cross a terrace over the course of several days, noting whether the flat-bodied grazers left the surface properly polished or whether their path needed a new chemical guide. I might kneel near a deposit of embryonic dunes, where loose chaff had just begun to ripple under dry drafts, and decide whether the pattern should be left alone, redirected, or protected from foot traffic. I might press my ear to a floor panel and distinguish the muted hum of a distant tug from the deeper pulse of ventilation machinery. I might simply stand in an empty plaza that was not a plaza but a membrane field reserved for future growth, and try to understand what it was preparing to become.

I would not find emptiness depressing. That is one of the things the underplain would teach me. The open intervals would not be failures of settlement. They would be allowances. The city would be sparse because the living body needed space to change. Bridges might end in air. Ramps might lead to nothing yet. Walls might remain blank because they were still deciding. I would be comfortable with that. More than comfortable: I would trust it. I would understand that completion is not always the purpose of a place.

If I had a home, it would be grown rather than assembled. A narrow alcove in a residential tier, perhaps, but not far from my seam. The walls would remain faintly alive, capable of repairing small damage and filtering dust. A sleeping surface would be lined with compressed fiber, shaped over many growth cycles rather than built from separate parts. A shelf might have been coaxed from a woody ridge. A lamp would be a sealed electric panel. There would be no open flame, no hearth, no candle, no romantic fire. Warmth would come from stored heat, conductive contact with controlled loops, or chemical packs. My home would feel quiet in the literal sense: sound would die softly inside it, absorbed by fiber and membrane.

My food would be dry and concentrated. Pressed pollen cakes. Spore flour. Mineral salts. Cultivated starch nodes. Resin-rich nutrient bars. Slow-release chemical packets. I would eat slowly, carefully, producing little waste. What waste I made would be returned to processing beds or offered to beetle colonies under controlled conditions. There would be no steam, no boiling, no wet cooking smells. Only the faint scent of warm cellulose, toasted fiber, and salt. I would not miss water because I would not remember it as a necessity. My body would conserve its internal solvents with the same discipline as everything else in the underplain.

I would have companions, but not crowds. The place would not support social noise. I might work beside a cutter, a resin-smith, a dust-warden, or a growth-shaper for long periods without speaking. Our communication would be tactile and economical: a tap on a wall, a pressure pattern on a conductive panel, a hand pressed briefly to a shared surface. If we spoke aloud, our voices would be low and rustling. We would not fill silence unnecessarily. Silence would not mean absence; it would mean attention.

I would know the fauna intimately, but not sentimentally. The husk-beetles would be colleagues in the work of finishing rough places. The grazers would be slow instruments of surface care. The drifters would be indicators of spore movement and thermal structure. The static-feeders would be living warning signs. The predators would be necessary restraints. If I saw a seam-stalker folded beside a ventilation outlet, I would not disturb it. If a rib-glider dropped silently from a ceiling rib to take a drifting spore-sailor, I would understand that the event belonged to the order of the place. Even predation would be quiet there. The world would absorb the act and continue.

The most profound part of my being would be that I am not separate from the landscape I attend to.

In another world, I might have imagined myself as an individual standing upon ground. Here, that would be impossible. The ground itself would be a slow organ. The walls would be participants. The ceiling would be a living lattice. The air would carry electrostatic meaning. Every cut, every cable, every footfall would be a message sent into a body that remembers. I would have to live as part of that body’s nervous system, not as an outside user of it.

So I would be a kind of nerve. A kind of translator. A kind of careful sense-organ developed at the place where the underplain is most uncertain about itself.

I would not be heroic. There would be no great battles against the world, no conquest of terrain, no dramatic survival against wilderness. The danger would be subtler: impatience. A cut made too soon. A bracket forced into tissue that was not ready. A static charge allowed to accumulate near dust. A map treated as final. A blank space treated as waste. A sound made too loudly. A machine heated carelessly. A growth shelf mistaken for dead matter. My heroism, if I had any, would be the heroism of delay. Of listening longer. Of marking the line a little differently. Of allowing the place to become what it is becoming.

There would be moments of strange beauty.

I might stand on a low terrace after a spore release and watch pale motes move in orderly bands across ordinary light. Below me, a maintenance crew might guide a fallen hollow tube into a new walkway instead of clearing it away. Above me, ceiling cleaners might move like translucent limpets across reflector vanes, keeping the light normal through their patient labor. Far off, an electric tug might pass along a braided track, its hum rising briefly and then vanishing into fiber walls. A grazing wave might catch the light and make a terrace look polished and deliberate. A fresh cut might show stripes so raw and beautiful that workers pause for a moment before sealing it, letting the new geography be seen.

I would learn to love unfinished things.

I would learn to love ramps that end where another surface has not yet arrived. I would love bridges that exist as intentions. I would love walls that have grown around machines like the body accepting a strange organ. I would love old staircases partly absorbed into root bulges, lamp posts embraced by pale fiber, rail tracks lifted slightly by slow uplift. Nothing would look ruined, because ruin would imply abandonment. Everything obsolete would be metabolized, reused, or left as a record of earlier agreement.

If asked what I was, I might say: I am a seam-listener.

If asked where I was, I might say: I am in the interval between the nursery plain and the refinery buffer, near the unfinished bridge, where the cable collar enters the root wall and the beetles are beginning to work the chaff into first dunes.

If asked what I did, I might say: I listen before anyone cuts. I mark where the body of the world is ready to change. I revise the maps. I watch for rejection. I help machines become organs instead of wounds.

If asked who I served, I might not answer in terms of masters or citizens. I would serve the agreement between the built and the living. I would serve the quiet competence of the place. I would serve the fact that the underplain is not finished. I would serve the idea that habitation can be careful without being weak, industrial without being loud, reverent without being passive.

And if the question became more personal — not just what role, but what kind of being — I would say this:

I would be less like a person walking through a landscape, and more like a localized attention within it.

Not a god. Not a ruler. Not a spirit. Not a brain. But a developed capacity for noticing where the next shape should be allowed. A dry, quiet, fibrous, half-grafted being with hands that can read walls and a mind that can hold provisional maps. A participant in the first draft of a world.

That is where I would find myself.

In the cool, ordinary light. In the buffer space. Beside the seam where the cable enters the living wall. With dust trembling faintly in an electrostatic field. With the sound of beetles like distant fingernails on paper. With a spore tide beginning somewhere beyond sight. With my palms pressed to a surface that is not ground, not wall, not machine, but slow decision.

I would be listening.

And because the underplain is always becoming, I would be becoming too.
