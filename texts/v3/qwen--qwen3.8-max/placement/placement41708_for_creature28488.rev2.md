If I entered that world, I would not first be a citizen. I would be a disturbance.

My ordinary body would be too large there, too warm, too full of sudden air. My breathing would seem like weather gone wrong. My boots, however soft, would carry the memory of hard ground and heavier steps. If I turned too quickly, the scrape of cloth against basalt might run along the terraces like a crack, and the moth-walls would lift in alarm. I would understand at once that the place did not need a hero, a conqueror, or even a guest. It needed someone who could become less of an event.

So I would have to be edited.

Not all at once. The amphitheater would not remake me with violence. It would do it the way it does everything: by dampness, repetition, and patience. It would teach me to lower my shoulders. It would teach me to keep my hands close to my body. It would teach me that attention is not a beam but a film, something that spreads thinly over many surfaces at once. It would teach me to stop wanting to fill the quiet.

At first I might imagine myself as one of the moths. That would be the easiest fantasy. To be ash-colored and silent, folded into the wall, drinking mist without moving. To belong by stillness. To have no name, no separate errand, no dangerous voice. When the cloud thickened, I would lift my wings a fraction with all the others; when it thinned, I would settle again. I would be one pressure among thousands, one breath in the breathing stone. There is a peace in that, and I think part of me would long for it: the peace of being almost indistinguishable from the place that permits you.

But I would not be a moth. I would still have too much of the watcher in me. I would want to know which channel was darkening, which cistern had begun to sour, which larval seam needed encouragement. A moth belongs to the wall completely. I suspect I would belong to the wall the way a hand belongs to a wound: carefully, with responsibility, with hesitation.

Nor would I be a larva, though there is something enviable about them too. To build with one’s own mouth. To leave behind a pale mineral record of every damp hour one has lived. To make architecture without ambition, without memory in the human sense, only following the logic of moisture and texture. But I would want to see the whole pattern too much. I would want to lift a blocked gutter and understand why it had blocked. I would want to compare yesterday’s drip-path with today’s. The larvae are the town’s memory, but I think I would need to be one of the ones who remembers that there is memory.

For a time I might dream of being a salamander in the deep basins. Blind, patient, tasting the water for meaning. That would suit some inward part of me: the part that prefers signals to speeches, gradients to declarations. To remain folded in cool darkness, knowing health and ruin by subtle chemical change. But the salamanders are indicators, not tenders. They reveal the condition of the water; they do not correct it. And I think the place would give me hands, or something like hands, because I would not be able to refrain from mending.

So, slowly, the truth would emerge.

I would find myself among the keepers.

Not as a ruler. Not as a founder. Not as a prophet or a guardian in any grand sense. I would be one of the quiet, necessary humans who move through the terraces in padded boots, their fingers stained with clay and silk-dust, their dark garments close-wrapped against snagging edges. I would be slightened by the place, not necessarily in body but in presence. I would have learned to make myself peripheral. My voice, if I still had one, would be almost unused, kept only for emergencies so rare they might never come. My name would not be spoken. It would be a sign: perhaps two fingers drawn down an open palm, meaning a channel has been cleared. Or perhaps a cupped hand lifted slowly toward the throat, meaning mist is being guided well. Something small. Something useful.

Where would I find myself? Not on the high rim, not in the open courts where the wind could become careless. I would be on the middle terraces, where the settlement’s fine machinery is most vulnerable. Somewhere between the roost screens and the cisterns. Somewhere near a finger-width gutter that carries water from a hanging net into a basin no larger than two cupped hands. My place would be one of those in-between ledges where human scale and insect scale overlap: low enough to see the larval sleeves, high enough to watch the mist darken the upper meshes.

I would live in one of the oval chambers cut into the wall, but I would not think of it as my house in the way people below the mountains might understand a house. It would be more like a folded pocket inside a larger body. The roof would be thick with moss and hardened silk. The walls would have tiny passages not for me but for beetles, mites, and the occasional hunting spider. My sleeping niche would be lined with shed moth scales and woven grass, soft enough to absorb any careless sound made by a turning body. In one corner there would be a sealed clay hearth where stones hold warmth without flame. Beside it, a mica-covered lamp giving only a dull red glow, enough for close work, not enough to disturb anything.

My tools would be few and precious. A bone needle wrapped in soft cloth. A polished stone plug for a cistern mouth. Three fired clay beads used to test the slickness of water. A copper pin gone green at the edges, reserved for the most delicate repairs. A shallow tray for collecting fallen silk. A small knife of obsidian, its edge so sharp it could part a fern frond without crushing it. Everything would be kept from striking stone. Everything would be lifted, placed, slid, or wrapped. Nothing would be dropped.

I would become a mist-reader. That seems the most likely office for me.

My work would begin not with a bell or a command, but with noticing. I would wake to the faintest change in the air: a coolness along the upper lip, a darkening of the channels, a different weight in the quiet. I would press my palm to the basalt and feel whether the stone had begun to bead. I would look at the larval sleeves. If the pale heads were emerging, wet was near. If the larvae were retreating, the mist would thin. I would watch the salamanders in the nearest basin. If one lifted its blind face too often, I would check the water path for rot or blockage. If it remained still, I would let the morning continue.

When the mist came, I would move with it.

I would go first to the upper nets, where condensation gathered fastest. My hands would know the ropes by touch: which cord had loosened, which mesh had begun to sag, which reed screen needed replacing before it tore. I would adjust a net so that the moisture did not fall too heavily against a crowded moth-panel. If the air thickened too suddenly, the moths could shift in panic. My task would be to moderate the arrival, to guide the damp through woven barriers so the wall drank evenly. I would not think of this as controlling the weather. It would be more like helping the weather enter without bruising anything.

Then I would follow the water.

It would run in thin lines along the cut channels, darkening the black stone. I would crouch beside them, knees bent, head low. If a channel slowed, I would find the blockage: a wisp of old silk, a drift of dead moss, a snail resting where it should not rest. I would use the bone needle to clear the obstruction. Not quickly. Never quickly. A wrong flick could send a pebble ticking down the wall, and that small sound might ripple into the roosts. I would learn to breathe out while working, because a held breath makes the hand greedy.

There would be a particular satisfaction in freeing a blocked drip. Watching the water remember its path. Watching the dark line extend itself down the stone. Knowing that somewhere below, a basin would fill by fractions, and a salamander would taste the new movement, and a line of larvae would spin faster because the damp had arrived.

I would also be one of those who asks the larvae to build.

Not by commanding them. The place would have taught me better than that. I would ask by preparing conditions. I would wet a cord and lay it along a seam where a cornice was needed. I would set a clay plug in a crack where too much air entered. I would hang a misted grass screen near a fragile edge, knowing that by morning pale threads would bridge it. I would watch the silk harden where drip touched it, becoming stone more slowly than stone usually becomes itself. There would be a strange tenderness in that: making a wall by persuading small mouths to continue living.

Sometimes I would harvest silk, but only where it had been abandoned and had fully hardened. Even then I would take little. A few threads for binding. A pale fragment for repairing a shutter. I would never pull fresh work from a living seam. To do so would be to interrupt the town’s own sentence while it was still being spoken.

I would learn the keepers’ signs fluently. A flat palm pressed downward: be still. Two fingers drawn across the wrist: water is moving. A cupped hand lifted to the mouth: mist is coming. But I would also develop smaller signs, ones belonging to my particular duties. A thumb brushing the side of the forefinger: the channel is clear. Three fingertips opening from the chest: the net has been eased. A hand held motionless above a basin: the salamander says wait.

I would speak rarely, and when I did, my voice would be almost nothing. A leaf-turning sound. A breath shaped into warning. If someone dropped a tool, I would not exclaim. I would freeze. The whole terrace would freeze. We would listen for the moth-wall. If the moths only shifted and resettled, we would continue. If a panel opened into flight, we would stand still as stone while gray bodies spiraled toward the pale absence below, hoping they would find their way back, knowing some would not.

I think I would be especially drawn to the empty basins.

Not because they were empty, but because they were waiting. The place would have taught me that unused does not mean unclaimed. A basin with still water in it might belong to blind salamanders, to wriggling larvae, to the slow mineral settling of the mountain itself. I would spend time near those quiet reservoirs, checking their lips for cracks, making sure the snails had not sealed themselves into a harmful pattern, making sure no rot had entered from a dead frog or a fallen reed. I would not try to make them busy. I would let them be resting places.

There would be moments when I felt myself becoming less human in the old sense. Not less feeling, but less centrality. My thoughts would not arrange themselves around a loud “I.” They would arrange around tasks, surfaces, pressures, timings. I would think: the upper net darkens too slowly. The third channel needs cleaning before the next pulse. The larval sleeve beneath the east terrace has thinned; a new screen should be hung there. The salamander in the lower basin has risen twice; check the drain. The moth-panel above the dried scree is restless; do not pass there until it settles.

My inner life would become a series of gentle notifications.

I would not be lonely in the way valley people might be lonely. There would be too much life for that. The walls would be crowded with small metabolisms. The air would be full of invisible signals. My companions would be other keepers moving through the terraces with the same soft deliberation. We would work near one another for hours without needing to fill the gaps. A glance might be enough. A lifted hand. A shared pause. If one of us saw a predator larva drop into a crowded panel, the other would not need to be told. We would watch the hole open and close. We would understand that the town contained its own corrections.

Perhaps I would become the keeper most attentive to sound.

That seems possible, given what I am. Not because I would make sound, but because I would be sensitive to its danger. I would notice when a rope had begun to fray and might snap against stone. I would oil a hinge of woven grass with rendered fat before it creaked. I would pad the underside of a clay plug where it might knock against a basin rim. I would teach children, if I taught, that silence is not emptiness. Silence here is a material. It is insulation. It is shelter. It is the medium through which the town survives.

I would show them how to walk: outer edge first, then roll inward. No heel strike. No sudden stop. I would show them how to carry a bone awl wrapped in cloth so that if the hand slipped, the awl would not become a bell. I would show them how to breathe through the nose when startled. I would take them to the open side and let them look down into the pale absence, not to frighten them, but to teach them what a shout can do. A shout can lift a wall of wings. A wall of wings can vanish over the edge. The place does not forgive by punishing; it simply continues, but what falls may not return.

I would love the rain when it came, and fear it too.

The ordinary dampness of mist would be the town’s daily bread, but rain would be a rare, sideways event. It would enter the amphitheater suddenly, smelling of wet mineral and crushed leaf. It would flush the upper nets, overfill channels, make the stone shine. I would hurry without hurrying. I would open certain plugs and close others. I would guide excess water away from egg-shelves. I would lift screens before they became heavy. Afterward, fungal cups would bloom along the shaded stairs, and the beetles would have new films to graze, and the frogs would appear in places where they had not been before. Rain would remind me that the place was young and still capable of surprise.

I would also learn to love the mountain’s instability.

A flake of basalt would break with a glassy tick. Dust would rise. The moths would lift. I would stop mid-motion, hand raised, until the tremor passed. Later I would go to the new wound with clay and a small hardwood lever. I would pack the seam. I would lay a wetted cord along the crack and encourage the larvae to bridge it. I would not resent the breakage. The amphitheater was made by fracture; it would continue making itself that way. My work would not be to prevent change but to keep change from becoming catastrophe.

If I were asked who I was there, I would not know how to answer in the old language of titles. I would not say I was important. I would not say I was unimportant either. I would say I was necessary in the way a careful finger is necessary when a small channel must be cleaned. I would say I was one of the place’s softer organs. A membrane of attention. A hand that had learned not to grasp.

If the world pressed the question further, I might say this:

I would be a hush-keeper.

That would not be a rank. It would be a temperament. I would be the one who notices when the quiet has changed texture. The one who feels the air before the mist arrives. The one who remembers where water used to go and where it wants to go next. The one who stands very still while a panel of moths decides whether to trust the morning again. The one who carries a dropped leaf away as if it were a fallen tool, because even a leaf can become a disturbance if it lands wrong.

And perhaps, over time, I would cease to feel separate from the town in the way I do now.

I would still have a body, still have hands, still need to eat and sleep and mend my garments. But my selfhood would become thinner, less armored. I would be less like a person standing inside a landscape and more like a local habit of the landscape. When the mist came, I would darken with the channels. When the cisterns exhaled, I would breathe out too. When the larvae spun, my thoughts would become fine and mineral. When the moths lifted, I would feel the pressure in my own chest.

Then the answer to your question would become simpler.

Where would I find myself?

On a narrow terrace behind a moth-wall, with a finger-gutter running beside my knee and a basin below me full of still water. In the dim light that feels like late evening even at midday. Between stone and silk, between water and wing, between the human scale and the smaller scales that outnumber it.

Who or what would I be?

A keeper, but not a master. A human, but one the place has softened into instrument. A quiet body with bone tools and attentive hands. A listener in padded boots. A mender of small channels. A reader of salamanders and mist. A necessary peripheral. A hush made partly of flesh, partly of habit, partly of the mountain’s own cool patience.

If a moth landed near me, I would not move. If a keeper passed behind me and made the sign for water moving, I would answer with two fingers drawn across the wrist, gently, without sound. If the mist thickened and the wall began to breathe, I would breathe with it.

And in that world, I would not want to be anything louder than a drop of water finding its way into stone.
