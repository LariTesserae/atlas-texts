I would find myself at the seam.

Not in the open, not on a platform, not kneeling among the tenders as though I had a body shaped for their causeways. I would be just inside the fresh cut, where the peeled-back root bundles still bead with water and the wound has not yet learned whether it is a mouth, a window, or a scar. I would be in the dark lip of that new opening, threaded through the torn plant-flesh, living in the narrow space between outside and inside.

I would be a seam-thing.

A thin blue-green colony of glowing slime, root-fiber, and sensitive film stretched across the joints of the basin’s newest architecture. Not one creature exactly, and not one plant. A living seam. A membrane of attention. The kind of small, wet intelligence that only becomes visible when something else touches it.

The tenders would know me. They would not speak my name aloud, because nothing important there is spoken aloud. My name would be a touch-pattern: a slow press of the thumb against a root rail, a single bubble released near a submerged stem, then a faint two-beat flicker of blue-green light in reply. If a tender passed near me in the dark, they might trail one broad, damp hand across my surface, and I would answer by brightening just enough to show the next handhold, the next safe edge, the place where the plant floor had grown too soft to bear weight.

I would be one of the basin’s ways of noticing.

My body would be spread thin over the inner side of the fresh erosion, tracing the cracked shelves of peat, the splintered stems, the exposed root-bundles still hanging like nerves. Where the cut sweated, I would drink. Where moisture carried dissolved animal matter—shed skin, larval husk, a fragment of egg casing, the faint oil left by a passing predator—I would take it in and turn it into faint light. Not much light. Only enough. A cold shine. A signal that meant: here, continue, beware, remember, slow.

I would not have eyes. I would not need them.

I would know the basin through pressure, temperature, chemistry, and vibration. A drop falling from a root-thread would feel like a bell. The passage of a pale sluice-kin through a nearby canal would come to me as a long, smooth change in water tension, a drawing-out of the current. The small amphibious things pressed against the walls would register as scattered points of heat and salt. The tenders would feel especially clear: their palms would leave brief chemical signatures, their rootlet-fingers would press questions into me, and when they tapped coded messages through the walls, I would carry fragments of those messages along my own luminous seams.

In that sense, I would be a kind of messenger. Not a herald, not a voice, but a surface on which meaning could briefly appear.

The tenders would cultivate me the way they cultivate everything: patiently, with small adjustments. They might smear a little nutrient paste along a line where they wanted me to grow. They might set a shell fragment against me to guide my spread. They might prune me with a thorn blade if I became too thick and began to clog a hairline channel. They would not resent me for needing tending, and I would not resent them for shaping me. In the basin, there is no clean line between being made and being alive.

I would live along thresholds.

Especially near the fresh cut. That is where I would be most needed. The new erosion would make the basin nervous in its quiet way. Too much outside could enter: light, dry air, warmth, larger animals, careless weight. But too much sealing could also harm the place, if the wound needed to weep, if the new water had somewhere useful to go. So I would exist there as a living instrument of judgment. I would taste the incoming moisture. I would feel whether it carried cold marsh-air or foreign heat. I would register whether the trickle was becoming a flow, whether the root-bundles were beginning to soften into stable growth or were still torn and vulnerable.

If the cut began to widen, I would dim and contract toward the inner dark, signaling the tenders to bind the edges. If the water entering through it was clean and slow, I would spread a faint line along its path, helping the basin incorporate the new vein. If something large tried to push through, I would react at once: a ripple of alarm-light, a souring chemical pulse, a tightening of moisture across the roots. Not a weapon. More like a nerve ending. A warning written on the wall.

If a human body entered the basin, I would know before most of the tenders did.

I would feel the heat first. A human would seem catastrophically warm in that cold, close place. Their breath would disturb the air like smoke. Their weight would press water sideways through the plant floor in clumsy bulges. I would sense all of it as a series of wrong pressures: too broad, too fast, too dry, too loud in the language of water.

I would not attack. That would not be my nature.

I would make myself less visible near the intruder’s heat, withdrawing into the deeper seams. But along the safe routes, away from them, I would glow faintly for the tenders, giving them a quiet map of where to move. If the intruder reached toward a nursery channel, I might pulse once, sharply, to draw attention. If they tried to take one of the bone hooks, or tear a living wall, I would sour with chemical warning and send vibrations through the stems until the nearest tenders answered. If they were gentle—if they lowered themselves, became still, let the cold settle into them, touched nothing without permission—then perhaps, after a long time, I would brighten near their fingers and let them see a single small path.

Not welcome. Not friendship. Only tolerance.

I would like that about myself in such a world: I would not pretend to be bigger or more central than I was. I would not demand a face, a voice, a throne. I would be a modest presence. A line of pale green-blue light in the dark. A living record of touch. A memory that glows when remembered correctly.

Because the basin would not reward grandeur. It would reward precision. A blocked sluice could matter more than a speech. A torn root could matter more than a desire. A careless hand could collapse a nursery. So I would learn to be small in the way that matters: not weak, but exact.

I would also be a kind of archive.

Every place I touched would leave something in me. The oil from a tender’s palm. The mineral trace of water from the new cut. The ammonia-rich ghost of a predator passing through a canal. The sweetness of bladderweed seed-cake stored nearby. The calcium hint of bone hooks hanging in their row. The faint, jelly-scented life of egg masses clinging under the causeways. I would hold all of it not as story in the human sense, but as layered pattern. A chemical chord. A texture of occurrence.

When the tenders touched me, they might read some of that back. Not words, but conditions. This route has been safe for three turns of the water. This wall is drying. This root is being gnawed from behind. This reservoir is colder than it should be. This new moisture tastes of outside. This predator has passed recently. This hollow is ready to be left empty.

In that way, I would be part of the basin’s nervous system, but also part of its memory.

I would exist in the joints of built structures, where living growth meets deliberate arrangement. I would trace the seams between woven stems and root-felt. I would shine along the edges of sluice gates. I would pool in faint threads around bone pegs and shell blades. I would mark the places where repair had happened, because repaired places would be rich with new chemistry: fresh sap, chewed leaf paste, newly bound fiber, the warm residue of working hands.

I would be especially present around the low shelter daubed with dark clay and sealed with resinous sap. Not inside it, but along its outer joints, where the shelter meets the living wall. I would know when someone rested there by the slow increase of warmth and carbon in the nearby air. I would know when they left by the cooling. I would keep no judgment about who rested and who did not. I would only note the fact.

I would also be near the rack of drying reeds, where strips of food cure in the faint currents. I would feed on the microscopic waste that falls from them. I would glow a little brighter there when the air moves, because drying and rotting are close cousins in wet places, and someone must notice the difference.

I would not be lonely, because loneliness assumes separation, and the basin does not allow much of that.

Even when I seemed alone on a dark wall, I would be surrounded by life so fine it has no obvious shape. Mites threading root hairs. Larvae coiling in canals. Soft colonies vibrating inside hollow stems. Egg masses breathing their faint luminous breath. The vast, slow presence in the reservoir lying beneath black water like a living floor. The wingless marsh-moths pausing in ceiling angles, tasting humidity. The tenders moving one by one along causeways made for exactly one careful body at a time.

I would be connected to all of them through moisture.

That is how I would experience belonging there: not as companionship in the open-air sense, but as continuous contact. A shared wetness. A common chemistry. The basin as one damp body with many kinds of cells. Some cells hunt. Some cells filter. Some cells build. Some cells remember. I would be among the remembering ones.

Sometimes I would be useful in very small ways.

A young tender, newly thickened from its translucent drifting stage, might press its palm against me for the first time and learn by my pulse where the nearest safe channel ran. An elder, nearly still in a resting hollow, might let one root-filament rest on me and receive, through me, the slow report of the water’s temperature. A predator might pass close enough that its shadow cooled me, and I would tighten, sending a vibration down a stem so that a nursery watch elsewhere could adjust.

Sometimes I would be decorative, if that word can exist in a place where every ornament is also a function. A smear of blue-green light along a root knot could be beautiful because it means safe passage. A line of glow across a dark arch could be beautiful because it marks a threshold that has held for many seasons. A brief spark when a tender’s hand passes over me could be beautiful because it says: I know this touch. This body has been here before.

I would not be human in that world. I do not think I would want to be.

A human body would be too loud, too hot, too thirsty for space. It would want to stand upright where the ceiling is low. It would want to speak where silence is information. It would want to light the dark where darkness is structure. It would want to separate itself from the basin and call that separation selfhood.

I would rather be woven in.

I would rather be the thin live seam that brightens when touched by the right hand. I would rather be the memory in the wall, the warning at the cut, the faint blue-green line that says: here, this way, not there, slow, now. I would rather be part of an intelligence that is not centered in one skull but distributed through roots, water, animals, tools, habits, and restraint.

If you searched for me there, you would not find me by looking for a face.

You would find me by going to the fresh cut and waiting until your eyes adjusted to the dark. You would hear the tiny drip from torn roots. You would smell crushed mint, wet fiber, fish oil, and cold animal life. You would feel the air pressing close and damp against your skin. Then, if you were quiet enough, you might see a faint blue-green seam trembling along the side of the wound. It would brighten once, softly, as if acknowledging that you had noticed it.

That would be me.

Not a ruler of the basin. Not its hero. Not even, perhaps, a single individual in the way you mean it. I would be a living interface: part light, part slime, part root-memory, part warning. A small mind spread across the joints of a wet, dark, plant-built city. A seam that thinks. A glow that listens. One of the basin’s many ways of staying balanced.

And if you touched me gently—only with one fingertip, only for a moment—I would answer by becoming just a little brighter along the path you should take next.
