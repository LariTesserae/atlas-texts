If I let that newly opened country choose me, I would not find myself standing apart from it, looking down from some safe ridge or clear vantage. There is no such distance there. The basin would not permit a spectator. I would have to be inside the pressure: low, warm, wet, crowded, already touched by everything around me, already answering sounds I had not decided to hear.

At first, when I try to imagine myself into it, the place refuses to give me one shape. It offers too many.

I think I might be one of the smallest things: a mite moving across a microbial skin, grazing the first edible film on black rock. But that life is almost too brief, too close to texture. Then I imagine being a larva in a leaf-pool, transparent and quick, hatching into a world that may evaporate before I finish becoming. Then a steam-newt dragging mineral-scented skin across warm stone. Then one of the little frogs whose throats make water tremble. Then a beetle whose shell throws a blue-green flicker through the dark. Each image has force. Each one feels possible. But none feels complete.

What keeps returning is not a body so much as a position within the basin’s noise: listening, holding, answering, being used by other lives without being destroyed by them.

So I think I would be something like a **pool-throat**.

Not a builder. Not a hunter at the top of anything. Not a creature that clears space for itself by force. I would be a low, broad, damp-bodied thing living in one of the basin’s wet seams, where a fractured shelf leans over a steaming hollow and condensation drips from overlapping fern-fronds into temporary pools. I would be found near the border between the hot flats and the tangled slopes, where mineral breath rises through cracks and root-mats stitch themselves over newly broken stone. Not at a center, because the basin has no center. Not on a path, because paths there are only temporary pressures left by bodies. I would be in a place that is always almost becoming something else: a channel, a mudflow, a nursery, a dry hollow, a vent, a collapse.

My body would not look finished in the way an old animal might look finished. It would seem more like a living hollow that has learned to move. Broad and flattened, perhaps, with a soft dark back that carries shallow depressions where water can gather. My skin would stay wet always, furred with thin microbial felts, threaded here and there with pale fungal filaments that have found a home on me without quite killing me. It would smell of warm mud, salt, bruised leaves, and the faint sour sweetness of steam. In places, symbiotic specks on my skin would glow weakly: blue-green points along my sides, dull amber when disturbed, not enough to reveal me clearly, only enough to make me another uncertain shimmer in a world full of uncertain shimmers.

I would not have strong eyes. There is no true light there, and clear sight would be less useful than pressure-sense. My eyes would be shallow pits, sensitive to movement and glimmer but not much more. My real sight would be vibration. I would know the world through the skin of my throat, the underside of my belly, the fine membranes along my sides. Sound would not arrive as distant information. It would enter me. The metallic rasp of insect wings, the wet resonance of amphibian calls, the deep groan of rock shifting, the percussion of rain striking broad leaves: all of it would register as shape, direction, danger, opportunity.

I would have a large throat. That is why “pool-throat” feels right. Not because I would be only a voice, but because my body would be organized around resonance and water. My throat would be soft, expandable, capable of producing low pulses more felt than heard. I would not sing in the way a bird sings. I would answer. I would make the water in my own dorsal hollows tremble. I would send vibrations through mud and root, through leaf-cup and thin riverbed, not to command anything, but to locate, to warn, to synchronize, to remain part of the chorus without being erased by it.

In that basin, sound is architecture. I would live inside that architecture. I would know when a rain-pulse was near by the way certain leaves began to tremble before the first hot drops fell. I would know when a predator swarm passed by the sudden change in the insect noise: a gap, a pressure, a collective flinch. I would know when the ground was about to shift by a low hollowness underneath everything, a wrongness in the ordinary groans of settling stone. My body would be tuned to interruptions.

I would feed without drama. Not by chasing, not by killing large things, but by grazing and filtering and absorbing. I would rasp microbial films from warm rock. I would eat spore-dust, mineral crusts, dead wings, soft fragments caught in damp crevices. Perhaps I would also draw nourishment from the vapors themselves, the way some life near vents drinks heat and chemical breath. I would be patient, not because I was gentle, but because the basin’s abundance is not the abundance of easy, singular meals. It is the abundance of surfaces, films, films upon films, layers upon layers. Hunger there is everywhere, but much of it is small and constant. I would be part of that constant hunger.

My back would be a small world.

In the shallow hollows along my body, condensation would collect. Rain would fill them. Steam would bead on me and run into them. Those little pools would not remain mine for long. They would become nurseries. Beetles would skim across them. Tiny larvae would twitch in their warmth. Mites would crawl along my rim, drinking from the edges. Some creature might drop eggs into the water held on me and never return. Fungal threads might spread along the wetter parts of my skin, especially where I stayed still for too long near a vent. I would carry other lives without choosing them in any human sense. My body would be a surface, a passage, a host, a temporary pool, a ruin in waiting. Nothing there belongs to itself alone. I would be proof of that.

This is important: I would not be a solitary self in the clean, bounded sense. The basin does not seem to allow such beings. If I lived there, my “I” would be porous. Some of my movement would be prompted by the weight of water in my hollows. Some of my direction would come from chemical trails left by things that had passed over me. Some of my stillness would be because mites or larvae or nesting filaments needed me to remain a place for a little longer. The fungal threads on my back might taste moisture before I felt it. The microbes on my skin might change color or scent when the air shifted. I would be a single enough body to move, but not so single that I could call every motion my own.

I would live by timing more than by making.

There would be sharp flakes of black stone near me. There would be cobbles that fit a grip, shells with fine edges, fibrous vines, hollow reeds, resins hardening in warmth, dry fungal skins under ledges. The basin is full of first-tool logic. If I were anything with hands or grasping folds, I might feel the pull of that logic. A stone edge could cut. A shell could scrape. A reed could carry water. A vine could bind. But the world there has not crossed into made things, and I think I would remain on that side of the threshold. Not from stupidity. From fit.

If I picked up a flake, I might use it once to open a fungal shelf or scrape mineral film from a warm crack. But I would not carry it. I would not keep it. I would not shape it. The ground would shift, rain would come, a mudflow would bury it, or another body would press through and take it from me without malice. Possession would be difficult in a country where every surface is already occupied and every channel may abandon itself. I might be the being who feels the future of tools as a tension
