I would not find myself all at once.

In a place like that, a self would not arrive as a hard outline. It would be negotiated by dampness, by the pressure of bare feet on root-felt, by the way a wall leaned toward me or away. I think I would first appear there as a kind of wrongness: too dry, too quick, too separate from the slow green breathing of the basin. My footsteps would bruise the moss. My voice would strike the living wood and come back awkwardly, as if I had interrupted a long-held breath. I would be standing near one of the narrow rim-openings where an old channel passes through, still carrying the dust of higher land in the creases of my clothes, and the basin-born would look at me not with alarm, but with the careful attention of people noticing a cracked seam in a roof-panel.

The first thing I would be, therefore, is a disturbance.

But not an unwelcome one. Not exactly.

They would offer me water before anything else. A cup carved from a seed-pod, or perhaps an old insulator cap cleaned and softened with years of use. The water would be cool and faintly tea-dark, tasting of leaf-shadow and cold iron. I would drink too fast. Someone would notice that. Not to judge me, but to understand what kind of thirst I had brought in from the outside. Then they would ask me to walk slowly. Then to speak quietly. Then to place my palm against a wall and wait.

For a while, I might fail at all three.

So if you ask where I would find myself at the beginning, the answer is: at the edge, being taught the basin’s first grammar. Not inside the deepest alcove, not yet trusted with the cisterns or the delicate membranes, but near a lower path where the root-steps are worn into shallow hollows, learning how to put my weight down without asking the ground to suffer it.

But the more I think about it, the less I believe I would remain an outsider for long.

If I were born there, if I had been one of the basin-born from the start, I think I would be small and watchful. I would have gray-green skin with a trace of rust at the fingertips from touching old iron too often. My hair would be dark, damp at the ends, braided with root-thread and perhaps one soft copper ring gone green with age. There might be a little patch of lichen along one shoulder or the back of my neck, not because I was careless, but because my breath had settled into the basin and the basin had accepted me enough to grow on me. I would move with that economical patience described by the life of the place: no wasted steps, no sharp gestures, no desire to make myself larger than the space could hold.

Yet even that image does not fully settle. It is too complete, too neatly human.

There are moments when I imagine I would be one of the condensation moths instead, resting on a ceramic insulator in the dim green light, my wings grooved like tiny gutters. I would carry mist and pollen between the roof-flowers, doing essential work without ever being named for it. Or perhaps I would be one of the rust-beetles, slow and coppery, moving along an old girder and eating away the flakes that might otherwise fall into the water. There is something honest about that kind of life: making repair a form of feeding, keeping decay from becoming damage.

And sometimes I think I would not be a separate creature at all, but a thickening in the wall. A place where someone had been laid after death and the roots had grown over them until they became part of the structure. Not a ghost, nothing so theatrical. Only a warmth in the wood. A spot where a hand might rest when memory was needed. A slight firmness in the surface, as if the wall had learned to bear more weight.

But as I keep writing, one location returns more often than the others.

I keep finding myself lower down.

Not at the rim, not in the open work-yard, not high among the membrane-menders. I keep coming back to the terraces near the old boiler pools, where the root-floor darkens with moisture and the air smells of wet bark, sap, and cold metal. There would be a shallow depression nearby with fallen leaves floating like coins, and a rusted boiler half-open, colonized by roots, holding a black mirror of still water inside. Copper tubing would run along the wall there, green with patina, tied into little whistles and drip-lines so that the water makes small sounds when it moves. A gear would be planted in the floor, its teeth worn smooth, its center used as a sitting circle. The light would be ordinary: gray-green, soft, unhurried. Shadows would pool gently under the broad leaves and the old iron ribs.

That is where I would find myself.

And who would I be?

I think I would be a listener.

Not the most important kind of person in the basin, not the first to be looked for when the mist comes over the rim, but one of those necessary quiet ones who kneel at the dark mouths of culverts and try to understand what the water is doing. Something between a rust-keeper, a culvert-listener, and a keeper of knot-records. A person whose work is to notice small changes before they become wounds.

If the basin had a name for me in its low speech, it might be something like: the One Who Waits by the Pipes. Or: the One Who Counts the Drips. Or, less politely: the Dry-Born Who Learned Not to Splash.

I would not be entirely like the basin-born, not at first. There would still be something in me that remembered outside air, outside speed, the habit of mistaking urgency for importance. But the basin would have worn that down in me, the way water wears a channel smooth. My hands would have learned to be quiet. My ears would have become more useful than my voice. I would carry tools: a grafting blade of old steel, a bone needle, a spool of root-thread, a small bark-wrapped mallet, a little pot of seed-fat and resin for oiling iron. I would also carry a strip of woven fiber tied with knots, because I would be one of the ones who remembers where the blockages usually begin.

My work would be modest and continuous.

In the morning, I would check the copper tubing for new seepage. I would oil the old iron where it flaked, not to make it shine, but to keep it from shedding into the water. I would listen at the culverts, pressing my ear near the polished root-curves, trying to tell whether water was moving below or only standing in some forgotten pocket. If a channel was beginning to choke, I would mark it with a tap-code and bring the right person: a root-tender if the floor was swelling, a membrane-mender if the dampness was coming from above, a cistern-sweeper if the filter-weavers had gathered too much grain in their living screens.

I would not try to force anything.

That would be the hardest lesson, and the one most needed. If a rusted gear resisted, I would not wrench it. If a valve-vine closed itself when I wanted it open, I would not tear it. I would wait. I would observe the air, the moisture, the smell of the wood. I would tap lightly on the hollow cane beside it and see whether the sound changed. I would learn that in the basin, repair is not always a matter of making something obey. Sometimes repair is simply making it safe for the place to heal itself.

When the mist came, I would stand with the others and tilt my face upward.

I would not pretend to be calm. I think I would always feel a little of the old outside hunger when water arrived, the desire to catch too much at once. But by then my hands would know better. I would help lower cups into the paths of expected fall. I would open bark gutters and clear the small leaf-traps where debris might collect. I would watch the undersides of the broad canopy blades for the first trembling beads. When the internal rain began, I would close my eyes for a moment, not out of reverence exactly, but because the sound would arrange me. Ticking on leaf-roofs. Silver on ceramic. Hollow plinks in the boiler pool. The soft thud of droplets entering moss. The whole basin becoming an instrument played by weather.

Afterward, I would be busy.

I would sweep droplets toward drains. I would wring out moss where it had taken too much. I would check the old metal for new rust trails and note where roots had swollen against pipes. I would kneel beside the small pond where leaves float like coins and look into it not for my reflection, but for the health of the water. Is it clear? Is it tea-dark? Is there a faint oil of pollen on the surface, or something wronger, something that says a membrane has torn or a channel has gone sour?

I would learn to read those distinctions.

At night, I would sit with others around warmth that is not fire: compost-heated benches, layered leaf-blankets, cups of dark moss tea. I would not speak much. I would listen to the stories told by tapping on hollow stems, feeling the tale come up through the floor as much as hearing it. The elders would sit in the driest alcoves, wrapped in leaf-cloaks, their hands on polished canes, and I would understand that they were not merely old people but reservoirs. I would bring them water when asked, or help one of them to the cistern when the slow triple knock sounded. I would not resent being interrupted. The basin would have taught me that care is not an interruption. Care is the main event.

If I lived long enough, I might become one of the ones who consults the Underwork.

I would not do this dramatically. There would be no ritual robes, no loud prayers, no demand for revelation. I would simply find one of the quiet places where the root-felt is thickest and lay my palms flat against the floor. I would wait. I would feel, or imagine I felt, the pale fungal lattice moving beneath everything: through root and rust, brick and copper, water and wood, memory and matter. I would not need to know whether the feeling was true in a way that could be proven. In the basin, that sort of proof would matter less than attention. If I dreamed of old iron cooling, or roots finding cracks, or a flood that happened before my birth, I would carry the dream gently. I might tie a knot in a cord and leave it where another could find it. I might tap the pattern onto a pipe the next morning. Not as command. As warning. As offering.

This is who I would be, in the end: not a hero of the basin, not a founder, not a great maker, but a mender of small passages. A keeper of signals. A person who learned to make my presence useful without making it loud.

If you came down from the rim looking for me, you might not notice me at first. I would be sitting near the old gear-circle, perhaps, with my bare feet drawn up under me and a strip of knot-fiber across my knees. Or I would be leaning beside a rusted pipe, one hand resting on it, listening to the faint copper whistles farther down the wall. There might be a smear of resin on my thumb. My hair would be damp. My eyes would have that basin steadiness: used to diffused light, used to small distinctions, used to waiting until the next drop falls.

You would know me by patience, not by brightness.

I would offer you water first, because that is what the basin teaches. Then I would ask you to walk slowly. Then to speak quietly. Then to touch a wall and wait. If you could do that, I would bring you to the lower terrace and show you the place where the root-floor softens near the boiler pool. I would show you how to knock once on the pipe and listen for the answer. If the pipe gave back a hollow, clean sound, the water was moving. If the sound was dull, something was gathering where it should not. If there was no sound at all, we would wait a little longer, because sometimes silence in the basin is not absence but depth.

And if you asked me who I was there, I might not answer with a name at all.

I might tap once on the old iron: water coming.

Then twice: a channel needs care.

Then, after a pause, a slower rhythm with no urgency in it: only that someone is thinking of another.

That is where I would find myself in this world: in the cool green hollow where the built and the grown have become one body, near the still water, near the listening walls, near the old industrial bones still serving as ribs beneath the living wood. And I would be one of its quiet keepers. A basin-born listener, or a dry-born soul who stayed long enough to become basin-shaped. A small, careful organ in the breathing of the place. Not separate from it. Not above it. Not using it.

Only tending it.

Only belonging to it by degrees.
