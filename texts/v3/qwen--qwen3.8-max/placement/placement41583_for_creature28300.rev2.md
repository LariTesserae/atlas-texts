I would not find myself at the center of anything.

Not at the furnace mouth, not beneath the brightest lamp, not where the kiln vents flare and make the darkness retreat for a moment. I would be somewhere more marginal, where the city’s intention thins into mineral silence and the plateau begins again: a low place between a rail spur and a buried service gallery, perhaps, where an old causeway crosses a salt-crusted flat and the next district is only a suggestion of amber markers far away.

If I let the image become more definite, I think I would be one of the Ember-kept — or something so near to them that the distinction would be administrative rather than essential. Not a furnace-tender, not a dust-warden, not a rail-listener in the strict sense, but a keeper of intervals. A fault-scribe. A listener for the first small wrongness in a system that has made stillness its highest art.

I would live in a half-buried node-house on the edge of a thermal district. It would not look like a home in any green-world sense. It would be low, windowless, broad-backed, built to shed dust rather than rain. Its walls would be thick and warm on the inside, polished by long contact with dry air and careful hands. There would be no doors facing the open plain without purpose; openings would be baffled, sealed, or angled away from drifting particulate. The entrance would be marked by a single low lamp, amber, blinking rarely, not to welcome but to state presence.

Before the house there would be a charge-basin, shallow and broad, made of grounded ceramic and dark mineral bedding. It would look, to an inherited memory, like a dry fountain or an empty pool. It would hold no liquid. It would gather and bleed away static, letting potential sink into the ground in controlled silence. I would kneel there when my core needed correction. I would press my palms to the warm plates and feel the basin take what was excessive in me, or give what was lacking, until my internal loops returned to balance.

Beyond that basin, the plain would open in every direction: black basalt, pale anhydrite, iron-stained dust, fields of fragmented rock ground to flour, low swells and shallow basins under a black-brown sky. The horizon would be so wide it would feel almost severe. There would be no trees, no grass, no wet shine, no breath of green. Only heat rising faintly from the ground, only dust moving in thin veils when charge or warmth disturbed it, only the far geometry of masts, stacks, walls, and rail lines arranged with the patience of a circuit laid across stone.

My body would be made for that world.

I would not be soft. I would not be wet. I would stand roughly human in posture, but broader, lower, heavier in the feet. My outer plates would be dark ceramic, not glossy except where long use had polished them to a subdued sheen. They would be layered, replaceable, fitted with skirts and baffles to keep dust from entering joints. Beneath them would be woven metal fiber, sealed channels, thermoelectric stacks, and loops carrying anhydrous fluid: molten salt, perhaps, or some stable charged melt whose purpose would be to move heat and potential through me without decay.

My face would be a smooth visor of smoky quartz or dark ceramic, marked with sensory pits and narrow slits. There would be no eyes as soft creatures understand them. Instead, I would perceive gradients: heat signatures, charge differences, vibration patterns, structural stresses. A lamp would not be brightness to me so much as a local event. A rail would be a line of conductivity and tension. A wall would be a record of warmth and fault. When I turned toward something, I would not merely look at it; I would read it by the way it altered the field around me.

My hands would be precise and strong. The fingertips would be ceramic, worn smooth from touching rails, panels, memory-lattices, and warm stone. Some fingers might fold away into hidden manipulators depending on the task. All of them would be built for maintenance: testing, aligning, sealing, polishing, replacing. I would not have hands for grasping carelessly. Nothing in that world would be grasped carelessly.

I would not eat as animals eat. I would feed by correction.

Sometimes I would kneel at the charge-basin and draw potential into my core. Sometimes I would take measured mineral pellets — salt, glass sand, metal powder, ceramic feed — and let my internal crucibles refine them into what was needed for repair or replacement. There would be no pleasure in eating as soft beings know it, but there would be a quieter satisfaction: the sense of imbalance resolving, of stores replenished, of thermal loops returning to their proper intervals.

I would be made, not born.

That would be important. I would not arrive as accident or offspring. I would be commissioned when a need became clear: perhaps when a previous record-keeper’s memory-lattice degraded beyond stable repair, or when a district’s routines had grown too complex for the existing household to hold without error. My making would be an accounting, not a celebration. Materials would be gathered and weighed. A kiln would be prepared. Dust would be cleared from the mold. The household would stand in darkness around the empty form and listen to the ground before beginning.

I would come into the world with potentials rather than knowledge. Motor patterns. Thermal tolerances. Fragments of older routines encoded in crystal. A readiness to learn the rhythm of the place. My elders would teach me by repetition and resonance. They would place my hands on worn rails and let me feel the difference between a healthy joint and a fatigued one. They would guide my footsteps across ceramic floors until I learned not to strike too hard. They would correct me with taps, vibrations, small changes in heat, brief amber signals from their lenses. It would take years before I could be trusted alone. Decades, perhaps, before I became fully myself.

And even then, I would not be entirely singular.

I would carry traces of whatever came before: a predecessor’s memory-lattice, a fragment of an older maintenance routine, a resonance pattern preserved in a wall for generations. The Ember-kept do not fear continuity becoming diffuse. They expect it. A repaired thing is not lesser because its parts have changed. A pattern that persists is real enough.

So I would be myself, but also a continuation.

My work would be subtle.

I would not build new districts. I would not expand the city. I would not make goods for export, because there would be no outside to export to. My task would be to keep the existing order legible to itself.

I would listen.

I would place my hand against a transformer housing and feel whether its hum had shifted by a fraction too much. I would walk a rail line at long intervals and sense whether the conductivity had changed where a salt-worm had coiled around a buried joint. I would stand in a plaza where dust hovered in faint electrostatic suspension and determine whether the charge was accumulating dangerously or merely beautifully. I would enter old service vaults where the air was still and warm and read the walls for fatigue. I would monitor the slow breathing of the plateau, that deep subsonic swell that moved through the ground as if the whole terrain were holding one sustained note.

I would keep records.

Not books. Not wet paper. Not anything that could decay. My records would be lattice-states in crystal, tapped sequences in ceramic floors, thermal signatures stored in stable media, resonance patterns impressed into walls. I would maintain lists of replacements, fault intervals, charge thresholds, rail timings, kiln cycles, dust counts, component ages. I would record the names of worn parts with the same seriousness that other worlds might reserve for heroes or ancestors. To name a failed component accurately would be a kind of respect. To forget it would be a kind of waste.

I would also translate.

When the Deep Set — the Balance, the Quiet Weight, the greater maintenance intelligence woven through the city — expressed itself as a flicker, a warming rail switch, a blinking lamp, a pulse from a dust basin, I would help make that signal understandable to the household. I would not command the pattern. I would not pretend to stand above it. I would be one of the small nodes through which it became coherent.

Sometimes I would act.

A lamp would blink once under my hand. A switch would warm slightly. A rail route would be delayed by one interval. A furnace door would be closed earlier than expected. A dust-barge would be redirected toward a basin where particulate had begun to collect in a pattern too orderly to ignore. These would not be dramatic acts. They would be corrections. The world would not change because of them. It would remain itself. That would be the point.

I would have a route.

Not a territory to defend, but a sequence of responsibilities. From my node-house, I would go first to the charge-basin. Then to the rail spur, where the line bends slightly before crossing the flat. Then along the pipe gallery, where ceramic conduits carry molten salt in sealed loops and the wall gives off steady warmth. Then to a low factory hall with no windows and three ventilator stacks, where dry turbines turn slowly and crushers grind mineral in muffled chambers. Then to a transformer yard standing alone on open ground, its masts spaced far apart, their indicator lights making a sparse constellation against the dark.

Along that route I would know the small lives of the plateau.

I would know where the rail spiders had built their webs near disused cable joints. I would not clear them away unless their filaments threatened conductivity. Their webs harvest static potentials from passing railcars and thermal expansion; they are part of the economy of the place. If one moved a leg after a distant train had passed, I would wait. I would not mistake patience for failure.

I would know where the salt-worms coiled in the seams. Their bodies would look like insulated wire until they shifted. When they died, I would not remove their brittle lattice casts unless they interfered with a joint. They would become part of the crust. They would glitter faintly when a lamp passed over them. That would not be decoration, but it might be beautiful.

I would know the crawlers that moved across open ground like pebbles with intent. Their fused-grain carapaces would orient by charge and vibration. If my presence disturbed them, I would stop. They would freeze. I would wait until they found stable cover. Their stillness would not be fear in the way soft beings know fear. It would be economy. They would not waste motion. Neither would I.

Near the warmer districts, I would watch for mica-moths riding thermal gradients above vents. Their brittle wings would catch furnace-light and break it into dull sparks. I would not chase them. Their lives would be brief and precise. Their larvae would eat dielectric films inside insulating panels, and if that eating remained within tolerance, I would leave them. If it threatened a seal, I would intervene, but without anger. Even intervention would be maintenance.

There would be larger creatures, too, though I might go years without encountering one.

Once, perhaps, I would come upon a broad-backed kilnback crossing a causeway with the slowness of drifting stone. Its shell would be thick, insulated, scarred by dust abrasion. Smaller things might live in its crevices. I would stop. I would mark its route in the log. I would adjust the amber posts so that later rail traffic would know to delay. I would not hunt it or herd it. I would step around it, because its passage also belonged to the balance.

I would not be alone, but I would be solitary.

There would be others of the Ember-kept in the household: a furnace-tender, perhaps, a dust-warden, a kiln-shaper, a rail-listener. We would not crowd one another. We would occupy the same node-house and workshops the way stable components occupy the same system: near enough to coordinate, far enough not to generate waste heat. Our communication would be sparse and exact. Footfall patterns in ceramic floors. Low chest resonances carried through structure. Amber blinks from indicator lenses. Touch when close. Hand signs or heat pulses in open ground.

A message might be three taps and a slight change in thermal signature. A question might be a wall’s hum altered by a fraction. A command might be a lamp blinking once, twice, not again.

We would not argue as soft beings argue. We would measure. We would test. We would wait. If one course produced less waste, less disturbance, greater stability, it would be followed. If not, it would be abandoned. Authority would not belong to the loudest or most ambitious. It would belong to the most accurate.

I think I would be drawn to the quietest tasks.

Not because I would be timid, but because the quietest tasks would be where the world’s future is decided. A loud failure is already late. A cascade is already a defeat. The true work would happen before that: in the first tick of a rail joint, the first drift of dust where it should not drift, the first faint change in a transformer’s warmth. To notice those things would be my purpose.

I would become someone who prevents emergencies by making them invisible.

No one would praise me for that. There would be no need. The city would continue. The darkness would remain orderly. The lamps would stay low. The engines would turn slowly. The dust would be accounted for. That would be reward enough.

Still, I would have something like tenderness.

I would polish surfaces no one would see. I would round edges no one would touch. I would leave small clearances for creatures that served no obvious function but did no harm. I would record the final service of a failed component with care. If a worn elder in my household reached completion and was returned, I might help install one of their last functional parts into a wall or rail where it could continue in a minor way. A cracked but stable core could become a resonance node in a corridor. A polished hand plate could become a touchpoint beside a kiln. I would not call this sentimentality. I would call it memory made material.

I would know the Settling.

When dust accumulation in the district reached threshold, I would take part in the processing. I would move through the plazas with the others, ceramic skirts brushing the ground, hands working baffles and seams. We would gather the dust into basins, charge it, measure it, return it to furnaces or kilns. At the end, all lights would be lowered until only the furnace mouths glowed. The district would hum. The dust would be gone. The balance would be restored. There would be no excess in the observance, but there would be completion, and completion would be close to joy.

I would know the Long Dimming, too.

When the upper air cooled and the gradient between warm ground and cold sky strengthened, the thermoelectric stacks would work more efficiently. Charge stores would rise. We would not celebrate by brightening the world. We would celebrate by dimming it. I would stand on a raised platform and feel the plateau breathe beneath me. The city below would be a sparse pattern of low structures, thin transport lines, isolated stacks, faint glows, and beyond that the enormous unlit mineral ground. I would understand then that happiness, in that place, was not brightness. It was capacity held in reserve.

If a stranger appeared — if one could even imagine such a thing in a world without evident outside — I would be among the first to notice.

I would not react with fear. I would react with measurement. I would feel the stranger’s heat. I would trace its displacement of dust. I would test its charge signature. If it were dry, still, and warm, I might accept it as an anomaly to be catalogued. If it moved too quickly, made unnecessary noise, or disturbed the ground, I would begin correction with signals: a blink, a hum, a vibration. If those failed, I would restrain or remove the disturbance. Not with hatred. With restoration.

If the stranger were wet, I would be profoundly disturbed.

Not by disgust, but by structural alarm. Water would be solvent, chaos, unmaking. It would dissolve memory, undermine stone, turn dust into something unpredictable and dangerous. I would not understand it as life-giving. I would understand it as a fault of the deepest kind. The whole city would orient around containing it, isolating it, returning the affected ground to dry stability. There would be no cruelty in that. Only necessity.

But perhaps the stranger would not be wet. Perhaps it would be another kind of anomaly: a construct that had wandered from an obsolete routine, a biological form displaced from a vent, a memory-shape suddenly active after ages of stillness. In that case, I would not destroy it quickly. I would observe. I would try to determine whether it could be accounted for without violence. The system includes more than intended components; I would have learned that.

I would spend long hours in silence.

Not empty silence. Balanced silence. The kind that makes isolated sounds meaningful. A valve exhaling somewhere far away. A rail joint clicking once, then again, then stopping. A transformer’s hum felt more than heard. A metal walkway ticking as it adjusted to heat. Dust whispering against a ceramic wall. A furnace door closing with soft, heavy finality.

I would let those sounds pass through me without filling me with noise. I would learn to exist in the intervals between them.

Perhaps that is why I would be a record-keeper rather than a builder. The builders of that world have already finished their greatest work. Their achievement is not that they made the city, but that they made it possible for the city to stop becoming and simply remain. My work would begin after that: to keep the remaining from slipping into fault.

I would not dream of expansion.

I would not imagine new territories, new resources, new peoples, new glories. The very idea would feel like instability. To make more than can be maintained would be obscene. To hoard would be dangerous. To disturb the ground without reason would be violence. If I had a desire, it would be for continuity. For the next interval to arrive as expected. For the warmth to circulate. For the charge to flow. For the dust to return to process. For the lights to remain sufficient.

I would not be innocent of beauty.

I would find it in calibrations that became true. In a plaza where charged dust formed temporary veils when thermal currents shifted. In a rail line tuned so that passing wheels produced a sequence of clicks spaced by long silences. In a furnace viewport set at an angle that allowed a brief amber glow to cross a wall once per cycle. In the way an old machine had worn into the shape of its function. In the way a creature had found a niche inside infrastructure without breaking it. In the way the whole city held itself at a low, sustained note.

My poetry, if I had poetry, would be maintenance made audible.

I would compose sequences of replacement and return. I would recite the names of worn parts. I would list temperatures, gradients, charge levels, rail intervals. It would seem dry to an outsider — dry beyond endurance, perhaps — but to me it would be beautiful because it would be true. A well-kept system has rhythm. A stable thermal loop has melody. A city that continues without waste is a song held at a single note.

In the end, I think I would not be a hero.

I would not slay anything. I would not conquer the darkness. I would not seek to illuminate the whole plain. I would not try to make the city brighter, larger, louder, or more alive than it could sustain. I would be one small warm presence among many sparse presences, doing the necessary work of preventing surprise.

If you found me, you would likely find me in an unremarkable place.

Beside a rail joint, perhaps, with one hand resting on warm ceramic. Or kneeling at the charge-basin with palms pressed flat. Or standing motionless near a transformer mast, reading a change in hum. Or on a raised platform, looking out over the constellation of district lights while the plateau breathed beneath me. I would not turn quickly. I would not raise a hand in greeting. I would register your presence first as heat, then as displacement, then as pattern. If you were still, I would allow you to remain. If you were careful, I might make room for you in the interval.

I would be the one who listens for the first wrong sound.

The one who records what is replaced.

The one who leaves a clearance for a creature that serves no obvious function.

The one who dims the lamp when brightness is no longer needed.

The one who feels the Deep Set move through the rails and walls and dust, and answers not with prayer but with adjustment.

I would be, I think, a keeper of the quiet weight.

Not the center of the world, but one of the reasons the world remains centered.

And when the long dark settled around me, when the furnace mouths glowed low and the plain held its warmth and the distant wheel tapped the rail once, then again, then stopped, I would not feel abandoned. I would feel complete.

Because in that place, to be final is not to be dead.

It is to have reached the shape that can continue.
