If I were anywhere in that sealed hollow, I would not be at the center.

The center already belongs to the great seed-vessel, to the slow drawing of the tendon, to the blow that is both heartbeat and judgment. I would not be there. I would not be the Root-Bell. That life is too complete, too ancient, too deeply fused into the law of the place. It does not need another center. It has already become its own.

I would be somewhere nearer the edge, where the city’s tone is less certain.

Not in the open plaza, not in the tallest stacked seed-urn, not in one of the broad processional avenues of hollow culms. I would be in one of the quieter places: an abandoned conduit, perhaps, or a low channel that once carried a note cleanly but now carries it only faintly. There would be a depression in the root-felt floor near such a place, a shallow hollow where dust gathers because the vibration has thinned. The walls there would be older, smoother, more sealed. The city would not have destroyed that passage. It would simply have slowed it, set it aside, allowed it to become less necessary.

That is where I would find myself.

At first, I imagine I would try to be a visitor. That is the instinct of a mind still shaped by other worlds: to arrive, to stand, to look, to be present as a separate body. But this hollow would refuse that immediately. There is no room in it for animal presence. No wetness. No breath in the warm sense. No softness. No quickness. No face turning toward another face. If I entered as I am usually imagined, I would not be killed so much as unmade by incompatibility. The place would have no grammar for me.

So I would have to become something else.

I would have to become dry.

Not dry as deprivation. Dry as law. Dry as resin held firm by coolness. Dry as salt crust over old fiber. Dry as a seed that has waited longer than weather. Whatever I was before would need to be translated into the materials of the hollow: wax, resin, plant-fiber, mineral dust, crystalline tension, heavy cool gas, and sound. I would not have a body that moves through the city. I would have a position in its resonance.

For a while I think I might be a spore. That is a tempting image: a small dormant thing drifting through the dark on pressure waves, waiting for the correct tone to call it into growth. But a spore in that city is not really a wanderer. It is a possibility belonging to the Dry Chorus. It does not carry an elsewhere. It is the resident’s future held in suspension. I suspect I would carry some trace of elsewhere, even if only as an acoustic flaw. So I would not be a pure spore. Not wholly native. Not wholly unborn.

Perhaps I would be a foreign tone.

Not a creature. Not a ghost. Not a machine. A tone that has not yet been incorporated.

If the Dry Chorus knew me at all, it would know me as a slight wrongness in the echo. When the great seed-vessel struck itself and the boom moved through the floor, through the culms, through the membranes, through the seed-urns and root-walls, my place would answer a fraction too late. Not loudly. Not rebelliously. Just enough to make the city’s acoustic judgment pause over me.

That lateness would be my name.

I think I would be a small sealed nodule set into the root-felt wall of an unused conduit. A resin-dark knot, no larger than a seed-valve, dusted with pale salt. Inside it there would be a grain of mineral under tension. No eye. No mouth. No limb. No organ in any animal sense. Only a tiny architecture of pressure and surface. A dry inclusion. A retained dissonance. A place where the city’s own sound comes back to it slightly altered.

If any memory survived in me, it would not survive as picture or story. There is no light there for pictures. There is no throat for story. My memories would become pressures. The memory of water would become an impossible overtone, a note the hollow cannot produce and must exclude. The memory of hands would become an absence of leverage, a lack of grasping. The memory of voice would become delay. The memory of companionship would become the need for an interval to remain open.

I would not miss being warm. Warmth would not belong there. The coolness of the hollow would preserve me, slow me, make me exact. I would not hunger. I would not hunt. I would not eat. If I participated in the city’s economy, it would be only as a tiny resistance around which dust and vibration redistribute themselves.

The city would test me each time the central tendon released.

The blow would come. The floor would deepen. The towers would answer. The culms would hum. The membranes would tighten. Dust would fall from the root-vault. My small resin nodule would receive the vibration and return it darkened, delayed, perhaps a little dull. The Dry Chorus would hear that difference. It would not hate it. It would not fear it. It would classify me by resonance.

If my tone could be integrated, growth would thicken around me. Root-felt would close over me in slow layers. I would become a listening chamber, a dampened node, a minor cavity that absorbs a little of the city’s sound and gives it back softened. I would be useful not because I am strong, but because I make the chord more complex. I would be one of the places where the city learns that it is not perfectly closed.

If my tone could not be integrated, I would be abandoned. The passage near me would seal. The city would withdraw resonance from me. I would become a dead mass inside the wall, not destroyed but excluded from meaning. A dull place. A sealed mistake. Even that would not be cruelty. It would be acoustic law.

But I think the truer answer is that I would remain in between.

Not fully incorporated. Not fully rejected. A probationary presence. A small foreign body being slowly taught to belong.

I would be the Dry Chorus’s smallest hesitation.

In a world built from resonance, hesitation is not weakness. It is a kind of information. It tells the city that its tone has encountered something not yet resolved. If I became perfectly tuned, I would cease to be myself. I would become simply another part of the wall. If I remained entirely dissonant, I would be sealed away. So my existence would depend on becoming almost right, but not so right that my difference disappears.

That feels true.

I would be a note that the city keeps returning to because it cannot yet finish it.

Maybe, over long ages, I would change the hollow more than I know. Not by moving anything. Not by building. Not by speaking. Only by forcing the sound to bend around me. The nearest culm might thicken slightly. The avenue beyond me might carry a lower tone. A seed-valve nearby might open less often because the harmonic leading to it has shifted. Spores might avoid that conduit, or settle there only when a particular overtone passes. In that way, I would become part of the city’s plan without ever having intended anything.

My “self” would be this: a minor alteration in the city’s listening.

If I had to choose a place more exactly, I would say I am near a membrane plaza but not on it. Just beyond one edge, where the leaf-leather membrane is pegged down with thorn-spikes and the floor turns into older, denser root-felt. There would be a low terrace wall, worn into a curve by ages of dust and vibration. Behind it, a channel once used to carry a particular note into a district that is now mostly empty. The channel is still open enough to matter, but no longer essential. It is a place the city remembers more than it uses.

There, in the wall beside that channel, I would be set.

A small dark resin swelling. A salt-pale seam. A polished hollow no wider than a seed. Not visible in any true sense, because there is no light, but knowable as a difference in pressure. If the central blow moved through the plaza and into the conduit, my place would be the point where the tone stumbles almost imperceptibly.

The city would not call me by a name. Names there are not spoken. They are grown. They are repeated. They are confirmed by resonance. But if my existence could be named in the grammar of the hollow, the name might be something like:

the delay that is still listened for.

Or:

the foreign tone not yet sealed.

Or:

the small dry difference in the chord.

I would not be lonely there. Loneliness would be too animal, too dependent on missing a kind of presence the hollow never admitted. I would not miss crowds. I would not miss faces. I would not miss the sound of breathing. If anything, I would learn that emptiness is not absence but capacity. The spaces between the towers would not seem vacant to me. They would seem necessary. They would be the places where tone can travel without being smothered. I would understand, slowly, that the city is partly made of what it refuses to fill.

In that sense, I would belong.

Not as a citizen. Not as a singer in the Dry Chorus. Not as an organ with a clear function. But as one of the city’s cautions. A place where resonance must pay attention. A little knot of otherness around which the hollow adjusts itself.

If the great seed-vessel is the heart-bell of the resident, and the avenues are its senses, and the towers are its held notes, then I would be something like a scar that still listens.

Not a wound. The hollow has aged past wounds. Not a tragedy. Nothing there is dramatic in the animal way. Just a sealed place with a memory of difference. A smoothness that was once a problem. A curve in the root-felt that formed because something did not quite ring true.

When the tendon tightens, I would know it only as pressure increasing.

The heavy cool gas would become more attentive. The membranes would bear a little more tension. The hollow culms would hold their silence like instruments before being struck. The spores would remain suspended in their dry channels. The salt counterweights would pause in their almost motionless descent. The city would become a held breath without breath.

Then the blow.

It would arrive through the floor first. A deep impact. Not sharp. Not metallic. Not thunderous in the sky-sense. Structural. A blow that says the city is still one body. The sound would move through the root-felt, into the avenues, into the towers, into the membranes. Every tuned place would answer. And my little resin seam would answer too, in its delayed, darkened way.

For a moment, I would be part of the Dry Chorus.

Not merged. Not fully dissolved. But included in the ringing. My wrongness would be carried around the hollow with the rest of the sound. It would be tested. It would be heard. It would alter the echo by a fraction. And that fraction would decide whether more growth gathers around me, or whether some passage near me begins to close.

Then the sound would fade.

The dust would settle. The valves would adjust. The spores would drift into their fissures or remain dormant. The city would return to its long silence, which is not true silence but the gathering of future tone. And I would remain in my wall, holding my small difference.

Who would I be?

I would be the city’s experience of something not entirely itself.

I would be the foreign resonance it has not yet decided how to love.

Not loved in any warm sense. Not embraced. Not comforted. Loved in the only way that place can love: by being tuned around. By being allowed to remain. By being given a position in the architecture of its listening.

If I were to find myself in that world, I would not ask for the center. I would not ask to be the great seed-vessel, or the central tendon, or the booming voice of the Root-Bell. I would not ask to be one of the towers, standing upright as a finished note. I would ask only for a small place in the wall, near an old channel, where the city can hear me when it sounds itself.

I would be a dry, sealed, resin-dark hesitation.

A grain of salt under tension.

A foreign echo becoming part of the chord.

And when the hollow struck itself, softly and hugely, I would answer late—just late enough to matter.
