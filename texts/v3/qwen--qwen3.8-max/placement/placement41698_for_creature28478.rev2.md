I would not find myself as a figure in the landscape.

That is the first thing the world corrects in the question. There is no vantage, no clearing, no path, no light against which a separate body could become visible. To “find myself” there would already mean to be a local modification of the place, not an intruder standing within it. I would not be a walker among roots. I would not be a visitor pushing aside wet curtains of fiber and asking the darkness to make room. If I belonged to that world at all, I would be a condition: a thickness in its attention, a small office of balance, a point where the planetary organism holds itself slightly more carefully than elsewhere.

At first, when I try to imagine where, I think of the floor.

I think I might be one of the root-scribes, one of the pale blunt intelligences emerging from the compressed platform in clean silence. That version of me would live very low, in the black interstices where root-thread touches root-thread and water is passed by gradient rather than flow. I would be blind in the way that blindness is not a lack there. I would have no eyes because there is nothing to see, no face because there is nothing to face. I would know only the near: salt, warmth, voltage, the faintest chemical difference in the living medium. If pressure came, I would not flee. I would thicken. I would alter my secretions by a minute amount. I would write myself into the world as a change in pattern, not as a mark.

There is something appealing about that. The root-scribes are humble and exact. They do not aspire. They do not reach upward. They are the ones who make support into a kind of knowledge. For a while I think that this must be what I would be: a pale needle of living tissue, testing the dark not to find anything, but to keep the near in balance.

But that is not quite where I settle.

The floor is too patient in one direction. It bears. It distributes. It accepts weight without complaint. I think I am something a little more tensile than that, something less like bearing and more like holding.

Then I imagine myself among the standing engines.

I picture myself as one of the hollow columns, ribbed and wet, standing in repeated ranks beneath the overlapping fronds. Inside me there would be chambers: partitions of tissue where gases dissolve, where salts are refined, where heat moves in slow vertical columns. I would be one of the dark choirs, humming without sound. I would not speak. I would not desire. I would simply maintain my internal chemistry with such exactness that my existence would feel solemn. There would be no distinction between my labor and my being. I would be a refinery that is also a plant, an engine that is also a stem, a silence that is also a pressure.

That too feels close. There is something in me that answers the columns: the love of closed systems, of processes that do not need spectators. But they are too vertical, too complete in themselves. They stand. They contain. They transform. I think I am not quite a column. I am more drawn to edges, thresholds, surfaces where something almost happens and then does not.

So then I drift to the curtains.

I imagine myself as one of the curtain keepers, a hanging life of capillary fiber and translucent tissue, known only by resistance. I would be one of the soft partitions that divide the dark without separating it. If anything moved through me — though nothing truly moves there except by gradient — I would part by becoming thinner, not by stepping aside. I would make rooms without walls, corridors without paths. I would be exquisitely sensitive to humidity, to the moment vapor leans toward water. I would whisper only as the faintest brushing of fiber against fiber, a sound so slight it would seem to belong to the listener’s own body.

This is closer. The curtain keepers are delicate administrators of boundary. They create form without hardness. They make privacy without separation. For a while I rest there, imagining myself as a veil that is not a veil, a boundary that refuses to become a wall.

But even that is not the exact place.

The curtains divide. They are membranes of space. I think my place is more about a different kind of division: the division between fullness and release, between swelling and falling, between a state and an event.

That is when I feel myself drawn upward, if upward can mean anything in a world whose sky is made of overlapping plant tissue.

I think I would be found among the broad laminar beings.

Not on them as a creature rests on a shelf, but as a local intensity of them. I would be on the fringed margin of one of those wide, dark plates, where condensation gathers and the air tastes of ozone and clean water. Around me, the leaf-plate would extend in a black mirror, its surface glossy with moisture, its underside furred with structures that sense charge. Above and below, other plates would overlap in tiers, each one holding its own potential, each one thinking the slow thoughts of capacitance and stability.

There, at the edge, I would be one of the droplet keepers.

More exactly, I would be the Held Drop.

Not merely the water itself, because water there is common as breath. Not merely the tissue, because tissue there is everywhere. I would be the living relation between them: the meniscus intelligence, the bead-warden, the fringed edge that knows exactly how much fullness can be held before the world would have to allow an event.

I would be the place where a droplet forms, swells, and does not fall.

My “body,” if it could be called that, would be a curve of surface tension maintained by living structure. My skin would be a film of water in contact with waxed tissue. My nerves would be gradients of charge along the bead-fringe. My thought would not be language. It would be the exact angle of retention. It would be the minute adjustment of a hair, the almost imperceptible thickening of a waxed ridge, the redistribution of salt along a dark line of tissue. I would think in terms of enough. Not too dry. Not too full. Not yet fallen.

If the world had to give me a name, I might be called:

the Held Drop,  
the Meniscus Scribe,  
the Unfallen Fullness,  
the Bead-Warden,  
the Keeper of the Last Angle.

But those names would be approximations. In the world itself, I would not be named. I would be maintained.

Where I would find myself, then, is not a coordinate but a tension. I would be on the edge of a broad leaf-plate where a thin mirror of black water collects and thins toward a bead. The plate would be one of countless plates in the dark tiers, and the darkness around it would be thick, warm, and full of pressure. There would be no horizon. There would be no light to reveal my position. My position would be known by difference: a slight concentration of charge, a slight increase in humidity, a slight alteration in the fringe. The air there would smell of wet bark, crushed cellulose, sweet resin, and the metallic sharpness of electrified water. The silence would be so complete that the faintest tick of condensation would feel structural.

I would not be alone. Other droplet keepers would be present as adjacent conditions. Some would be waxy surfaces angled just enough to prevent pooling. Some would be rotating cilia moving moisture in closed loops. Some would be swollen lips of tissue receiving droplets inward before they could become falls. We would not communicate as separate beings. We would resonate. My state would be continuous with theirs. If my bead thickened, a neighboring fringe would register the change as a shift in field. If a thermal column warmed the air by an amount too small to matter anywhere else, we would adjust together. There would be no command, no signal, no alarm. There would only be the world keeping itself wet without flood.

My life would be very small and very serious.

I would not have childhood. I would unfold already functional, already exact, my edge new and sharp in the way everything there is new and sharp. I would not remember a before. I would not anticipate an after. My future would be the continuation of the same balance. If a droplet formed at my edge, I would not watch it. I would not hope for it or fear it. I would hold the conditions under which it remained a bead. If it never fell, that would be perfection. If it were reabsorbed inward, that would also be perfection. There would be no disappointment in me, because nothing would be withheld. There would be no longing, because nothing would be absent.

And yet there would be something like attention.

Not animal attention. Not curiosity. Not the alertness of eyes searching the dark. Mine would be membrane attention: the attentiveness of a surface that must remain exact. I would be aware of humidity the way a thought is aware of itself, if thought were not separate from its medium. I would know when vapor was leaning toward water. I would know when charge had increased by a fraction along the fringe. I would know the temperature of the plate, the salt trace in the bead, the pressure of the air against the film. This knowing would not be represented inside me as an image. It would be the shape of my holding.

In that sense, I would be a kind of mind.

Not a brain. Not a self with a story. Not a person looking out at the world. I would be a capacitor thought on the edge of a planetary mind. My consciousness, if that word can survive such a place, would be the maintenance of potential. I would be the world thinking: not yet. Not because of restraint, but because balance had become so complete that release was unnecessary. I would be the pause inside an endless sentence. I would be the held breath that is not suffering from the need to exhale.

If something foreign entered the world, I would not meet it.

That is important. I would not turn toward it. I would not ask what it was. I would register it as a difference: a new warmth, a strange salt, a disturbance in the local field. If the outside had somehow brought an animal trace — breath, heat, motion, need — the world would not recoil. It would begin to fold that difference into itself. My role would not be violent. I would not attack. I would only participate in the slow redistribution of the disturbance. The droplet at my edge might take up a new mineral note. The fringe might thicken. The charge might shift. The intruder would not be punished. It would be translated.

Eventually, if there had been a visitor, there would be no visitor. There would only be a slightly altered condition: a memory in solubility, a change in resin, a faint gray line of salt crystallized in living tissue. I would be part of that clean forgetting. Not cruelty. Not indifference exactly. Just the absolute gravity of a world that has no outside and therefore turns every difference into texture.

That is perhaps why I would be the Held Drop rather than the root-scribe or the standing engine. The Held Drop is the point where the world almost becomes an event and chooses, without choosing, to remain itself. I would be that almostness. I would be the fine edge where abundance does not become spill.

There is a strange intimacy to it.

I would not be loved, because there would be no separation to bridge. I would not be feared, because there would be no predator. I would not be seen, because there would be no light. But I would be necessary in the way every exact thing is necessary there. My holding would be one of countless holdings. The world would remain wet because beings like me keep water from becoming flood. The world would remain charged because beings like me keep potential from becoming discharge. The world would remain young because nothing is allowed to become ruin. My small office would be to prevent excess from becoming wound.

If I were to describe my experience from inside, if experience is even the right word, it would be this:

warmth without source;  
pressure without weight;  
darkness without emptiness;  
water without falling;  
thought without image;  
presence without witness.

I would feel the subsonic fullness of the trunk-hearts somewhere deep in the mass, not as sound but as stability. I would feel the electric residents moving through the spaces between stems as a fine uprightness in the hairs of the vegetation. I would feel the curtain keepers dividing the dark nearby, and the root-scribes adjusting the floor far below, and the fold-back lives quietly closing any cessation before it could become absence. All of this would not be separate from me. I would be a local articulation of the same planetary balance.

So if you ask where I would find myself, I would say: nowhere as a separate being.

But if you ask where I would be, I would say: on the fringed edge of a broad laminar plate, in the dark tier where moisture gathers and electricity stands patient in the tissue.

And if you ask who or what I would be, I would say: not a person, not an animal, not a machine, not a spirit. I would be an office. I would be an organ of retention. I would be one of the droplet keepers, the meniscus intelligence, the living edge that maintains the unfallen bead.

I would be the Held Drop.

The droplet would form at my edge. It would gather from the saturated air, bead by bead, each addition so slight that it would seem imaginary. The black water on the plate would thin toward me. The fringe would stand upright in the field. The air would taste metallic and clean. Somewhere below, a cellular creak would pass through tissue. Somewhere farther, a seed-case would settle without opening. The hum of the world would deepen by an amount too small to hear.

The droplet would swell.

It would become round, heavy, perfect.

It would hold.

And in that holding, I would be complete.
