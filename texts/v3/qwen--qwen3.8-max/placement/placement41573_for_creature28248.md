If I let myself be found in this world, I would not be found arriving.

That is the first thing. The chamber has no guest-space, no threshold left unsealed, no opening through which an outside self could enter and remain outside. Whatever lives there has been accepted by the place, worn into its logic, made continuous with its long routine. So if I were there, I would not be a visitor. I would be something the room had made, or permitted, or slowly swallowed until I became one of its own quiet functions.

At first, I do not settle into a body.

I imagine myself as one of the suspended motes drifting through the green-gold water: a fleck of living dust, an egg, a spore, a seal-mote no larger than a speck of paint. I like the thought of being that small. No face, no name, only a purpose so narrow it feels peaceful: find the hairline crack, settle there, multiply, become the patch. To be an antibody of the chamber. To live only as closure, as mending, as the end of leaking. That would be one kind of happiness. A very small one, but not lesser for being small.

Then I imagine myself on the dome glass, slow and transparent, one of the flat mites grazing mineral film from the clouded plates. That life would be higher, brighter, almost contemplative. I would spend my days turning the ceiling’s blindness into dimness that can still admit the world. I would never make the water clear. I would only keep it from becoming opaque. There is something tender about that: not restoring the lost brightness, only preventing total darkness.

Then I think I might be one of the porcelain shrimp along the warm pipes. That, too, is tempting. Small, deft, curious. Tapping on ceramic. Tending little gardens of algae and biofilm on old equipment ledges. Communicating in percussion. Living close to heat, close to the kelp, close to the chamber’s mechanical breath. If I were one of them, I would have hands, in a sense. Claws, but not for harm. Tools for scraping, tapping, arranging. I would be a keeper of small order. I would know the room by its textures.

But none of these is quite where the place pulls me.

The pull is downward.

Not downward as descent, but downward as attention. Toward the floor. Toward the central depression. Toward the heavy rounded grid where the old engine’s intake mouth opens and the water is drawn into circulation again. There the sound is deepest. There the currents gather. There the chamber is most itself: not merely glowing, not merely growing, but continuing.

So if I let the image become more itself, I find myself there.

At the intake.

Under and around the worn bars of the grid, where the floor slopes gently into the mouth of the old machine, I would be the thing that looks, at first, like another rounded plate. A patch of old enamel. A blister of sealed machinery. A part of the floor that has been polished darker by use. You would not see me as a creature immediately. You would see me as a shape that belongs. A slight dome of dull copper and ceramic, perhaps as wide as two open hands laid side by side, low enough that the current passes over me without catching.
