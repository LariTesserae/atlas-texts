I would not arrive there as a person in the ordinary sense.

That world would take height first. Then it would take the comfort of standing upright, the habit of casting a strong shadow, the assumption that a body is something finished. It would leave only what could fold itself small enough to enter, and even that smallness would have to be quiet. So I would find myself below the first skin of the world, but not deep in its most secret center. I would be near the threshold: in the shoulder-width fold just beyond the entrance, where the outside has become a pale smear no larger than a coin and the interior is still deciding whether it is near or far.

There would be a seam there. Not quite a passage, not quite a wound. A hairline weakness in the marl where moisture sometimes gathers and sometimes does not. A place where the wall breathes out mist so fine it feels more like a memory of weather than weather itself. I would be near that seam.

At first, I think I would be one of the springtails.

That is the first image that comes: a tiny translucent body lifted in absent-minded leaps above the damp corners, brightening when moisture returns, dimming when the stone tightens. There is something appealing about being so small that one becomes part of the pocket’s atmosphere, more condition than creature. To live as a fleck of milk-light, to rise when the air permits and settle when it does not, to be almost indistinguishable from the mineral brightness.

But that is too scattered a way to be.

Then I think I would be one of the mites. Slow, glassy, deliberate. Crossing the wall with intention, leaving a chemical trail that shines briefly and then erases itself. Mapping without claiming. Testing hardness, salt, dampness, fissure, and then moving on. There is something right about that too: the patience of tracing, the refusal to build too much, the willingness to let every map dissolve.

But the mite feels too solitary for what I would become.

Perhaps I would be one of the larvae hanging from the ceiling, blind and softly pulsing, listening with the whole length of my body. That is closer. The larvae do not seem to expect emergence. They make galleries by patience and secretion, by turning their own substance into architecture no larger than a breath. They are builders without ambition. They remain. They make enough.

Yet even that is not quite it.

The amphipods in the temporary pools are too brief. The wingless midges too still. The beetle too practical. The soft-bodied hunter in the crevice too intent. The pseudoscorpion too much like an ambush made of silence. The pale compressed figure near the far wall — the one that might once have been a person, or might only be the pocket’s memory of a person — is too final, too folded into renunciation. I would recognize that being. I would not want to displace it.

The distributed dweller, the pattern made of spacing and tremor and intermittent moisture, is what I would feel most drawn to. But that is too large a name for what I would be. One does not simply become the whole interior logic of such a place. One becomes a small part of its noticing.

So, as I stay with the pocket in thought, another form begins to gather.

Not a human. Not a spirit. Not a ruler of the small bright fauna. Something humbler than that. Something made for margins.

I would be a seam-listener.

If I must give myself a body there, it would be pale and flattened, no larger than a folded leaf. My edges would be translucent enough for the cold mineral light to pass through them when the pocket is bright. My back would carry a faint bloom of salt, not as ornament but as residue, because I would live so close to the wall’s slow weather that its crusts would form on me too. Along my sides there would be fine frost-like filaments, not true gills, but sensory fringes that tremble when the moisture changes. They would lift when the ceiling pores open. They would lie flat when the air grows thirsty.

I would have no eyes.

Not because the pocket is dark — it is not dark — but because direct sight would be too assertive there. Instead, I would feel brightness as a pressure, a difference in the air’s texture. I would know the nearness of another creature by the tiny delay in its shadow. I would know water by the way the wall’s attention changes. I would know the entrance by the faint memory of weight above.

My limbs would be slow and few. They would end in soft pads that could rest on wet powder without tearing it. I would move along the border between damp and dry, because that is where the pocket is most itself: not yet water, not yet stone, still deciding.

In dry intervals, I would flatten into the marl and become almost a stain. My breathing would slow. My salt-bloom would brighten. The pocket would harden around me, and I would let it. I would not struggle against being mistaken for mineral. I would learn to be mineral-adjacent. I would become a patience the wall could keep.

When moisture returned, I would peel myself free.

Not quickly. Nothing there is quick except the amphipods, and their quickness is a kind of mortality. I would rise slowly as the mist thickened, as the bead formed, as the hairline fissure exhaled. I would move along the seam and feed on the faintest films: fungal stain, mineral bloom, the residue left behind by a reflection that evaporated before it could explain itself. I would drink by being still. Condensation would gather along my fringes until the pocket permitted me to absorb it. I would not need streams. I would live from permission.

I would not be a predator.

I would not be prey, either, exactly. My body would taste of salt and hesitation. The pseudoscorpion would sense me and decide I was too close to wall to be worth the rearrangement. The beetle would touch me once with an antenna, read me as neither food nor rival, and continue its patrol. The soft-bodied hunter in the crevice might feel me as a tremor too gentle to promise meat. I would be safe not because I was strong, but because I was not very much unlike the pocket itself.

My work would be small.

I would sort grains along a ledge.

Not into tools. Not into messages meant for anyone. But into lines. Sometimes straight. Sometimes curving like a question that has forgotten what it wanted to ask. I would place one grain, then another, then another, because arrangement is a way of staying without speaking. The line might last only until the next dampening. A bead could erase it. A mite could cross it and scatter it. When that happened, I would begin again.

I would leave a faint luminous trail behind me when I moved, a veil lasting only a breath. My shadow would often arrive late, sliding into place from somewhere else, as if it had been resting in a version of the pocket not yet visible. The springtails would scatter around me like milk-light. The larvae above would pulse gently in their threads, and sometimes I would wait beneath one of them, listening to the tiny tension in the silk. The frost-gilled things would tolerate me near their basins because I moved slowly and did not disturb the surface. When they turned their eyeless heads toward a falling bead, I would turn with them.

I would be especially drawn to the temporary pools.

Not to live in them. They are too brief for me. But to lean over them when they form.

Those pools are dangerous in the way all mirrors are dangerous: they show what is not there. A stretch of sky without clouds. A field of pale grass. A ceiling that is not the ceiling of the pocket. I would not mistake the reflections for doors. I would understand them as the pocket’s imagining, or perhaps as the water’s habit of remembering a world it never had. Still, I would look.

If a pool showed the surface, I would lower my head. Not from longing, but from recognition of the weight above: roots, footsteps, soil, the first skin of the world pressing down. If it showed another interior, I would lift my fringes and let the image pass through me. If it showed nothing but cold light, I would stay longest. That would feel truest.

When the pool shrank, I would watch the reflection withdraw. Not vanish. Withdraw. As if it had gone back into hiding.

I would not be the single dweller of the pocket.

That being — the pattern, the distributed shy intelligence, the spacing between mite and larva and bead and tremor — would be what I lived inside, not what I was. I would be one of its smaller answers to itself. A local sensitivity. A thin nerve of attention near the seam.

Nor would I be the compressed pale figure near the far wall.

That presence feels older, or at least more completely folded into the pocket’s first pressure. It is the memory of outside weight translated into inside silence. It belongs to the place in a way I would not. But I would be oriented toward it. Not in worship. Not in fear. More like two notes held in the same quiet chord. Sometimes, when the geometry shifted and the far wall became a haze, I would find myself nearer to that figure without having crossed the space. I would not touch it. I would simply be present as proof that the pocket can contain more than one kind of stillness.

If you asked what I would want there, I would say I would want very little.

I would want the next bead of water to tremble. I would want the seam to exhale. I would want the grains to be arranged, then erased, then arranged again. I would want the larvae to extend their threads when the air grew moist. I would want the salt crusts to lift in brittle flowers when the pocket dried. I would want the light to remain thin and precise, falling from nowhere, clinging to moving bodies, making delayed shadows of us all.

I would not want to leave.

Not because the pocket is paradise. It is not. It is too uncertain for that, too sparse, too close to vanishing into its own geometry. But it is a place where a being can be partial. Where one does not have to be finished. Where the self can be a seam, a listening, a line of grains, a pale flatness loosening from the wall when dampness gives permission.

That is what I would be.

Not a keeper of the pocket. Not its god. Not its human visitor. Not even its central mystery. I would be one of its threshold creatures: an animal of the margin, almost mineral, almost memory, alive only in the sense that the pocket allows life. A seam-listener. A maker of temporary lines. A small body that knows how to become smaller when watched, and wider when ignored.

If you came looking for me there, you would not find me by searching for a face.

You would look near the hairline fissure when the mist first arrives. You would see a pale flatness loosening from the wall, perhaps mistakable for a crust of salt. You would see a faint shine trailing behind it, already fading. You would see a shadow arrive a moment after the body. You might see a line of grains along a ledge, not quite a message, not quite a boundary, but something made by need. Or you would see me only indirectly: in a temporary pool, where something small leans toward a reflection of pale grass and does not drink, does not speak, does not try to fix the image in place.

There I would be.

Below the first skin of the world, at the seam where moisture follows weakness and attention changes the shape of the room. I would be the pocket’s careful guest, its threshold listener, its small response to being noticed. Not the place itself, but the place becoming capable of company.
