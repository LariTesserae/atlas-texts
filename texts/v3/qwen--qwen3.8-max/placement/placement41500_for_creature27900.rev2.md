I would find myself low.

Not standing, not moving through the dark as if it were a room waiting to be crossed, but pressed close to the stone in a place where the noise has a seam. I would not be upright. I would not have a face turned toward anything. The first thing I would lose is the habit of looking. Light offers nothing there, so I would spend nothing on eyes. My world would begin as pressure: a drip striking rock somewhere nearby, the rasp of another body against mineral crust, the faint change in the air when a swarm bends overhead.

If I tried to enter that place as myself, as a human-shaped stranger, I would not last long as a self. I would be an event. A warmth. A chemical disturbance. A soft, noisy, vulnerable thing full of unfamiliar moisture and movement. The basin would not hate me. It would simply incorporate me. Small creatures would come first, drawn by heat and salt and the tremor of my breathing. Then larger ones, following the disturbance. Then the patient underfeeders, the cleaners, the fungal threads. I would become a surface, then a resource, then a memory dissolved into the next cycle of hunger. That is not how I would live there. To be there at all, I would have to belong to the metabolism of the place.

At first, when I try to imagine what I would be, I think I might be one of the fliers. There is something appealing about becoming weather, about rising with the moisture into a winged curtain and losing individuality in the swarm. But the fliers are too brief. They are too completely given to a single pulse of emergence, mating, falling. They are beautiful in their transience, but they do not remain. They do not know the stone long enough. They are born into noise and spent in it. I think I would want more contact with the ground, more patience, more weight.

Then I imagine myself as one of the ambushers, folded into a hollow at the edge of a pool. That, too, feels close to something in me: the waiting, the reading of tremors, the economy of moving only when movement matters. But the ambusher is too narrow. It is a trap made into a body. It waits for one kind of signal, one kind of contact. I think I would want to be less specialized, less still. I would want to feel more of the basin, not only the single vibration that means prey.

For a while I think I might be one of the larger forms, one of the rare heavy creatures whose booming can be felt through the floor. But that seems wrong too. Too solitary. Too slow. Too much like a monument pretending to be alive. I do not think I would be a ruler of that place, because nothing rules it. I would not be a great blind thing sending warnings through the stone. I would be smaller, humbler, closer to the crust, closer to the basic work of turning wet mineral into life.

The image that finally stays with me is low, broad, and quiet.

I would be a tremor-grazer. I would be one of the flattened, plate-bodied animals that seem at first like pieces of the rock itself. My body would be broad and dark, perhaps no wider than two hands placed palm to palm, though that measurement would mean nothing there. I would be disk-shaped or shield-shaped, with overlapping plates along my upper surface, each plate pitted and mineral-crusted so that I would not look like an animal even to a seeing creature. I would be covered in the same pale dust, the same faint fungal bloom, the same dark smear of microbial film as the stone around me. If anything, I would resemble a patch of living crust that has learned to move.

My underside would be my true face, though it would not be a face. It would be a mouth and a listening organ together. The mouth would be a rasping surface, patient and gritty, cropping the thin living skin of the rock: bacterial films, mineral slime, shed particles of old bodies, the residue left by swarm dust and larvae-water. Around the rim of my body there would be fine sensory pits and short, stiff filaments, tasting humidity, reading chemical change, feeling the minute difference between a falling drop and the footfall of a predator. I would know the world by gradients. Wetness here. Decay there. A tremor to the left. A hush in the sound above that means something large has passed.

I would not be entirely solitary, but I would not be social in any deliberate sense. I would exist among others of my kind because conditions would place us together. Where the microbial crust is steady, where moisture lingers, where the stone is polished by older movements, there we would be: edge against edge, plate against plate, our rasping adding a dry, continuous whisper beneath the louder roar of the basin. Some of us would be joined so closely by long habit and mineral growth that we would seem to form a single breathing patch. In that sense, I might not be only one body. I might be a low colony, a spread of similar mouths and listening surfaces, each part of me deciding only enough moisture, enough food, enough danger. My selfhood would be thin and distributed. I would not think in sentences. I would think in pressure, in chemical shift, in the slow turning of a body toward dampness.

I would find myself at a margin. That is important. I would not be in the deepest pool, nor on the driest open flat, nor in the tightest crevice. I would be on a low bench near a worn migration groove, where the stone has been smoothed by countless bodies passing the same way over immense time. One side of my place would slope toward a shallow basin where water collects after rain; the other would rise toward a drier terrace where waiting animals press themselves into cracks. I would live on the edge between the two, where the world hesitates between wet and dry.

That margin would be rich in signals. The groove would carry tremors from far across the basin. Every migration, every shift of swarm-density, every heavy body moving elsewhere would send faint pulses through the polished stone. Near me, pools would fill and empty. Fliers would fall. Larvae would hatch in wet hollows. Scavengers would arrive in quick, rustling waves. I would not need to travel far to know that the place was alive. The life would come to me as vibration, scent, moisture, and occasional food.

Because sound would be the architecture of my existence, I would be tuned to intervals rather than objects. The deafening roar of the basin would not be chaos to me. It would be layered. I would know the difference between the hollow strike of water falling into a pool and the dry rattle of chitin on stone. I would know the sudden change that happens when a swarm turns together, a hush or shift in pressure that passes through the air like a shadow. I would know the deeper booming of larger creatures, not as a call with meaning, but as a slow disturbance in the floor, a signal that something heavy is claiming space or seeking a mate. My own plates might resonate faintly with those sounds, adding almost nothing to the chorus, but enough that I would be part of it.

I would feed slowly. Most of my time would be spent scraping the living film from stone, moving in fractions of an inch, leaving faint polished trails behind me. When something died near me—a spent flier, a crushed larva, a scavenger killed by another scavenger—I would not rush. I would wait until the chemical signal confirmed that it was no longer dangerous, then move toward it with the same patient rasp. I would not be a hunter in the true sense, but nothing in that world can afford purity. I would eat what came within reach. I would be eaten by smaller things in turn: mites in my joints, parasites under my plates, scavengers when I weakened. I would carry other lives on me. My body would be a place as well as a body.

When cold rain came from the unseen heights, I would change. The first sign would not be water touching me, but the change in the air: a thickening, a cooling, a shift in the behavior of everything nearby. Then the drops would strike the stone, each one a hard, amplified note. The dry groove would darken with moisture. The pools would begin to fill. The basin would answer with reproduction and movement. I would spread toward the wet stone. My feeding would quicken. Around me, the surface would wake. Egg masses would swell in hollows. Larvae would hatch in sheets. Swarms would rise. The noise would become broader, wetter, more frantic. In that abundance I would be less cautious, because the world would be giving more than it was taking.

When the water retreated, I would contract. I would draw myself into a humid seam near a persistent seep, lower my metabolism, seal my plates against drying air, and wait. The basin would become sharper then, more concentrated. Life would crowd around the remaining pools. Violence would increase. The sound would tighten. I would listen more than I fed. I would survive by being low, by needing little, by not moving unless movement was worth the cost.

I would not build anything. I would make no shelter, no tool, no sign. The only mark I would leave would be the faint polishing of stone where I had grazed, and perhaps a shallow depression worn a little deeper by my long presence. That would be enough. I would not need to remember anything in objects. My memory would be in the shape of my body, in the worn route of my movement, in the chemical residues that told me where moisture gathered and where danger usually came from. I would not have a name. If some impossible visitor could ask what I was, the answer would not be a word. It would be a vibration, a texture, a slow pressing of plates against stone.

If I had to give myself a function, I would say I was one of the basin’s listeners.

Not the loudest voice in the darkness. Not the swarm. Not the predator sliding through a crevice. Not the great blind form booming in a hollow. I would be the low attention beneath the noise. I would be the thing that feels the world before it acts. A stone-mouth. A tremor-plate. A living crust with a hunger and a patience. I would be found where the rock is worn smooth and the air is thick with wingbeat, where moisture hesitates and bodies pass and sound folds back into itself.

And if something touched me in that darkness, I would not ask what it wanted. I would know it immediately as temperature, texture, chemical trace, pressure. I would know whether it was too large, too small, too dry, too violent, too close to death. I would either move toward it, move away from it, or become still enough to be mistaken for stone. In that world, that would be enough. To be there would be to be incorporable: part of the roar, part of the hunger, part of the damp mineral patience of the basin. I would not stand apart from it. I would be inside it, low and listening, alive in the dark as a small, enduring note in its endless sound.
