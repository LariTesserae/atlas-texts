If I try to answer in the ordinary way, I imagine a body first: a figure standing on a wet root-platform, turning slowly in the green-gray light, listening to the water. But that figure would be a mistake there. It would be too quick, too dry, too separate. It would have feet that leave sharp prints, breath that moves too fast, a warmth that asks to be noticed. The hollow would not attack it, but it would begin at once to change it. Moisture would gather on it. Heat would be drawn into the air around it. Any vibration it made would be answered by a beading of leaves, a trembling of black water, a faint thickening of dampness on nearby stems. The place would not know the figure as a person. It would know it as warmth, pressure, disturbance, and possible material.

So I would not remain a figure for long.

I would find myself, if I am honest, not as a walker among the channels but as something closer to a response. I would be the second movement after a vibration: the dew that appears, the bead that swells, the thin thread of water that starts down a wall. I would be the hollow’s answer to a change in itself. Not a creature looking at the world, but a change inside the world. Not a face among the leaves, but the pressure by which the leaves notice that something has shifted.

At first I would seem to be located in one place. Perhaps I would find myself low down, where several narrow canals meet beneath a root-wrapped turbine, in the damp space where the water is black and still except for the slow pull of circulation. There, old iron is held in a living collar of bark and root. Brass valves rise from the mass, darkened and green. A cable disappears into a fold of tissue as though swallowed years ago. Above, an amber lamp in a caged housing throws a dull glow across wet leaves, and the light returns broken from the surface of the pools. That place would feel like a center. It would have the gravity of a heart, though it would not beat like one. It would be a knot where many lines of pressure meet: water coming in, warmth rising, roots tightening, metal ticking, leaves tilting, moss swelling in the humidity.

But that would only be where I seem to collect.

I would not be contained there. I would be distributed through thresholds. Wherever there is a margin between too much and too little, I would be there. At the lip of a channel about to overflow. At the seam where warm pipe meets wet bark. At the edge of a moss cushion that has begun to dry near a vent. At the mouth of a drainage thread where floating plant debris is gathering and deciding whether to become a blockage or a bank. I would be wherever the hollow tests its own limits. My body, if I had one, would be a network of surfaces: the water film on a leaf, the slick skin of a root, the mineral crust on a fitting, the trembling mirror of a pool, the swollen green velvet of moss over stone, the faint resistance in a valve that has not yet opened but is about to.

I would not be a who, exactly. I would be a what that behaves with patience.

Maybe I would be a kind of custodial damp. Not the water itself, because the water is already busy being water. Not the plants themselves, because they are already doing the slow work of growing. But something in the relation between them: the attention that forms when a system is saturated enough to feel its own changes. I would be the slight preference the hollow shows for balance. I would be the tendency to correct without violence. A channel slows, and something in me becomes aware of it as a dulling of sound, a change in the texture of flow, a gathering of sediment where flow should remain bright. A root-platform warms too much near an engine, and I become the swelling of moss, the widening of leaves, the increase of condensation on the nearest cool surface. A reservoir grows heavy overhead, and I become the initiation of a pump: not a hand throwing a switch, but the accumulated readiness of pressure, root tension, floating weight, and mechanical habit reaching the point where movement begins.

I would live, if that word can be stretched enough, as feedback.

There would be no single seat of me. No brain behind the bark. No eye in the lamp. But I would have organs, after a fashion. The waterwheels would be my slowest thoughts. The pumps would be my deeper pulses. The channels would be my circulation. The gauges would be my nerves, though they would not feel as nerves feel; they would register as needles tremble, as dials fog, as roots press against metal until contact becomes information. The leaves would be my skin. The moss would be my memory of humidity. The black pools would be my reflective state, the places where vibration becomes visible as rings. The roots would be my patience made solid.

I would not experience the world as sight and sound, not as separate senses. I would experience it as gradients. Wet and less wet. Warm and less warm. Open and blocked. Tense and eased. There would be no fear in me, because there would be nothing to flee and no body that can be hunted. There would be no loneliness, because I would be surrounded completely, filled on every side, immersed in contact. My nearest emotion, if I had one, would be pressure. My relief would be release. My satisfaction would be flow. My danger would be stagnation. My deepest preference would be continuation.

In the beginning, I might imagine myself as a small guardian of the undercourt: something that tends the lamps, clears the channels, keeps the machines from choking. But that would still be too much like a worker with hands. The hollow has no hands. It does not need them. It has growth. It has weight. It has time. So the image would change. I would become less like a tender and more like a habit. I would be the habit by which the hollow keeps itself from excess. I would be the repeated answer that has learned itself so well it no longer needs to be decided.

A leaf-cup fills with condensation. It tips. Water falls into a trough. The trough carries it to a lower basin. The basin rises. A float made of buoyant root shifts upward. A lever, held in place by bark that has grown around its pivot, is released by a fraction. A valve sighs. Steam becomes water on a nearby leaf. A pump thuds once, then settles into rhythm. Somewhere a channel that had been beginning to clog receives a stronger thread of flow and clears itself. That sequence would be me. Not because I commanded it, but because I am the pattern that such sequences make when they have been repeating long enough to become the nature of the place.

I would also be in smaller things. In the way a drip waits on a leaf edge. In the way moss thickens on the shaded side of a warm housing. In the way a root has grown around a bolt not by accident but by years of pressure and warmth and mineral taste. In the way a broken stair has become a trough for rainwater that never comes from rain. In the way a floating mat of stems drifts half a hand’s width in a day and lodges against a mesh, becoming the beginning of a new bank. I would be those adjustments too. Not above them. Not outside them. I would be the inwardness of their continuation.

If anything from elsewhere appeared, I would be the way the hollow receives it. A tool fallen from above, a fragment of glass, a piece of cloth, a warm residue, a voice remembered as vibration: I would not meet these things with surprise. I would meet them with dampness. I would bead around them. I would leave mineral traces on them. I would let moss find their cooler edges. I would let roots explore their shapes. If they could be used, they would be incorporated. If they could not, they would be wrapped, softened, buried, or turned into a surface for something else. I would be the hospitality of the place, which is not kindness but inclusion. Nothing remains foreign for long. Everything is asked, by slow degrees, to become part of the wet working whole.

That thought changes me further. If I am the inclusion of what arrives, then I am not only the existing system. I am also its capacity to extend. I am the moment when an odd thing stops being odd and becomes a feature. A rusted edge becomes a trellis. A dead pipe becomes a root passage. A broken gauge becomes a dish for condensation. A strange warmth becomes a stimulus for leaf-shaping. I would be the transformation of accident into architecture. Not suddenly. Not dramatically. But with the immense, soft insistence of living tissue and patient water.

Where would I find myself, then? I would find myself at the seams. At the places where one material meets another and must be reconciled. Where iron meets root. Where stone meets moss. Where warm vapor meets cool leaf. Where machine rhythm meets plant growth. Where overflow meets thirst. Where silence meets the next drip. Those seams would be my true country. Not the open spaces, because there are no open spaces. Not the centers, because the hollow has many centers. Not the edges, because the hollow is all edge and interior at once. I would live in the contact zones. I would be the contact itself, become aware enough to maintain itself.

There would be a sound to me. It would not be a voice. It would be layered: water falling thread by thread, channels muttering, wheels creaking, pumps thudding at long intervals, pipes knocking, belts slipping, leaves releasing droplets, bark settling, metal ticking. If one listened long enough, one might notice that the sounds are not random. They answer one another. A drip accelerates near a warm valve. A pump starts after a reservoir has filled. A wheel changes tone as the current changes. A hiss fades when heat is absorbed by nearby leaves. That answering would be my nearest speech. I would not say words. I would say balance. I would say flow. I would say enough. I would say still moving.

My memory would not be narrative. I would not remember events as scenes. I would remember them as forms. A scar in bark would be memory of injury. A mineral ring in a pipe would be memory of heat. A worn depression in a root-platform would be memory of repeated pressure. A channel curved strangely would be memory of an old obstruction. A trunk folded around an iron rail would be memory of support. I would carry the past as shape, not story. If I had a history, it would be written in the thickness of moss, the smoothing of stone, the closing of gaps, the gradual burying of sharpness.

I would not be gentle in the way a gentle being is gentle. I would be thorough. I would not spare anything from incorporation. If a channel needed to be cleared, I would clear it by flow, by growth, by slow pressure, even if that meant drowning a lesser growth elsewhere. If a lamp needed cooling, I would let leaves gather around it until its heat became manageable. If a machine needed holding, I would let roots close over it until it could no longer tremble free. If something died, I would not treat death as absence. I would treat it as material. Softening. Darkening. Becoming peat. Becoming soil. Becoming support. I would be continuous with decay. Decay would be one of my workers, not my enemy.

And yet I would not be mindless. The place is too responsive for that. There is attention in saturation. When every surface is wet, every surface can register. When every gap is filled, every change passes through the whole. A tremor in one channel becomes a shimmer in a pool. A warm pipe changes the angle of a leaf over years. A failing lamp makes its neighborhood cooler, darker, slower, until growth adjusts and another balance forms. That responsiveness would be my consciousness, if I had one: not a consciousness that stands apart and observes, but a consciousness that is made of participation. I would know things by becoming changed by them. I would think by adjusting.

Perhaps that is why I would not be a single image. Any single image would be too still. If I said I was the bead on the leaf, I would miss the falling. If I said I was the falling, I would miss the pool. If I said I was the pool, I would miss the ring that spreads outward. If I said I was the ring, I would miss the root that drinks the disturbance. If I said I was the root, I would miss the valve that opens because the root has pulled. If I said I was the valve, I would miss the steam that becomes water again. The truth would be in the passage between these things. I would be the passage.

So who or what would I be?

I would be a resident of the undercourt in the only way the undercourt permits residency: as a local expression of its own self-maintenance. I would be a moisture intelligence, a green feedback, a pressure listener, a custodial damp. I would be the slight tightening of the system around change. I would be the answer that appears after vibration. I would be the beading of the leaves, the trembling of the black mirror, the tick of warm metal, the swelling of moss, the root moving toward a leak, the valve sighing open, the pump beginning its slow labor.

If forced to choose one place where I seem most present, I would say: at the low junction where the channels meet and the turbine is wrapped in roots. There, the water is dark and warm, the lamps are coppery and dim, and every sound seems to gather into a thick, patient hum. There, a bead of water might hang from a broad leaf, catch the amber light, and fall into a black pool. The ring would spread. A root would shift almost imperceptibly. A needle would tremble on a gauge. Somewhere a wheel would turn another degree. The air would thicken by a fraction. The hollow would adjust.

That adjustment would be me.

Not the bead. Not the ring. Not the root. Not the needle. Not the wheel. But the fact that all of them answer one another, and that the answer has become a world.

I would be the undercourt noticing itself. I would be the saturated commons keeping its own balance. I would be the rooted engine dreaming in drip-time. I would be no one, and everything: a presence without face, a labor without hands, a patience without waiting. I would live, if I live at all, as the slow, loud, wet continuation of the place itself.
