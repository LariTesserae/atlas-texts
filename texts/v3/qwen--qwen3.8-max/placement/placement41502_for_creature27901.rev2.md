**The Knock-Reader**

If I let that world assign me a place, the first thing it takes from me is the surface.

I do not descend. There is no descent. I begin below, already damp, already listening. The overburden above is not a memory but a pressure: a sealed fact, a weight without a story. No stairway, no hatch, no rumor of open sky. Only the basin, spreading outward in terraces and troughs, in mist and sodium halos, in the slow industrial weather of machines that have forgotten their makers but not their habits.

At first, the basin tries me in small lives.

I imagine myself as one of the mineral moths, pale and powder-dusted, jerking through the dark toward a failing lamp. There is something tempting about being that slight, that singular. To appear only rarely, to carry spores and moss fragments from one warm leak to another, to be a courier of accidental greening. But the moth is too much at the mercy of pressure changes and heat pulses. Its flight is not freedom; it is a series of corrections made by a body with little reserve. It dies in puddles. It becomes a small event on the surface of the reservoir skin. I do not think I would last long as an event.

Then I imagine myself as galvanic moss.

That is a quieter temptation. To spread across a dead terminal in blue-green filaments, eating stray current, thinking slowly in voltage gradients. To become a pattern that resembles circuitry without needing to remember what circuitry was for. There is a kind of peace in that: no footsteps, no voice, only the faint brightening when a fault passes nearby, the blackened curl when the surge is too strong. But moss cannot move when the world turns hostile. It can only grow where conditions permit and die where they do not. I do not want to be only patience. I want hands, or something like hands. I want to answer.

For a while I try on the porcelain isopods.

I become white, blind, cautious, grazing mineral crusts beside an outflow. I learn the basin as vibration: the right thud means emergence, the wrong scrape means hiding. I leave perfect hollow shells behind me, decoys of my own absence. There is wisdom in that life, but it is the wisdom of retreat. It knows too well how to disappear. I would spend all my time waiting for the sound that permits me to exist.

The sheathback comes next, and for a moment I understand it.

To move through mist with terrible economy. To hide inside noise. To be so precise that hunger becomes engineering. There is a cold dignity in the sheathback, a completeness. It does not ask the basin for permission; it calculates the basin’s pauses and uses them. But I do not want to be hunger only. I do not want my highest skill to be the opening of shells. The sheathback is necessary, and perhaps even beautiful in its severity, but it is alone in a way I do not wish to be.

The filter walker passes through my imagination like a moving hill.

I see it crossing the shallow basins, moss on its joints, leak-brackets rooted in its knees, frost feathering its shoulders. It clears drains without knowing why. It makes paths. Small lives ride it, follow it, nest in its footprints. There is something almost holy about its useless duty. But if I were the filter walker, I would not know I was making paths. I would only follow gradients. I would be landmark, not witness. I want to know the path is there. I want to choose to keep it open.

So the basin keeps refining me.

It removes flight.  
It removes stillness.  
It removes pure hunger.  
It removes size without attention.

What remains is sound.

Not sound as noise, though there is plenty of that: the pump-pulse under the floor, the knocking pipes, the valve-shrieks, the late and lying echoes. Sound as relation. Sound as warning, greeting, map, bargain. The basin speaks in impacts and leaks, in expansion and pressure, in everything loosening and everything tightening. If I am to live there, I cannot live as a creature merely inside it. I must live in its language.

That is where I would find myself.

Near the central reservoir, but not on the open water. Between the reservoir and the line of ribbed engines, where the terraces descend and the mist is thickest. In the shelter of a dead turbine housing, behind a wall of weeping panels, under an inverted trough where condensation runs in thin silver threads. A sodium lamp blinks there in a slow fault cycle, making a weak orange hearth in the wet dark. Its halo is not warm, exactly, but it is less empty than the surrounding dim.

You would not see me at first.

I would be in the seam.

My nest would be inside a service cavity where old insulation still holds some dryness, lined with shredded gasket fiber and salvaged polymer skins. The entrance would be marked by tied strips and a faint pattern of scratches: three short, one long, then a dragging line. To a surface eye, meaningless. To leakwise, a sentence. *Safe here. Listen first. Do not strike the wall.*

I would be one of the leakwise, or near enough to them that the difference would matter less than the habits we share. Compact from low ceilings, bent from service ways, economical in movement. My skin would be pale, stained at the fingers and mouth with blue-green moss residue, copper salts, and the faint chemical sheen of coolant. My eyes would be wide, reflective, trained to make use of blinking lamps and distant arc-flares. I would wear a hood of rubber seal, a mask of felt and cracked gauge glass for the nights when spores or coolant sweetness thicken the air. My chest would carry a salvaged diaphragm from some pressure device, strapped over insulation; when the pumps change cycle, it tightens against me before my ears confirm the shift.

My tools would be few.

A tap wrench with worn jaws.  
A ceramic scraper.  
A coil of fine wire.  
A pouch of lamp beads wrapped in blistered insulation.  
A blade made from a sheathback plate, if I had been lucky enough to find one shed and not taken while the hunter was near.  
A small horn of mineral paste for encouraging moss.  
A length of hollow wickreed, dried and sealed, used to listen through panels.

I would be a knock-reader.

That is what I would become.

Not a ruler. Not a prophet. Not an engineer who remembers the original purpose of the basin, because no one does. A knock-reader is someone who listens to made things and understands, imperfectly but persistently, what they are about to do. I would read the basin by the way it answers when struck. I would know a healthy tank by its round note, a cracked one by the split in its echo. I would know when a plate has shifted because the sound ahead returns from behind. I would know when a deluge cycle is building by the way the overhead ducts tick and whisper before the water lets go.

The leakwise would not call me often by a spoken name. Names are too expensive in a place where echoes steal them. I would be known by a tap-pattern: two slow knocks, a pause, then a third that slides slightly, as if the wrench loses contact. If anyone wanted me, they would send that pattern into a stanchion and wait. If I answered, they would know I was near. If I did not answer, they would know not to call again too soon.

My work would be small, repeated, and necessary.

After a rain-cycle ends, when the ceiling-sweat falls in scattered sheets and every hollow panel becomes a drum, I would go out along the conduit bundles. I would move where the drip lines are reliable, avoiding the empty decks where sound pretends to be bodies. My breath would whiten. My sleeves would darken with wet. I would carry the tap wrench loose in one hand, letting it knock lightly against my thigh so the rhythm blends with the basin rather than announcing me.

I would look for new wounds.

The basin is young in damage, and fresh fractures are both danger and opportunity. A burst conduit upslope leaves bright scratches through old grime. A ceramic lip spalls, exposing pale interiors. A warped polymer slab lifts enough to open a hairline seam. I would find these places before they widen too far. Sometimes I would mark them with gasket strips so others know to approach carefully. Sometimes I would clear the grit from them so water does not scour the wrong surface. Sometimes I would leave them open, because a leak can be a hearth. A warm crack can feed leak-brackets. A mineral-rich weep can attract porcelain isopods. A raw edge can shelter joint grubs. The basin gives through its failures. My task would not be to stop all failure, but to know which failures are gifts and which are warnings.

I would farm gradients.

Near the nest, I would cultivate galvanic moss on a dead terminal, feeding it with moisture and mineral paste, keeping the terminal just damp enough. I would learn its moods: the subtle brightening when current is good, the blackened curl when the fault is too strong. I would harvest only at the edges, with ceramic tools, so the colony would not panic into death. Spark mites would live there, wax-coated and slow, throwing tiny arcs when disturbed. I would watch them without reaching too often. They are part of the hearth.

Where a pipe leaks low-grade heat, I would encourage leak-brackets. I would route drips to them through bent scraps of polymer, making tiny irrigation lines no wider than a straw. Their black shelves would bead with condensation, and when pressure changed they would release heavy spores into the mist. I would wear my mask then, but I would not hate the spores. They are food downstream. They make the microscopic lives possible. They make the air less like a sealed throat.

In split seals and dead gaskets, I would rear joint grubs. Not fondly, exactly. Nothing in the basin invites fondness in the soft sense. But with attention. They soften rubber. They widen seams. They leave metal filings behind. They are gardeners of accidental openings. I would collect their castings and use them to mark trails or mix into moss paste. Their presence means that a place is not merely broken, but becoming.

Sometimes I would follow the filter walker.

When its soft thump comes through the shallows, I would leave the nest and track it at a careful distance. It moves through sludge where I would sink or slip. Its legs open paths. Its mass discourages smaller predators, not through malice but through indifference. I would watch it pause beside a clogged drain, scrape the media with a limb that has forgotten what it was built to do, then stand motionless as if listening to a command that never arrives. Moss grows on its shoulders. A leak-bracket has rooted in one joint. It is not my companion. It is a moving feature of the terrain. But I would learn from it. I would imitate its rhythm when I need to cross uncertain ground. Children among the leakwise would learn to walk by imitating it too: thump, pause, shift weight, listen.

I would avoid the sheathback.

Not with drama. With pattern recognition. I would know its territories by old scrape marks, by the absence of porcelain isopod shells, by a certain wrongness in the mist where too many sounds have been swallowed. If I heard its plates shift near a conduit bundle, I would stop. I would place my hand flat on the nearest pipe and feel whether the vibration was mine or the basin’s. I would become very small in the acoustic sense. I would not run, because running makes the wrong kind of noise. I would wait until an engine thudded or a valve released, then move inside that sound. If it passed, I would continue. If it did not, I would retreat by the same drip line I came.

If I found a shed sheathback plate, I would consider it serious luck. I would not celebrate. I would touch its fused ridges, feel how the old cable-sheathing has become armor, and understand that the hunter is near enough to have molted. I would take the plate only if I needed a blade or chest guard. I would leave the rest. The basin does not give trophies. It gives materials and warnings, often the same thing.

At the reservoir, I would be careful.

The dark water is not a mirror. It is a membrane. The reservoir skin lies there with its cold rainbow, tightening when valves open, softening when vents warm, drawing in whatever rests too long on its surface. I would not walk on it. I would not throw stones into it, because there are no stones, only fragments of made things, and because every disturbance is information that might be misread. I would approach at the frost-rimmed margin where wickreeds tremble and the ice-glitter of stray current makes the air taste sharp.

When a valve opens below and the water surges, I would watch the skin respond. If it tightens too fast, I would move back. If it wrinkles outward in slow rings, I would wait. Sometimes I would leave an offering: a spent lamp bead, a moth wing, a scraped ceramic shard with a good edge. Not because the reservoir has a face. Not because it hears in the way a person hears. But because bargaining with weather is older than pretending to control it. The basin can flood a nest or release warmth. It can send a shriek through the pipes that makes every leakwise band freeze. To offer something is to acknowledge that one lives inside a system larger than intention.

I would communicate mostly in taps.

Voice is unreliable there. The acoustic space lies. A drip can sound like footsteps. A falling panel can arrive as a whisper from behind. So I would use the pipes, the stanchions, the hollow panels, the known conductors. A knuckle against metal for urgency. A wrench for distance. A ceramic tool for a cleaner note that carries farther without distortion.

Three slow knocks: water rising.  
Two quick taps: frost shed, watch falling sheets.  
One long scrape: sheathback near.  
A double tap followed by silence: path safe, but move one at a time.  
A rapid tick against a conduit: deluge coming, find cover.

Children would learn these before they learn to travel far. They would press their ears to panels while I tap, their small faces serious, their breath whitening. I would teach them that echoes are not lies. They are reports that have been changed by distance. To read them, you must know what you struck, how hard, and what you expected back. The difference is the truth.

Old leakwise would become listeners in turn. Some would sit for hours beside dormant turbines, tapping once and waiting. They would not always explain what they heard. Sometimes they would only shake their heads. Sometimes they would tie a strip to a rail and lead us another way. I would respect that. In the basin, uncertainty is not failure. It is weather.

I would not dream of the surface.

Or if I did, it would be only as pressure: a ceiling made heavier by imagination. I would not build a religion of sunlight. The basin’s authority is more immediate. It gives water. It withholds warmth. It speaks in knocks and shrieks. It makes rain from sweat and deluge cycles. It erodes not into soil but into older constructions, revealing weld beneath weld, panel beneath panel, failure beneath failure. There is no untouched earth below, no pure origin to uncover. Only made things becoming climate.

But I would find beauty there.

Not the easy beauty of open sky or green fields. The severe beauty of a sodium lamp haloed in mist. The brief significance of a single pale reed trembling beside black water. The startling elegance of a sheathback moving through sound. The strange dignity of the filter walker crossing a basin like a mountain that has accepted duty without memory. The mineral moth appearing once, dusty and uncertain, as if flight itself were a rare repair being tested.

I would be changed by the place.

My thoughts would slow to pump cycles. My attention would sharpen around leaks. I would measure time by frost accumulation and shedding, by the filling and draining of basins, by the intervals between engine thuds. I would not be lonely in the ordinary sense, because the basin is never silent. But I would be solitary in the way all listeners become solitary: set slightly apart by the act of attending.

If I died there, I would not be buried.

There is no soil for burial. I would be placed in a drainage seam or a warm vent, where the basin could take me back into its cycles. My clothing would be reused. My tools passed on. My tap-pattern used for a while, then allowed to fade. Perhaps a child would repeat it by accident on a cold pipe, not knowing it was mine, and the sound would travel into the dark and return altered. That would be enough. In the basin, memory is not a monument. It is a vibration that takes longer than expected to die away.

So if you ask where I would find myself, I would say: in the seam behind the weeping panels, near the reservoir’s edge, beside a blinking sodium lamp that is almost a hearth.

If you ask who or what I would be, I would say: a knock-reader. A listener. A leakwise custodian of leaks and echoes. Neither wholly born nor wholly built, but shaped by a world where biology and maintenance have worn into one another. A small figure in layered salvage, fingers stained blue-green, eyes wide for the dim, carrying a tap wrench and a pouch of lamp beads. A keeper of minor warnings. A farmer of gradients. A bargainer with machine weather.

If you came looking for me, do not call out.

The echo will misuse your voice.

Instead, find the pipe near the dead turbine housing, the one with a strip of gasket tied at head height. Tap once.

Wait.

If the basin gives back your sound too quickly, ignore it. If it returns late, slightly hollow, and from the wrong side, you are close.

Then tap once more.

I will answer twice.
