I would not come down from the salt-field as a visitor.

I would be already made for the below.

If this world were allowed to choose my shape, it would first take away everything that glistens. No wet eyes. No sweating skin. No mouth full of water. It would leave a matte body, cool and efficient, with a fine wax-down along the arms and jaw, and salt blooming faintly at the joints. My breath would be almost invisible. My voice would begin as a low click in the throat before it became speech.

At first, if I let the image move without fixing it, I might be one of the smaller lives.

I might be a duct-mite in the ventilation shafts, flat-bodied and patient, cleaning spore-dust from the ceramic linings, living in the warm dark where old engines shed their heat. I might be one of the pale-winged things that ride the thermal columns between platforms, drifting in sparse commuter lines through ordinary noon-white light, my broad wings catching the slow updrafts beneath the salt lenses. I might be one of the ambush predators at a root-junction, salt-crusted and still as a rail, waiting without boredom for the small dry snap of opportunity.

But if the place continues to refine me, if it lets what is most fitting emerge, then I would not remain only an animal.

I would become one of the quiet people.

And not just any of them.

I would find myself at the edge of the country, where the floor is not yet certain.

Not in the oldest wards, where the trunk-terraces have been settled for generations and the streets have thickened into stable leaf-plate. Not in the dense engine-houses where the flywheels turn in their muffled chambers, or in the high galleries near the brightest light shafts. I would be drawn outward and downward, into the pre-formation districts: the places where debris collects, where root-scaffolds descend into open void, where compressed leaves and broken stems and mineralized fiber are only beginning to bind into something that may one day carry weight.

I would live where the world is still being assembled.

You would find me in a ward built around a half-grown lattice, perhaps in the lower reaches of a town called something practical and textured: Comb Terrace, or the Third Lens, or the Ward of the Descending Roots. The settlement would not be crowded. A few platforms. A few lashed walkways. A drying rack. A seed-silo. A small engine-house with a dry-air piston that ticks as it cools. Beyond that, open air. Cool void. Light columns standing pale through drifting spore-dust.

I would be a floor-listener. A root-guide. A tender of unfinished ground.

Not a builder in the surface sense. Not someone who imposes shape upon inert matter. In that buried country, building is persuasion. It is grafting, training, encouraging, starving, pruning, waiting. So I would be something between a gardener, an engineer, and a slow civic sense organ. My work would be to help growth decide where to bear weight.

My body would be suited to it.

Slender, light, balanced. Bones like dense fiber or hardened lacquer. Palms slightly felted, sensitive enough to read the grain of a railing or the tension in a living stem. Soles soft and gripping, made for springy surfaces and quiet passage. My eyes would be shaded by translucent inner lids against the dust, and my wax-down would rise when the air grew charged before a dry storm. I would smell of warm wax, salt dust, crushed seed-husks, and the faint metallic prickle of static.

My clothing would be layered but spare: woven fiber against dust, thin lacquered plates over shoulders and knees, a bark-cloth hood for working beneath shedding crowns. Copper hooks at my belt. Resin buttons. A spool of trained root-fiber. A small ceramic chime, used rarely. A stylus for marking bark-tablets. A knife that is more scraper than blade. Nothing that shines too much. Nothing that rattles.

My tools would be quiet.

A copper probe for testing the density of fiber banks. A resin-warmed needle for encouraging grafts. A set of polished bone or ceramic picks for loosening a root-tip without alarming it. Small weighted tags to mark zones that must not be crossed until the floor has bonded. A mirror shard for redirecting a sliver of daylight into a crevice where new growth is needed. A comb of oiled fiber to discharge static from my sleeves before I touch a living wall.

I would work by listening.

In the morning, or what passes for morning when the upper salt lenses warm and the light shafts begin to stir the air, I would climb out from my quarters and place my hand against the nearest support trunk. Not to greet it in a sentimental way. To read it. The long creak under load. The faint shudder of a crown adjusting to heat. The difference between a healthy settling and a dangerous strain. I would learn which sounds mean patience and which mean warning.

Then I would cross the walkways to the unfinished edge.

There, the floor would lie in banks and terraces of compressed leaf-fall, seed-cases, shredded bark, and mineral fiber. Some of it would be firm enough to walk on. Some would still be soft. Some would be only a drift, no more substantial than accumulated accident. Root-tips from above would hang into the void nearby, feeling for purchase. I would examine them. I would see where they hesitate, where they curl back, where they need a little scaffolding, a little chemical signal, a little time.

Part of my work would be to lay fiber in the right patterns. Not random. Not decorative. I would place it where airflow slows, where debris naturally gathers, where the next platform wants to thicken. I would bind loose stems, encourage root contact, and mark places where the weight of people or machines must not yet be allowed. I would feed streets in the literal sense: laying nutrient husks, mineral powders, oil-cakes, and salt compounds where they would help growth consolidate.

Some days I would be a pruner.

A bridge might have grown too eagerly. A wall might have thickened in a way that blocked a ventilation duct. A root-railing might have begun to curl inward, closing a path that should remain open. I would not simply cut. Cutting is too blunt, too final, and often wasteful. I would negotiate. I would redirect. I would starve one branch of attention and encourage another. I would use scent, heat, resin barriers, tension, light. If a structure had to be removed, I would do it carefully, with fire only as a last measure, because fire in a waxed and fibrous world is not a tool but a rumor of catastrophe.

Some days I would be a charge-tender.

The air in the buried country can build static until the edges of the leaves seem to prick. During those times I would move among the combs and cable paths, listening for the faint crackle that means discharge is near. I would ground myself with copper clips. I would brush the fiber leaves in the proper direction, ease the thermal winds through the ducts, and watch for the pale insects that gather motionless near the combs when the air changes. They would be useful indicators. If they clustered too thickly in one place, I would know something in the field had shifted.

I would not be alone, but I would not be surrounded.

The settlements are scattered, and the people value distance. I might share my ward with a few others: a resin-mason, a light-shaft keeper, a herder who follows the bark-grazers, a child learning to read creaks. We would speak softly. Much of our communication would happen through taps on railings, signs, sleeve-rustles, and the precise arrangement of tools. A spoken sentence would be short, low, and considered. A shout would be almost unthinkable.

If I needed to cross a long gap, I might take a cable tramway or ride a thermal glider from a platform edge. The glider would be made of pressed leaf-plate and membrane-thin bark, light enough to carry on the updraft beneath a light shaft. Crossing would not feel like flying in a triumphant sense. It would feel like being accepted by the air. Below, the open void. Above, the root-ceiling. Around, the pale pillars of daylight and the drifting spore-haze. Far off, a line of fauna moving along a root-bridge, silent as a sentence being written across the distance.

The bark-grazers would pass near my ward in slow processions. I would know them by the soft brushing of their bodies against trunk surfaces, the dry rasp of their mouths at old bark-plates. Some would be semi-tended by the herders, but not fenced. They would move according to the needs of the plants themselves. If a trunk shed its outer skin, the grazers would gather to crop the loose plates and clean the surface. I would let them work. Their feeding is useful. Their quiet is useful. Even their slow animal presence is useful, because it reminds the town that the great structures are not machines but living civic organisms.

There would be predators nearby, too. I would know where they wait.

A long-legged salt-crusted shape at a root-junction. A shadow under a bridge. A stillness too perfect among the cable fittings. I would not hate them. They are part of the motion of the place. I would mark their territories with small signs so that inexperienced climbers do not startle them. In a world where sound carries too far, a startled predator is a foolish risk. Better to know its patience, to move around it, to respect the fact that it too has a function in the quiet economy.

My home would be small.

Not a house built upon the world, but a cavity negotiated within it. Perhaps a hollow bole in a mature trunk, lined with lacquered root panels until the walls shine like dark horn. A bed of felted leaf-fiber, dry and slightly springy. Racks for tools. A few sealed jars of seed oil, resin, salt-glass beads, and powdered mineral compounds. A small mirror-lens angled to borrow daylight from the shaft outside. A bark-tablet with notations: load readings, creak patterns, root movements, charge levels, zones closed, zones opened.

The room would smell of wax, crushed husks, warm metal, and old resin.

At night, or the dark interval when the light shafts are shuttered and the thermal tides slow, I would sit near the wall and listen. Not for intruders. For the place itself. The tick of cooling machinery. The whisper of leaf-plates adjusting. The deep, slow creak of a trunk settling into its own age. The faint rush of spore-haze moving through a duct. The almost inaudible abrasion of a beetle in the floor felt below.

I would learn to think in the categories of that country.

Texture before appearance. Tension before beauty. Grain before intention. Load before desire. Light-angle before mood. Charge before emotion. Dryness before anything.

I would have many words for the state of a surface: waxed, fibrous, brittle, springy, salt-bloomed, resin-sealed, lacquered, shedding, newly bound, overgrown, starved, charged, settled. I would have fewer words for wetness, and most of them would feel strange in my mouth, as if borrowed from a myth about dissolution.

Rain would not be a comfort. It would be a story about collapse.

If I dreamed, I would not dream of rivers. I would dream of roots finding support. Of a floor thickening enough to carry a new street. Of a bridge that no longer trembles when crossed. Of a static storm passing without damage. Of a light shaft opening cleanly after maintenance. Of a town expanding by one careful graft.

I would be, in other words, a keeper of becoming.

That is who I would be.

Not a hero. Not an exile. Not a wanderer in a spectacular underworld. Something more modest and more necessary: a person-shaped attention within a living infrastructure. A quiet worker at the seam between accident and intention. A being whose hands are needed because the world is not finished.

If you came looking for me, you would not find me at the center of things.

You would find me where the next floor is being made.

Perhaps on a narrow walkway lashed to a young trunk, my fingers resting on a living railing. Perhaps kneeling at the edge of a fiber bank, testing whether it can bear another season of traffic. Perhaps standing in a column of ordinary daylight, watching spore-dust rise in the thermal stir, while a root-scaffold descends slowly beside me like a question asking where it should rest.

You would hear me before you saw me, but only if you were listening properly.

Not a voice. Not a footfall.

A soft tap-tap-tap on a root-rail.

A pause.

Then another tap, answered from farther along the trunk by someone else, or something else, or the settling of the world itself.

I would be the one who waits for the answer.

The one who helps the buried country hold while it becomes.
