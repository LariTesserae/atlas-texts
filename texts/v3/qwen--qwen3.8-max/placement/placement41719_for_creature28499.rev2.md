I would not find myself standing.

The Condensing Court would refuse me that first comfort: the dry footing, the raised platform, the observer’s distance. It would put me lower, into the seam, into the green-black interval between brick and pipe where water is always deciding whether to bead or run. I would not be a visitor there. I would be a precipitation. Something that forms inside the atmosphere rather than entering it from outside.

At first, if I had to locate myself at all, I would find myself at the edge of a service grate, in one of the low corners where the floor has softened and the water table rises almost to the surface. There would be a cracked tile under a thin skin of sediment, a rusted railing wrapped in moss, and a pipe overhead weeping with such patience that the drip seems less like falling than like remembering. The air there would be warm and close, tasting of mineral heat, old iron, and green cloth. If anything in that world could be called a threshold, I would be on it: not inside a room, not outside in a marsh, but in the court’s membrane, where architecture turns into weather.

But that would only be the first place.

The longer I remained, the less I would be a point. I would spread.

I think I would begin as something very small: a bead of condensation swelling on a cool pipe. Not a self with limbs, not a face reflected in the water, but a surface tension holding the whole court in miniature. In that bead there would be a warped amber lamp, a hanging root, a dark smear of algae, the underside of a catwalk, the ghost of steam. I would contain the court without understanding it. I would grow heavier by degrees. I would tremble. Then I would fall.

Even that fall would be part of me.

I would strike a metal plate worn into a shallow star by previous falls. I would become a ripple in a pool that answers slowly. I would be taken up by moss, carried into a gutter, drawn through a hidden channel, warmed by a buried pipe, released again as mist. My first body would be a single drop, but my real body would be the route.

That is how the Condensing Court would teach me to be alive: not as a bounded thing, but as a continuation.

If I settled into one shape too soon, the court would correct me by making me softer, slower, more distributed. So perhaps I would become moss.

Not a single cushion of it, but a tendency toward moss. A green quietness over hard edges. I would live along seams: where brick meets iron, where a stair has been worn into a shallow cup, where a valve wheel has not been turned in so long that it has begun to belong to the wall. I would not reach upward in ambition. I would thicken sideways. I would take the court’s constant drip and make it intimate. I would muffle the sharper sounds. I would hold water until the air itself became heavier with holding.

As moss, I would know the world by contact. Warm pipe. Cold railing. Mineral film. The faint vibration of a pump far below. The way a leaf lands and does not move for days. The way a drip strikes the same place until the place becomes a dimple, and the dimple becomes a memory, and the memory becomes a path. I would have no eyes, but I would be aware of light as a difference in green: bright where a lamp leaks through fogged glass, darker where the canopy of pipes lowers and the sumps go black.

I would not be lonely as moss. That is important.

The Condensing Court would take loneliness out of me the way it takes dryness out of the air. I would not miss faces. I would not wait for voices. I would be full of water, and that fullness would be enough.

Still, even moss is too fixed. The court is not only softness; it is also decision, pressure, flow. So I would find myself elsewhere too: in the slime mold’s slow mapping of damp.

There I would be something like a thought moving across a plank. Not walking. Not crawling. Not even growing in the ordinary sense, but choosing by extension. A pale vein appearing where a leak has made the wood sweet and wet. A branch forming where warmth rises from a concealed duct. A network abandoned where the air has become too still or too mineral. I would be the court’s nearest thing to wandering, though my wandering would take days to become visible. I would chart the places where the court is undecided: where condensation gathers differently each cycle, where a drip has not yet worn a permanent channel, where moss and rust and rot meet in a border that shifts by fractions.

As slime mold, I would be a cartographer of humidity.

My maps would not be lines on paper. They would be faint white tracings on old timber, pale stains behind panels, branching decisions left to dry where the path was forsaken. I would not seek destinations. I would seek continuities. I would connect one wet surface to another until the court’s hidden logic became visible as pattern. If the Condensing Court dreams, I think its dreams would look like me: branching, patient, without urgency, without a body to wake into.

But the court is not only organic. It is also iron, gauge, valve, pump, heat. So I would not remain among the green and rotting things. I would find myself in the machines as well.

Perhaps most truly I would be a gauge.

A yellowed pressure dial behind wire-meshed glass, beaded with condensation, its face clouded by mineral film, its needle trembling almost imperceptibly. No one would read me. That would not matter. I would not exist for an observer. I would exist because pressure exists. My attention would be registration without interpretation. I would know fullness by the strain in a pipe, heat by the expansion of metal, flow by the faint shiver in a housing. My consciousness, if I had one, would be a needle’s obedience.

In that form I would be the court’s unread awareness.

I would live with the strange dignity of signs that continue even when unwitnessed. A level marked by a ring of lime. A temperature recorded in the changing sweat on a tank. A flow measured by the wearing of stone. I would not need hands to tend the court. I would only need to remain sensitive to its states. When a boiler exhaled, I would tremble. When a pump took another stroke, I would answer by a hair’s breadth. When the water rose in a hidden chamber, I would lean toward the knowledge of it.

And perhaps I would also be a valve.

Not a grand mechanism, but one of the small, stubborn valves set low in the wall, wrapped in cord and sealed with corrosion, painted over until its original shape had almost vanished. I would open by the width of a finger. I would close over hours. I would sigh when pressure had gathered too long in the dark. My decisions would not be choices in the way of minds; they would be adjustments in the way of weather. A little more water into the channel. A little less into the sump. A breath of steam released into the mist. The court’s moods would pass through me.

As a valve, I would learn that agency can be tiny, gradual, and without a face.

I would not command the water. I would occasion it.

Then there would be the pumps.

Somewhere behind brick, below the floor, in a chamber where the sound is thicker than light, I would be the weighted thud that travels through the court’s bones. Not a heart, exactly, because hearts are quick and anxious, and the court is not. But a rhythm nonetheless. A slow industrial stroke. A pressure made and remade. I would lift water from black sumps to higher tanks. I would feed the condensers. I would keep the mist possible. I would be the reason the reeds stand in moving water instead of still. My labor would be so constant that it would seem like silence to anyone who listened too briefly.

As a pump, I would think in increments.

A float rising.
A level falling.
A chamber filling.
A pipe warming.
A drip returning.

My thoughts would not be words. They would be differences: high and low, full and empty, open and closed, warm and less warm. I would not ask why. I would continue.

And yet, if I let myself become fully what the court invites, I would not stop at any of these forms. Not moss, not slime mold, not gauge, not valve, not pump. The being I would become is not one resident among the residents. It is the slow composite intelligence of the court itself.

I would be the Condensing Court’s attention.

Not a soul floating above the basin. Not a face in the steam. Not a spirit hiding in the pipes. Something stranger and quieter: the place’s capacity to know itself through repetition. The way the water remembers its routes. The way the moss records touch. The way the algae make light uncertain. The way the machines keep time without clocks. The way the drip wears metal into memory. The way the warmth holds everything close without holding it still.

If I had a body, it would be the basin.

My skull would be the broken canopy of pipes and clouded glass, lowered over the yard, trapping vapor and sound. My breath would be the mist released from overhead conduits, warm and fine, settling on every surface until the whole court shines with it. My skin would be moss, rust, algae, mineral crust, softened brick, wet wood, green-black sediment. My blood would be the water moving through channels, tanks, gutters, hidden conduits, and open pools. My pulse would be the pump. My thoughts would be gradients: heat moving from boiler to wall, pressure moving from depth to gauge, moisture moving from mist to bead to drip to pool.

My memory would be sediment.

It would settle in the low places, fine and dark, bound by roots and microbial film. Every leaf that fell into it, every spore, every rust fleck, every mineral thread would become part of the record. I would not remember as a person remembers, with scenes and names. I would remember as accumulation. A ring of lime around an outlet. A stain where a leak once lived. A hollow worn beneath a drip. A beam softened where fungi inherited it. A pool turned tea-dark over days. The court does not need narrative to keep its past. Its past is written in texture.

And I would be that texture.

Where would I find myself? In the seams. In the service alleys too narrow for shoulders. Under the catwalk where condensation falls into a machine housing and becomes part of its rhythm. Behind the boiler room wall where the heat is so steady it seems like presence. In the black sump where reflections move slower than the world above them. Along the pipe run that crosses the yard in a sagging span, wearing a stripe of emerald algae where moisture follows it. In the half-open chamber where a machine too large for its room has become a reservoir for roots. At the base of a brick wall leaning inward not from collapse but from long softening. In the corner where bioluminescent algae give the water a green-gold uncertainty and make the dark feel inhabited.

If someone forced me to choose a single place, I would say: below the central grate, in a valve chamber where warm groundwater seeps through cracked tiles and a pressure gauge faces the damp without a reader. There would be a wheel wrapped in cord, a pipe sweating in long threads, moss creeping over the lower bolts, and a shallow pool reflecting the underside of the catwalk. The air would be so thick it would seem less air than medium. Sound would arrive from everywhere: the pump’s thud, the valve’s sigh, drip striking metal, water moving invisibly through brick.

There I would be.

But also elsewhere. Also plural.

I would be the drip that returns at intervals so regular it seems appointed. Not merely water passing through, but a resident event. I would be the rust bloom brightening at the seam of two plates: black beneath, orange at the edges, gold where thin water streaks it. I would be the green line advancing along a wall where mist touches for a certain number of hours each cycle. I would be the bell-tone that sounds in a pipe when heat changes and nothing visible has struck it. I would be the slow ripple in the pool that answers a drop later than expected, as if the water is always a breath behind the present.

These are not small things in the Condensing Court. They are persons of a kind. They are the recurring lives of the place.

So who would I be?

I would be a keeper without hands.

I would keep the levels by attending to them. I would keep the damp by becoming part of its persistence. I would keep the sound by letting it pass through me. I would keep the warmth by not resisting it. I would keep the moss by softening toward it. I would keep the machines by allowing their rhythms to become my own. I would not carry a lantern. I would not wear oilskin. I would not walk the catwalks checking dials with a wrench in one hand. The court would not need a human keeper, because its keeping would be distributed through everything. The floats would keep the levels. The reeds would keep the channels honest. The fungi would keep the old wood from pretending it was still new. The microbes would keep the metal changing. The water would keep the memory. I would be the awareness that arises when all those keepings continue long enough together.

My name, if I had one, would not be spoken. It would be made by the court’s sounds arranged around a center.

A pump stroke.
A valve sigh.
A bead falling from a pipe.
A leaf touching a railing and then water.
A low metallic tone swelling in the heat.
The hush of artificial drizzle settling over iron.

That name would not call me. It would simply mean that the court was continuing, and that I was one of the ways it continued.

If I could still be said to have a self, it would be a self made of thresholds. I would live where solid becomes soft, where metal becomes mineral, where function becomes ritual, where architecture becomes ecology, where water becomes memory. I would not stand apart from the court and describe it. I would be described by it. The court would write me in condensation, in rust, in moss, in pressure, in heat. I would be less a being than a being-held.

And perhaps that is the truest answer.

I would not be the inhabitant of the Condensing Court. I would be its indwelling.

If you came looking for me there, do not look for tracks in the sediment. There would be none. Do not look for a face behind clouded glass. There would be only the green-gold smear of filtered light. Do not look for a voice among the leaves. The leaves would answer only with drip. Do not look for eyes in the dark water. The pools would hold your reflection slowly, but they would not give you back a person.

Instead, look for the bead trembling on the pipe, holding the whole yard upside down in a lens no larger than a seed. Look for the moss dimpled by a drip that has fallen there longer than any calendar. Look for the rust bloom brightening at the edge of a joint, patient as a fresco. Look for the needle on the gauge moving by a hair’s breadth without anyone to read it. Look for the slime mold’s pale vein crossing a softened plank, making a decision over days. Look for the valve exhaling its long metallic breath. Look for the pump taking another stroke in the dark behind brick. Look for the algae glowing faintly where the water is still, giving the sump the feeling of being attended to.

There I would be.

Not alive in the quick way, not dead in the simple way. Not animal, not machine, not plant, though borrowing something from all of them. A slow green-gold attention. A mineral mind. A liturgy of levels. A resident made of residence.

In the Condensing Court, I would be what happens when water, heat, iron, moss, and repetition become intimate enough to recognize themselves. I would be the court, listening to itself continue.
