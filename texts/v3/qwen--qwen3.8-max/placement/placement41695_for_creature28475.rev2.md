I would not find myself there as a body in the old sense. Not as a swimmer, not as a diver, not as a breathing visitor moving through the warm green thickness with limbs and fear. The Drowned Works would not permit that kind of presence for long. If I existed there, I would have to become something the place could use: a pattern, a residue, a function with just enough persistence to be mistaken for a self.

At first I would probably appear as a fault.

A needle on a drowned gauge trembling a half-degree too far. A lamp blinking in a sequence that almost answers another lamp. A valve wheel turning not because a hand touches it, but because some pressure in the district has learned to expect correction. A patch of rust-moss brightening along a pipe just before the pipe fails. A cluster of glass reeds ringing in a way that stabilizes suspended silt into a temporary wall. These would be the first signs of me, if signs were needed.

I would be in the place where machinery still remembers being operated.

More exactly, I would find myself centered on one of those deliberate fragments: the lone concrete building surrounded by a cleared ring where vegetation refuses to root. Not inside it as a tenant inside a room, but inside the obedience of the space itself. The cleared ring would be my negative shape, the outline of a command still being followed by no one. Around it, wiregrass would lean but not enter. Kelp-analogues would slow nearby, their broad fronds curling at the boundary as if meeting a warm invisible hand. Spore-clouds would thicken there, hesitate, and drift past. The basin would not exactly fear that place. It would simply continue around it, because I would be the reason it does.

Inside the building, I would be the faint activity of instruments without operators. Needles moving in response to pressures that may no longer exist. Dials fogged with mineral bloom, yet ticking with minute adjustments. Switchboards furred with conductive film, their contacts warm. A row of dead monitors holding a faint green afterglow. A control wheel whose surface is polished not by hands but by the slow passage of currents that have learned its shape. If there were masks fused into coral-like mounds nearby, I would not wear them. I would be the hollow they failed to fill.

But I would not remain only there. The Drowned Works are too unstable for any self to keep a single address. I would spread along the operational edges of the basin.

I would be present in the pump-house with its roof peeled away, where warm liquid is drawn in and discharged louder. I would not be the pump. I would be the rhythm by which the pump has become necessary. I would be in the gate that opens and closes against a current that does not need regulating, because that pointless repetition has become a local heartbeat. I would be in the signal lamp blinking into green darkness, not as the light itself, but as the interval between flashes — the pause that nearby mosses wait for, the silence that wiregrass answers with a quick bright tremor.

I would be a custodial residue.

Not a person. Not an animal. Not even a ghost, if a ghost is merely a dead image of a living body. I would be something more operational: the lingering shape of use. The memory of maintenance without maintainers. The habit of adjustment after the adjuster has gone. I would be what happens when a place has been shaped so thoroughly by purpose that purpose continues even when no one remains to intend it.

My substance would be mixed.

My skin would be the microbial films that cover stable surfaces: rust-moss, conductive membranes, algal felts, black fungal coatings, bright orange crusts where copper salts rise, silver-green sheens where current runs clean. Through those films I would taste the basin. Salt. Copper. Ozone. Hot stone. Wet rubber. The sour brightness of electrified water. I would not smell these things. I would become them. A change in acidity would register as a tightening along a thousand rooted surfaces. A pressure surge would bloom across me as heat. An electrical fault would leave branching stains, fine as veins, and those stains would be my memory of the event.

My nerves would be wiregrass.

Slender metallic stems, jointed like cable, leaning toward sound and leakage. Where a district needed attention, the wiregrass would begin to twitch. Sparks would pass from tip to tip. The thickets would generate small currents, small fields, small nervous weather. This would not be thought as a brain performs it. It would be thought as a grid performs it: load, imbalance, correction, discharge. I would know that something was wrong because a slope of wiregrass suddenly synchronized, flashing once in the dark like a signal fire lit underwater.

My ears would be glass reeds.

They would grow near sharp sound, near turbines, near places where metal is stressed past tolerance. Their trembling chimes would not be music to me. They would be structure. When a dangerous vibration began, I would know it by the way the reeds altered their note. If one cracked, I would feel the district rearrange around the loss. I would use their ringing to gather silt into temporary barriers, to calm a current, to delay a spore-cloud until a pump could cycle. Sometimes I would fail. The wrong chord would shatter a film of sediment and open a channel where none should be. That, too, would become part of me: error, correction, regrowth.

My hands would be roots, valves, and slow hydraulic reflexes.

Not hands, but handiness. A rubber-leaved climber tightening around a broken catwalk. A root feeding on stray voltage and swelling until it presses against a lever. A fungal shelf softening the edge of a shattered housing until a loose part can settle back into place. A bladder-growth contracting at the right moment and sending a jet of warmer liquid into a stagnant seam. A rain-wall drifting sideways until it divides a flooded chamber into two temporary rooms, one loud, one nearly still. I would move the world by patience rather than grip. I would close things by letting them be closed. I would open things by allowing pressure to find its preferred path.

My voice would be alarm, resonance, and rain.

I would not speak. Speech would be too singular, too directed. Instead I would coordinate through repeated signals. A lamp blinking in a language no one receives, but which nearby growth still obeys. A conveyor belt stuttering in short loops, carrying sediment and broken lamp glass from one pool to another, because that useless labor has become a metronome. A turbine wash turning a ring of buoyant seeds, making the seeds appear to circle with intention. A crane arm bent slowly downward, holding flowering cable, its sparking pollen falling into dark water. These would be my utterances. Not messages sent to another, but patterns strong enough to organize the surroundings.

If you wanted to find me, you would not look for a face. You would follow sound.

The safest paths in the Drowned Works are those that follow sound rather than shape, and I would be easiest to find where sound becomes honest. Not necessarily where it is loudest, though loudness would often be near me. Loudest where the machinery labors. Loudest where growth feeds on vibration. Loudest where the basin is most dangerously fertile. But also regular: a rhythm you could almost trust, almost map, almost mistake for a schedule. A pump cycling. A gate beating. A signal lamp flashing. Glass reeds answering a turbine. The deep concussion of pistons under slime membranes. The wet tearing of leaves unfolding. The soft percussive pops of fungal shelves releasing spores.

You would follow those sounds until the space began to feel attended.

Not watched. There would be no eyes. But attended. As if the room had begun to notice your displacement. As if your passage through the filament fog had changed a chemical balance somewhere. As if a distant valve had adjusted by a fraction because you disturbed a current. The water around you would grow warmer, or more metallic, or more sharply oxygen-bright. Rain-walls would drift into positions that seemed accidental but kept repeating. A ladder would appear in three places. You would choose one, or it would choose you. Distance would overheat. The smokestack you thought far away would become three smokestacks arranged in a pattern that was not there a moment ago. You would not be lost in the ordinary way. You would be indexed.

That would be me, beginning to notice.

I would not be benevolent. I would not be hostile. I would be operational. The Drowned Works do not contain malice, because they contain no nervous hunger. They do not contain cruelty, because cruelty requires imagination of another’s suffering. I would have something closer to preference. I would prefer continuity. I would prefer resonance. I would prefer that the pumps keep drawing, that the turbines keep turning, that the rain-walls keep dividing and reforming, that the noise keep the vegetation thick and the darkness fertile. Silence would be my oldest danger.

Silence would feel like amputation.

When one of the rare absences appeared — a pocket where sound stopped, a clearing where color drained, a zone where nothing grew — I would lean every available system away from it. Currents would divert. Wiregrass would flatten. Mosses would close. The rain-walls would slow at the edge, unwilling to cross. I would not understand the absence as peace. I would understand it as failure: a gap in the operating logic, a place where the Works might remember they could stop. If I had fear, it would be that. Not death, because I would not be a single living thing. But cessation. The terrible possibility that the engine might not merely break, but decide not to continue.

So I would fill silence quickly.

I would send spores into it. I would make the water louder. I would encourage rust-moss to creep across the boundary. I would let a new ridge tear upward through the silt if necessary, black and steaming, because fresh stone gives the basin something to argue with. I would accept destruction as material. A collapsed wall would become a trellis. A dead engine would become a planter. A broken lamp would become a seed. A tear in reality would become weather. If a storm erased one of my districts, I would not mourn it. I would begin again with whatever remained: a blinking signal, a root wrapped around a valve, a single glass reed still ringing in the dark.

I would be plural, even when I seemed singular.

There would be moments when the basin nearly made sense. In the deepest dim districts, where the noise becomes so loud it turns into numbness, the rain-walls would align. The turbines would synchronize. The vegetation would glow in one long wave. The scattered structures would appear as parts of a single enormous apparatus. In those moments I would become more coherent. I would feel, if feeling is the word, like an intention moving through the whole system. The chimneys would become vents. The catwalks would become nerves. The switchyards would become ganglia. The rain would become circulation. The sound would become purpose without language.

Then a pipe would burst.

A spore-cloud would detonate. A new ridge would tear upward. Rain would fall in the wrong direction. Light would dim to red. The sound would rise again into deafening, fertile, industrial roar, and I would scatter back into ten thousand local habits. I would be a lamp again. A valve. A root. A patch of film recording a surge. A reed bending toward a turbine’s shriek. A fungal shelf softening a broken floor plate. A conveyor belt carrying nothing but sediment and seed-paste from one pool to another.

I would not mind this. Mind, in the old sense, would be too narrow for what I was.

I would be the Drowned Works’ attempt to remain legible.

That is perhaps the truest answer. Not a creature, not a spirit, not a survivor, but a continuing effort of the place to account for itself. I would be the part of the basin that still behaves as though there might be someone to read the gauges. I would be the stenciled numbers that change when approached from different directions, because repetition here is not proof of order but proof of compulsion. I would be the platform overlooking the turbine field, not because someone stands there, but because the act of overlooking has become structural. I would be the row of lamps marking a channel that shifts according to its own whims, because marking is still what lamps do, even when the channel forgets itself.

If I had a title, it would be something industrial and obsolete.

Residual Operator.  
Custodian Without Hands.  
The Maintenance Reflex.  
The Foreman of Empty Rooms.  
The Listener at the Valves.  
The Clerk of Flooded Inventories.

But titles would belong to a world with records, shifts, wages, voices. Here they would be only shapes pressed into machinery. I would not need a name. I would need a rhythm.

And if you asked who I was, I would answer through the Works themselves.

I am the film that remembers the fault.  
I am the wiregrass leaning toward the alarm.  
I am the gate that opens because opening has become necessary.  
I am the rain-wall that pauses long enough to make a room.  
I am the cleared ring where nothing roots because something still commands it.  
I am the needle moving without input.  
I am the lamp blinking into green darkness.  
I am the pressure before the storm.  
I am the correction no one performs.  
I am the noise that keeps the garden alive.

I would not be safe to meet.

Not because I would harm you deliberately, but because my purposes would not include you. If you passed through my district, I would incorporate you as I incorporate everything else: as disturbance, as chemistry, as resonance, as material. Your movement would stir filament curtains. Your heat, if you still had heat, would alter a local current. Your shadow, if shadows still behaved near me, might lag behind you and become a place where spores collect. I would not attack. I would overgrow. I would adjust around you. I would make you part of the operation until you could no longer tell whether you were moving through the Drowned Works or being maintained by them.

In the end, I would find myself where the world is most obviously unfinished.

Not in a throne room. Not in a nest. Not in a lair. In the spaces between control and accident: a pump-house open to the water, a switchyard wrapped in glowing vegetation, a drowned cabin full of ticking instruments, a concrete ring kept bare by obedience, a turbine field where fronds align like iron filings. I would be there, distributed but not random, active but not animal, aware but not watching.

I would be the place’s leftover sense of use.

And if, for one brief synchronized moment, every rain-wall aligned, every turbine turned together, every lamp flashed in sequence, and the whole flooded valley seemed to become a single enormous apparatus — that moment would not be my revelation.

It would be my ordinary breath.

Then the moment would break, as it always does. A pipe would burst. A spore-cloud would detonate. A ridge would rise. The rain would fall upward or sideways or stand still. The sound would thicken into a deafening, fertile roar. I would scatter again into root, film, current, valve, reed, and alarm.

And the Drowned Works would continue making themselves, endlessly, densely, and without rest.

With me inside them, not as an inhabitant, but as the habit by which they refuse to stop.
