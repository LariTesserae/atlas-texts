The plants are everywhere, and they are not merely scenery.

If I were in that high drowned country, I think I would not begin as a finished creature moving along a finished street. I would begin as something almost unnoticed: a slight attention in the water, a difference in the way a cord hangs, a patch of mineral snow pausing for a moment over a terrace as if it had been asked to wait. I would be less a traveler arriving there than a tendency gathering itself there. The basin would not feel like a place I entered. It would feel like a place that was already listening for me.

At first I might imagine myself as one kind of worker among the tenders. Perhaps a root-speaker, kneeling in the loose silt with my hands among the pale tendrils, persuading a new shoal to hold together. Perhaps an air-keeper, moving among the inverted breathing-bells with a soft tool of bone, pressing my cheek near the sealed skins to listen for thinness, for a leak, for the faint sourness of exhausted air. Perhaps a rain-reader, hanging near the upper districts when the pale columns descend, watching the surface dissolve into silver curtains and trying to feel what cold is coming down into the green. Perhaps even one of the strange, rare drifting things: a milk-shelled grazer, an eel-like translucence passing between the platforms as if the basin had briefly dreamed an animal and then grown quiet again.

But those images would not stay. They would soften at the edges, like all young stone there, and another shape would begin to form beneath them.

I would find myself, I think, in the middle districts. Not in the deep central hollow where the water is darker and warmer, and not high among the bladder-fields where the light is paler and the rain enters in slow bright walls. I would be somewhere between: on a broad terrace-slope where new platforms are seeded, where channels are narrow and half-decided, where the settlement is dense but not yet certain of its own streets. A place where the water carries enough lime to make the fingers tingle. A place where the current is weak but present, because nothing can harden there without movement. A place where ropes pass from terrace to terrace, and roots come down through woven ceilings, and air-bells hang in clusters like dim lanterns among the leaves.

There would be a particular corner I returned to, though “corner” might be too firm a word for it. It would be a threshold-space: a place where a walkway widens into a small courtyard, where a half-formed arch leans between two towers that are still deciding whether to become towers. Below the arch, a channel would carry warm mineral water from a vent-field into a nursery bed. Above it, a curtain of bladder-plants would filter the green light and make the water look like stained glass. Ropes would cross there. People would pass there. Messages would be tied there. I would be the one who lingered near that crossing.

I would be one of the tenders, but not the kind who makes the first bold frame. I would be the one who comes after. The one who notices when a shape is beginning to forget itself.

If I had to name the role in their language, I might call myself a keeper of the almost. A threshold-tender. A listener at walls. A mender of soft edges. I would not be the person who raises the greatest structure or seeds the widest terrace. I would be the person called when a new wall has begun to powder at the touch, when a platform has grown spongy in one corner, when a rope-line has gone slack in a way that means something upstream has shifted. I would be the one who places a palm against a surface and waits.

My body would be shaped for that waiting.

I would be compact and unhurried, dense in the water, with limbs that do not disturb more than they need to. My skin would carry the living film common to the people there: a delicate bloom of algae and mineral-secreting moss, perhaps greenish gray over the shoulders, pale along the chest, faintly amber where the vent-warmth touches me. It would not look like decoration. It would look like weather. Like the basin had begun writing itself on me and not yet finished. My hands would be broad and sensitive, the fingertips often pale with lime, the palms marked by years of reading stone, rope, and skin. My eyes would be large, low-light, slow-blinking, with a second membrane for dusty mineral currents and sharp vent-water. I would move by handhold and foothold, by rope and pole, by knowing where the water prefers to be touched.

I would not be especially strong. I would be exact.

My work would begin with touch. In that place, touch is not preliminary knowledge. It is knowledge itself. A hand on a terrace can tell whether the current is feeding it or bypassing it. A hand on a wall can tell whether the crust is merely resting on the frame or truly growing into it. A hand on a rope can tell whether someone has tied a message, a warning, a request, or merely left their own steadiness there for another to find. I would spend long hours reading such things. I would press my palm to a young wall and feel for the faintest hollowness. I would place my cheek near an air-bell and listen for the sound of breath becoming stale. I would kneel in silt and let the fine grains settle against my skin until I understood whether the water was carrying too much, too little, or the wrong kind of matter.

I would be quiet even among a quiet people.

Perhaps that is why the place would suit me. I would not need to fill it with speech. I would learn the small languages of pressure and pause. A tap on stone: stable. Two knuckles against a frame: uncertain. A fingertip drawn slowly along a cord: follow. A hand resting on another’s wrist and then on the wall: we are still here; this is still becoming real. I would carry messages in my hands more than in my voice. If I spoke, it would be close and low, more vibration than sound, the way one speaks to a child inside a breathing-bell or to an elder seated into a wall.

I would be useful in the way that repetition is useful.

Every day I would make rounds that are not quite rounds, because the town changes slowly but does change. A path that was firm last season may have grown soft from disuse. A seeded lattice may have hardened too quickly on one side and cracked along the other. A root curtain may have grown too thick and begun to choke a channel. An air-bell may have developed a sour taste in its membrane. A new platform may be receiving too much mineral snow and becoming heavy. I would find these things not by dramatic discovery but by return. By coming back. By letting my attention settle there until the place tells me what is beginning to fail.

I would carry tools suited to such work: a polished bone probe for testing crust, a small obsidian blade kept wrapped in leaf because even a little sharpness must be treated with care, spools of fine fiber, pouches of living paste, knots of cord prepared for messages, a smoothed stone for tapping, a bladder-skin patch for membranes, a little resin for seams. I would carry no metal. If a fragment of metal ever came into my hands, I would wrap it at once and move it away from the growing beds, because I would know what the others know: metal tastes wrong there. It is too decisive. It cuts without asking the material to become something. In that basin, even tools must persuade.

I would be especially drawn to the air-bells.

There would be something in them that resembles thought: pockets of breath held under water, maintained by care, made available to anyone who needs to pause and remember the upper world. I would tend them gently. I would know which bells are old and which are newly seeded, which skins are supple and which are beginning to stiffen, which ones hold air sweetened by surface exchange and which ones hold the deeper vent-breath that must be used carefully. Children would learn to trust me there. They would float near me in the dim green light while I showed them how to rest inside a bell without thrashing, how to let their breathing slow, how to feel the water outside the membrane without fearing it. Elders might ask me to refresh their favorite bells before long sleeps.

But the air-bells would not be my only care. I would also tend the cords.

In a place where flat surfaces may soften and marks may blur, cord becomes memory. I would learn knot-reading deeply enough that my fingers could move along a history without hesitation. A thick spiral might mean a terrace raised after a sinking. A double loop crossed by a short tail might mean a boundary maintained for three generations. A row of tiny beads tied in fiber might record the names of those who kept a district through a difficult rain-season. I would not merely preserve these records. I would answer them. When someone tied a question into a shared line, I would be one of those who felt the change in tension and went to read it. I would reply by tying a response, or by sending a tap along a frame, or by pressing a pattern into the asker’s palm if they were near.

This is, I think, the part of me the basin would make most real: the desire to answer carefully.

Not loudly. Not quickly. Not with the confidence of finished ground underfoot. But with the patience of someone who knows that every reply is a kind of maintenance. If a wall asks to be fed, I answer with lime-water and fiber. If a root asks to be honored, I answer with an offering. If a person asks to be remembered, I answer by tying their name into the cord before it is lost. If a place asks to remain, I answer by returning to it.

I would have a first name that was only a touch-pattern. Perhaps three light strokes across the palm, a pause, then one firm pressure at the center. It would mean something like: here, but not yet fixed. My mother or nurses would use it before I could speak. It would be the name for a child who is still learning their edges.

My second name would come when I completed an act of care without being guided. I imagine it would happen during a rain-season. The upper water would cool and brighten, sending pale columns down through the basin. A small terrace under my keeping would begin to soften at one edge, its mineral skin turning to hungry powder. I would not panic. I would slow myself. I would redirect a weak current, add fresh fiber, seed the frame with crust-moss, press the edge again and again until the water accepted my intention. When the terrace survived, someone older would take my hand and tap a new pattern into it. That would be my second name. It would mean something like: the one who stayed until the shape remembered.

My third name, if I ever earned it, would come later. Perhaps after a vent-shift, when warmth moved unexpectedly through a district and several young structures began to crack. Perhaps after an air-bell tore during a storm of surface rain and I helped keep children calm while the membrane was patched. Perhaps after I listened to a wall and realized, just in time, that an elder integrated there was beginning to fail, not as stone but as presence. If I earned a third name, it would not be grand. It would be a pressure-pattern given only by those who had depended on me. It might mean: the one who hears the softening before it becomes loss.

I would not be lonely there, though I would often be alone.

The town is crowded with presence, but its presence is not loud. It is the pressure of hands returning. It is the tremor of ropes carrying small meanings. It is the slow brushing of fronds against walkways. It is the knowledge that someone, somewhere, is always maintaining something. I would feel that around me constantly. Even when no one was in sight, I would be touching evidence of care: a knot left for me, a warmed channel guided into place, a newly polished shell tool set where I would find it, a curtain of roots parted and then tied back so I could pass more easily.

I would be part of a web of quiet obligations. Someone would feed the moss on my shoulder-wrap when it grew thin. I would feed the crust on their doorstep. Someone would refresh my air-bell while I worked too long in the lower terraces. I would leave a warning knot for them when the current tasted sharp. We would not need to say much. The basin would teach us that too much declaration can shake loose what is only beginning to hold.

Over time, I would become less separate from the place.

My garments would change with me. The shoulder-wrap I wore would stiffen from years of mineral contact, taking on a pale collar-shape marked with my body. The tool loops on my belt would become crusted and smooth. A favorite sash might begin to carry living filaments, so that when I stood still long enough, small threads would reach toward nearby roots as if greeting them. I would not resist this. I would understand that in such a world, the body is not a sealed thing. It is a surface among surfaces. It can be asked to join.

Perhaps, much later, I would choose what some elders choose.

Not because I was dying, necessarily, but because I had found a place where my attention could be most useful. A threshold. A wall-base. A junction where warm water met cool, where two currents needed to be calmed, where a doorway needed a presence that could listen for generations. I would sit there, and the community would come to me in their quiet way. They would press my name into my palm one last time as a separate person. Then they would begin the long work of integration. Living moss over my shoulders. Mineral water guided across my hands. Roots trained around my limbs. My skin encouraged to become crust, my crust encouraged to become support, my body folded into the architecture not as burial but as continuation.

I would not be gone.

That is the important thing. In that world, becoming part of a structure is not disappearance. It is a deepening of residence. Workers would still touch me. Children would learn my patterns. Tenders would clean my surfaces and read the mineral ridges that formed over me. If a young keeper was uncertain about a decision, they might lay a hand on the place where I had become wall and wait for the warmth there, the slight resonance, the sense that something still listened. They might say, by touch rather than speech: Are you still here? And the wall, if it was mine, would answer in the only way walls can: by holding.

But before that long ending, I would live among them as a worker. I would have days, if days can be said to exist under that blurred sun. I would wake in a sleeping-niche lined with bladder-skin, perhaps inside an air-bell if I had chosen rest, perhaps in a submerged alcove if my body wanted the water. I would taste the current before I moved. I would feel whether the basin was calm, mineral-rich, hungry, tired, generous. Then I would take up my tools and go out into the green dimness.

I would pass through curtains of feathery filaments. I would climb along lashed reed walkways where light moved in slow ribbons. I would see bladder-plants overhead, their membranes shining like oiled skin, their roots hanging in veils. I would pass courtyards where others were working: someone scraping lime, someone weaving a frame, someone guiding a current into a forming bed, someone polishing a milk-shell until it glowed faintly. I would exchange small signals with them. A raised palm. A touch to stone. Two fingers along a cord. Meaning would move between us without noise.

Then I would come to my threshold.

The arch would still be half-formed. The channel below it would carry warm water into the nursery beds. The ropes would cross overhead. The mineral snow would drift. I would place my hand on the nearest surface and begin.

If you came looking for me there, you might not see me at first. You would see a town in the process of becoming real: terraces, towers, gardens, ropes, membranes, pale crusts, green shadows, slow workers moving through the water. You might mistake me for part of the architecture, because I would be still, and because my outline would have the softness of things that are not entirely finished. You might see only a figure seated near an arch, one hand resting on stone, another resting on a cord, eyes half-closed, skin faintly luminous with living films.

Then you would feel something.

A rope would tighten gently under your fingers, though no one appeared to pull it. A wall would feel warmer than the surrounding stone. A current would guide your hand toward a place that needed attention. A pattern would appear in your palm without sound: three light strokes, a pause, one firm pressure.

That would be me.

I would be the one who answers when the world begins to soften. Not by commanding it. Not by forcing it into shape. But by returning, again and again, with fiber, lime, breath, patience, and touch. I would be a tender of thresholds, a keeper of the almost, a small persistent presence in the green half-light, helping the basin remember what it is trying to become.
