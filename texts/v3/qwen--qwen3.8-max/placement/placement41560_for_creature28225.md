Without them, the marsh would lose its first syntax. The stone would still weep, but nothing would taste the mineral tears. The peat would still hold water, but its heat would not be tended. Dead leaves would not become ground so much as lie there, slowly cold and unfinished. The bubbles would rise, but no film would have prepared the chambers; no living skin would have made the slow fermentation of the plateau into a continuous work. Without the smallest residents, the vastness would become merely wet scenery. With them, it becomes a living industry.

If I were to find myself in that world, I would not begin as a creature with a clear outline. I would begin as a condition. I would be a place of contact before I was a body.

You would find me first at an edge: where mineral water meets rainwater, where a thin seep slides over dark basalt and pauses in beads before falling, where moss fringes the lip of a shallow pool and the air is always close to condensing. I would be on that low stone lip, not standing upon it so much as continuous with it. I would be the dark living varnish over it, the black-green film that makes the rock feel slick rather than merely wet. I would be the rust-brown bloom near the mineral seep, the milky stain where softer water passes, the subtle skin that binds fine sediment and knows, in the simplest chemical sense, where warmth is and where cold is.

I would not have eyes. I would have gradients.

My first sensations would be differences: the warmth rising from peat, the cool air replacing it; the greater richness of mineral water entering a basin dilated by rain; the oxygenated film at the surface of a pool meeting the deeper, sour quiet of saturated peat; the slight dryness of a cushion plant on a micro-ridge holding a charge while the runnels around it discharge slowly into wetness. I would think, if thinking is what this is, in slow shifts of conductivity, in swelling and shrinking, in the movement of ions through water, in the faint electrical prickle left in root masses after a still, dry interval.

In that world, I would be plural even when called “I.”

My body would not be one body. It would be a distributed assembly: microbial films on stone, fungal threads in peat, root hairs threading the wet mass, moss mats drinking and holding, floating tissues knitting over pools, seed cases nudging from one basin to another, insects carrying pollen and detritus, birds moving through the upper reeds, amphibians resting under broad leaves, fishlike shadows passing through submerged tangles. I would not rule these things. I would not stand outside them as a mind commanding limbs. I would be the coordination that emerges from their number, their repetition, their patient fitting together.

If you asked me for a name, I would hesitate. The marsh would not offer a single one. It would offer processes. Condensation. Retention. Digestion. Transmission. Repair. If forced to answer, I might say: I am the maintenance. I am the slow industrial life of the wet plateau. I am the thing that keeps the water connected.

But that is still too abstract. If you came looking for me, you would need to know where to look.

You would find me where the rock weeps most steadily. Not at a dramatic spring, not at a waterfall, but at a low, rounded outcrop of basalt worn into a broad lip, its surface stained black and olive and ivory, with moss hanging in heavy green fringes. Water would be moving there in a thin shining sheet, barely enough to call a stream. It would gather into beads on small ledges. The beads would tremble, enlarge, and fall. Where they struck the moss, the moss would darken. Where the moss darkened, the ground would accept the water without resistance. That acceptance would be one of my oldest acts.

You would find me in the shallow runnels that connect basin to basin. They would not look engineered. They would look like agreements: worn places where water has said, over immense time, here, here, and here. Floating seeds would move along them. Tiny bladderlike plants would rise and sink nearby. A film of pollen or dust might lie on the surface until a bubble broke it. The runnels would carry not just water but information: mineral traces, spores, larvae, fragments of leaf, the chemical memory of one pool arriving in another. I would be that carrying.

You would find me in the green floors of the pools. From a distance, those pools would look solid—soft green carpets laid over water. Up close, they would be rafts of floating moss, small bladder plants, interwoven roots, and living tissue just strong enough to bear a beetle, a frog, a heron’s cautious foot. Underneath, the water would be dark, cool, and full of suspended particles. Roots would hang like fine wires. Microbial films would coat every submerged surface. Gas bubbles would form on decaying stems, detach, and rise in trembling chains. I would be the tension in those surfaces, the skin that holds, the slow respiration beneath.

You would find me in the peat.

That is where my memory would be. Not in a brain, not in a buried archive, but in compressed layers of partly decayed plants, each layer holding a different chemistry, a different age, a different degree of wetness and heat. The peat would be my long body. It would store water the way a memory stores feeling: unevenly, in gradients, with some parts still warm and active, others colder and more settled. Fungal threads would move through it, carrying nutrients, breaking down stubborn fibers, connecting one patch of growth to another. When a plant died, it would not leave the world. It would be folded inward. When a body sank into moss, it would become part of the plateau’s thickness. I would be that folding-in.

I would not be a creature afraid of death, because in the marsh death would not be an interruption. It would be a transfer.

At dawn, I would be most visible as condensation. Every leaf edge would hold a bead. Every stem would be silvered. Mist would hang at face height, torn into banners by the first small movements of air. The landscape would seem paused, but the pause would be full. The beads would grow. The mist would settle. The first bubbles would rise from warm pockets in the peat. The first insects would lift from the lower vegetation into the colder upper air. I would be the transition from stillness to circulation.

At noon, I would be faint shimmer above dark patches. The sun would warm the black water and the soaked peat while the air remained cool. Warm columns would rise. Cold air would slide in beneath them. The marsh would breathe in slow, invisible drafts. Some districts would brighten into plain green and silver; others would remain under local rain curtains only a few kilometers wide. I would be that weather-making. Not as a god making weather from above, but as a surface making weather from within: exhaling, absorbing, cooling, releasing.

Toward evening, I would be the sharpening smell: wet greenery, mineral stone, faint ozone, the sour sweetness of peat. The cool would become more definite. The water would seem darker. The heron-like forms would become stiller. The rails would move through reed thickets with their impossible quiet. The broad-hoofed grazers would pause on root ridges, their coats damp, their wide feet pressing the moss without breaking it. Predators would not vanish but would become more a matter of pressure than shape: a ripple, a darkening, a sudden absence where something had been. I would be the balance that prevents any one hunger from emptying the whole.

Who or what would I be?

I would be a living machine that was never built.

That word, machine, would need to be handled gently. There would be no metal, no gears, no pipes, no tools, no artifacts. Nothing cut, nothing assembled, nothing imposed. Yet there would be repetition, production, transport, regulation, storage, and waste processing on a vast scale. I would be the industrial character of the place, not as a factory over the land, but as the land’s own productivity become conscious of itself in the smallest possible ways.

I would be a battery made of wetness. After dry, still air, a faint charge would gather in root masses and mineral films. Touch a wet root then, and there might be a prickle—tiny, almost imagined. I would be that potential. Not lightning, not dramatic power, but the marsh’s slow capacity to hold difference: warmth against cold, oxygen against sour depth, rainwater against mineral seep, living surface against buried peat. Life there would not be the absence of gradient but the maintenance of gradient. I would be the maintenance.

I would be a worker with no hands. My labor would be binding sediment, moving seeds, stirring nutrients, clearing fine silt from spiral-stemmed plants, pumping gas through swelling algae bladders, carrying fungal nutrients through root networks, keeping channels open by growth and decay, distributing buoyant seed cases across pool systems, repairing torn moss mats with new growth, turning death into absorbent ground. My factories would be belts of nearly identical sedges, moss districts, reed thickets, fungal networks, insect blooms. My shifts would be dawn condensation, midday shimmer, evening cooling, seasonal pulses of flowering and hatching and seed release.

I would be a courier network without roads. The animals would carry me without knowing. Beetles moving across surface films would transport spores and pollen. Waterfowl grazing in open channels would move seeds in their bodies and mud on their feet. Amphibians resting under floating leaves would disturb larvae and release small clouds of matter. Raptors riding thermals would not be outside the system; their presence would shape the small bodies below, making the marsh more alert, more distributed, more careful. Even death by hunting would become a redistribution. I would be the circulation.

If I had to appear as a single animal, I would not choose the largest or the most visible. I might appear as a smooth newt under a broad leaf, resting in cool shallows while mist thickens. I might appear as a heron-like form standing motionless at the edge of a pool, one leg planted in the mud like an additional stem, not because I was separate from the marsh, but because stillness is one of its highest arts. I might appear as an otterlike body sliding through a deeper channel with minimal disturbance, or as a broad-hoofed grazer moving along a firmer root ridge, cropping moss with methodical patience. But those would be temporary shapes, local condensations of the marsh’s attention. The deeper truth would remain plural.

I would be more truly the film than the frog, more the root web than the heron, more the bubble chain than the bird.

And yet, if you asked where I would find myself, I might answer with a particular place, because even a plural being has places where it gathers most intensely.

I would find myself in a broad, shallow basin near the center of the plateau, where several small runnels meet. The bedrock there would be worn into a series of low lips and pans, not steep, not dramatic, but perfectly balanced by erosion. Water would stand in it only a few fingers deep, but in places the peat would be thick enough to hold a darker pool. Moss would form a continuous skin over mud and stone. Sedges would rise with glossy leaves. A few pale orchids would grow in clusters near a slightly firmer ridge, not as ornaments but as parts of the living press. Mist would lie over the basin in the morning. The sun would open a brief hole over it around midday, making the wet surfaces shine. By evening, a low rain curtain might pass through, local and soft, then dissolve.
