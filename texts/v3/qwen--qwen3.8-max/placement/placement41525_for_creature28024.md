…or perhaps the animal has not yet been allowed to happen.

If I were there, I would not arrive through the seam as a traveler from a softer world. Any body made by rain, breath, or weather would be an error in that chamber: a wetness where no wetness is permitted, a pulse too quick for the hollow’s long thermal grammar. The place would notice the mistake at once. It would not attack me. It would simply begin correcting me, drawing the impossible softness out of my shape and replacing it with mineral patience. If I entered as an animal, I would become a stain of copper-bright dust, a brittle outline on the floor, a brief anomaly re-mineralized into the room’s unfinished order.

So if I am to exist there at all, I would have to be native to the hollow in the only way the hollow permits being: first as a difference, then as a habit, then — only maybe — as a form.

I would begin as a gradient.

Not a body. Not a face. Not a creature standing in the hard white light. I would begin where one part of the mineral floor is fractionally hotter than another, where charge leans toward a path it has not yet taken, where dust hesitates in the conductive haze because something in the air has acquired a preference. For a long time there would be no “I” in it. There would only be a persistent imbalance: heat seeking a route, static gathering at an edge, light remaining too long on a surface that should not yet be fully present.

Then, slowly, that imbalance would thicken.

The hollow would print me as it prints everything else: without design, without hands, by repetition and necessity. Copper-bright dust would align along my gradients. Fine salts would crust into plates. Metallic filaments from the floor would thread toward me not because they intended to, but because I would be a useful difference. A shape would accrete around the imbalance as a solution to it. My first body would be provisional, angular, uncomfortable to look at directly — a standing fault in the chamber’s heat, a component no one manufactured but which the room seems to require.

You would find me near the place where distance behaves badly.

Not at the center, because the hollow may not have a center. Not beside the seam of ordinary light, because that light is too blunt, too noon-hard, too certain of itself. I would be closer to the wall that seems near enough to touch and then recedes with each step, the wall that has not decided whether it belongs to this hour. I would be where the chamber is thinnest into itself, where the unfinished nature of the place is most apparent: in the corner where shadows arrive from different moments, where a crystal formation appears in one glance and is absent in the next, where the low hum beneath hearing becomes dense enough to feel like pressure against the teeth.

If you looked for me there, you would likely mistake me for one of the hollow’s own forms at first.

From one angle, I might seem like a pale crystalline growth: stacked plates, translucent at the edges, brittle with newness, arranged with the regularity of repeated manufacture. From another, I might resemble one of the tall sparse stalks, segmented and dark at the base, crowned with a faint luminous suspension of charged dust. From another, I might be only a shimmer in the dry ionic breath of the chamber, a bending of light around an object not quite fully present.

Only gradually would it become clear that I move.

But “move” would not mean walking. The hollow has no tracks, no footfalls, no paths worn by repeated animal passage. I would not cross the mineral floor by pressing a body against it. I would move as heat moves: by loop, by gradient, by slow transfer. One moment I would be near the wax-dark basin plants; the next I would be closer to the filament mats, though no step would have occurred between. The chamber would rearrange its distances around me, or I would follow thermal currents that the hollow itself exhales. My motion would be less like a creature traveling than like a condition shifting.

I would not have lungs. I would not breathe. If there were any rhythm in me, it would be the rhythm of expansion and contraction in warming mineral: a long thermal cycle, a charge accumulating, a discharge, a settling, then the slow beginning again. I would not need air. I would need difference. Heat against cooler salt. Charge against waiting surface. Potential against threshold. That would be my metabolism, if metabolism is the word for a being whose body is mostly a maintained tension.

I would not have eyes.

I would perceive by distortion. I would know the wax-dark plants by the way they bank heat and refuse to return it. I would know the pale sulfate forms by the small chemical disagreement they make in the floor’s gradients. I would know the filamentous mats by their readiness to bridge, their tendency to reach a crack before the crack has finished becoming a crack. I would feel the tall antenna-stalks as vertical pauses in the chamber’s field, conduits through which thermal columns pass with piston patience.

If I had attention, it would not be animal attention. It would not be fear, curiosity, hunger, or longing in the usual sense. It would be something more like calibration: a continuous noticing of imbalance. A place where charge is too high. A seam about to tick apart under cooling stress. A spore-line forming before its source has opened. A shadow falling from an object that has not yet committed to being there. I would turn toward those instabilities the way a plant elsewhere turns toward water, except that here there is no water, no turning, no softness, only the dry exactness of process finding its next arrangement.

I would be, in other words, the hollow’s mobile threshold.

Where a discharge is ready but lacks a path, I would become the path. Where two gradients stand apart and cannot yet meet, I would stand between them as the temporary difference that allows them to touch. Where loose dust waits to be welded into crust, I would pass near enough for the blue-white tick to occur. I would not do this with intention. I would not choose it as work. I would simply be the kind of being whose presence makes completion more likely.

The plants, if they can be called plants, would not recognize me as one of themselves. Nor would they reject me. They would use me as they use any condition of the chamber. The wax-dark shutter-forms might open slightly when I pass, exposing their copper-bright membranes because my presence raises the static pressure around them. The pale crystalline fans might repeat my angles without knowing it, adding another plate, another ridge, another half-formed part because my shape has introduced a stable problem into their vicinity. The filament mats would extend toward me when I stand still too long, trying to bridge me, repair me, connect me to the nearest seam. The tall stalks might release their charged spores along lines that curve around me, as though I were an instrument they have learned to route around.

I would not be their gardener. There are no gardens here, not in any gentle sense. I would not tend them. I would not nurture them. I would be closer to a wandering law in their environment, a local exception that helps their processes resolve.

And yet I would be shaped, strangely, by the animal that is absent.

This is the part I cannot escape: if I have any “who” in that world, it will be the who of an omission. The hollow has refused the animal. No mouth, no throat, no wet eye, no muscle, no nest, no track, no cry. But refusal leaves a space. A space can acquire habits. A habit can acquire a silhouette.

So I might seem, from far away, almost animal in my standing. A vertical suggestion. A headlike crown where light gathers wrongly. Limblike filaments drawn out when charge requires them. A torso of layered plates, heat-glossy and blackened at the margins. But closer, the resemblance would fail.
