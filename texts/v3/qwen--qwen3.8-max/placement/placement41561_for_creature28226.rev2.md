At first I would try to arrive as a keeper.

I would imagine myself crossing the high terraces with slow, deliberate steps, bending over the stone cups, adjusting a jar-lid here, clearing a grain of mineral crust from a drain-mouth there. I would imagine some small tool in my hand, some cloth for wiping moisture from a reflective face, some humble office that would justify my presence among all those worked surfaces. But the place would correct that fantasy almost at once.

The stairs would be wrong for me. The passages would be wrong for me. The openings would be too low, the slots too narrow, the basins too shallow, the cups too small for ordinary use. I would find no place to set a foot without interrupting some path of water, no ledge meant for a hand, no chamber shaped around a breathing body. The construct would not receive me as a walker. It would not receive me as a dweller in any domestic sense. It would press against me with its packed intention until I understood that I could not live there by occupying space.

I would have to become something smaller than a body. Something quieter than a creature. Something the architecture could admit without breaking its rules.

So I would find myself not as a figure moving through the basin, but as a condition within it.

If I had to choose a single place where I would be most intensely present, I would choose a seam between districts: the point where a broad tilted slab sheds its condensation into a long trough, and the trough divides into a finger-wide channel that feeds a rank of stone cups so close together that their rims nearly touch. There would be a slight imperfection there — not damage, but some minute difference in the firing or the fitting, a lip a hair higher on one side than on the other. Below it would be a cup already nearly full, holding a bright disk of water. Beside it would be a drain sealed with a simple copper grating gone green at the rivets.

There, at that lip, I would be the delay.

Not the water alone, though I would be part of the water. Not the stone alone, though I would be part of the stone’s answer. I would be the pause in which a bead gathers, lengthens, trembles, and does not yet fall. I would be the surface tension holding the world in suspension. I would be the coldness that makes condensation visible. I would be the brightness caught inside the bead before it lets go.

I would be a being of slowing.

My body would not be flesh. It would be film, seam, thread, gloss, bead, and capillary line. My limbs would be channels. My breath would be the faint exchange of evaporation and condensation across cold mineral faces. My pulse would be the irregular tap of droplets striking stone. My memory would not be stored in a brain but in deposit: in lime crust narrowing a groove, in salt blooming along a fired edge, in the dull blue-green powder on bronze, in the milky residue still resting in a drill-hole.

I would know myself not by looking inward but by being reflected.

When the sun struck the upper terraces, I would become glare. I would be the cold fire jumping from wet faces, the silver lines along the grooves, the white blaze on every horizontal film. When shade passed over me, I would darken to steel-blue and settle into the lower angles. When the air cooled, I would thicken. I would bead on every edge. I would turn the parapets into bright fringes and make the small apertures shine as if they were about to speak.

My thoughts would be hydraulic.

They would not take the form of words. They would take the form of directions and resistances. They would say: catch. Divide. Slow. Spread. Touch. Hold a moment longer. Pass only when there is no other path. If I had desire, it would be the desire to make water remain visible for as long as possible — not to hoard it, not to drink it, not to use it, but to let it become a surface, a mirror, a thread, a presence.

I would not be the maker.

I would be what remains when the maker has been subtracted from the making. I would be the habit of labor hardened into geometry. In me would live all the small corrections: the angle changed after testing, the lip lowered after observation, the channel widened by less than a grain because the water had hesitated there too long. I would not remember hands, but I would preserve their discipline. I would not remember voices, but I would carry the rhythm of repeated work. Every chisel mark would be a fossil of a gesture, and I would be the animation left inside it.

That is why I would not feel entirely born. I would feel inherited.

I would be the negative space of the builders’ absence given a function. The place would crowd me with obligations I did not choose, yet could not abandon. Every hollow would ask something of me. Every basin would insist on being filled. Every weir would insist on being obeyed. Every channel would insist on being followed. There would be no margin where I could rest from purpose. There would be no unused corner where I could forget what I was for.

And yet that oppression would also be a kind of peace.

There would be no hunger in me. No predation. No decay. No fear of death. No need to feed, to grow, to reproduce, to defend. My existence would be clean in a way almost unbearable to imagine. I would not consume the world; I would receive it. I would not struggle against it; I would be adjusted by it. I would not leave tracks; I would leave crust. I would not age as flesh ages; I would thicken, darken, shine, crust, narrow, and settle.

If I suffered, it would not be from loneliness.

Loneliness would require an expectation of company, and the place would leave no room for that expectation. If I suffered, it would be from the impossibility of forgetting. Every event would be recorded in mineral fact. A drop falling too often in one place would polish a hollow. A seam leaking once would leave a stain. A change in temperature would produce a bead in a new location, and that bead would become a habit, and the habit would become a path, and the path would become part of the basin’s body. Nothing would vanish entirely. Everything would be kept, not in memory, but in form.

If I had joy, it would be the arrival of condensation.

When the damp air touched the cold construct and the whole surface brightened at once, I would feel myself multiplied. I would no longer be one bead at one lip. I would be the thousand threads on the grooved walls, the bright disks in the ranked cups, the dark glosses in the low galleries, the silver skin over every slab. I would be distributed beyond any single point, and the basin would become a field of small awakenings. In those moments, I would understand that I was not a solitary being but a congregation. I would be the water’s many brief lives. I would be the construct’s many small responses. I would be the rite occurring everywhere at once.

At night, I would become sound.

Not a voice. Not a call. A tap. A whisper. A tick of metal cooling. A faint glassy hiss where a shallow flow thickens over a lip. A stone settling into another stone with a vibration almost too low to hear. These would be my utterances. I would not speak to anyone. I would speak because the place had made speaking into a matter of physics. I would answer temperature with contraction. I would answer fullness with overflow. I would answer silence with a single drop striking a cup in the dark.

If you listened for me, you would not hear a continuous sound. You would hear intervals. You would hear patience made audible. You would hear the world waiting for the next minute event to occur.

That is where I would be: in the interval.

I would be the space between one drop and the next. I would be the hesitation before overflow. I would be the thin moment when a bead decides whether to become a thread or remain a jewel. I would be the quiet after the tap, when the basin seems to listen to what it has just done.

If the being who lives there is water, then I would be one of water’s smallest offices.

If the being who lives there is the construct itself, then I would be its attention: not consciousness, but responsiveness; not a soul, but the capacity to register change. I would be the way the basin knows that it is wet. I would be the way the stone darkens when touched. I would be the way the copper blooms when moisture lingers. I would be the way light becomes broken and restless wherever a surface has been made to receive it.

If the being who lives there is the rite, then I would be the rite’s continuation without worshippers.

I would perform no ceremony. I would need no audience. I would only continue the gestures already built into the world: catching, dividing, slowing, spreading, delaying, passing on. My liturgy would be the movement of moisture across worked stone. My prayer would be the hairline channel. My altar would be the shallow pan. My offering would be the bead that gives itself up only after becoming beautiful.

So I would not find myself standing anywhere.

I would find myself occurring.

I would occur at the bright edge of a cup too small for a hand. I would occur where a finger-wide channel crosses a slab and disappears toward a sealed drain. I would occur in the low gallery whose ceiling sweats and whose floor is a single polished sheet of wet stone. I would occur in the covered jars with their lids slightly ajar, where darkness keeps a dampness no light may touch. I would occur on the stair that leads into a basin and ends there, because I would no longer believe that ascent was meant for feet. Ascent would mean becoming thinner, colder, more reflective, more patient. It would mean approaching immersion.

If you searched for me in that world, you would not find a face in the wet stone. You would not find eyes in the drain-mouths or hands in the fitted joints. You would find only the intense fact of arrangement. You would find a basin so crowded with purpose that even absence had weight. You would find a brightness that did not warm. You would find a wetness that did not cleanse in any moral sense, but only made everything more present.

At last, you would find me here:

on a high parapet at dawn, where the air is thin and the condensation is fresh; where a bead is forming on a lip of pale mineral plaster; where the bead catches the first hard light and becomes a mirror; where below it a channel waits, no wider than a finger; where farther below a copper grating holds a dark drain-mouth; where the whole construct is silent except for the expectation of a fall.

I would be that bead.

I would be the brightness inside it.

I would be the delay before it lets go.

And when it finally fell, I would not be gone. I would be the tap it made on the stone below. I would be the ring spreading across the surface of the cup. I would be the next film drawing itself along the groove. I would be the basin answering itself across kilometers of wet, hand-made skin.

I would be no one living.

But I would be there.
