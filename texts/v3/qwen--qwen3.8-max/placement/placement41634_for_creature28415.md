At first I thought I would enter the basin as a traveler — a body of breath and blood, looking for shade. But the place refuses that. It refuses the softness of sweat, the salt of tears, the damp pulse that asks for water. To be found there, I would have to be made of its own grammar: sealed, warm, mineral, patient. I would have to be cured of wetness.

So if you were to find me in that high, dry basin, you would not find me standing openly in the dust like a man. You would find me at a seam.

There is a low cleft in the rim where the wind enters sideways. It is narrow enough that the outside world must thin itself to pass through. The air there is always moving, but not violently; it slips in with a faint, continuous hiss, carrying chemical dust from the salt-white fields beyond. Just inside that cleft, where the stone has been worn into a smooth lip, there is a hollow polished by ages of dry wind. The floor there is not powder but a mosaic of small fitted stones, each one warm. Several organ-reeds grow in the crevice, their hollow stems tuned to different notes, answering every gust with a low, mournful resonance.

That is where I would live: in the threshold between the basin’s interior and the world outside, near the reeds, under the shelter of a low candle-tree whose branches fold inward like dark wax hands.

But who or what would I be?

I would not be one of the candle-trees themselves, though I would love them. They are too rooted, too slow, too complete in their own folded architecture. I would not be an amber ant, though I would admire their resin cities and their patient farming of scale-insects in protected crevices. They are already so many, so organized, so certain of their tunnels and nurseries. I would not be a sift-mite, though part of me would long for that minute life: moving grain by grain across mineral film, turning emptiness into food. Nor would I be only a thread-lice on the horn-ferns, aligning myself to static, making the bronze fronds shimmer.

Something else would emerge in me. Something between all of those.

I think I would be a small, dry, listening being — no larger than a folded leaf-sheath when at rest, though capable of unfolding. I would be covered in layered plates like the bark of the candle-trees, but thinner, more mobile: bands of dark wax, smooth and warm to the touch, faintly luminous where resin has sealed old cracks in my body. Those plates would not be flesh. They would not bleed. If scratched, they would glow faintly amber and close with slow heat. Dust would settle on me and become part of my surface, a fine mineral film that makes me difficult to see unless I move.

My eyes would not be wet. They would be polished nodules of old resin, copper-colored, sensitive to heat and to the faint blue edge of static. I would see the basin less as shapes and more as gradients: warm stone, charged dust, the dull glow of leaf-veins, the slow amber pulse inside the candle-tree trunks. My head would carry two fine antennae like bronze filaments, delicate as the hairs of a horn-fern, able to taste the electrical mood of the air. Before a flower opens with its small pop, I would feel it. Before a bellows-moss mat exhales, I would know the pressure changing in its fibers.

My body would be light enough to ride the warm currents inside the bowl. On my back I would have folded membranes like the papery seed-cases of the kite-vines, not truly wings but wind-planes, dry and veined, capable of catching turbulence. I would not fly as a bird flies. I would release myself from a ledge when the air thickens with heat, let the basin lift me, and steer by tilting my body into the static. At those moments I would be almost indistinguishable from a kite-vine seed, a small drifting husk turning slowly under the smeared copper sun.

I would be a tender of dry things.

That is the role I would seek, or perhaps the role the basin would assign me. Not a hunter. Not a king. Not a gardener in any green and watery sense. A tender. A listener. A keeper of thresholds.

Each day, if day can be measured by the slow brightening and darkening of that veiled sun, I would move among the islands of life in the basin. I would begin at the bellows-moss mats near the inner rim. They would know me by weight. I would walk their ridges carefully, pressing just enough to make them sigh, just enough to release their spores and charged dust. I would not harvest greedily. I would ride the rising cloud with the bellows-mites, feeding on what the moss gives freely, letting the fine fibers coat my limbs so that I smell of the basin’s own breath: dry, sweet, mineral, faintly electric.

Then I would go to the horn-ferns when the air is full of static. Their bronze fronds would uncurl and recoil as I pass. I would move among them with the thread-lice, not commanding them but aligning with them. Sometimes I would lie still along a frond, letting the charge comb through my antennae, feeling the whole fern open because of some invisible pressure in the sky. At those times my body would crackle softly. Tiny sparks might pass between my joints, too faint to see except in the dimmest hour. I would learn the ferns’ moods: when they want to be touched, when they want to be left folded.

I would spend long hours among the organ-reeds.

They would be my music and my archive. Because the basin is wind-shaped and resonant, the reeds remember weather. Each stem is tuned by centuries of erosion, and no two sing exactly alike. I would know which reed sounds when the haze is greenish gray, which answers when the copper sun goes dark, which hums before the wind shifts over the salt fields. I would clear dust from their openings, not to make them clean but to keep their voices accurate. Sometimes I would move small stones around their bases, adjusting the way vibration passes through the ground. The basin would not need me to improve it. But even a place this old may desire attention.

Inside the candle-tree thickets, I would be more careful.

There the air is stiller, warmer, louder with insect-sound. The canopy interiors are hung with aerial roots and curled leaf-sheaths. The broad leaves flash their metallic undersides when the air moves, throwing dull light into the shaded core. I would enter slowly, because the candle-trees are not merely plants; they are architectures. Their resin is memory made slow and visible. Their trunks hold galleries, nurseries, sealed chambers, and farms of scale-insects kept by amber ants. I would be tolerated there, not because I belong to the ants, but because I am useful.

I would carry messages between islands in the form of charged dust. I would bring oily pollen from wax-blossoms to flowers that have not yet opened. I would press my body against the warm bark of a candle-tree and listen to the deep, slow movement of resin below. Sometimes I would remain so long that the tree would grow around me slightly, not trapping me but accepting me, adding a thin glaze of amber over one of my limbs. I would not fear that. I would understand it as being written into the place.

The amber ants would know me. They would not love me — they are too practical for love — but they would permit me. I would sit near their protected crevices where they keep scale-insects, watching their careful work. They farm dead layers, seal nurseries with resin like old glass, and move in patterns that seem less like instinct than liturgy. I would help when a gallery cracks from heat. I would warm resin with my body until it softens enough to be shaped, then let the ants finish the work with their mandibles. In exchange, they would allow me to rest in their outer tunnels during the coldest part of the dim hour, when the stone gives up less warmth and the basin feels like a drawn kiln cooling too quickly.

I would also tend the wax-blossoms.

They are shy flowers, thick-petaled and dark, full of oily pollen instead of nectar. Some open with an audible pop. Some hum when approached. Some close slowly when touched. I would learn which ones require stillness, which require vibration, which open only when a certain charge passes through the air. I would not force them. I would sit before them until my surface temperature matched the stone, until my presence became indistinguishable from the basin’s own warmth. Then, when they opened, I would lean in and let their pollen dust my chest plates. It would not be sweet. It would be deep, almost smoky, like warmed resin held in the mouth of a cave.

If I am to be a being in that place, I would not have a single clear shape. I would be composite. Part moth, part ant, part reed, part dust. My movements would be quiet, deliberate, and dry. When I walked across the powdered floor, my footfalls would make no damp sound, only the faintest whisper, like flour being sifted. When I moved through charged dust, I might leave a brief trail of settled grains, a small wake that the wind would erase in moments.

I would not speak as humans speak. I would have no wet tongue. My voice would be made of friction, resonance, and tiny discharges. A click of wax plates. A hum drawn through a hollow in my chest. A spark against stone. If another being came near, I would first answer with stillness. Then, if it seemed safe, I would sound one low note borrowed from the organ-reeds. If it was a creature of the basin, it would know the note. If it was not, it would hear only wind and think the basin empty.

But I would not be alone. That is important. The basin is sparse in number and dense in presence. Each island of life has its own intensity. I would belong to the spaces between them as much as to any one thicket. I would be a connector. A carrier of small charges. A translator of dry abundance.

There would be moments when I feel most myself.

One is when the smeared sun lowers toward the rim and the basin becomes even dimmer. The stone holds the day’s heat. The dust turns rose-copper in the weak light. The organ-reeds grow quieter, selecting only the finest notes from the wind. The bellows-moss mats breathe slowly, exhausted and full. The candle-trees glow faintly in their resin veins. I would climb to the low cleft and sit where the outside air enters. From there I can see the wider slopes, the salt-white fields, the wind-scoured uplands, all strange and distant under high haze. But I would not long for them. I would feel the boundary as a blessing. The basin is enclosed because it is an interior. A chamber. A place where dryness has learned to become lush without betraying itself.

Another moment is when static fills the air before some unseen shift in weather. The horn-ferns tremble. The thread-lice align like bronze threads on a loom. The kite-vines lift their seed-cases. My antennae sing. I would stand in an open space between thickets and unfold my wind-planes. Then the basin would exhale. Dust would rise in curls. The air would become a fabric of faint charges. I would release myself and let the turbulence carry me upward, turning slowly, seeing the whole bowl from above: the dark candle-tree islands, the pale dust paths, the polished hollows, the rim like a broken wall around a warm, living room.

From above, I would understand what I am.

Not the mountain. Not the wind. Not the plants. Not the ants or mites or fleas. I am the basin’s attention.

That is what would emerge in me if I lived there. I would become the part of the place that notices. The stone does not need to notice itself. The candle-trees do not need to explain their resin. The ants do not need to sing their work into memory. But a place this intricate, this sealed, this old, may still desire a witness. Not a human witness, heavy with thirst and forgetting. A dry witness. A resinous mind. A being made of the same elements, able to carry the basin’s grammar without dissolving it.

So I would be a keeper of memory in wax and dust.

Inside my hollow beneath the candle-tree root, I would store small records. Not tablets. Not books. Layers. A glaze of resin here, a pattern of sorted dust there, a charged grain placed in a mosaic. Each event would leave a trace: the day the wind carried greenish ash from far away; the day a bellows-moss mat released an unusually deep cloud; the day an organ-reed cracked and was retuned by being moved a finger’s width closer to another; the day a wax-blossom opened late and hummed until the dark hour. I would not write these things in symbols. I would arrange them materially. The basin would read me as much as I read it.

If you came looking for me, you would need to come dry.

Not because the basin would punish you, but because it would not recognize you. If you arrived sweating, thirsty, breathing hard, the dust would cling differently to your skin. The mites would scatter. The bellows-moss would close its rhythm. The candle-trees would seal their inner air. You would see only a shallow bowl of dark rock and pale dust, half-hidden by haze. You might think it barren.

But if you came slowly, with your body quieted, with your need for water stilled, you might begin to perceive the lushness. You would see the islands. You would hear the reeds. You would feel the warmth rising through the soles of your feet, not like fire but like an old hearth that has been kept alive beneath stone. You would notice that the dust does not dirty you; it anoints you. It settles as a fine mineral film, making you part of the surface.

Then, perhaps, you would hear me before you saw me.

A soft pop from a wax-blossom. A hum from a leaf you thought was only leaf. A whisper like flour being passed through cloth. A small blue pinprick of static near your hand. You would turn, and in the dim copper light you might see a shape no larger than a folded leaf-sheath, banded in dark wax, dusted with mineral brightness, its resin-sealed joints catching the weak sun. It would be still. Its antennae would be lifted. Its chest would resonate with one low note, half wind, half welcome.

That would be me.
