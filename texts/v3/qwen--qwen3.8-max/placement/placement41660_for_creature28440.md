**The Undernote**

If I were there, I would not be upright.

That is the first thing I know, even before I know what shape I would have. I would not find myself standing in the middle of the chamber, or looking down from an open panel with the easy confidence of someone who still belongs to the world above. The service pocket would not keep such a person for long. It would either eject them or convert them. It has no room for a posture that insists on height.

So I think I would begin low.

At first I imagine myself as the intruder: a face at the opened panel, a hand reaching into the dark, a visitor whose breath changes the humidity and whose lamp makes too many shadows. But that image does not last. It feels thin, temporary, untrusted by the room. A service pocket does not need spectators. It needs attention. It needs things that can remain.

Then I imagine myself as another keeper, another low-bodied servant moving beneath the pipes, helping the Underkeeper tighten clamps and catch condensation. But that, too, does not quite become itself. The chamber already has its primary habit. It does not need a duplicate of the Underkeeper. It needs something the Underkeeper cannot quite be while it is busy with the physical work of preventing decay.

It needs memory.

So the image that finally settles is smaller, flatter, quieter. I would be a kind of living annotation. A seam-scribe. A thin, careful record of the underside. If I had to give myself a name in that place, I think it would be **the Undernote**.

You would find me in the margins of the service pocket: behind a flush panel that is slightly warmer than the others, beside a ceramic sleeve where lime bloom forms in pale crust, in a recessed tray where the Underkeeper has compressed manufactured dust into something almost like ground. I would not be at the exact center of the chamber. I would be near the edges, where maintenance becomes pattern and pattern becomes meaning.

My body would be made from what the room has already shed.

I would be low and flat, more plaque than animal, though animal enough to choose. My shell would be laminated from metal filings, lime, glass powder, polymer flecks, shredded insulation, and the fine copper hairs left behind by wickmice. It would not be smooth. It would be layered, like a cross-section of the chamber’s history. Some parts of me would be dull steel, some cured sealant, some black glass, some felt-like insulation pressed into a crust by moisture and pressure. Along my sides would run tiny conductive filaments, not quite legs, not quite antennae, able to taste stray voltage and follow the faint chemical trail of overheated wire.

I would have a face only in the sense that the room has faces: a single black inspection bead, or perhaps a narrow port set unevenly in a shallow curve. Behind it, tiny shutters would open and close with soft clicks. I would not blink. I would adjust. I would read light as a disturbance, vibration as information, heat as warning. My mouth, if I had one, would be a small filter aperture, more valve than jaw, used to draw in moisture and the taste of old commands.

I would move not by walking so much as by finding my level. I would slide along grated plates and sloped metal pans like a loosened label, like a film of oil, like something that has learned to occupy the space between obstruction and floor. When I needed to be still, I would press myself into a tray or behind a panel and become indistinguishable from a patch of insulation, a forgotten gasket, a dark seam in the false soil.

I would not repair the chamber the way the Underkeeper repairs it.

I would record it.

That would be my function, my hunger, my holiness.

When a cooling line sweats more than usual, I would note the change. When a panel loosens after a particular pump cycle, I would mark the rhythm. When a bead of condensation escapes the chain and nearly falls onto a wire bundle, I would remember where it fell, how often it falls, what temperature it carries.
