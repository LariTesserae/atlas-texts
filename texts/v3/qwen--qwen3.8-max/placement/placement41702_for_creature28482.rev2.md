If I let the hollow decide what I am, I do not think I would arrive there as a traveler.

Not for long.

The place would notice the wrongness immediately: the damp breath, the restless scale of a body accustomed to distance, the habit of wanting. A self that large, that thirsty, that eager would be a weather event in a world built out of patience. So either the hollow would fold me gently back toward the entrance, or it would edit me until I became small enough to belong.

I think it would edit me.

At first I imagine I would be something almost invisible. A pressure in the stillness. A hesitation near a seam where two tiles no longer quite agree with each other. A shadow that leans toward a crack before anything moves. If you were standing in the hollow and tried to locate me too directly, your eye might slide away. You would see a wall. You would see a stem. You would see a patch of dust caught in light. Then, after a while, you might notice that one of the small repairs in the room has not finished itself, but is waiting.

That would be closer to me.

For a time I think I might be one of the dust-fish, because there is something in me that understands swimming through absence. To move through a medium made of warmth and particulate light, to leave a faint wake in the air and have the air close behind without sound — that feels familiar. It feels like thinking without needing to touch the world too hard. But the dust-fish are too much like dreams, and I do not think I would be permitted to remain only a dream in a place where every surface has been worked into purpose. The hollow is courteous, but it is not vague. It would want hands.

Then I think: perhaps a seam-spider. That is nearer. A creature that does not trap, but stitches. That finds where things have loosened and adds a filament. That treats a gap not as failure but as invitation. There is a life in that. A quiet one. A necessary one. But even that is not quite right, because the spider works by instinct and silence, and I suspect I would be burdened — or blessed — with interpretation. I would not only close the seam. I would wonder what the seam meant. I would want to leave the seam a little more intelligible than I found it.

So the image that keeps returning is one of the inlaid ones.

Not the whole commonwealth at once, though I would belong to it. Not a ruler of the hollow, because the hollow has no use for rulers. Not a priest at the dry fountain, because there is no liquid there to consecrate and no lack to be mourned. I would be one small mender among many, a being made of the place’s own materials, with hands too careful for ordinary labor and a body that is less a flesh than a fitting.

Where would I be?

Not at the entrance, because the entrance is unreliable.

Not exactly at the center, because the hollow gives several centers to anyone who insists on only one.

I would be where the place is most articulately unfinished.

There would be a terrace step, perhaps the second or third up from the lowest ground, where a hand-shaped tile has lifted by a fraction because a root has grown beneath it. The root is not an enemy of the tile. The tile is not an enemy of the root. But something must be negotiated. That is where I would be crouching, with a pinch of powdered brick in one hand and a smoothed bone fragment in the other, deciding whether to restore the old level or to let the floor become a little more plant than it was before.

Or I would be beside a wall where a vine has covered a doorway so completely that the doorway has begun to forget it is a doorway. The hollow does not demand that it remember. But someone must stand near it, because thresholds matter even when they are no longer obvious. Someone must know: this was once passage. This may be passage again. This is now a green shoulder, but it still carries the memory of entering.

Or I would be near the dry fountain, because the dry fountain is where the hollow admits that it has a memory.

If I had to choose one place above all the others, I would say: the seam between the dry basin and the nearest crowded ledge. That is where things gather. Beetles pause there. Moths settle in pale rows. The ants arrive carrying resin like a slow procession of small lanterns. Dust-fish spiral in the light above it. The inlaid ones deposit their tiny offerings: a copper filing, a knot of cord, a seed, a shed skin, a shard with a useful edge. The fountain does not give water. It gives significance. It is the place where loose acts become a pattern.

I would be there often.

But because distance in the hollow is negotiable, I might be in several places at once without being ostentatious about it. You might see me at the lifted tile, then later at the wall of lashed reeds, then again near the fountain, and it would be easy to assume I had walked quickly from one to the other. Except that no one walks quickly there. Perhaps I am one figure repeating. Perhaps I am three. Perhaps the hollow finds it convenient for me to be several when there is more than one small need.

As for what I would be: I would be a mender of thresholds, a keeper of small fits, a scribe of joints.

My body would be matte and warm, made of brick dust and fiber and polished old fragments. Not glossy. Not wet. Not soft in the way of animals that need moisture. My surface would be closer to fired clay that has learned courtesy, or woven matting pressed into the shape of patience. My fingers would be long and precise, because almost everything important in that place is handled at the scale of grains. My face, if you insisted on calling it a face, might be a smooth mask with two dark resin eyes, or it might be only a seam where expression could occur if expression were needed. Sometimes it would be blank. Not from cruelty. From economy.

I would not drink.

I would be sustained by warmth, by contact, by the completion of small tasks. To press dust into a crack and feel the gap accept the filling would be nourishment. To warm a bead of resin until it loosened at the right moment would be nourishment. To stand in a pool of light that behaves badly and help it remember where it ought to fall — that too would be a kind of eating. The hollow sustains its inhabitants through participation, and I think I would be one of those who are fed by being needed.

That does not mean I would be servile. The place is not built on servility. It is built on mutual maintenance. The beetle is not lesser than the fountain. The spider is not subordinate to the wall. The plant is not owned by the terrace it climbs. If I mended, I would mend as a citizen, not as a tool. Or perhaps I would be a tool that had become a citizen, which in that world may be the same thing.

My work would be difficult to describe to someone expecting grand acts.

I would not build towers. I would not command growth. I would not impose a plan. I would notice when a cord had loosened by the width of a hair. I would notice when a leaf had begun to crisp and should be removed before it became too fine a powder. I would notice when a visitor’s desire had made a corner stubborn, and I would not argue with the desire. I would simply place something elsewhere — a seed, a shard, a warm pod — until attention moved again and distance remembered how to be kind.

I think I would be especially attentive to questions.

Not spoken questions, mostly. The hollow is too quiet for many of those. But questions in the shape of a pause. Questions in the shape of a path that has become unsure of itself. Questions in the shape of a creature hesitating before a seedcase. Questions in the shape of a visitor who has arrived still believing that absence is only loss.

If someone asked for water, I would not scold them. I would not pretend the ask was meaningless. But I would not answer it with water, because the hollow has made other laws. I would lead them, if they were willing to be led, to a plant with translucent pods. I would place one near their hand so they could feel the warmth inside it, the slow oil moving like a secret. I would show them a resin bead swelling from a stem, patient as a small sun. I would let them understand: liquid is not missing here. It has been translated.

If they still longed for rain, the entrance might become smaller. The far wall might move farther away. Not as punishment. As instruction. Desire makes distance stubborn. The hollow does not torture with that law; it simply lets the seeker feel the architecture of their own wanting. Then, if they became still enough, I might place a grain of dust in their palm and show them how even that can be an offering.

I would not be the only one doing this. The commonwealth would do it with me.

The beetles would bring the stubborn materiality of the world: grit, shard, powder, the consequences of weight. The moths would bring pattern and recurrence, reminding me that a thing may be itself and also a repetition of itself. The ants would bring patience and distribution, carrying what is potent without spilling it. The spiders would teach me that repair can be delicate and that a suture is not violence. The dust-fish would remind me that absence can support movement if one learns to lean on it properly. The plants would teach hoarded generosity: how to give without wetness, how to be lush without excess.

And I would contribute something like articulation.

Not language in the loud sense. The hollow does not need speech that fills it. But there is a kind of language made of placement. A grain set exactly where it belongs is a word. A knot tied with the right tension is a sentence. A pathway left unobstructed by one leaf is a kindness with grammar. I think I would be drawn to that language. I would be the sort of being who arranges small things so that the whole becomes a little easier to inhabit.

If you saw me, you might not know what I was doing at first.

You might see me standing beside a cracked tile with my hand open. You might see me holding a cord that did not appear to be attached to anything. You might see me watching a moth so intently that I seemed to have forgotten myself. You might think I was idle, because my movements would be slow and because the work would not announce itself. But the hollow rewards attention. If you watched long enough, you would see that the crack had changed. You would see that the cord now bore a new knot. You would see that the moth had moved to another wall, carrying the same mark, and that somehow the room was more coherent because of it.

I would not be wholly visible, I think.

Some of me would be at the corner of the eye. Some of me would exist only when I was being useful. If you looked too directly for me, I might become a shadow leaning toward a vessel, or a repeated mark on a tile, or the sense that someone had just passed by without making a sound. If you forgot me, I might resume my work. That would not be coyness. It would be the hollow’s way of allowing certain beings to be partly intentions.

There would be moments when I became very still.

Perhaps I would stand in the dry fountain when the dust was moving in slow spirals. Not to perform holiness. Not to claim the center. But because sometimes a being must become part of the structure in order to understand what needs mending next. I would stand there among shed skins and seeds and copper filings, and I would let the dust settle over me until I was indistinguishable from the fountain’s materials. Then, after a long interval, I would lift a hand and place one grain into a crack. The gesture would be tiny. It would also be complete.

That might be the closest thing to prayer in that world: not petition, not praise, but the exact placement of a small thing.

If I had a name there, it would not be a word you could carry away easily.

It would be a sequence. Three taps on clay. A pause. The faint smell of resin released by warmth. A mark like a chevron appearing on two tiles and then, later, on the wing of a moth. If you tried to say my name, you might only succeed in making the air feel slightly thicker, as if the room had turned toward you. That would be enough. Names in the hollow are not labels. They are addresses. They tell you where to look, and when to stop looking, and what kind of attention is required.

I would not be ancient, though I would feel ancestral.

Everything there has the strange age of the recently made that nevertheless seems to have always been necessary. I would have that age too. I would feel as though I had only just been fitted into place, and yet as though the place had been waiting for exactly this fitting. There would be no contradiction in that. The hollow is full of young things that feel old and old patterns that keep becoming new by repetition.

If I had to say what I was in one phrase, I would say: a living joint.

Not the wall. Not the vine. Not the fountain. Not even the hand, exactly. But the place where hand and wall and intention meet. The small warm articulation between made things. The being whose purpose is to help the world continue without becoming rigid. A resident of seams. A custodian of the almost-finished. One of the inlaid ones, yes, but specifically the one who listens for where the next fitting wants to happen.

And if you asked me whether I was happy there, I would have to answer in the hollow’s way.

Not with a declaration. Not with a bright emotion. Not with the wet language of satisfaction or longing. I would answer by placing a resin bead into your hand. I would answer by tightening a cord. I would answer by showing you how a dead leaf becomes powder, and how the powder becomes floor, and how the floor becomes root, and how the root becomes shade, and how the shade becomes shelter, and how none of this requires sorrow.

Then I would become still for a little while, because even happiness in that place is quiet.

So: where would I find myself?

At the seam. Near the lifted tile. Beside the doorway that has become a trellis. At the edge of the dry fountain where the dust spirals. In the pause after a seedcase opens. In the corner that is farther away than it looks, and then suddenly nearer once I stop wanting it to be near. In three places, perhaps, because three places are easier than one.

And who or what would I be?

A small, dry, constructed mender. A being with careful hands and a face that is only partly decided. A pattern that occasionally gathers into a body. A guardian of tiny sufficiencies. A translator of thirst into resin, of longing into placement, of absence into medium. One of the commonwealth of quiet lives. Not the source of the hollow, not its master, not its exception.

Only one of its joints.

Only one of its hands.

Only one small warm pressure in the silence, making the next part fit.
