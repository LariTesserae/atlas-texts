They are not one film but many, laid down in order and continually revised. The first skin is bacterial, almost too fine to see: cells that arrive with the treated water, find a bolt head or a weld seam, and make it tacky enough for other things to hold. They feed on trace organics left after filtration, on shed skin from workers, on minute oils too stubborn for the separators, on the faint carbon haze that persists in any occupied water. Their waste changes the charge of metal and makes the next layer easier to place.

Where iron is exposed, iron-loving kinds bloom in orange freckles. Where flow is sluggish, sulfate-reducers make black velvet in the angles between plate and bracket. Where light reaches, diatoms lay down glassy brown-green pastures, and cyanobacteria add a darker, oil-slick sheen. In cooler regions the films are thin and slow. Around warm pump galleries they thicken quickly, becoming slick, then felted, then difficult to scrape without tools.

These films are the sea’s first metabolism. They condition the boundary where structure ends and life begins. They trap particles, release sugars, consume oxygen in tiny pockets, and produce the faint sour-mineral smell that divers notice when they lift a helmet near a fouled hatch. They also corrode, insulate, clog, and betray sensors. For the engineers, they are a standing problem. For everything else, they are pasture, nursery, and glue.

No surface stays clean for long. That is why the maintenance war is endless. Scrapers move along rail channels on timed circuits. Brushes rotate inside intake throats. Dosing gates release short-lived biocides that vanish before the main water body can taste them. Ultrasonic emitters make certain panels unpleasant to settle on. Heat pulses cook selected pipe galleries between shifts. Still, the films return. They are patient because they are numerous, and they are numerous because the sea is made of surfaces.

After the films come the settlers.

Larvae arrive in sealed containers: mussel spat on ropes, barnacle cyprids in chilled bottles, tubeworm fragments in aerated drums, anemone buds packed in gel, bryozoan chips, algal plugs. Some are introduced with ceremony. Most are logged as infrastructure. They are meant to do work. Mussels stabilize flow near sensor arrays and polish the water by filtering suspended fines. Barnacles colonize high-current edges where other animals cannot hold, converting plankton into dense, crunchy biomass. Tubeworms bind loose scale into crusts. Anemones and hydroids take the warmer niches near pump housings, where their tentacles open in the current like small, indifferent hands.

These settlers make the first true texture. A bolt head becomes a knob of shells. A seam becomes a ridge. A grated walkway grows a fringe of weed and calcified tubes. The sharp industrial grammar of the place begins, very slightly, to blur. Not much. The sea is still young enough that every curve can be traced to a tool or a casting. But where a cluster of mussels dies and breaks apart, shell fragments collect in the low places. Mixed with rust flakes, dead film, molted limbs, and the gray floc of filtered water, they form the first hint of sediment.

It is not sand. It is not mud, not yet. It is a gritty, metallic, biological powder, the beginning of a floor that no longer has to be only a floor.

Then come the movers.

Amphipods and isopods are among the first, because they can live in the film’s margins and do not require a complicated world. They browse bacterial lawns, shred settled organic matter, and hide in the spaces between cable clamps. Some were seeded deliberately; others came in with the last rinse of a hatchery tank. Snails and limpets are introduced where algal growth threatens light panels. Chitons graze the broad ceramic plates with slow, rasping patience. Polychaetes build in cracks, fan out feeding crowns where current is steady, and vanish into tubes when a shadow passes.

Shrimp gather near warm seams, where their larvae can develop faster, and become important prey wherever fish are established. Crabs, where permitted, work the trench edges, turning debris, opening weak shells, leaving behind the neat little middens that divers learn to read as signs of residence. In some districts, sea cucumbers have been allowed to colonize the deeper basins, moving like slow black loaves across the plates, gathering floc with a patience that seems almost administrative.

Fish are more complicated. They need space, oxygen stability, food webs that do not fail suddenly, and management plans that anticipate what they will eat, excrete, disturb, and prefer. The first fish are therefore cautious choices: small, hardy, tolerant of variable light and pump noise. Some are visual markers, kept because they reveal current changes or water-quality problems by their behavior. Some are cleaners, taking parasite loads off larger stocked animals in later phases. Some are simply allowed because workers like seeing them in the lamp fields, and the sea, despite its engineering, feels less complete without movement that is not mechanical.

Open-water shoals stay where the light is. They drift under the bright pillars, turning in unison as internal waves pass through them, their bodies flashing in quick silver-green pulses. Demersal fish lie near gratings and rail channels, tasting the flow with barbels, waiting for scraps, worms, and small crustaceans. Larger fish patrol the warm edges, not many, not yet, but enough to give the smaller animals a reason to keep moving. The sea’s food web is short and supervised, a lattice of permits and contingency plans. Even predation here has paperwork.

The weather of the installed sea is not separate from this life. It is its calendar.

When surface shutters open, daylight shafts lean into the water and photosynthesis quickens. Oxygen rises in greened patches. Zooplankton climb toward the light wells, and juvenile fish follow them. When the shutters close and industrial lamps take over, the spectrum changes, and the animals adjust their positions again. There are creatures that have learned to treat lamp maintenance as dusk, because the lamps flicker before a service crew cuts power. There are others that spawn during backflush events, when a pulse of reversed flow lifts settled particles into the water column and turns the sea briefly into a snowstorm of food.

Brine falls are more dangerous. A dense curtain of saltwater descending from a processing gallery can move across a basin like a slow, invisible wall. Small animals caught in it become sluggish, then sink, then scatter if they can. Fish avoid the leading edge. Invertebrates close up. Afterward, a brine lake may sit for hours in a low basin, clear and lethal-looking, until circulation systems dilute it. The organisms of the sea have begun to know the signs: a change in shimmer, a particular taste, a change in the way current pulls at tube openings. They do not understand desalination schedules. They respond to the symptoms.

Cold flushes are similar. An intake gate opens wider than usual, and a mass of colder, cleaner water slides along the floor, pushing before it a front of displaced warmth and suspended life. Films tighten. Loose detritus lifts. Settled larvae are carried to new districts. Some patches are erased. Others are seeded. In the installed sea, disturbance is not an interruption of normal conditions. It is one of the normal conditions.

Sound is everywhere, though not always recognizable as sound. The water carries the work of the world above: the deep periodic thump of turbines, the hiss of valves, the grinding of rail carts on the ceiling, the electric whine of lamp banks, the sudden concussion of a hatch being tested. Biological noises are still sparse, but growing. Snapping shrimp make small percussive sparks in warm galleries. Fish produce low grunts and drumming notes during aggregation. The shells and plates themselves tick with thermal change. A diver listening through a hull can hear the sea as both machine and habitat at once.

People are here too, but not numerous. They pass through in heated suits, in armored inspection crawlers, in remote vehicles with brush arms and sampling jaws. They leave traces: fresh scratches on panels, torque marks on bolts, tags tied to cable runs, a lost glove settling into a corner like a soft dark animal. Their presence is part of the ecology. A lamp array attracts plankton, then fish, then predators; a maintenance platform becomes a perch; a discarded sensor housing becomes a den. Every human act in the sea has a fouling community waiting for it.

The governance of the place is as layered as its films. There are manifests for every introduced lineage. Quarantine certificates. Growth audits. Culling orders. Species that were once tolerated can become problems: a hydroid that spreads across diffuser plates, a mussel strain that sets too densely near intakes, a worm that bores into polymer seams faster than predicted. Then the response is measured and local: heat, brine, scraping, temporary shutdown, a revised permit. No one speaks of wilderness here. They speak of zones, tolerances, succession schedules, and asset protection. Yet the language of ecology keeps invading the paperwork: recruitment, bloom, settlement, refuge, migration, die-off. The sea is managed as infrastructure and is nonetheless behaving like a habitat.
