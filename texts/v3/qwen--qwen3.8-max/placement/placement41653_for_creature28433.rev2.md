If I tried to enter the Cloudline Yards by the upper passenger lift, I would be wrong immediately.

I would come up into that terrible brightness, that high white glare of enamel and glass, and I would stand with my eyes streaming, my hands useless, my sense of scale broken. The basin would open around me too largely. I would see the gantries, the tank farms, the terraces, the rail lines, the greenhouse galleries, the clouds moving below like slow white livestock, and I would think: this is too big, too open, too bright for a single life. I would try to find myself in it and fail, the way one fails when looking for a small creature in a landscape painting.

So I would not stay there.

At first I would probably imagine myself as one of the people. That seems the natural first guess. A Yard-born person, perhaps, with tinted goggles pushed up into dusty hair and soft-soled boots testing the warmth of plates. A Leakwarden with copper probes and chalk, going from seam to seam, deciding which drip is loss and which is gift. There is a dignity in that. I can feel the appeal of it: the careful hands, the brass tags, the authority to mark a leak with a small painted sign that says preserve. I could spend years learning the difference between weep-damp and seam-damp, between a mineral crust that means stable water and one that means failing metal. I could become someone trusted in the small wet places.

But even as I try that on, something in me resists the upright body.

A Soundkeeper, then? One of those who sleep near turbines and wake when the rhythm changes? That also feels close. I like the idea of listening for the future in the hum, of knowing by footfall whether a deck is loose, by echo whether a culvert is flooding. I like the seriousness of it, the way silence is treated as a wound. But the Soundkeepers belong to the great voices of the Yards: engines, transformers, rails, pressure valves. They stand with palms against walls and hear the whole basin breathing. That is too central, too human in its responsibility. I think I would be drawn downward, away from the loud chambers, into the quieter water-margins.

A cat, perhaps? That thought arrives with immediate seduction. A scarred, heat-seeking cat sleeping on a transformer housing, belonging to no one and everyone, permitted first passage through hatches. Cats have the right life there: they know the warm surfaces, they move without explanation, they are loved without being required to speak. But a cat is watched. A cat is interpreted. If a cat refuses a hatch, people notice. I do not think I would want to be an omen, not even a soft one.

A frog? No. Beloved, yes, but too exposed. The frogs live in the evening chorus, in the basins where children come to listen. Their life is a public indicator. If they stop calling, questions are asked. I would rather be less legible. Less easily read.

Then the image changes.

The light dims. The brightness above becomes a greenish glimmer seen through water. The sound changes from open air to enclosed resonance: the tick of condensation falling into a cistern, the low vibration of a pump heard through stone and metal, the muffled bell of a lift change somewhere far above. I am no longer standing. I am no longer walking. I am in the dark, in the damp, in the hidden plumbing of the made mountain.

I would be an eel.

Not the only answer, perhaps, but the one that feels most mine.

You would find me low. Not in the open basin, not on the sun-blasted roofs, not among the flowering balconies where bees work the terrace blossoms. You would find me below the visible streets, in the water-bearing layers of the Cloudline Yards: in the cisterns, overflow channels, filter troughs, service tunnels, and forgotten reservoirs where condensation gathers and stays. My country would be the wet seams. My roads would be pipes.

More precisely, if an eel can be said to have a district, I would live beneath the Third Condensation Rack, where the upper fog combs bead with silver in the early hours and the cold-faced tanks sweat into shallow drainage channels. There would be a cistern there, not large, set into a concrete trough below a stairwell. Its lip would be crusted with pale mineral deposits. A vine would have found the crack beside it and sent down thin roots that dangle just above the waterline, trembling whenever someone passes on the grate above. In the evening, frogs would call from a nearby basin. The water would be warm enough for life, but not hot enough to make me sluggish. It would taste of copper, algae, wet brick, and the faint sweetness of leaf litter carried in from a terrace garden.

Above me, people would say: “That pool is good. It has an eel.”

That would be enough of a reputation.

I would be long and dark, not glossy like a river eel in a storybook, but thickened by age and adapted to the mineral water of the Yards. My back would be olive-black, mottled where old algae stains have settled into my skin. My belly would be dull brass. One fin would have been torn years ago by a pump intake guard, leaving me with a slight tremor when I turn. I would carry a scar across my jaw from a piece of loose grating, or perhaps from a rat that thought better of it too late. My eyes would be small and patient. I would not look wise. Eels do not look wise; they look like questions that have gone to live in drains. But I would be attentive in the way only water creatures can be: tasting the whole environment, reading changes before they become visible.

In the Cloudline Yards, that would make me useful without my intending to be.

I would know the leaks by flavor. A clean condensation leak would arrive bright and thin, carrying almost no mineral burden, tasting of cold air and metal. A leak from a heat main would be warmer, faintly sweet, with a ghost of oil and old paint. A leak from a cooling line would taste of salts and treatment compounds; I would avoid it before a human inspector knew it had begun. A blocked drain would change the pressure in the water long before anyone heard the overflow. A failing seal would introduce a bitterness so subtle that only something living inside the system could notice it first.

The Eelkeepers would understand this.

One of them would come to my cistern in the morning, after heat-up, when the whole Yards begin to tick and expand. She would wear waterproof boots and gloves thinned at the fingertips. She would tap twice on the grate before lifting it, because in the Yards one knocks before opening anything. Then she would lower a small lantern over the water, not to dazzle me but to see the surface. If I rose to the light, moving slowly, she would say nothing much. If I was absent, she would frown and follow the channel downstream with a probe. If I lingered near a certain outflow and refused to enter it, she would mark the pipe with chalk. If I avoided a tank I had once favored, she would summon a Leakwarden.

I would not be a pet. I would be a living gauge.

Children would invent stories about me, of course. They would say I remember the old heat mains before they were sealed. They would say I have swum through floors no one visits anymore. They would say I carry messages between the deep tanks, or that I once pulled a drowned toolbag into a sump and saved someone from blame. Some of these stories would be almost true. In the Yards, stories do not need to be factual to be useful. They teach caution. They teach affection. A child who believes the eels are old enough to remember will be less likely to pour waste water into a drain.

I would not mind the stories. They would make people gentler.

My day would begin with vibration. The morning heat-up would travel through the walls as a long metallic stretching. Somewhere above, condensation crews would be working the fog combs with soft cloths and shallow pans. The first collected water would enter the channels in careful pulses. I would feel each pulse move through the cistern like a rumor. Beetles would skim the surface. Carp would shift in the deeper tanks, slow and indifferent. Water-striders would gather where the drip-pattern disturbed the film. I would rest in the shade of a ceramic lip until the light above turned green with reflected sun.

At midday, the Yards would become loud and bright above me, but my world would remain dim. The glare would strike the upper plates, the transformers would click, the traction lines would hum, and the sound would come down through structure into water as a continuous pressure. I would know the difference between a rail load passing overhead and a crane swinging its arm. I would know when workers gathered near a hatch by the changing resonance of their boots. I would know when rain was coming before the first drops fell, because the air pressure in the culverts would alter and the surface of the cistern would tense.

Rain, when it came, would be a festival for the hidden waterways.

The whole surface above would awaken. Gutters would tick and run. Temporary streams would gather along inclined service roads, carrying leaf litter and bright mineral dust toward catch-basins. The cisterns would fill faster than usual. The filter marshes would rise. In the frog pools, voices would multiply. I would move then, because rain opens the Yards like a hand. I would pass through overflow channels that are dry for days, under grates where children press their faces, past roots that hang in the dark like fingers. I would taste the sudden abundance: pollen, dust, flower sugar, warm enamel, wet moss. For a little while, the made world would become almost wild without ceasing to be itself.

Then the rain would stop. The sun would return. The water would retreat into cisterns, sumps, filter beds, and reservoir pockets. The Yards would steam. The damp geography would reassert itself: wet seams, dry roofs, lush corners, stubborn mosses. I would settle again in my shaded place, perhaps with a new leaf clinging to my flank, and listen to the water find its level.

There would be moments when I was seen.

A maintenance worker might lift a hatch and find me coiled in the dark beneath a valve cluster. A child might peer into a basin and see only a slow black ribbon moving under green water. A cat might watch me from a grating with that particular feline mixture of interest and disbelief. Once, perhaps, an apprentice Greenhand would mistake me for a root until I moved, and then sit back laughing, hand on chest. I would not be frightening. I would be one of the ordinary marvels of the place: too strange for outsiders, entirely necessary to those who live there.

The people would not try to make me speak. That is one of the things I love about the Yards. They understand that not all knowledge comes in human language. They already listen to frogs, cats, rust, moss, transformers, and silence. An eel can be part of that chorus without being made to perform.

At dusk, the lamp sequence would begin. The basin above would warm into patches of amber, white, and greenish electrical blue. The light would reach the water only in fragments: a tremble of gold on a cistern wall, a blue flicker along a pipe. The frog chorus would start, rough and tender against the machine drone. I would feel the change more than see it: the day heat leaving metal, the night contraction beginning, the water cooling by degrees. Somewhere a Soundkeeper would press a palm to a wall and listen. Somewhere a Leakwarden would mark a drip preserve. Somewhere an elder would tell a story about a flood that happened before most of the walkways were re-plated. I would hear none of those words directly, but I would live inside their consequences.

At night, the Yards would become intimate.

The great loudness would not cease, but it would spread out, lose its daytime sharpness. The heat would rise from grates in slow exhalations. Cloud vapor would curl through the lower passages. The air would smell of wet leaves, hot metal, ozone, algae, oil, and rain-soaked dust. In the hidden layers, water would speak more clearly: drip, tick, bead, run. I would move through the dark with more confidence then, because night belongs to things that do not need glare. I would pass under sleeping homes, under bolted cabins, under floors where children’s small boots had crossed all day. I would be beneath them and apart from them at once, part of the system that kept their world temperate and alive.

This is where I would fit.

Not in the open, not in the bright center, not in a role that required me to stand upright and declare myself. I would be in the seam. I would be in the joint between made and living, between infrastructure and ecology, between water that is managed and water that insists. I would be small enough to be overlooked, present enough to matter.

I would not need to own ground. There is no ground there anyway.

That thought, which might be unsettling elsewhere, would feel like freedom in the Cloudline Yards. Everything is built; everything is maintained; everything is negotiated. To be an eel there would be to accept that the world is assembled. There would be no fantasy of bedrock beneath me, no untouched soil, no original river. My water would be condensed, collected, filtered, warmed, redirected, shared. My body would live inside that fact. I would not long for a wilder place. I would understand that care can become a habitat as surely as stone can.

Perhaps that is why I would be an eel rather than a person. People in the Yards must choose. They must decide which leak to stop, which to preserve, which vine to tie back, which nest to move, which silence to investigate. They carry moral weight. I would rather be one of the lives around which those decisions gather. Not innocent, exactly — I would eat small things, disturb sediment, vanish when frightened, and sometimes block a narrow pipe with my own stubbornness — but not burdened with administration. I would be a resident, not a steward. A presence, not an authority.

Still, I would belong.

If the Yards had a way of naming me, it would not be a grand name. The Eelkeeper might call me “that long one under the Sweet Leck,” or “the dark in the third trough,” or simply “the eel that stays.” Children might have a shorter name, something silly and affectionate. A Leakwarden might refer to me indirectly in reports: eel present, water acceptable. That would be enough. In the Cloudline Yards, names are often local, acoustic, practical. They belong to sounds, routes, drips, warm surfaces. I would belong to a pool. That would be identity enough.

If you came looking for me, do not begin at the lift.

Go down instead. Find the service stair beside the condensation rack where the railings bead with moisture in the early hours. Follow the water sound, not the loud machinery. Past the transformer cabinet padded with moss, past the fern bed in the cable tray, past the basin where the frogs call in the evening. Kneel where a shallow trough passes beneath a grate. Wait until your eyes adjust. The water will look dark at first, then green, then full of small movements: a beetle, a trembling root, a shadow that may be a carp. If the Yards are calm, if no pump has stopped, if no silence has opened its wound in the basin, you may see me rise.

Only for a moment.

A dark shape, slow and sinuous, turning under the surface. A faint ripple against the mineral-crusted lip. Then I would slip back into the channel and be gone into the made dark, carrying with me the taste of the whole system in my skin.

And that, I think, is who I would be in the Cloudline Yards: not a master of the place, not a hero of its walkways, not even a citizen in the full human sense, but one of its hidden listeners. An eel in the seams. A small, old, water-dark intelligence moving through the joints of the world, loved in the way such things are loved there: quietly, practically, and with careful hands.
