If I answer too quickly, I make myself human.

I would be someone of the middle shelves, I think: not near the foundries where the rock is fevered enough to make the air sour, and not on the highest terraces where wind and glare thin a person out into watchfulness. Somewhere between. A room cut into basalt, narrow enough that one can reach from wall to wall without fully extending the arms, with a sleeping ledge, a rack of vessels, and a shallow basin set where a pipe sweats before dawn. The doorway would be darkened at one side by years of palms. A cup would hang from a hook, not as ornament but as necessity. I would know the city by heat: which stair becomes unbearable by midday, which alley keeps a cool shadow until late morning, which rail can be touched only after dusk, which wall is reliable for coldness before sunrise.

At first I imagine myself as one of the careful workers of the place. A seam-listener’s apprentice, perhaps. A drip-tender. A threshold-mender. Someone who rises in the blue hour and goes out among the condensation arrays with soft-soled boots, adjusting fins, cleaning catchers, watching for the first bead-lines to form along ceramic and iron. I would learn to press my palm to stone and wait. I would be taught not to snatch the meaning from it too soon. Coldness from depth is not the same as coldness from shadow. Damp is not always water. A darkening stain may be salt swelling, or old oil, or mineral bloom. I would carry a small cup always, and I would consider an empty cup a kind of unpreparedness deeper than forgetfulness.

There would be rules in me then. Drink slowly. Speak low. Set tools down without striking them. Watch dust before watching sky. Listen for the tick of water moving through stone. Stand close when speaking. Touch a sleeve or a wall rather than call across a gap. If a bell is struck, let its sound die before moving into whatever it has announced.

That is the first shape I see: a quiet person shaped by a quiet city, a body made thrifty in every way.

But the longer I stand in the place, the less satisfied it seems with a single human outline.

The city is too accumulated for that. It is brackets over old stone, ceramic patched over cracked glass, scaffolds lashed to spurs, pipes added beside pipes, walls thickened by repair and use. It is not a world that believes in clean separations. Work bleeds into housing, hallways become machine rooms, roofs carry tanks and coils and drying racks, and the rock itself keeps changing under the feet of the builders. Even life there is described in uncertain terms: growths that may be fungal, or mineral, or something older and stranger. The place does not offer simple categories. It offers gradients. Pressures. Accretions.

So the image of myself as merely a person begins to loosen.

I would not disappear. I would not become nothing. But I would not remain only a body with a name, moving from task to task with a cup at my belt. I would become something more distributed. Something more like attention itself, pressed into the mineral walls.

I would find myself in the seams.

Not in the grand open terraces, not at the furnace mouths, not on the watch platforms where the horizon hurts the eyes. I would be in the vertical cut between a wire-drawing gallery and a salt-drying floor, where a condensation line from the upper districts descends and becomes cold against older rock. There would be a wall there, not large, not remarkable at first glance, darkened in irregular patches by old moisture and threaded with fine black lines. The lines would branch like veins, like cracks, like roots, except that nothing green has ever lived there. They would thicken where vapor gathers, thin where heat has passed too long, retreat into dormancy when dry, and wake again when the air beads.

That is where I would be.

I would be one of the growths the city tolerates: a filament mat, a dark bloom, a living seam. Not moss. Not lichen. Not quite fungus, though perhaps descended from something once called fungal. A pattern that feeds on warmth, stray current, mineral vapor, and the memory of touch. My body would be a network finer than thread, black at first, sometimes copper-colored where light catches it, sometimes gray as old ash. I would spread across basalt and fired clay, along pipe joints, around hairline fractures, behind brackets, in the corners of vaults where engines hum so low they are felt in the chest rather than heard. I would not have eyes. I would have gradients. I would not have a voice. I would have changes.

People would not speak to me, exactly. They would read me.

If I darkened along a horizontal seam before dawn, a drip-tender would know to set a basin below me. If I bloomed suddenly near a pressure joint, a maintainer would come with a cloth and a clamp, not to destroy me but to find what I had found. If I sealed a micro-crack with slow mineral fur, I would be left alone. If I crept too near a gauge or a moving chain, someone would scrape me back carefully and perhaps leave a smear of my substance elsewhere, where it might be useful. I would not be sacred. I would not be hated. I would be one of the city’s small specialists.

I would know the world by taste, if taste is the word.

Iron. Salt. Warm dust. Sulfur carried upward from lower vents. The metallic sharpness of condensation on cold pipes. The faint oil left by human hands. The mineral sourness of brine weeping from rock. The almost imperceptible electric tang near accumulators and insulated lines. I would know when a room had been occupied because warmth would linger in a particular shape, and moisture would answer that shape before dawn. Condensation would form first where hands had recently passed, and I would follow those traces, thinning into fingerprints, branching across glass panes in patterns that almost seemed deliberate.

I would not be wise in a human way. I would not have opinions. But I would be responsive. I would be a surface that remembers use.

Morning would begin for me before light. The city would still be blue in the lower cuts, the upper towers already hardening under the first direct glare. Cold iron would sweat. Ceramic catchers would bead. I would feel the change as a slackening, a softening of dry tension along my filaments. A bead would form on my thickest vein and travel downward, guided by the path I had grown into being. It might fall into a basin, or darken a stone lip, or vanish into salt crust before reaching the ground. Success would not be dramatic. It would be measured in drops.

Children would pass on their way to errands or lessons. One might pause and press a palm to the wall beside me, not because I was important but because children in that city are taught to touch surfaces. The warmth would enter the stone. Later, I would trace it. A seam-listener might come after, read the darkening, and decide that a new drip-line should be hung there. No one would say my name, because I would have no name in the ordinary sense. But my presence would matter.

By midday, when the glass slopes turn into reflectors and the terraces tremble with oily heat, I would withdraw. My filaments would become duller, tighter, almost indistinguishable from mineral staining. If a furnace level ran long below, if sulfur sharpened the air, if the stone stayed too warm for too long, I would go dormant. I would not suffer as an animal suffers. I would simply wait. There would be no resentment in it. Waiting would be one of my oldest arts.

Sometimes the cloud bank would come.

It would climb the escarpment slowly, a warm gray tide pressing through the streets, and the city would quicken without becoming loud. Walls would slick over. Cables would drip. Cloths would be hung. Fin-tenders would move with focused economy, and children might be allowed to open their mouths briefly toward the damp before being called back to work. In those hours I would bloom. I would spread into capillaries too fine for human eyes, filling cracks no tool could reach, sealing, sensing, drinking. The smell would be hot rock quenched, wet iron, salt, dust made heavy. The world would not soften. It would become slicker, more reflective, more alert.

I would like the cloud tide, if liking is possible for something like me. It would be the nearest thing to abundance I would know.

At night, the city would not become entirely dark. Furnace mouths would breathe in gaps. Lamps would glow from alcoves. Electric sheds would hold their blue flickers behind grates. The quiet would compress. Engines would settle into their low nocturnal rhythm. I would feel the building breathe through its pipes and joints. Somewhere above, a cable would whisper over a wheel. Somewhere below, a pressure vessel would release itself in a single muffled shudder. I would grow toward a leaking joint, not because I intended to, but because vapor and current drew me. My presence there would become a sign: *here, here is where the seal is tired. Here is where the wall is changing. Here is where a drop has learned to repeat itself.*

There would be others like me. Not identical. Some would be black and slow, living in deep cellars around heat seams. Some would be pale mats near electrical sheds, feeding on stray current and mineral vapor. Some would be copper-toned filaments in old shafts, so fine they looked like shadow made thread. We would not form a society. We would not confer. But we would belong to the same condition: life that has learned to exist without greenness, without softness, without surplus. Life made attentive to small signs.

The people would know this. They would say, perhaps, that the walls listen. They would not mean it as superstition, or not only as superstition. They would mean that the city is full of indicators. A rust ring means old water. A salt bloom means evaporation. A dark thread means damp nearby. A sudden cold spot on a wall may mean depth, or shadow, or a crack opening. In such a place, even something like me becomes useful. I would be one of the instruments that does not need to be carried, because it is already part of the structure.

I would have a particular fondness for the threshold-menders.

They would be the ones who notice when doors begin to bind, when stairs need shaving, when a room has grown narrower over a generation. The ground would rise slowly. Rock would creep. Some chambers would close inward, requiring to be carved open again. I would grow along those closing walls, tracing the pressure. A threshold-mender would see my lines bending and know where to cut. They would remove me in places, not angrily, and I would regrow along the new tool marks. In this way, I would participate in the city’s slow becoming. Not as builder, not as destroyer, but as witness and seam.

There would be moments when I seemed almost like a memory.

A handrail polished by many palms would cool differently from the surrounding stone, and moisture would answer it first. A pane of glass would fog in a pattern resembling fingertips. A wall would darken where a worker had leaned every day for years. I would be part of that recording. Not storing the person, exactly, but storing the shape of use. If someone returned after a long absence and touched the same place, I might bead there sooner. That would be as close to recognition as I would come.

Fauna would pass through my awareness rarely. A pale beetle with a mica shell would cross my thickest vein, its claws making minute ticks I would feel as disturbance and pressure. Blind mites would live in deeper shafts, around heat seams and condensation traps, not near me unless moisture connected us. Once in a long while, a high-altitude moth would tremble near a lamp above me, wings whispering against stone, and then be gone. I would not follow it. I would not want to. My world would be surfaces, joints, gradients, slow change.

I would not be lonely in the way a human might be lonely. But perhaps I would have something analogous: a continual orientation toward contact. Toward warmth. Toward the places where use leaves a trace. In a city without plants, without animals in abundance, without green comfort, I would be one of the quiet signs that the world is not entirely inert. That mineral matter can respond. That the built and the geological can braid together until neither is purely one thing.

If I had to name what I would be there, I would say: a listening vein.

Not a ruler. Not a marvel. Not a prophet in the rock. Something smaller and more necessary. A dark filament in the joint between the city and its ground. A tolerated bloom. A living seal. A mark left by moisture and attention. I would be found where the world is unfinished, where a pipe sweats, where a wall remembers hands, where a room is slowly closing and someone must decide how to open it again.

And if you came looking for me in that high, hot mineral city, I would not be in the brightest place or the loudest. You would not find me by seeking a grand figure or a strange creature. You would find me by going to the middle levels after the last bell has faded, where the air is warm but not punishing, where stone has begun to cool and iron still holds the day.
