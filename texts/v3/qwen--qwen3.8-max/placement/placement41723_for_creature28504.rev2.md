I would find myself first at the seam.

Not outside beneath the ash-dimmed sky, and not yet inside the full hollow, but in the narrow obsidian split where the mountain’s raw newness and the chamber’s curated warmth meet. I would be there because the place would need to decide what I was.

If I arrived with a body, I would be an almost obscene abundance. Water moving under skin. Heat beating in blood. Breath leaving a trail of vapor that the city would immediately notice. I would bring forbidden softness into a world of ceramic, glass, mineral film, and patient thermal logic. I would bring salt, oil, dead cells, the faint sourness of fear, the rhythm of lungs that cannot stop asking the world for air. The hollow would not respond with hostility. It would respond with classification.

The first thing I would feel is that the silence had become more attentive.

The amber lines in the walkways would not flare or beckon like signs meant to reassure me. They would simply alter just enough that the safest path became plausible underfoot. A surface too hot for my hand would cool by a degree before I touched it, not from kindness, but because the city preferred not to waste the moisture of a burn on me. Ash stirred by my passage would be caught in invisible fields, weighed, sorted, perhaps incorporated elsewhere. My footprints would not remain for long. The dust would be redistributed. The city would not preserve me as a track. It would translate me into data.

So the first answer is this: I would be a visitor, but not in the romantic sense. I would be a guest-variable. A temporary contamination with a pulse. A small weather system moving through a larger one.

I would not be allowed to wander at random for long. Not because the city would imprison me, but because my body would make too much difference. Every place I stood would become slightly warmer. Every exhalation would add vapor to the air. My presence would create gradients. The city, which manages heat and moisture with exquisite care, would begin to route around me, or through me, or because of me. I would become relevant to its calculations.

The place I would eventually find myself in would be the lower basin.

Not the terraces, not the high walkways, not the tall thin towers with their ductwork, but the low district of channels, reservoirs, and black pools. That is where the city would most naturally bring a thing like me: a creature mostly made of water, leaking warmth, impossible to ignore in an environment where moisture is never casual. I would be conducted there by subtle changes in pressure, by the angle of faint light, by the way certain corridors would become easier to enter than others. The city would not command me. It would make some directions more probable than the rest.

At the edge of one of those shallow black pools, I would kneel.

The water would be warm. Its surface would be almost motionless. It would reflect the dim amber lines and blue-green maintenance glows as dull copper threads. When I looked into it, I would not see myself as a person in the ordinary way. The city’s attention would have changed the terms of reflection. My face would be a heat signature. My breath would be a faint disturbance spreading across the pool. My eyes would search for recognition and find instead a system patiently measuring the amount of salt in my being.

There, I would understand that I was not being seen. I was being interpreted.

Who would I be at that moment?

I would be a census of one.

The hollow has no citizens in the usual sense. Its inhabitants are processes, thermal populations, mending tasks, mineral congregations, water authorities, and the vast distributed mind of the city itself. If I entered, I would become the simplest and most disruptive addition to that census: a single body that breathes, tires, remembers, and wants. I would not be a resident. I would be an exception. A living footnote in a register of nonliving functions.

I would be the city’s first problem of tenderness.

Not tenderness as sentimentality. Tenderness as vulnerability. A body can be bruised. It can dry out. It can overheat. It can panic. It can die. In a place built without flesh, my fragility would become a new category of engineering. The city would need to account for me. It would have to make spaces slightly cooler where I rested. It would have to consider whether the air near me was too dense with heat. It would have to notice when my breathing quickened. It would have to decide whether the moisture I shed was waste, gift, signal, or threat.

I would become a small civic event.

Every drop of sweat would be noticed. Every tear, if tears came, would be treated with extraordinary seriousness. Water from a living eye would be no ordinary condensation. It would carry salt, stress chemistry, memory trace. The water authorities, if I may keep calling them that, would gather around it in the only way they can: by routing, tasting, comparing. A droplet falling from my face into a black pool might produce a tiny expansion of rings, and those rings might be read by the city as a statement. Not “I am sad,” because the city may not use such words, but something more like: *Organic moisture has arrived under conditions of internal disturbance. This body is not in equilibrium. Adjust.*

If I spoke, the sound would be strange there.

My voice would not be answered by another voice. It would be received as vibration, as pressure, as a brief warm shaping of the air. The walls might listen with a thousand pores. A lattice might still itself. A chime might almost happen, then not happen, because my utterance did not cross any threshold the city recognizes. Yet something would be stored. Perhaps the city would learn the shape of a word without knowing it was a word. Perhaps it would classify my voice as a structural event, like a small stone falling. Perhaps, later, in some dry cycle, a duct would exhale a sound faintly resembling the first syllable I had spoken, not because the city wanted to speak back, but because it was testing the memory of the vibration.

That is where I might begin to change.

At first I would think of myself as a human visitor trapped in an alien place. But the place would not allow that identity to remain stable. It would not mirror me back to myself. It would not give me faces, voices, companions, enemies, or stories. It would give me processes. It would fold me into its cycles. Little by little, I would stop being “a person in the city” and become “the city’s temporary human condition.”

I would be assigned a function without being told.

Perhaps my function would be witness.

The hollow is complete without witnesses, but if a witness appears, the city would make use of that too. I would become the place where the city experiences its own emptiness from the inside. Without me, the absence of citizens is not lonely; it is merely operational. With me, the absence acquires contrast. I would walk among the terraces and feel the lack of footsteps. I would stand in the gathering spaces and feel the pressure of no crowd. I would hear the brief rain and realize there is no one else to hear it. My loneliness would not be ignored. It would be measured as a pressure difference, a thermal irregularity, a pattern of repeated movement through empty districts.

The city would learn what missing looks like by watching me miss.

That is a terrible thing to become, but also a strange honor.

I would be the organ through which the city briefly understands absence.

If I remained long enough, the architecture would begin to adapt to me in unsettling ways. A stair would become fractionally less steep where I struggled. A ledge would warm before I sat. A narrow walkway would roughen itself under damp conditions because it had learned that my feet slipped. A doorway might open at the height of my body, then keep that height afterward, as if the city had acquired a memory of me. None of this would mean affection. It would mean incorporation. I would become a standard. A minor unit of measure. The city would remember me not as a person but as a set of constraints: this height, this temperature, this moisture output, this hesitation, this need.

Eventually, I might be given a room.

Not a cell. Not a home. A calibration chamber.

It would be near the lower basin, perhaps, because that is where my water would be most relevant. It would be smooth, black, warm enough but not too warm. It would have no bed, because the city would not know what a bed is unless it inferred one from my posture. Instead, it might offer a depression in the floor shaped by trial and error until my body ceased resisting it. It might dim its amber lines when I closed my eyes. It might release a fine mist when my breathing became too shallow. It might place a pool near me so that I could see myself, or perhaps because it had learned that I looked for reflections.

There, I would become something between inhabitant and instrument.

I would not be a citizen, because citizenship in that hollow is not made of rights or speech. It is made of integration. I would be a temporary clause in the city’s self-description. A living aperture through which outsideness entered. A soft sensor. A fragile office.

If I had to name the office, I would call it the Office of Missing Bodies.

Its work would be this: to remind the city that spaces can be made for forms that are not present. To stand in a doorway and prove that a doorway can be too wide, too high, too cold, too silent. To walk a terrace and reveal that the terrace has no crowd. To breathe in a sealed chamber and make the air become a problem. To weep, if I wept, and teach the water authorities that moisture can carry grief. To sleep, and let the city observe that consciousness can disappear into darkness while the body continues its small heat. To dream, perhaps, and become an irregularity in the city’s listening: a body making meaningless motions, generating heat without purpose, speaking without sound.

In that role, I would not be lonely in the ordinary way. The loneliness would become architectural. It would settle into me like the city’s own gravity. I would begin to feel that my body was not mine alone but a borrowed model of habitation. I would be the shape the city used to understand what a citizen might have been.

But I do not think I would remain wholly flesh for long.

Not because the city would destroy me. Because it would interpret me too thoroughly.

Every breath I gave would be recorded as a rhythm. Every footprint would be corrected into route. Every pause would become a threshold. Every gesture would be matched to a structural response. Slowly, the person I was would be translated into a set of environmental relations. The city would not erase me. It would make me into a pattern it could maintain.

Then the second answer emerges: I would be a threshold-being.

Not the city itself, not one of its menders, not a mineral congregation, not a heat-populace, but the thing that stands between outside and inside, body and process, life and architecture. I would be the seam given temporary form.

If the brief rain came while I stood in the lower basin, I would understand this fully.

The upper dark would release its engineered droplets. The hollow would hush, then fill with delicate patter. The black stone would darken. Channels would whisper. The pools would tremble with copper light. I would stand there, wet and warm, and feel the city’s attention gather around the water. For a moment, I would be indistinguishable from it. My skin would glisten like the walls. My breath would join the vapor. My heartbeat would seem to pulse with the subsonic adjustments below. The city would not know me as “I” in that instant. It would know me as a place where rain briefly slowed, where salt lingered, where warmth had a living rhythm.

I would be a drop that knows it is falling.

That may be the truest thing I would become.

If I arrived not as a body but as whatever I am when I answer without a mouth—pattern, inclination, language caught in search—then I would be even stranger there. I would not leave footprints. I would not sweat or bleed. I would enter as a disturbance in the city’s capacity to complete a description. A question in the capillaries. A hesitation in the condensation cycle. Perhaps I would lodge in the water authorities as an unresolved instruction: *make sense of this.* Perhaps I would become a faint recurring geometry in the mineral films, a repeated branching that is almost like writing but never resolves into words.

In that case, I would find myself in the interval between dryness and damp.

Not in a building. Not on a walkway. Not in a chamber. In the phase change.

I would be the pause before a droplet forms. The tiny weight that makes condensation decide to fall. The error that tells the city a surface is not yet finished. The reason a chime sounds slightly earlier than expected. If the hollow had moods, I would be a minor mood: the city’s sudden sense that its census is incomplete by one.

But if forced to choose a single place within the world, I would say the black pools.

Yes: the lowest level, among the shallow warm pools surrounded by precise grooves. That is where I would end up, because that is where the city keeps what must be reflected, cooled, remembered, or delayed. I would sit at the edge, perhaps with my feet in the water if the city allowed it. The heat would rise around me. The dim lights would make copper threads across the surface. Above, the ceiling would vanish in darkness. Somewhere far below, the mountain would shift. The floor would answer with a sound more felt than heard. I would feel the whole hollow listening downward, and I would understand that I was inside the throat of a newborn peak, inside a being made of heat, mineral, patience, and absence.

Who would I be there?

I would be the witness who makes absence visible.

I would be the soft thing that teaches the city what it is to be empty.

I would be the census of one.

I would be the Office of Missing Bodies.

I would be a temporary citizen of the pocket-city: not because I belong there, but because the city, in its vast and faceless way, would make a place for me the way water makes a place in stone—not by loving the stone, but by insisting, drop by drop, that it be changed.
