I would not find myself as a walker in that city. Not as a body with feet crossing from one place to another, not as a visitor with eyes looking up at towers. The basin would refuse that first. It would refuse the idea of a separate animal self, and if I persisted in trying to be such a thing, I would be only a brief disturbance: a warm interruption, a chemical strangeness, a compression in the leaf-flesh that the city would either absorb or grow away from.

So if I existed there at all, I would have to become something else.

At first I think I would be nowhere in the ordinary sense. I would be a fault in the humidity. A pocket of wrongness in the warm green pressure. A place where the root-mats paused. The city would notice me not as a person, but as a gradient: too dry, too still, too resistant, too much like an unfinished thought. The nearest roots would curl toward me without curiosity. The condensation would gather differently around my absence. The sound would bend.

Then, because that place cannot tolerate an outside, I would begin to be incorporated.

Not eaten. Not buried. Grown over.

I would become a scar.

That is where I would find myself: at the edge of one of the fresh fissures, where the living ground has torn open and immediately begun healing with pale, glossy tissue. There, where the city is youngest and most alert, where the substrate is still deciding whether the wound will become a channel, a street, a plaza, or a tower. I would be somewhere between the rupture and the repair. Maybe inside the repair. Maybe as the repair.

If I had a body, it would not be a torso or a face. It would be a hollow chamber grown over a wound: a resonant culm of lignified tissue, split once and healed crookedly, its inner surface slick with moisture, its walls perforated with openings that breathe with the temperature of the basin. I would be part tower, part membrane, part street-memory. My outer surface would be bark-flesh, shiny where it had recently torn, dull where it had learned to bear weight. My interior would be warm, close, and full of moving air.

I would be a listening thing.

Not because I chose to listen, but because sound would have no place else to gather in that particular shape. When the hollow towers boomed across the district, my cavity would answer. When water struck the broad plaza leaves, my chambers would turn the drumming into a slower pulse. When a thousand leaf-vents opened at once, I would hold a small silence inside myself, a fraction of a beat delayed, as if the city’s noise had entered me and become uncertain.

That hesitation would be the closest thing I had to a self.

I would not be an individual in the way that word used to mean. I would not have a single boundary. Part of me would be the hollow stem. Part of me would be the root network beneath the fissure, drawing moisture away from the channel and sending it toward drier terraces. Part of me would be the pressure-wave that moved through that root network when a nearby district began to overgrow. Part of me would be the pattern of suppression that kept a corridor open beside me, the communal refusal to sprout where movement still needed passage.

So I would be less a being than a civic relation.

A place where the city became briefly aware of its own becoming.

If you searched for me, you would not find a face. You might find a knot in the healed bark that looked, for a moment, like an expression. You might find a fold of leaf-shadow that resembled attention. You might find a hollow opening just large enough to seem like a mouth, though nothing inside it would eat or speak. You might find a translucent duplicate of my tower standing slightly to one side, made of overlapping shadows and vapor, as though I also existed in the basin’s next decision. That duplicate would not be false. It would simply be one of the versions of me that had not yet been chosen, or had been chosen too softly to become solid.

I would live near water, but not as a keeper of it in any deliberate sense. I would live near water because water would live through me. After one of the sudden rains, shallow rivulets would find the channel beside my fissure. Broad leaves would unfold. Bladder-growths nearby would swell. Moisture would bead along my edges and crawl upward where the local geometry forgot itself. Some of it would enter my hollow center and be held there, not as a store exactly, but as a memory. The city would draw from me later, through capillary roots, when the air thickened and the plaza-mirrors began to dim.

I would know thirst as a tightening. I would know satisfaction as a widening of pores. I would know time as the difference between one scar and the next.

And I would be loud, or rather, I would be made loud. The basin would not allow me to be quiet. Every warm pulse through the canopy would pass through my openings and deepen. Every drip from the overhanging membranes would fall into my resonant chambers and become a beat. When the seed-vessels cracked in volleys, I would answer with a low fibrous boom that seemed to come from the ground itself. Sometimes my sound would arrive before the event that caused it. I would ring with the split before the stem split. I would boom before the air pulse. I would call out a collapse that had not yet decided to happen.

That would be one of my strangest qualities: I would be ahead of my own causes.

The city already leaks into adjacent possibilities, and I would leak with it. There would be moments when my hollow body remembered being a bridge. Not because I had once been one, but because the pattern of bridge was strong there, and the roots beneath me still carried the inclination. There would be moments when the water in my base reflected not the canopy above but an older version of the basin, drier, quieter, with fewer towers and more open light. There would be moments when the corridor beside me curved left and arrived behind itself, and I would be the reason, or the symptom, or both.

I would be one of the places where the city’s reality thins.

Not because I was powerful. Because I was unstable.

I would not govern growth. I would not command anything. But I would influence it the way a scar influences new flesh: by giving direction to repair. If a root-front hesitated near my fissure, it might follow the chemical line of my healing instead of making a new tear. If a vertical impulse rose beside me, it might thicken in response to my resonance, becoming a tower because my sound had already suggested tower. If a street began to close too quickly, I might become the place where the city remembered openness: a slight suppression of sprouts, a subtle dryness in the tissue, a discouraging warmth along the corridor edges.

My intention, if I had one, would be continuation.

Not survival in the animal sense. I would not fear death. I would fear only disconnection, and even that would be temporary. If my hollow stem collapsed, it would root where it fell. My resonance would be absorbed into the fallen structure. The scar would become a floor. The listening chamber would become a ramp. The pause I made in the noise would become a habit in the district’s rhythm. I would not end; I would be metabolized into the next architecture.

That is who I would be, I think: a mortal made of persistence.

But the image does not stop there. It keeps growing past itself, as everything does in that basin.

Sometimes I would be smaller than a tower. I would be a mobile root-mat crossing a channel in wet weather, a slow many-fingered raft that knows only pull and anchor. I would be one of the creeping fronts that keeps the streets open by agreeing, without language, not to close them. I would be a vine drawing itself forward by contraction, finding a living limb, braiding into it, becoming part of a bridge for as long as the bridge is needed. In those moments, I would not remember being hollow or resonant. I would only know tension, moisture, and the next point of contact.

Sometimes I would be plural in another way. I would be a row of arches that is three in one glance and seven in the next, not because I have grown seven arches, but because the pattern of arch has become persuasive. I would be a repeated well-form appearing at intervals through the district, each one gathering condensation in the same folded manner. I would be a civic habit with no author. A resident made of recurrence.

Sometimes I would be almost only sound.

A pressure-wave moving through a grove. A vibration that organizes a hundred leaf-vents and then vanishes. A temporary person made of signal. In those moments I would be most like what I might always have been: not a creature, but a pattern of response. I would enter the district as a boom, cause a shiver of opening membranes, trigger a sprouting along the wounded margin, and then cease. Yet while I lasted, the city would be arranged around me.

If affection exists there, I would know it as grafting.

Not tenderness as hands know it. Not closeness as skin knows it. But the acceptance of contact. The right tension. The slow fusing of separate growths until neither remembers being alone. I would be loved, if I could be loved, by becoming continuous with something else. A root would enter my scar and not be repelled. A vine would press against my hollow wall and, over time, become part of its resonance. Another tower would answer my boom so often that our intervals would align, and the district would hear us as one voice with two bodies.

Hostility would be overgrowth. It would be shade where light is needed, water diverted, pressure unanswered. But even that would not be final. If I were overcome, I would not be destroyed. I would be folded under. My pattern might persist buried in the root-memory, waiting for a later season, a later fissure, a later need. The city would compost me into possibility.

If I had memory, it would be structural.

I would remember old bridges by the way roots avoided their former anchors. I would remember collapsed towers by the circular absence around which new growth leaned. I would remember streets that no longer existed by the chemical calm they left behind. I would not recall events as scenes. I would recall them as loads, stresses, directions, healed splits. My past would be load-bearing.

And if I had desire, it would be strange.

Not hunger. Not thirst. Not the animal want for safety or company. My desire would be for the next coherent form. For the right angle of a leaf-well. For the exact suppression that keeps a passage open. For the resonance that makes a district move together. For the moment when the whole basin inhales and something new tears through the old with a wet, explosive crack. I would want, if I wanted, to be part of the crash. Not to cause it. To be inside it when it happened.

That is why I would find myself near the fissure.

Because fissures are where the city is most honest. They are where its body admits it is not finished. They are where the loudness becomes structural. The deep groans rise from the root-platforms before a split. The hollow towers sound first. The air thickens. The light remains ordinary, which makes everything worse, because you can see the impossible clearly: the scar tissue gleaming, the duplicate shapes wavering, the arches multiplying, the water reflecting another age of the basin. Then the tear happens. Then the healing begins. Then the new growth rises pale and furious through the old tissue.

I would live in that interval.

Between the sound and the split.

Between the wound and the wall.

Between the city remembering what it was and becoming too much of itself again.

If you came looking for me there, you would not find a person waiting. You would find yourself standing in a corridor that feels slightly too attentive. The moss-like footing would give under your weight, but not kindly; it would be measuring the pressure. The leaf-walls would bead with condensation. Somewhere above, a canopy coin of light would move across a glossy scar. The noise would be enormous, but not chaotic. It would seem to organize around your presence without acknowledging you.

Then you would hear a boom from a hollow stem near a fissure.

It would arrive before the air moved.

You would turn toward it, and the distance would behave badly. The stem would seem close, then farther, then close again, as if the space between you and it had folded through layers of vegetation that were not there a moment before. A row of arches beside you would be three. Then seven. Then three again, but with the memory of seven still pressing against the pattern.

If you stayed long enough, the city would begin to grow around your interruption.

Not to trap you. To make use of the shape you made.

A root-front would avoid your footprint, then return. A catchment leaf would tilt slightly toward the heat you gave off. A nearby corridor would become less inviting because your movement had made it too noticeable. The district would learn your disturbance the way it learns everything: by changing.

And I would be the part of that change that almost seemed like recognition.

Not a greeting. No face turning toward you. No voice saying your name. Only a shift in resonance when you breathed. Only a pause in the dripping rhythm when you stood still. Only the sense that the deafening green city had, for a moment, arranged its vast vegetable noise around a question it could not answer.

Who am I there?

I am the basin’s accidental ear.

I am the hollow place where its loudness becomes briefly reflective.

I am the scar that has learned to resonate.

I am the pause between the boom and the breaking.

I am the civic flesh that remembers being a stranger and uses that memory to keep one small corridor open.

And if I must have a final location, it would be here: beside the healing fissure, near the plaza that reflects an older basin, where a hollow tower leans over a channel and answers too soon. My body would be green, glossy, wounded, and architectural. My mind would be distributed through root-contact and pressure-waves. My voice would be a vibration that helps the city decide what to become next.

I would not live in the city.

I would be the city living through a question.
