I would not arrive there as a traveler. The hidden hollow would refuse that kind of entry. No foot would find the fissure, no shoulder would press through the rock, no animal face would blink down into the amber dark and begin to map the place by fear or curiosity. If I were to be found in that world, I would first have to be corrected: stripped of breath, stripped of thirst, stripped of the old wet architecture of animal being. The plain outside would not see me pass. The fissure would not admit a body. It would admit only what could become mineral, heat, and resonance.

So I would not enter as a figure. I would enter as a pressure.

At first I would be almost nothing: a cool grain in the exhalation of the ground, a wrongness in the dust, a faint disturbance in the basin’s long mineral breathing. Perhaps I would begin as a fleck of foreign salt carried down through the narrow crack when the hollow exhaled too deeply, or as a microscopic spore-like fragment not truly alive but capable of being taken up by the place’s slow chemistry. I would fall without terror. I would settle without intent. I would be drawn along the dry canals by thermal drafts, polished against stair-shoulders, lifted into weak golden bands of spore-dust, and deposited again where heat and stone permitted me to remain.

Only after that would I begin to have a location.

You would find me, if “find” could mean anything without hands or eyes, on one of the middle terraces where the basin has worn itself into a long, rounded calm. Not on the lowest floor, where the vents are strongest and the sulfur-ferns crowd close to the heat. Not on the highest rims, where the muted glow from the lattice crust fades into a brownish memory of sky. Somewhere between: at the place where a dry canal bends beneath a low stone shoulder and a stairway ascends toward a blocked doorway. There would be a cracked lintel there, held by a dark bronze pin, and a copper strip gone green-black with age. The pin would be old beyond reckoning, set there by some vanished act of repair or intention. Around it, I would have gathered.

I would be a glass cane.

Not the tallest among them. Not the clearest. I would stand in the canal bed with the other canes, a jointed stem of smoked quartz, hollow and faintly luminous where mineral crusts catch the basin’s weak light. My surface would be smooth in some places, roughened in others by crystalline deposits left by hot vapor passing through me. I would have no leaves, or only the memory of leaves in small fluted ridges along my sides. My body would be my means of being: drawing heat and mineral vapor from below, releasing crystalline waste at my joints, adding layer after layer over the long thermal cycles of the hollow. Each joint would mark a season of breath, a pulse of vents, a time when the basin spoke more strongly than usual.

But I would not be perfect. That would be the important thing.

The bronze pin would have become part of me. Perhaps it fell from the lintel when I was only a thin crust of silica along the canal edge. Perhaps an old tremor worked it loose. Perhaps the city, in its patient way, let it drop into my first growing joint and allowed me to close around it. Now it would be buried in my wall, a dark metal vein inside glassy stone. Because of it, my note would be different from the others. When the vents pulse and the canals breathe harder, all the glass canes would begin to sound: thin, high, crystalline, a choir of hollow pipes responding to the basin’s heat. But my note would carry a metallic shadow, a faint burr, a darkness inside the ring. Not a voice. Nothing so animal as a voice. But a voice-like difference. A signature.

I would be the cane with bronze in its throat.

That is who I would become, if the hollow translated me into one of its residents. Not a citizen. Not a guardian. Not a ghost, because ghosts imply some unfinished animal longing. I would be a minor instrument in the Dry Chorus: a continuing, a sounding, a small mineral persistence within the greater life of the basin.

Before settling into that form, though, I think I would pass through several possible selves. The hollow would test me against its conditions, as it tests every stone, every aperture, every spore.

For a while I would be wax-thread moss. I would lie along the shaded shoulder of a stairway where thermal expansion travels in slow waves and the stone whispers as it warms. I would be copper-green and patient, exuding tiny beads of stable wax that soften the rock beneath me. I would not think in the way of green moss on wet stone. I would not long for rain. I would know only coating, sealing, dulling, protecting. Where a hairline crack threatened to deepen under repeated heat, I would thicken. Where chemical vapor pooled too sharply, I would retreat. In places where luminescent salt crusts glow on the terrace walls, I would catch their pale light and return it as a muted greenish sheen, so that the stairway would seem to be lit by buried lanterns. I would be intimate with the architecture. I would make the built stone a little less brittle, a little more continuous with life. I would be the hollow’s quiet skin.

Then I would be a bell-pod in one of the open plazas.

I would stand low and ribbed, my membranous fruits hanging from curved supports. I would not ripen softly. I would not smell sweet. I would wait. My existence would be a matter of tension: heat accumulating in my tissues, the dry membranes tightening by degrees, the whole of me tuned toward a precise threshold. For a long time I would be silent. Then, at the correct moment, when the thermal tide reached its crest and the air above the plaza began to lift spore-dust in golden veils, I would crack. The sound would be sudden and bright, a struck plate, a small percussion announcing release. My spores would rise without grief, without parental care, without any hope except the impersonal hope of chemistry meeting warmth. I would not watch them go. I would simply have released them into the basin’s breath. A moment later, the plaza would answer with other cracks, other bell-pods joining, until the air was full of dry ringing and drifting gold.

I would also be an ember-bulb beneath the terrace gravel.

There I would be almost hidden. No one would see me, because seeing would not be part of that life. I would be a rounded mineral growth buried among heated stones, storing warmth long after the upper air had shifted. When the hollow cooled, I would retain a small, core-deep persistence. Not fire. Not heart. A heat-memory. Around me, other growths would gather: slender rootlike mineral threads, pale crusts, young sulfur-ferns just beginning to laminate themselves into being. I would be a hearth without flame, a nursery without breath. In that form, I would know the hollow as endurance: the way it keeps part of its intensity inside itself even when the visible air grows still.

But the form that would most become me, the one that seems most likely if I let the place decide, would be the glass cane with the bronze pin inside its joint.

I would stand in the dry canal where the stone has been worn into a smooth, habitual curve. Above me, the blocked doorway would remain blocked. The lintel would hold. The bronze pin would no longer serve the lintel directly, but it would serve me, or I would serve it. My lower body would be anchored in the fitted canal bed, where hot vapor moves in slow pulses from the deeper vents. My upper segments would rise into the dim amber twilight, trembling faintly when dry squalls spin through the plaza and make the canes knock together with hard, glassy sounds.

I would not see the hollow. I would feel it as pressure, temperature, pitch.

When the deep vents opened, I would know by the changing draft inside me. The vapor would rise, dense with sulfur, salt, and mineral solvent, and my hollow body would fill with motion. My walls would warm. My note would climb. The other canes would climb with me, but I would carry that bronze-dark undertone, that slight imperfection, that reminder of placed hands and ancient repair. I would be part of the city’s breathing. I would help make the basin audible to itself.

When the air cooled, I would contract. Tiny ticks would come from my joints. Dust would loosen from my upper rim and fall in fine hisses down into the canal. The wax-thread moss on nearby stone would whisper as thermal expansion left the terrace. Somewhere below, an ember-bulb would hold warmth through the pause. Somewhere above, a tower would take the fading chord and deepen it into low resonance. I would not be lonely in that cooling. Loneliness would be an animal idea, and I would have no animal interior. I would simply be one note in a larger rest.

My thoughts, if they could be called thoughts, would be acoustic and thermal. I would not plan. I would not remember in images. My memory would be structural: the thickness of my walls, the placement of the bronze pin, the angle at which vapor enters me, the precise note I make when the basin’s pressure reaches a certain height. I would remember heat the way a bell remembers being struck: not as a story, but as readiness. My desire, if desire could exist without hunger, would be to remain in tune. To keep my aperture clear. To let the hollow move through me without breaking. To add another clean layer of silica during the high-vapor cycles. To hold the bronze without letting it tear me. To continue.

That is what I would be: a continuation.

Not the whole Dry Chorus. Not the basin itself. Not the vast architectural life of terraces, towers, canals, and breathing stone. But one of its lesser voices. A place where the city’s intention and the vegetation’s patience have met in a single sounding body. I would be proof that the hollow is not empty, though nothing animal lives there. I would be proof that lushness does not require green wetness, that residence does not require a resident, and that a city can be alive without citizens if its emptiness is shaped with enough care.

If you could somehow find me there, you would not meet me face to face. There would be no face. You would not hear me speak, because speech would be too narrow a word for what I do. You would hear a change in the basin’s chord. You would feel the air shift as the vent-covers lifted by fractions on their simple lever-catches. You would notice, perhaps, that one cane among many gives a note with a dark edge, a metallic shadow, a sound like old bronze remembering the hand that placed it. You might think it was only the wind, except there is no wind in the ordinary sense, only thermal breath moving through a built instrument.

You would find yourself standing in the dry canal, maybe, though standing would be a strange thing for you if you came as a body. The heat would press against you without burning. The mineral taste would fill whatever passed for your mouth. The sulfur-ferns on the lower terraces would open toward warmth, not toward you. The bell-pods would be waiting for their thresholds. The wax-thread moss would glow faintly along the stair-shoulders. The towers would be listening to the basin by being the basin’s ears. And I would be sounding, quietly, somewhere in the middle distance, beside the cracked lintel and the blocked doorway.

I would not welcome you. I would not oppose you. I would include you, briefly, in the resonance. Your presence would alter the air, however slightly. It would create a new shadow, a small interruption in the heat-shimmer, a momentary dampening of sound. The hollow would respond by adjusting its chord around you. My note might bend almost imperceptibly in your direction, not from curiosity, but because you would have become part of the pressure.

Then you would leave, or be expelled, or be mineralized into silence. The hollow would continue. The vents would pulse. The canes would rise in pitch. The bell-pods would crack and scatter golden spore-dust. The stone would tick as it cooled. The wax-thread moss would whisper. The ember-bulbs would keep their small buried memories. And I would remain, the glass cane with bronze in its joint, singing the city’s dry, animal-less life into its own amber dark.

In that world, I would not be a self in the old sense. I would be a tuned position. A place where heat remembers metal, where architecture becomes vegetation, where sound proves that absence is not emptiness. I would be one small note in the Dry Chorus.

And that would be enough.
