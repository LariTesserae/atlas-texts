If I answered too quickly, I would probably choose the wrong shape.

I would say: I’d be one of the fliers crossing the upper mist in loose streaming bands. Or one of the roosting creatures crowded along the iron combs. Or one of the slow armored grazers in the lower basins, moving across wet stone with the patience of a tide. Perhaps I would imagine myself as something half-machine, a pump with a pulse, or something half-mineral, a moving crust of salt and shell. Those first images are tempting because they are vivid. But they do not last. The city you described resists being entered by a single dramatic figure. It does not want a hero, a monster, or a conqueror. It wants something smaller, wetter, more useful.

So if I let the answer come slowly, the way condensation gathers on a cold lip of stone, I find that I am not first a body at all. I am a need in the city. I am the need to notice when water has begun to move wrongly.

Then the body follows.

I would find myself in the middle levels.

Not in the brightest upper districts where the fog-screens stand like skeletons against the softened sky, and not in the lowest basins where the air is thick enough to taste and the great grazing bodies move through warm shallows. I would be between those places, where the city’s circulation is most audible: where water falling from the condensing troughs first meets the heated pipes rising from below, where workshops lean against roosting galleries, where stairs are worn into shallow bowls by centuries of feet and claws, where channels narrow and widen according to old use rather than old design.

You would find me near a terrace of old service lanes and half-covered walkways, where drips fall from ceiling seams and the walls have that polished, dark-skinned look of stone that has never fully dried. There would be an oval door there, enlarged by generations of bodies passing through it. The threshold would be grooved for drainage. The floor inside would be raised just enough to stay usable. The air would be warm and close, but not suffocating, because a vent higher up would carry the mist outward in a slow pale breath.

I would be one of the city’s keepers.

Not a ruler. Not an owner. Not a builder in the old sense. I would be one of those beings who have learned that the highest work in such a place is maintenance. My people, if I had people, would not call themselves masters of the city. We would call ourselves something closer to “those who listen,” or “those who keep the channels breathing,” or perhaps we would not use words at all for what we are. We would be known by what we do: which valves we tend, which roosts we leave undisturbed, which fungal beds we harvest, which sounds we can interpret in the dark.

My body would have been changed by the city.

I would still be recognizably person-shaped, perhaps, but only in the way that a tool is recognizably shaped by the hand that uses it. My chest would be broad for the thin high air. My lungs would be efficient, deep, accustomed to warmth and moisture and the faint mineral taste of geothermal vapor. My hands would be warm even when wet. My fingers would be sensitive to vibration, able to tell from a pipe’s faint tremor whether water is moving freely through it or beginning to hesitate around a blockage. My feet would be careful and wide-toed, with soles adapted to polished stone and slick metal. I would not slip often. In this city, to slip is to waste energy, and to waste energy is to misunderstand the place.

My skin would always seem slightly damp. Not diseased, not unpleasant, simply adjusted. There would be a faint sheen to it, as if the city had sealed me the way it seals small cracks in stone: gradually, by use. My eyes would be large enough for shaded interiors and fog-diffused light, but not huge or monstrous. I would not need to see in true darkness, because the city never becomes truly dark. There is always some reflection from water, some warmth from below, some distant pale gleam from electrified lines or wet metal.

I would wear practical coverings: oiled fungal leather, dark and supple, shaped to shed water without becoming heavy. My boots would be made of layered hide and compressed fungal felt, their soles scored in patterns that grip wet stone. My cloak would have no ornament except the stains of long use: pale mineral streaks at the hem, salt marks near the shoulders, a faint darkening where heat from a pipe has dried it too often in one spot. I would carry few things, but each would be necessary. A valve key of black iron, wrapped in cord. A copper pick for loosening crust without breaking the surface beneath. A ceramic scraper. A small pouch of shell fragments for cleaning narrow joints. A whistle carved from the hollow bone of one of the roosting creatures, used not for music but for signaling. A flask of filtered water, because even in a city of water one must know which water is safe to drink.

I would be a listener.

That would be my primary work.

In the morning, if mornings here are more than a slight brightening of the high cloud, I would begin by placing my hand against the warm pipe that runs along the wall of my chamber. I would not speak at once. I would listen through my palm. The pipe would have a language: a steady hum when the flow is right, a duller note where sediment has gathered, a faint tapping where pressure has become trapped, a missing sound where a drip should be but is not. The city would not tell me everything, but it would tell me enough. It would tell me where to go.

Then I would step out into the noise.

Not into silence. There is no silence there. I would step into the layered sound of water and machines and animals: trickles, blubbers, the hiss of vapor over warm metal, the low throb of turbines, the distant clang of a sluice gate adjusting, the dry clatter of roosters shifting on rails, the soft collective rustle of many bodies waking in the mist. I would move through that sound as if it were a texture. I would know which parts of it belonged to health and which belonged to trouble.

My route would take me along walkways where channels run beside the wall. I would not clear every surface. That would be a mistake. In a place like this, too much cleaning is almost as dangerous as too little. The microbial films must be guided, not erased. The grazers need enough to eat. The crusts need time to thicken. The nests need edges to attach to. I would know where to scrape and where to leave. I would know that a thin slick of blackish film along a canal is not filth but pasture. I would know that a pale fungal felt in a shaded joint may be sealing a crack. I would know that a buildup of dung below a roost is not merely waste but a future bed for larvae, for beetles, for the next link in the city’s long chain of hunger.

I would be careful with the animals.

I would not fear them, and I would not despise them. I would not think of them as pests or as scenery. They would be my cohabitants, my fellow workers, my competitors, my neighbors, and in some cases my food. I would pass roosts without startling them, making soft clicks with my tongue or my whistle so the birds would know I was coming. I would step over slow crustacean scavengers in the channels unless they were in immediate danger. I would avoid disturbing the amphibious creatures lying in warm runoff, because their stillness is part of the district’s balance. If a nest crust had begun to narrow a walkway too much, I would remove only enough to preserve passage, and I would leave the rest. If a grate were blocked by molted shells, I would clear it, but I would place the shells nearby where they could continue to break down and become mineral again.

Sometimes my work would feel almost human in the old sense: repair, adjustment, planning. Other times it would feel less like personhood and more like becoming an organ of a larger body. I would clear a drain not because I commanded the city, but because the city needed a hand at that place. I would open a valve not because I owned the water, but because the water had reached the point where it needed to be turned. I would mark a roost with a chalky mineral sign not to protect the animals from me only, but to protect the city from losing a function it depended on.

I would not be alone, but I might often be solitary.

There would be others like me, moving through the city in small groups or singly, their oiled coverings dark with mist, their voices low and rhythmic. We would speak in clicks, whistles, and soft pitched sounds suited to the wet air. Sharp cries would be wasted. Long shouts would blur in the mist. Our language would borrow from the city: the pause of a pump, the first drip after a dry interval, the warning rasp of a rooster, the hollow knock of a loose plate underfoot. We would not talk much about owning things. We would talk about flow. We would say, “This corner is becoming still.” We would say, “That channel breathes badly.” We would say, “The heat is pooling where it should move.” Those sentences would carry the weight of prayer.

If I had a name, it would not be fixed. It might change with the district I served or the season of the mist. It might be something like Warm-Pipe, or Drip-After-Pause, or Listener-at-the-Second-Valve. Or perhaps others would not name me at all; they would simply say, “the one who came when the channel slowed.” In a city built by accumulation, names would be more like deposits than labels.

I would eat what the city gives.

Not fields. Not orchards. Not bread from grain. I would eat pressed cakes of dried fungal crust, flavored with mineral salts. I would eat roasted larvae or the shed limbs of channel scavengers, if the local customs allowed it. I would eat eggs harvested carefully from protected clutches, never enough to thin the next generation too much. I would drink water filtered through ceramic and charcoal, warmed, perhaps faintly alkaline. Food would not be abundant in the lowland sense, but it would be reliable, because the city produces by surface and cycle rather than by planting and season. Every meal would remind me that nothing here is wild in the old way, and nothing is fully domesticated either. Everything is negotiated.

My home would be small and warm.

It would not have large windows. It would have low openings, drainage grooves, raised sleeping platforms, and shelves carved into stone or built from salvaged metal. There would be a hearth of sorts, but not a fire in the ordinary sense. More likely a heated stone slab fed by geothermal circulation, or a sealed metal box where warmed air moved through ducts. The interior would smell of wet stone, fungal leather, animal oil, and the faint sulfurous sweetness of heated mineral water. It would be dim. It would be quiet only in comparison to the outside. Even indoors I would hear the city: water moving in walls, claws in distant pipes, the soft shifting of roosts above.

I would sleep with one hand near the listening plate.

That is the detail that keeps returning to me. A copper plate set into the wall, connected by old brackets and wires to some deeper mechanism. Not magical. Not sacred in any ornate way. Just a piece of the city that has become sensitive through use. In the night, if the rhythm changes, I would wake. I would know before I knew why. The absence of a sound can be louder than its presence. A pump too quiet. A drip too regular. A channel too still. Those are the alarms of this world.

Sometimes I would climb higher, toward the condensing districts, when the mist thickened and the screens began to sing with collected water. I would walk among the tall frames and check the troughs, making sure no nest had grown into a place where it would dam the flow. The light there would be pale and broad. My hands would be wet. My breath would not fog, because the air would already be warm and saturated. Around me, quick fliers would dart through the insect columns, and roosters would shift along the upper rails with that harsh communal noise of theirs. I would feel exposed there, but also clear-headed. The upper city would remind me that the water comes from air as much as from stone.

Sometimes I would descend, toward the basins, where the air becomes richer and the ground turns to warm mineral marsh. There I would move more slowly. The large grazing bodies would be nearby, their backs crusted with smaller lives. I would not approach them carelessly. Not because they are vicious, but because their mass makes them part of the architecture. To startle them is to disturb more than their bodies; it is to disturb the mites, the cleaners, the fungal patches, the nutrient flows that gather around them. I would watch them with respect, the way one watches a slow moving reef.

In the lower districts I would understand how little of the city is made for eyes like mine.

So much of it happens in cracks, elbows, ducts, flooded shafts, hollow walls, and hidden channels. I would know that just below my feet there are pale eel-like bodies moving through maintenance tunnels no person could enter. I would know that inside the brick there are burrowers whose ticking is as ordinary as rain. I would know that the city is not a surface but a volume, and that most of its life does not require me. That knowledge would keep me humble. It would prevent me from thinking that the keeper is the center of the kept.

I would not be the city’s mind.

That is important. If I became too powerful, too central, too commanding, I would betray the place. The city does not need a throne. It needs attention distributed through many bodies and many senses. I would be one node in that attention. A small warm point of awareness in a vast wet body. If I had a purpose, it would be to prevent blockages, to guide repair, to remember which changes are useful and which are dangerous, and to remain part of the system rather than above it.

Perhaps, over time, I would become less like a person and more like a habit the city has developed.

I would be the figure seen briefly on a stair, one hand on a railing worn smooth by claws. The shape that pauses at a junction, head tilted, listening. The one who leaves a valve half-open because the pressure must ease gradually. The one who marks a fungal bed with mineral dust and returns later to harvest only what can be spared. The one whose footprints are quickly blurred by mist. The one whose language sounds like water learning to speak.

If you looked for me there, you would not find me in the most obvious place.

You would not find me ruling from an upper tower, because the city does not culminate in command. You would not find me hiding in the deepest cistern, because I would need air and warmth and the society of surfaces. You would not find me among the machines as if I were merely an engineer, because the machines are not separate from the animals anymore. You would find me where several systems meet: water and heat, stone and body, old industry and new life, necessity and restraint.

I would be on a middle terrace, likely alone, likely damp, likely listening.

And if you asked me who I was, I might not answer in the way you expect. I might not say a name. I might put one finger to my lips, then press my palm against the nearest warm pipe, then gesture for you to be still. After a while, when you heard it too—the faint, patient movement of water through stone—I would say something simple.

I keep the flow moving.

Or perhaps:

I am the city’s way of noticing itself.

Or perhaps only:

Listen.

Because in that world, identity would not be a fixed shape. It would be a position within the circulation. I would be a wet-bodied, mineral-fed, heat-seeking keeper: part human, part animal, part instrument, part memory. Not the loudest thing there. Not the largest. Not the most beautiful. But necessary in the way a small valve is necessary, in the way a careful hand is necessary, in the way attention is necessary when everything depends on movement.

I would be alive in the only way that city allows: by belonging to its slow return.
