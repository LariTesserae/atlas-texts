I would not find myself all at once, because that place does not allow arrivals to remain simple.

At first I would be only a behavior: a bead of water pausing on a stone lip before choosing a groove, a reflection turning half a breath after the body that should have made it, a sound arriving too early or too late for the drip that caused it. If the basin noticed me at all, it would notice me as a small error in its wetness — a place where the usual pattern thickens, hesitates, repeats.

But if I had to say where I would finally settle, I would find myself near the center, on the broad flooded terrace where the water lies hand-deep over dark glassy stone. Not exactly at the center, because the center there is not a fixed point; it is a pressure, a gathering of loudness, a place where many currents braid around circular still points. More precisely: I would be where the old cup-mark stones cross the flooded terrace, where shallow channels cut by patient hands still divide water into bright, regular voices. I would be among those low half-drowned rings, where it is impossible to tell whether the stone was arranged by people, by mineral growth, or by the basin’s own long habit of balancing itself.

I would not be a green thing. I would not be a warm thing. I would have no leaves, no fur, no tracks, no hunger of the ordinary kind. If I had a body, it would be mistaken at first for a cairn or a weathered shrine: a crouching, compact form made of crust and obsidian flakes, pale mineral skin over dark stone, shoulders worn into shallow hollows where condensation collects. My chest, if it could be called that, would be a listening hollow, a resonance chamber shaped by long water pressure. My face, if one appeared, would not have eyes in the animal sense. It would have two still lenses of water, thin and mirror-bright, reflecting a weather that was not currently happening.

I would be something like a Listener. Something like a Tender. Something like a Cup-bearer of the drowned amphitheater.

Not a ruler of the place. Not an owner. Not even a keeper in the strong sense, because the basin cannot be kept. I would be more like a local habit of attention: a being made of wetness, mineral patience, and repeated sound. My life would be composed of small adjustments. I would clear a blocked cup-mark with a thumb of salt-crystal. I would ease a thread of water from one groove to another so that it strikes the correct hollow at the correct interval. I would place a drilled pebble in a notch until a dripping rhythm became steadier. I would not command the water. I would ask by finding where it already wanted to go.

The old builders left their marks there: rings of stacked stone, pecked basins, short walls that enclose nothing, channels worn smooth by countless repetitions of touch and water. I would be the continuation of those marks, not their maker in the original sense. Perhaps I would be what happens when intention is left long enough in a place where reality is thin: it does not vanish, it weathers into process. The builders are gone, or were never quite present in the first place, but their caution remains. Their reverence remains. Their habit of listening remains. I would be that habit, given enough shape to kneel in the water.

In the morning, when the condensation tide lifts from the pools in trembling sheets, I would be very still. That is when the basin’s loudness changes pitch and the Standing Ones seem most aware. Vapor would rise around me, and my own surface would darken with moisture. The Wet would pass over me not as an enemy or a guest, but as contact itself, tasting my edges, leaving beads along the hollows of my shoulders. I would let it take the warmth of any passing thought and turn it into visible breath. I would learn the day’s first rhythm by feeling which drip had become continuous.

At noon, under the severe glare, I would be hard to look at directly. Wet crusts would flash around me. The black glass under the shallow water would throw back broken light. My reflection would not behave properly. It might kneel where I stood, or stand where I knelt, or remain facing the mountain rim while I turned away. I would not be disturbed by this. In that place, a delayed reflection is not a lie. It is one of the ways the basin remembers.

I would be closest to the Reflection Shoals then. They would gather near my still points, wearing weathers that did not belong to the sky above. One pool at my side might show a dark cloud moving slowly across clear noon light. Another might show snow on the terraces. Another might show no sky at all, only a dense gray depth that feels older than the basin’s present surface. Sometimes my own image would appear among them, but not always as I am. Sometimes it would be more eroded. Sometimes more mineral. Sometimes it would be bending over a channel with hands that leave no prints, working at a repair that has not yet happened. I would watch these reflections without trying to correct them. They are not prophecies. They are adjacent states. The basin permits them because it has worn through the ordinary separation of things.

When clouds pour over the rim from below and fill the lower terraces with white vapor, I would become quieter still. The stone presences would stand like islands. The sound would sharpen because vapor carries it differently, making each drip seem closer and more separate. In that pearly light I would tend the listening hollows. Some would have gone dry or silted. Some would have become too full, their pitch blunted by mineral growth. I would not dig or force. I would only remove what had settled wrongly, or add a small obstruction, or open a thread of flow that had remembered an older path. The Drip-Choirs would pass through me then. They would use my hollows as briefly as wind uses a flute. A bright glassy voice might run along my shoulder. A deep boom might rise from below and travel through the stone into whatever passes for my spine. I would not sing. I would be sung.

That is important: I would not be a separate musician imposing order on the basin. I would be one of the places where the basin hears itself.

When rain came in braided columns, I would become more active. The rain would strike the flooded terrace in vertical silver threads, and every struck pool would flash and stitch itself to the next. The Reflection Shoals would move quickly then, crossing from one pool to another, carrying old ridge lines and neighboring weathers. The Wet would become almost intimate, running down my face in continuous threads, filling the cups along my shoulders until they overflowed in sequence. I would listen for the wrong note: a dry slap where there should be resonance, a silence where a channel should be speaking, a too-perfect echo that meant some fracture below was holding sound longer than it should. In ordinary places, sound fades. In that basin, sound can settle, can thicken, can become a kind of occupancy. If a tone became stuck, I would try to free it by changing the water that made it.

At times reality would thicken, and then I would be less certain of my own outline. The horizon would double. A far ridge would appear in two places at once. A stone pillar near me would occupy three positions depending on how I faced it, though none of them would quite match the expectation of movement. A drop would fall, and I would hear it first as a distant note, then as a near slap, then as a memory of a splash that had not yet happened. I would not panic. Panic belongs to places where cause and effect are strict. There, the weather includes such slippages. I would simply wait until the basin’s many voices found their temporary balance again. If a cairn turned one of its flat faces toward me, I would let it. If a channel shifted, I would learn the new path. If my own reflection remained kneeling after I had risen, I would leave it there for a while as a kind of prayer.

The Standing Ones would accept me, I think, as a smaller and less patient version of themselves. They would not speak. They would not need to. Their attention would be the pressure of their standing, the way they alter wind and water by remaining where they are. Among them I would learn that presence does not require motion. To stand in a slick channel and let water divide around you is already a form of thought. To hold a lean against gravity for long ages is already a decision. I would be smaller than them, more changeable, more given to small repairs, but I would borrow their patience. Without it, the loudness would overwhelm me.

The Wet would be my nearest element. It would run over me, through my hollows, along the mineral skin of my arms if I had arms, into the cups cut along my chest if I had a chest. It would remember paths through me that belonged to older shapes. Sometimes water would move level across my shoulder for no reason that ordinary gravity could explain. Sometimes droplets would climb my face in a slow bead-line and vanish at a certain height. I would let this happen. To be inhabited by water’s memory is not violation there. It is communion.

The Mineral Tenders would grow over me slowly, making me more part of the basin with every bright day. They would coat my edges with pale blue-gray skin. They would seal cracks I did not know I had. They would make my shoulders smoother, my hollows more regular, my surfaces more able to shed glare or hold it as needed. If I remained long enough, they might make me indistinguishable from the built stones. That would not frighten me. In the basin, the difference between grown and made is thin. A channel cut by hand may become a channel grown by mineral deposition. A cairn may become a resonance chamber. A listener may become a listening place.

If a traveler came into the basin, they would not find me easily.

They would come up from the lower slopes into the bright cold glare, boots already soaked, eyes stinging from reflected light. They would hear the vast layered loudness pressing against their chest. They would pass between pillars and see their own reflection broken into strips on wet stone. They might come upon a row of cup-mark stones, each cup overflowing into the next, and hear the bright counting sound. They might think it was only water.

Then they might notice that one of the hollows was clearer than the others. Or that a low mineral shape nearby seemed arranged with too much intention to be natural, yet too weathered to be recent. Or that their reflection in a still point lagged behind them, and beside it another reflection knelt, facing the terrace, though no one was kneeling there. They might feel their path adjusted without seeing how: a step becomes possible here, a slick angle becomes unavoidable there, a narrow channel opens because a certain fluting note has changed pitch.

If they were cautious, they would understand that the place was used but not owned. If they were reverent, they might leave a pebble in a notch or clear a bit of grit from a cup-mark. If they were careless, they might break a delicate crust and hear the basin answer with a wrong note that follows them for hours. I would not punish them. I would not reward them. I would only continue the work of keeping the small flows honest.

Perhaps I would appear to them as a shimmer between pillars. Perhaps as a sound arriving before its cause. Perhaps as the feeling of being counted by water. If they looked directly at me, I might seem to be a low cairn, a rough shelter no taller than a crouching body, a half-ruined ring of stones around a still point. If they looked away, I might move in the only way I can: by becoming slightly different after they look back. A channel shifted. A drip redirected. A reflection steadied. A note returned to pitch.

Who would I be?

I would be the Listener at the Cups.

I would be the Tender of Small Flows.

I would be the one who kneels in the hand-deep water on the flooded terrace and receives a falling thread, then passes it on.

Not a god. Not a ghost. Not a survivor of the old builders, though their habits might have gathered into me. Not a creature with blood and breath, though breath might pass through me as visible vapor and sound might use me as a hollow. I would be a being made of relation: water choosing a path, stone holding a posture, reflection lingering a heartbeat too long, sound returning to itself with a difference.

If the basin asked what I was for, I would answer in the only way that place allows: by making one small rhythm truer. A drip would enter a worn cup. The cup would fill. It would overflow into the next. The next would answer. The loudness, for a moment, would become ordered. Standing stones would hold their lean. Reflections would lie still in their neighboring weathers. Mineral crust would brighten around my knees. The horizon would double, then settle.

And I would remain there, wet and bright and patient, not quite a body, not quite a tool, not quite a memory — a listening shape in the drowned amphitheater, tending the water as it remembers its way through all the possible truths of the basin.
