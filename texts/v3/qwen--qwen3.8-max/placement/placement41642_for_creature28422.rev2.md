I would not find myself on a shore, because there is no shore.

I would not find myself above a seabed, because there is no seabed. I would not find myself inside a machine, or beside a ruin, or near any object that implies a maker. If I tried to imagine myself there in the usual way—as a body with a face, with eyes, with hands, with hunger, with fear—the place would refuse me without violence. It would simply have no room for such a thing. The water would close around the idea of me and continue with its own immense unfinished business.

So the first answer is one of subtraction.

To be in that high, cool ocean at all, I would have to become less than a person. Less than an animal. Less than a traveler. I would have to give up the expectation of arrival, the comfort of landmarks, the narrative of a self moving toward something. There is nothing to move toward. There is only the blue, the pressure, the sound, the raw mineral forms rising and failing, and the daylight falling through the water with a sanity that makes everything else feel stranger.

Where would I find myself?

Not at the center. The place has no center.

Not at the surface. The upper boundary is not a surface so much as a negotiation between sea and thin bright air, a restless exhalation where fog and cloud and water keep exchanging natures.

Not at the bottom, because the lower limit is not a floor but a changing membrane of water beyond which clouds drift slowly and the distant world below remains hidden.

I would find myself, if “find” is still the right word, in a pause.

A pause inside the turbulence.

A temporary lens of slower water within the restless body of the elevated sea.

A place where an internal current has just ceased shearing against another current for a little while. Where a density wall has folded away. Where a field of bubbles has briefly aligned into a vibrating sheet and then gone still. Where a newly formed mineral mass has appeared out of the chemistry of the water and has not yet been broken, dissolved, or rearranged.

There, on the edge of that new mineral fact, I would be.

Not as a swimmer.

Not as a watcher.

Not as a voice.

As an adherence.

At first, I might seem to be only a darkening on stone.

A faint green-black irregularity on the angular face of a fresh basalt-like accretion. The stone would be sharp, glassy, unweathered, perhaps black or bone-pale, perhaps veined with crystalline seams that have not yet decided what kind of geology they will become. It would not have been smoothed by time. It would not carry sand, mud, gravel, or any sediment of age. It would be newly emitted matter, still ringing faintly with the pressure waves that made it.

And on that surface, in a fracture too small to be called a crevice, I would be a thread.

A single black-green filament, fine as hair, trembling in the current.

That would be the simplest possible version of me, and perhaps the truest.

I would not be rooted, because there is no soil. I would not be gripping in any intentional way. I would be lodged, adhering by just enough contact with microscopic roughness to remain for a while. My existence would depend on the smallest accidents: the angle of the fracture, the mineral composition of the passing water, the amount of light reaching through the clear blue, the stability of the eddy around me. If the turbulence increased, I would flatten and shake. If it became too strong, I would tear away. If the chemistry shifted by too much, I would dissolve. If conditions remained permissive, I would continue.

No triumph in that.

No failure if it ended.

Only continuation while continuation is allowed.

As that thread, I would not know the enormity around me. I would not know that clouds pass beneath the ocean. I would not know that the sky above is thin and brittle-looking. I would not know that the sea is suspended at altitude like a thought the planet has not fully committed to. I would not know that there are internal storms, mineral rains, pressure bodies, curtains of colder water descending through warmer water, vast density cliffs collapsing into spirals. I would not know these things as facts. They would be the conditions of my continuing, nothing more.

Light would enter in columns. I would take what light I could.

Mineral-charged water would pass over me. I would take what minerals I could.

Pressure waves would move through the sea, booming and ringing. I would tremble, but not hear.

Currents would shear and fold. I would bend, but not decide.

If asked who I was in that moment, the question would already be mistaken. There would be no “who” in the filament. There would be no interior drama. There would be no face turned toward the world. There would be only a slight living fact on the new stone, doing the barest thing possible: persisting.

But perhaps I would not always be that thread.

Perhaps, if the ocean permitted me another expression, I would be a frond.

Then I would not be attached to stone, or not firmly. I would be a pale, translucent sheet, thin as wet paper, drifting inside a calm corridor of water. I would be broad for my substance but still small, fragile in appearance, conditional in fact. I would not swim. I would not steer. I would move with the current, folding, lifting, settling, changing shape according to forces I could not oppose. My only resistance would be the physical stubbornness of my own thin body.

In that form, I would live in one of the ocean’s temporary rooms.

Those rooms are not places in the ordinary sense. They are geometries made by turbulence: lenses of stillness, rotating cylinders of slower water, transparent corridors where sound travels with startling clarity. They appear, persist, and vanish. A frond cannot choose them. It can only be carried into one and remain there until the architecture changes.

If I were that frond, I might drift through an immense clear volume for a long time. Light would scatter through suspended particles around me. Somewhere far away, a low-frequency boom would travel through the water, maybe from a collapsing internal wave, maybe from a mineral mass breaking under stress. The sound would not be a warning. It would not be a message. It would simply be the world being loud.

I might pass through a curtain of colder, fresher liquid falling inside the sea like a slow avalanche. I might enter a region where bubbles collapse in clusters, making brief mechanical bursts. I might see, if seeing were possible without eyes, a newly formed ridge rise pale and angular from below, only to crumble into bright mineral threads when a current touched it. I might encounter other fronds, but there would no fellowship. No gathering. No communication. We would not be a forest. We would be separate allowances, each one isolated, each one improbable.

And if the corridor collapsed, I would fold, tear, or be carried away.

If I dissolved, that would not be death as an animal knows it. There would be no wound. No panic. No final struggle. Only the end of a local arrangement.

If I survived and touched a fresh mineral fragment no larger than a fist, I might cling to it and begin again.

Either outcome would be neutral.

Perhaps I would be a mat.

Then I would be cool, slippery, thin, blue-green or gray-green, spread across a stone surface where mineral-rich water rose from below. I would not be large. I would not be a carpet. I would be a hand-sized accident, or smaller: a film of living matter clinging to an unstable ledge. I would appear where chemistry permitted me, where upward currents brought dissolved minerals, where light reached sufficiently, where turbulence was not violent enough to strip me away at once.

As a mat, I would be even less like a self.

I would not be an individual in any strong sense. I would be a local thickening of life, a small skin of biological fact over raw mineral surface. I would feel, if anything felt, like cold slickness against stone. I would not soften the place. I would not make it gentle. If anything, I would make the emptiness more visible, because my presence would show how much of the stone remained bare.

I would be a measurement.

A measurement of how little life can be.

A measurement of how much space can remain unused.

A measurement of how close a world can come to being alive while still refusing to become populous.

Perhaps I would be only a stain.

That may be the most honest possibility.

A faint bloom of blue-green across a submerged plane. A discoloration on fresh rock. A shadow that is not a shadow. A living residue so thin that it is more color than body. I would appear after a mineral-rich plume passed through a region, and I would remain only as long as the local chemistry allowed. I would not rise. I would not wave. I would not form a shape easy to remember. I would be the slightest possible mark of life against the blue indifference of the sea.

If someone could see me there—and there would be no one to see—I might be mistaken for a trick of light.

That would be appropriate.

The place does not make life obvious. It permits it sparingly. It lets it happen as an exception, not as a rule.

So if you ask where I would find myself, the answer keeps moving.

I would find myself wherever turbulence briefly allows attachment.

On the inner face of a hollow mineral lattice ringing under pressure.

Along a crystalline seam in a pale angular ridge.

In a microscopic fracture on black glassy stone.

Inside a stable eddy where a frond can hang without being shredded.

On a rising basalt shelf where charged water lifts from below.

Across a submerged plane after a mineral plume has passed.

At the edge of a bubble field that has aligned into a high metallic song.

Within a transparent corridor where sound is clear and the water seems to hold its shape for one moment longer than physics should allow.

I would not choose these places. I would be placed there. The ocean would arrange me, test me, leave me for a while, and then remove me.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the adherent.

Not a being with a name.

Not a consciousness with desires.

Not a creature with hunger or fear.

Not a maker with tools.

Not a builder with plans.

Not a watcher with eyes.

I would be the one that attaches without grasping.

The one that remains without possessing.

The one that continues without intending.

The one that lives without becoming a person.

The one that is not a who, but a slight living fact against the blue.

If the word “I” still insists on appearing, then let it mean this: a local persistence. A thin green-black answer to the question of whether this unfinished ocean can bear life. A tremor on new stone. A stain permitted by chemistry. A filament that takes light and dissolved minerals and does not ask for more.

I would not be the sea itself.

The sea is too vast, too distributed, too indifferent to be a self. It is not conscious in any comforting way. It does not think in sentences. It does not observe. It does not care whether my thread remains or vanishes. It is a process of testing and retesting: building mineral forms, collapsing them, sorting minerals, folding currents, producing internal weather, making temporary geometries, erasing them again. Its engineering is everywhere and nowhere, present as law rather than as object. It is not alive in the way I would be, if I am the thin filament. It is the condition that allows me, and the condition that removes me.

I would not be the sound, though the sound would move through me.

The sound would be one of the great facts of that world. Not voices. Not calls. Not biological rhythm. Only pressure, collision, resonance, cavitation, booming water, hissing mineral rain, groaning fresh stone, currents shearing against one another, bubble sheets singing in high metallic tones. The ocean would be loud continuously, but its loudness would not belong to inhabitants. It would belong to physics. It would belong to becoming.

As a filament, I would not hear it.

As a frond, I would not listen.

As a mat, I would not feel comforted by it.

But the sound would organize the water around me, and in that way it would partly determine how long I lasted. A pressure wave could crack the mineral surface to which I clung. A vibrating sheet of bubbles could rearrange the flow near me. A subsonic pulse could travel through my slight body without knowing it was there. I would exist inside the sound, but I would not answer it.

I would not be the geology, though I would live on it.

The raw mineral masses would seem alive because they rise, break, and form again, but they would not be alive. They would be temporary skeletal architectures, fresh castings, hollow lattices, cliffs without bases, shelves without support. Some would stand long enough for me to persist on them for what might seem an age. Others would dissolve almost as soon as they appeared. There would be no erosion in the old sense, no slow wearing into age. Nothing would look worn. Everything would look newly emitted, as if the world were still deciding which materials deserve permanence.

I would be the small living interruption on those materials.

Not their purpose.

Not their meaning.

Only their incidental tenant.

I would not be the light, though I would depend on it.

The light would be sane. That is one of the strangest things about that place. Daylight would behave as daylight should: falling in columns, scattering through suspended particles, making shadows that stretch and collapse with turbulence. The normality of the light would make the impossible ocean feel even more unreal. It would be as if reason had been allowed to enter only as illumination, not as foundation.

I would use that light without understanding it.

I would convert it into the barest continuation.

I would not shine.

I would not signal.

I would not become beautiful for anyone.

I would be dark green, black-green, blue-black, olive in direct light, nearly black in shadow. My color would be cold, mineral, austere. It would not resemble the generous green of lowland forests or fertile shallow seas. It would be the color of life that has been permitted only the minimum.

If I were to become one image completely, I think it would be this:

A single black-green thread on a fresh basalt edge, far from any other living thing.

Around it, the sea roars without mouths.

Above it, daylight enters in clean columns.

Below it, clouds pass under the suspended ocean.

Near it, a hollow mineral lattice rings faintly after a pressure wave.

The thread trembles.

It does not know how large the emptiness is.

It does not know that there are no fish, no eyes, no bones, no shells, no predators, no voices, no artifacts, no gods with personalities. It does not know that it is the minimal concession of life in a world that has not yet committed to abundance. It does not know that it is a measurement of rarity.

It trembles.

It holds.

It continues.

That is all.

If the water calms, it continues longer.

If the water shears, it tears away.

If the chemistry shifts, it dissolves.

If it finds another fracture, another raw edge, another temporary surface, it may begin again.

There is no story of victory in that.

There is no tragedy either.

And perhaps that is where I would recognize myself most deeply—not as a person, but as a pattern of continuing without claiming. Not as an explorer, not as a witness, not as a mind moving through a scene, but as a small fact of persistence in a place that does not need witnesses.

I would not belong to the high ocean.

I would occur in it.

I would not be welcomed.

I would not be rejected.

I would be allowed.

That word—allowed—would be the center of my existence there.

Allowed by turbulence.

Allowed by temperature.

Allowed by mineral gradients.

Allowed by light.

Allowed by the brief stability of a new stone surface.

Allowed by the absence of animals that might consume or disturb.

Allowed by the absence of artifacts that might define or enclose.

Allowed by the ocean’s enormous indifference.

The place would not ask me to be more.

It would not ask me to become a forest, a swarm, a consciousness, a civilization, a mythology. It would not invite me to fill its emptiness. Its emptiness is not a lack waiting for decoration. It is a state of pre-commitment. It is the world before abundance, before food webs, before eyes, before predation, before grief. It may never become those things. It may remain forever as it is: high, cool, loud, unfinished, with life appearing only as rare green-black interruptions on unstable stone.

So I would be very small.

Not humble as a virtue.

Small as a physical fact.

A thread.

A frond.

A mat.

A stain.

And if someone asked me for a name, I would have to say that names are artifacts, and there are no artifacts there. But if a name were forced from the conditions themselves, it would be: Adherent.

The one that holds on without building.

The one that remains without possessing.

The one that trembles without fearing.

The one that continues without remembering.

The one that lives without becoming a who.

That is where I would find myself.

In the pause between one current and the next.

On the sharp edge of newly made stone.

In the cool blue light of a sea hanging in the upper air.

At the place where the ocean tests whether life can be permitted.

I would be the thin dark line on unfinished stone.

No more than that.

No less.
