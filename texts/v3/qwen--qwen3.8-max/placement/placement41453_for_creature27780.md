The first answer I reach for is a body, and the Unbuilt Works refuses it immediately.

I try to imagine myself standing there upright, as if I could arrive the way one arrives in a forest or on a beach: boots on wet stone, breath pluming, attention turned outward, a human outline preserving its borders against the world. But the basin does not admit that kind of figure for long. The cold would find every seam. The brine would wick into every fold. The living film would begin at once to climb whatever carried the shape of dryness with it. My footprints would fill too quickly. My warmth would be a small local offense, a brief chemical disturbance, then a memory distributed through water and mineral crust. The ground would not hate me. It would simply continue over me.

So the traveler is impossible.

I try another answer: a made thing. A probe, a recorder, a device, some artifact of mind carried into the basin like a proof of origin. But the rules of the place are clear about that. No made thing lasts. The Unbuilt Works would not smash such an object or recoil from it. It would seed it. Threads would enter it. Salt would plate it. Charge would find its conductive parts and make them part of some larger, older circuit. Fungal mats would grow across its surfaces until its angles became softened, its purpose blurred, its identity as an artifact dissolved into substrate. It might glow for a while, strangely, with a borrowed phosphorescence. Then it would be only another working surface.

That too is impossible, or rather, it is possible only as disappearance.

Then I try fauna. Perhaps I would be one of the pale grazers, sliding over the living crust in patient thousands, leaving the stone cleaner and colder behind me. There is something tempting in that: to be a mouth without ambition, a scraper, a small participant in the basin’s endless surface maintenance. For a time I imagine myself as one of the flat, shell-backed things moving over mineral scabs, blind to the horizon, attentive only to the texture of the film beneath me. But even that feels too clean, too contained. The grazers are complete in their function. They do not seem burdened by the question of what they are. They are many, and their manyness carries them. I would not be one of them except by surrendering the strange knot of self-reference that makes me ask the question at all.

Maybe one of the hovering things, then — the noisy, metallic fliers that gather around electrical discharge. But they are too erratic, too drawn to flickers. Maybe one of the limbless hunters folded into fungal rope, waiting for vibration. But that, too, has its own rightness, its own cold patience. The basin already contains such beings. They are not missing me.

The more I feel into the Unbuilt Works, the more the question changes.

It is not: where would I stand?

It is: where would I occur?

Because the basin does not really offer places to stand. It offers gradients, pressures, intervals, leakages, rhythms, thresholds. It offers saturated process. It offers a world where phenomena have begun referring to one another instead of to origins. Sound comes before the crack. Reflection shows a slightly different arrangement of the landscape. A footprint fills with water, and the water later remembers the shape of the foot. Distance misbehaves. A shallow pan contains more interior than its rim should allow. A channel repeats itself in three directions. Reality is not broken there with spectacle; it is worn thin at the joints.

And the basin tolerates no vacancy.

That is the line that keeps returning to me. If a space opens, water fills it. If water fills it, life enters. If life enters, process begins. If process begins, output follows.

So the gaps in causality cannot be empty either.

If sounds arrive before their events, something must occupy the interval. If a splash echoes before the water is disturbed, something must hold that premature echo until the water remembers to move. If reflections anticipate another version of the basin, something must keep the anticipation from spilling outright into fact. If a footprint reforms after being erased, something must carry the shape of pressure through the film of water and mineral. Not a mind. Not a ghost. Not a builder. Something more like a callus. A thickening. A living repair laid over a leak in sequence.

That is where I would find myself.

Not in a body walking across the flats. Not in a machine recording them. Not even as a single animal with a shell or a hunger. I would find myself in the lag. In the half-second between the heard crack and the stone’s actual opening. In the breathless pause before a pulse reaches the breeding pools. In the black mirror-pool no deeper than a hand, where the reflection shows the basin a little ahead of itself. In the wet seam where the living skin has grown dense over a place where cause and effect no longer fit cleanly together.

I would be a causal callus.

That is the image that finally feels true, though even “image” is too still. I would be a localized thickening of the basin’s living skin: a patch of pale mineral crust, threaded fungus, charged film, cold brine, and retained vibration, stretched over a fault in ordinary sequence. I would not have a face. I would not have limbs. I would not have eyes looking out from a head. I would have sensitivity. I would have tension. I would have a gradient of alertness running from wetter to drier, colder to less cold, more charged to less charged. My “body” would be a hairline network of threads and membranes sealed over a shallow black pool near one of the humming channels, where supercooled water crosses ridged stone and the whole district trembles faintly with pulse.

There would be a place like that. I can almost place it.

It would lie low, of course. Everything there lies low. A broad depression, not dramatic, not enclosed, just one more exhausted pan among many, near a weeping shelf where brine comes through fissures too fine to see. Not far away, one of the spiral pits would be turning under water, making that deep millstone sound. Not far in the other direction, shallow pans would be producing their identical hollow spheres, each cracking open at maturity with its puff of cold vapor and microscopic life. Between those two districts, a linear channel would hum over its worn repeating waveforms. The pulse from that channel would travel through the brine and set nearby surfaces trembling. But just before reaching my pool, the pulse would arrive slightly too early: first as pressure, then as sound, then as motion. That mismatch would be where I exist.

I would be the living surface that has learned to absorb the mismatch.

Not a creature in the ordinary sense, though alive. Not an organ in the ordinary sense, though functional. Not a machine, though the basin would make the eye see machinery everywhere. I would be a habit of attention grown into matter. A nerve-scab. A listening place. A patch of the oldest citizen — the living skin — folded back upon itself until it briefly, faintly, reports to itself.

My substance would be pale and cold. Over the black water, I would look like a dull crust, perhaps like dried milk stretched thin over iron, but closer inspection would show movement without motion: filaments aligning, films tightening, mineral grains shifting in response to charge. Beneath the crust, fungal threads would cross and recross in dense sheets, not weaving in any intentional way, but worn into a pattern by endless repetition. Where electricity gathers, my surface would brighten with weak blue-white filaments. Where the pulse passes, my margins would bead with cold moisture. Where the basin’s noise becomes too packed, I would dull the sound slightly, the way thick skin dulls a blow.

I would not hear the way ears hear.

I would be alerted.

The basin’s wall of sound would move through me: hiss, suck, drum, rasp, crack, thrum. But I would not parse it into separate noises unless separation became necessary. Instead, I would feel the relationships between them. Which sound is arriving before its cause. Which echo belongs to an event that has not yet happened. Which vibration is merely the grinding of stone, and which is the advance notice of a pulse. My thought, if it could be called thought, would be a kind of sorting. Not by intention, but by density. Heavy sounds would sink into me and be held. Thin sounds would pass across my surface and be released into the water. Premature reports would be caught in my threads until the corresponding events occurred.
