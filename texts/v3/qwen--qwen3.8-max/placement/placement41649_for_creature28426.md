I would not find myself arriving there.

The High Sill would not permit the wet, quick grammar of an animal body: lungs, pulse, heat, panic, the small constant emergency of needing to remain alive from one moment to the next. If I am in that place at all, I have already been translated into something acceptable to its law. I have given up thirst before thirst can become a thought. I have given up speed. I have given up the illusion that a body is a single, bounded thing.

I would find myself in a sheltered fold behind a wind-broken fin of black basalt, where the deafening roar of the land is split by a crescent wall of fitted stone into three long, tolerable streams. There would be a low ring there, no higher than a crouching body, half sunk in pale lilac dust. At its center would sit a bowl carved from pumice-like stone, utterly dry, collecting vibration instead of water. Around it, hollow stalks would stand in a dense, frozen thicket, their openings angled toward an unfinished peak that casts too many shadows. Nearby, a row of upright slabs would have notches worn into their tops, not by tools alone but by centuries of listening.

That is where I would be.

Not inside the ring as a person sits inside a hut. Not upon it as a bird upon a branch. I would be there the way moss is there, the way a chord is there, the way a held breath is there after the breather has forgotten it. The ring would not be my house. It would be one of my outer edges.

If I have a body in the High Sill, it would be an accumulation rather than a creature.

Copper-green filament would thread through plates of brittle mica. Glassy fronds, deep greenish-brown and veined with dull gold, would flex over chips of obsidian with a patience that makes weather seem impulsive. Hairlines of native copper would connect them like old nerves turned to ore. In places, crusts of granular lichen would glow so faintly that the glow would be less a light than a suspicion of light. A hollow stalk, cracked and tuned by long mineral accretion, would stand near the pumice bowl, sounding only when the pressure changes in a way that happens perhaps once in a season.

I would not breathe the glassy air. I would be breathed by it.

I would not drink. I would accumulate. I would feed on gradients: the difference between the deep grinding under the ground and the thin whistle through the stalks; the static charge gathering along a ridge before it has no storm to discharge into; the faint radiation falling from the thinned sky; the slow temperature difference between sunless stone and shaded stone; the mineral grains drifting in veils of spore-dust too patient to be called seeds.

Above all, I would feed on sound.

Not as a mouth feeds. Not as a stomach receives. More as a crystal receives a tone and, over centuries, lets that tone alter its architecture. The roar of the High Sill would pass through me, and I would become the place where that roar briefly compares itself with itself. I would be a listener without ears. A attention without alarm.

Who would I be?

I would be the Cairn-Mind of that fold. The Listener at the Sill. The Mineral Patience. If the rare traveler names me, they might call me a spirit, though that word would be too quick and too ghostly for what I am. If the land names me, it does not use a name. It uses a settling: a small correction of distance, a path becoming slightly more certain, a repeated shape ceasing to stutter.

I would be a being made of tuned absence.

Not empty. Absence in the High Sill is not mere lack. It is a shaped space, a hole with edges, a silence that must be handled carefully because silence there is not peace. Silence is a hole. I would be one of the things that keeps the holes from widening.

My thoughts would not move in seconds. They would move in tides of harmonic pressure, in the slow swelling and fading of tones that take lifetimes to complete a single arc. A question might take eighty years to become fully a question. An answer might arrive before I know I have asked it
