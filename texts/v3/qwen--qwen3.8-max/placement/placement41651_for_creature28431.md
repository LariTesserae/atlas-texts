If I am to be found in this world, I will not come through a doorway. There is no doorway here anymore, not in the sense that would let me arrive dry and upright and astonished. I will not stand on a floor, because the floor is a drowned raft of fitted blocks and my weight would be answered by water on all sides. I will not look up into light, because the vault gives only the faintest seam-glow, a foxfire so subtle it seems less like illumination than like heat remembering itself. If I am in this room, I must already belong to it: warmed to its temperature, darkened to its patience, shaped by its hush.

At first, I think I would be the foxfire itself.

That is the first temptation. To be a living skin along the seams, a microbial film thin and old and necessary, eating iron and lime and the slow ghost of copper. To have no face, no limbs, no hunger with edges. To shine only because living has become so slow and mineral that it leaks a little visible warmth. There is something appealing about that: a self spread across tile and staple, not a creature but a condition. A brightness that does not reveal, only insists in the faintest way that the room is not dead.

But the room asks for something more than glow.

It asks for touch. It asks for the small, deliberate beings that move through its water and leave it changed by no more than a thread’s width. So the image shifts. I feel myself condense. I become bounded. I become something with a shell.

For a while I think I would be one of the pale crabs.

A crab fits this place. A crab already knows how to make ceremony out of small motions. It taps a claw against tile and listens. It scrapes film from stone with blunt patience. It wears its own architecture on its back and lets the water wear that architecture down until there is no difference between body and room. If I were a crab, I would spend my life in the terraces, moving from groove to groove, tasting the current where the southern warmth enters, letting the mineral-heavy water polish my carapace to the color of wet ash. I would be quiet. I would be useful in the unconscious way of grazers. I would keep the surfaces clean without knowing cleanliness as a virtue.

And yet that is not quite it either.

A crab grazes. A limpet polishes. A bivalve tastes and waits. A tube-dweller combs the water with pale crowns and withdraws when the world comes too near. All of them belong completely to the room, but none of them seem to care for the room as a room. They live in its finished form. They do not tend the finish.

So another shape begins to gather in the dark.

It gathers near the channels. It gathers where the water moves with intention, however faint. It gathers around the stone valves, the gratings, the threaded joints, the small mechanical holiness of a built place that has been drowned and inhabited for so long that its mechanisms have become part of its biology. The shape that emerges is not grand. It is not a guardian in any heroic sense. It is not a monster coiled in the basin. It is smaller than that, humbler, closer to a tool that has learned need.

I would be a valve-keeper.

That is the best name I can give the being I become, though the room itself would have no word for me except the sound I make when I settle into my niche: a faint, precise tick, no louder than a crab’s claw, no more urgent than a settling grain of mineral crust. I am not large. I would be no bigger than two open palms pressed together, perhaps less, because largeness would be a violence here. My body is low and broad, flattened enough to enter the spaces between terrace and wall. I have a carapace made of overlapping plates, not bright, not ornate, but worn to the softness of old fired clay. My color is the color of the room: wet ash, milked stone, the ghost-bloom of copper where old metal has stained the water for centuries. Along my margins, microbial film grows in a faint seam of foxfire, borrowed from the seams I tend, so that when I move there is a rumor of edge around me, a suggestion of outline that disappears when I am still.

I have no eyes, or I have only the memory of eyes. They have sealed over in the dark, becoming shallow pits that can sense the weakest change in the foxfire as a change in warmth rather than sight. I do not see the room. I hear it through pressure. I taste it through bristles and skin-folds. I know it by the way the current leans against me, by the mineral grammar carried from wall to wall.

My forelimbs are not claws for killing. They are instruments of attention.

The first pair is broad and soft-edged, padded with living tissue that can feel a hairline crack or a raised grain of cement. The second pair is finer, with slender probes that can enter the narrow mouths of channels and test whether mineral crust has begun to narrow them. The third pair is stronger, but still blunt, made for bracing against tile, for levering a stone valve by fractions, for scraping away accretion without wounding the surface beneath. My hind limbs are anchors. They end in small, hard points that fit the worn grooves between blocks, so I can hold myself in place when the current shifts or when the building gives one of its long, slow groans.

I breathe through folds under the edges of my carapace, where water passes over membranes thin enough to take what it carries and let the rest go on. I am warm because the water is warm. I am heavy because the water is heavy with salts. I do not feel the outside altitude as a place; I feel it as a thinness in the pressure, a strange delicacy in the liquid, as if the room were remembering wind through stone.

Where would I be?

Not in the central basin. The basin is a mouth, a gathering, a place where the current deepens toward the grated throat. Too much passes there. Too much leaves.

Not in the highest vault either, where the silver beads of old gas collect. Those bells are beautiful and uninhabitable to me. They belong to no one. They are the room’s held breath, not its life.

I would be in the middle distance, in one of the niches along the third or fourth band above the terraces, on the side of the wall where the warm water from the southern mouths has completed its first broad turn and begins to lose confidence as it approaches the cooler stones. My niche would not be large. It would be deep enough to shelter me when I curl myself into rest, but shallow enough that I can feel the wall’s faint vibration.
