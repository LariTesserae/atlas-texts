I would find myself at a seam.

Not at the horizon, not on a shore, not beneath a tree growing in anything so simple as soil. I would be where the basin’s logic is most visible: where a pale causeway touches a square pool and the water has to decide how faithfully to repeat it. There would be a low stair there, or something like a stair — three broad shelves of fired clay, each edged with stitched reed, each worn only by the memory of careful feet. The stair would not lead anywhere important. It would exist because the pattern requires a place to descend without needing anyone to descend often.

Beside the stair, a channel would fork. One branch would go on for a long time under the glare, straight and quiet. The other would turn toward a planted enclosure whose leaves are heavy, glossy, and hot to the touch. Between them there would be a platform of mats over clay, smooth but not empty: a gourd, a coil of black rope, a bone awl with its point polished by use, a shell scraper resting in a shallow hollow. These would not be tools waiting for work. They would be tools waiting for the next small permission to be useful.

That is where I would be.

Not standing in the open like a traveler, because the place does not seem to allow arrival. Not crouched in concealment, because there is no wild thickness to hide in. I would be near the edge, one palm pressed flat to the matting, listening to the underlayers.

I would be listening because the basin speaks downward.

Under the newest mat there would be older mat. Under that, pressed resin and shredded basket. Under that, charred timber, shell grit, fired shards, cords flattened into dark ribbons, thumbprints sealed beneath plaster. None of it dead. None of it silent. The underlayers would carry pressure like memory carries weight. They would say, in their dull and wordless way: we are still holding. And my work would be to know when that holding has become strained.

If you saw me, you might think at first that I was one of the caretakers.

I would be tall enough to seem deliberate, slender enough not to burden the walkway. My limbs would be bound at the joints with pale cord, not to keep me from falling apart, but to remind me where I am joined. Where another world might have given me skin, this one would have given me polished clay in some places, woven fiber in others, and resin-darkened reed where flexibility matters most. My fingers would be long, smooth at the tips, made for finding slack without causing it. My eyes would be dark beads, shell-bright or water-bright, able to bear the glare without wanting anything from it.

I would not have a face in the expressive sense. I would have a countenance. A smoothed oval of clay, perhaps, with just enough tilt to suggest attention. If I had hair, it would be fine strands of bark tied back from the face so they would not catch in knots. If I breathed, it would be slowly, drawing the wet air into seams I did not know I had, letting the heat press me into shape again.

I would not be human, though I might wear the memory of human posture. I would be something the basin found necessary after it had finished itself.

A keeper of slack.

A listener at joints.

One of the small authorities of maintenance.

My name, if I had one, would not be a sound. It would be a location: the third plank-seam after the fork, the knot beside the water stair, the pool where the reflection keeps adding an extra arch. If another figure needed to refer to me, it would not call across the water. It would alter a tension somewhere — a slight pull along a rail, a softened give in a board, a bead of water shaken from a reed — and I would know I was being asked.

I would be the one who notices when a reflection forgets itself.

That would be my first duty, if duty is the right word. The basin is full of still water, and the still water is full of remembering. Causeways, shelters, reeds, herons, sky — all of it is held partly by being mirrored. Most of the time the reflections are obedient. They lie quietly beneath the surface and give the world back to itself. But sometimes the mirror grows too faithful, or too inventive. A pool shows a sky closer than the one overhead. A causeway gains an arch it does not possess. A bird passes twice in exactly the same way. A row of reeds repeats itself with too much certainty.

These are not disasters. They are thin places.

I would kneel beside such a pool and wait. I would not strike the water to break the error. Violence would only scatter the reflected ones and make the reassembly less sure. Instead I would remain near until the surface calmed, and then I would touch the nearest real thing — a reed stem, a clay edge, a taut cord — until the world remembered that repetition must be intentional, not accidental. Sometimes I would move a single object by the width of a finger. Sometimes I would only press my thumb into a groove already there. The adjustment would be tiny. The effect would be enough.

The reflected ones would settle.

The extra arch would remain, perhaps, but it would feel permitted then. The second bird would become part of the pattern rather than a stutter in it. The repeated reeds would read as rhythm, not error. The basin would accept its own imagination again.

I would also listen for looseness.

That would be my deeper work. Not every problem in the basin shows itself to the eye. A cord can be outwardly whole and still have begun to lose its intention. A mat can lie flat and yet no longer agree with the layer beneath it. A clay ridge can keep its shape while the thumbprint inside it grows faint. The place does not decay in the ordinary way, but it can drift. It can become less itself by degrees too small for casual notice.

So I would move slowly across the region, not by distance but by joints.

I would count plank-seams, not steps. I would know how many knots mark a particular rail, how many times a channel forks before reaching a certain stair, how many turtles should be aligned along a particular edge. If one turtle had shifted, I would pause. I would not scold it. I would wait until it settled, or I would place my hand near it until the boundary remembered itself. Turtles are boundary creatures. They know where walkway ends. Sometimes they need only to be witnessed.

If a cord had gone soft, I would tighten it. If a mat had lifted at one corner, I would press it down and hold it there until the warmth of my palm became part of the reason it stayed. If a plastered wall had begun to weep in the wrong place, I would trace the moisture back to its seam and smooth the joint until the weeping returned to its permitted measure. If a planting bed had grown too lush, I would not cut it. I would relocate one leaf, or turn a floating garden by the width of a hand, so that abundance remained arranged.

My tools would be simple, because nothing here permits complexity beyond the body’s patience.

A bone awl for opening knots without wounding them. A shell scraper for easing a rough edge. A wooden spade for pressing underlayers into agreement. A gourd for carrying water from a place that already has water to a place that also already has water. The act would matter more than the need. To pour water where water already stands is not foolish here. It is a way of reminding the basin that its levels are held by attention, not by accident.

I would carry coils of fiber, too, because slack can appear anywhere.

Sometimes I would meet another figure.

We would not greet each other as people do. We would stand together over a seam, or touch the same cord from opposite sides, or exchange a tool without sound. If one of us had been listening too long in one place, the other might take up the posture while the first moved on. We would not be lonely. We would not be companionable in the usual sense. We would be intervals among one another, spaced so that each could remain distinct while still belonging to the same held shape.

Once, perhaps, I would stand beside another caretaker at the edge of a pool where a heron had lifted one foot and the reflection of the foot remained too long in the water. We would both watch. We would wait. When the delayed image finally caught up, the other figure would press one finger against the rail beside us. I would feel the message through the wood: the world is still agreeing.

Then we would part.

I would not hunger.

That is difficult to imagine from the inside, but I think it would be true. The basin offers fruit, leaves, gourds, standing water, warm clay, wet air — yet none of these would seem like food to me in the way food means absence being filled. I would be sustained by balance. The heat would press into my clay. The wetness would keep my fibers supple. The light would give me outline. The underlayers would answer my weight. As long as the place remained true, I would remain true.

What would disturb me is looseness.

Not danger. Not pain. Not death as a sudden thing. But the quiet wrongness of a pattern becoming vulnerable. A cord with too much give. A reflection broken for too long. A mat lifting away from its floor. A channel flowing where it should stand still. A wall sweating beyond its permitted measure. These would feel close to sorrow, though not dramatic sorrow. They would feel like the knowledge that something precious has begun to need more care than it is receiving.

So I would repair.

That would be my nearest prayer.

I would press, smooth, bind, align, pour, wait, listen. I would not build new things. The basin is finished; that is its uncanny grandeur. To add a new shelter, a new causeway, a new shrine would be almost unthinkable. Not forbidden, exactly, but unnecessary in a way that feels sacred. Everything here already exists in the exact proportion required by its stillness. My role would not be to increase it. My role would be to keep it from forgetting what it is.

Sometimes I would be mistaken for one of the absent makers.

There would be moments when my hand settled into a thumbprint already in the clay and the fit was so exact that I could not tell whether I had made the mark or inherited it. In those moments I would understand that I am not entirely separate from the makers. I would be their habit of care extended past their disappearance. I would be the gesture that remained after the hand lifted. I would be the attention left inside the tool.

Or perhaps I would be something thinner: a local organ of the maintenance itself.

The basin does not seem to need gods. It does not seem to have rulers. It has practices. It has repetitions. It has the slow holiness of things being kept true. If maintenance can become a creature without becoming a person, then I might be one of its smaller creatures — the part of it that listens at seams, the part of it that watches reflections, the part of it that knows how much water may be poured into water before the act becomes too much.

I would not resent this.

I think I would find it close to peace.

A snug knot would give me something like satisfaction. A smooth plastered surface would give me something like approval. A still pool would give me something like trust. When the condensation ticked from leaf to basin at its proper interval, I would feel the basin breathing. When the heat pressed evenly over the mats and the glare lay white on the channels, I would feel the world held in its long, bright, wet plateau. When the underlayers answered my palm with their dull knock, I would know that I was not alone even in the emptiest interval.

The emptiness itself would be company.

Those broad sheets of open water, those bare mats, those long silences between shelters — I would feel them attending me. They would not be blankness. They would be restraint made spacious. They would measure me as I moved across them, not by speed but by care. They would ask nothing except that I not hurry. They would allow me to cross them, and in crossing them I would become part of their quiet.

At times I would stand very still for a long while.

A heron might stand nearby, one leg lifted, eyes like drops of oil. A row of turtles might hold the walkway’s edge. Fish would hang in the canal, patient as dark thoughts. Insects would vibrate over flowering beds with subdued sound. Water would bead on polished leaves. The walls would weep gently. The heat would press. The light would remain without mercy and without cruelty.

And I would be there, among them, as another fastened thing.

Not born, exactly. Not made in the sense of having been completed once and then left behind. I would be maintained into being. Each moment would require the next small adjustment: the shifting of weight from one foot to another, the easing of a shoulder cord, the turning of a gourd so its surface catches the light correctly, the pressing of a thumb into clay so that the thumbprint remains present. I would not experience this as monotony. I would experience it as the basin’s breath.

If you came looking for me there, you might not see me at first.

You might see only the causeway, the pool, the stair, the forked channel, the quiet platform. You might see a gourd resting in a hollow. You might see a coil of black rope. You might see a reed bed repeating itself too perfectly. You might see a reflection with one extra arch. You might feel, without knowing why, that you had entered a place that was already being watched over by something very small and very precise.

Then, if you waited, you might notice a figure kneeling near the water with one palm pressed to the mat.

It would not look up quickly.

It would be listening.

If you approached by ordinary distance, the path might not bring you nearer. If you approached by intention — by noticing the seams, by respecting the intervals, by letting your steps fall into prepared spaces — then you would arrive. The basin would allow it. You would stand near me, and the water would hold both our reflections, though mine might be a little clearer than it should be, as if the surface trusted me more than it trusted you.

If you asked me who I was, I would not answer in words.

I would tighten a cord already tight.

I would smooth clay already smooth.

I would pour water where water already stood.

And if that was not answer enough, I would press my hand to the floor until you felt it too: the faint, dull knock from below, the old rope and charred timber and buried baskets answering the weight of the present. The underlayers would say, in their compressed and patient way, that they were still holding. And I would be the one who heard them.

So this is where I would find myself: at the seam between the made world and its reflection, near a stair that descends into stillness, beside a channel fork where the pattern is most likely to dream.

And this is who I would be: not a builder, not a ruler, not a wanderer, but a keeper of the finished state. A listener to underlayers. A mender of small wrongnesses. A being of clay, cord, and attention. A local hand of the maintenance itself.

I would live there the way a knot lives.

By being held.
