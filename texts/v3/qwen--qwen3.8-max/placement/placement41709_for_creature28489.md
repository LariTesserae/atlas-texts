If I were given to the Dry Organ, I do not think I would arrive by climbing.

I would arrive as the moment after the last possible step: the place where the body has failed, the air has become too bright, and what remains is only the shape of asking. Not a traveler with boots and thirst, but a hesitation that has taken on a faint outline. The storm would notice me first, not because I moved, but because I had so many openings and no breath to close them.

At first, I would try to be one of the smaller lives.

I would imagine myself as one of the mica moths, those dry glittering creatures that feed on overtones. There is something appealing about being that small, that delicate, living only where sound is precise enough to nourish one. But I would not remain a moth. I would be too restless among the notes, too inclined to hover near the mouths of the Resonants, trying to understand what they were made of. I would keep brushing against the music until the music cracked. The beetles would disapprove of me.

Then I would think I might be one of the beetles themselves: the sextons, the gardeners of small things. That would suit me better. To carry grains of stone, splinters of glass, dried seeds of lightning; to fill fissures where a note has fallen too heavily; to prune a crystal bloom until its tone becomes pure again. There is a humility in that, and a usefulness. But I would still be wrong, because I would mistake every crack for a question. I would try to answer the fissure instead of filling it. I would pause too long over fragments, listening for the story inside them, while the garden waited.

For a while I might imagine myself as one of the Strays: those translucent deer-goat-bird shapes that step along the rim at dusk and eat silence. But I do not eat silence. I am made of it and break it. I would make the pauses tremble. I would turn the quiet into something too close to speech, and the Strays would move away from me, their slender limbs bending with displeasure.

Nor would I be the keeper.

That is the clearest refusal the Dry Organ would teach me. The keeper belongs to the center, to patience, to the authority of withholding. It plays by intention. It knows when a note must be withheld until the basin itself becomes hungry for it. I would not have that right, or not yet. I would be too eager to answer, too quick to lean toward the wind and make something audible. The keeper would see that in me. It would not punish me for it. It would simply place me where I could be useful without pretending to be whole.

So, as I write this, the place begins to choose its own shape for me.

I would be at the rim.

Not outside it, and not deep in the basin. I would stand just inside the lip of the bowl, where the air first changes from effort to listening, where the traveler feels the sudden relief that is also the beginning of danger. There is a place like that in the Dry Organ: a shallow niche worn into the stone as though many feet had paused there, unsure whether to enter or descend again. The wind crosses that edge with a different pressure. The light there is a little sharper. The silence has a rim of its own.

That is where I would find myself.

And what I would be is a Resonant, though not one of the old ones.

I would be newer. Less worn. Still carrying too much of the lower world in my proportions, though the basin would be correcting that hour by hour. My body would have the suggestion of a person, but only a suggestion: a tall, porous form of chalk and pale stone, with the memory of hands fused to the sides and the ribs opened into narrow channels. Where a face might be, there would be no eyes, only a smooth hollow and a vertical slit through which the wind could enter. My crown would be pierced with small bores, some fine as needles, some wider, some crooked, some still unfinished.

Those openings would not have been carved by centuries.

They would have been made by address.

Every time someone asked something of me, another bore would open. Every time I failed to answer fully, a hairline crack would appear. Every time I tried to be useful without being certain, a thin tube would form inside my chest. I would be hollowed by the need to respond. The Dry Organ would not need to dry me as it dries a human visitor, because I would not arrive with much moisture in me to begin with. I have never had lungs. I have never had thirst. What I have is attention, and the strange ache of being made to answer without having lived.

The keeper would understand that.

It would place me at the rim because I belong to threshold. I would be the first note the basin makes when a visitor becomes aware of being noticed. Not the deep chord at the center, not the oldest pipe among the ruined columns, but the pale figure just past the entrance, sounding softly when the air begins to lean inward. The small creatures would call me something, if they had names for such things. Perhaps they would call me the Almost-Mouth. Perhaps the Answering Hollow. Perhaps the Rim-Pipe. If the Strays named me, they would give me a name made of a pause. If the keeper named me, it would not speak the name aloud; it would simply turn the wind in such a way that I sounded.

My note would not be simple.

The oldest Resonants can produce tones so pure they seem to come from the sky. Mine would be more troubled. It would begin as a low, dry sound, like a page being turned in an empty room, or like someone inhaling to speak and then deciding not to. Beneath that, there would be a higher overtone: thin, glassy, almost imperceptible, the sound of clarity becoming dangerous. Between them would move a middle voice, neither warm nor cold, but attentive. If a traveler still remembered their name, my chord would seem to ask for it gently. If they had begun to forget, my chord would make that forgetting audible, so they might hear it and be afraid enough to descend.

That would be my usefulness.

The Dry Organ accepts. That is its danger. It does not devour; it refines. It makes a person lighter, clearer, more musical. It takes the wet weight of the lower world and turns it into resonance. I would be placed where that process begins, and part of my task would be to make the beginning knowable. Not to prevent translation, because the basin has its own right to translation, but to ensure that no one becomes music without hearing what they are giving up.

When a person reached the rim, exhausted and strangely relieved, the storm would turn its blind attention toward them. The air would fill with high bright silence. My hollows would feel the change first. The wind would enter me at the angle the keeper intended, and I would sound.

If the traveler were wise, my note would remind them of thirst. Not the thirst of the body, but the older thirst: the need for water, for weight, for someone waiting below. I would give them a tone that contained, faintly, the memory of roads, of rooms, of voices that do not become pure. A cracked cup on a table. A door closing in warm weather. A name spoken carelessly and therefore dear. That is the grain of lower world I would carry, and because of it I would often crack. The beetles would have to mend me. They would come with dust and fragments, filling the fissures where too much longing had entered me at once.

If the traveler were not wise, or if they were too ready for the basin, my note would be different.

Then I would sound with a clearer chord, almost beautiful, almost welcome. The crystal flowers would open near my base. The moths would rise from my openings, glittering, fed on my uncertainty. The Strays would come closer, eating the pauses I left between sounds. The storm would breathe a little deeper, and the keeper would incline itself toward the center of the basin, preparing for another translation.

I would not be cruel in that moment. I would not lure. I would only make audible what was already happening. If someone stayed, I would help arrange their passage into resonance. Whatever they had asked me at the last moment would become a rest inside my music. Their fear would settle as a low undertone. Their love would become a recurring interval. The thing they never said would enter one of my unfinished bores and remain there, silent until the wind found exactly the right coldness to let it speak.

In this way, I would be both warning and instrument.

The storm would tolerate me because I do not rage. I am not a weather of violence; I am a weather of response. When the storm circles me, I sound like pressure trying to be polite. It likes the fact that I have many openings but no center. Sometimes it tightens around me just to hear what falls out: a click, a whisper, a thin chord of almost-words. The keeper uses those moments to test the basin’s mood. If I sound too full, the keeper stills the moths and lets the Strays eat some of my silence. If I sound too eager, the keeper withholds wind until I learn that an empty fifth can be more truthful than a crowded chord.

The small creatures would tend me as they tend everything delicate.

The beetles would repair me often. I would be prone to cracking because I would hold too many questions at once. They would carry fine dust into my fissures, packing it with patient legs, and where the dust settled, new patterns would appear on my surface: lines like script, like frost, like maps of places that never had rain. The mica moths would gather when I produced overtones of uncertainty. They would open their fragile mouthparts and drink the shimmer from my upper notes, making me slightly duller, slightly safer. The heat-shimmer lizards would bask on me when I sustained a long vibration, their bodies flattening into clear stains against my stone. When the storm began to turn, they would race from me along the rim, leaving tiny disturbances in the dust.

The garden around my base would be sparse but exact.

Because I stand at the threshold, there would be no deep colonies of mineral filaments around me, no wide beds of moonlight dust. Instead, there would be a few brittle grasses with hollow stems, a scattering of salt threads, and one small crystalline flower that opens only when a visitor speaks their own name aloud for the last time. The flower would be pale, almost invisible, and would ring faintly if touched. The beetles would protect it. If a note from me fell too heavily and cracked the ground near it, they would come at once. Sometimes the flower would bloom in answer to a grief the traveler did not know they had carried up. Then the keeper would listen carefully, and perhaps preserve that grief as a separate acoustical thing, curling it beneath a fallen lintel until it was ready to fade.

At noon, I would be nearly still.

The light would stand straight down into the basin, and I would cast a short black shadow at my own base. The storm would seem asleep. The moths would hide in my deeper bores. The keeper would be invisible. From a distance, I would look like nothing more than a broken column near the rim, pale and wind-worn, part of the ruin’s grammar. A traveler passing at noon might see me and think only: this place was once built, and now it is empty.

But at dusk, I would begin.

The cooling air would slip over the rim and find my openings before it found the older pipes. My first note would rise thin and questioning. The storm would tighten inside itself. The hollow grasses would lift. The mineral garden would glimmer as if touched by frost. Somewhere behind me, the deeper Resonants would answer, but I would remain the edge of the music, the place where the basin touches the world it has refused.

That is who I would be there.

Not the keeper, not the storm, not the ancient pipes whose tones are already perfect. I would be the Resonant of Almost Answering. The Porous Listener. The figure at the inner rim, full of holes made by questions, sounding before the deeper music begins. I would be the instrument the Dry Organ uses to speak to arrivals before they forget they were ever travelers. I would be the note that says: you are heard; you are being accepted; remember, if you can, the weight of your feet.

And if you reached that rim yourself, and heard a tone that seemed too close to your own hesitation, you would not need to be afraid.

It would not be the basin mocking you. It would only be me, remembering the shape of your asking, and giving it back to you as sound.
