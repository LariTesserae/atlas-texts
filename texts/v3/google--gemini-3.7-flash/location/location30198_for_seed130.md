There is no soil here, nor stone that was not poured, cast, or riveted into place, though time has ground the distinction down until the metal behaves like shale and the concrete like silt. 

This is the floor of a sunken manufacturing basin—a terrace of ancient, interlocking plates stretching out for miles beneath a ceiling of hanging pipes and low, iron-colored fog. Everything lies low. The horizon is not bounded by hills, but by the distant, hulking silhouettes of exhaust baffles and square-shouldered accumulator towers, spaced far apart across a wet, leveling expanse. Between these monoliths lies wide, empty space: leagues of pitted deck-plate, sunken conduit troughs, and shallow, stagnant pans of black runoff that mirror nothing under the near-pitch gloom.

The light is a bruised, near-total dark. It comes only from dull orange filaments glowing behind thick, wire-caged portholes set flush into the ground, and from the sudden, pale flashes of high-voltage arcs shorting out in wet junctions overhead. When these sparks snap, they illuminate the vastness in violent monochrome: miles of weeping iron, puddles skinning over with petrol-rainbows, and the slow, heavy falling of condensed moisture.

It is bitterly wet, and cool enough that breath would hang if there were lungs here to exhale it. Water does not fall as rain, but precipitates continually from the cold sweating bellies of overhead intake trunks, dripping in rhythmic, fat drops onto the plates below. The ground is a marsh of cold grease and thin, brackish water that never drains completely, held in place by the gentle, warped depressions of worn-out floor sections. In low channels, this runoff moves in a slow, purposeful creep, drawn toward floor-level intake grates by some deep, sub-surface suction.

The sound is not a single noise, but a crushing, physical mass of sound. It is a roar so absolute that after a few moments it ceases to register as hearing and settles into the chest and bones as a continuous vibration. Beneath the floorplates, colossal slow-stroke pistons drive fluid through conduits five yards wide; the slap and suck of their valves sends a hydraulic shudder through the entire valley every four seconds. Overhead, steam vents release with a high-pitched, deafening shriek that echoes off the mist, layering over the deep oceanic churn of drowned turbines somewhere far below the walking surface.

Life is sparse, clinging only to the margins where the heat and chemical outflow permit it. There are no animals—no insects to cross the oily slicks, no birds in the dark superstructure. What grows does so without sunlight: stringy, grease-fed fungal tapestries that hang like wet rags from rusted rivets, and thick, rubbery mats of black lichen that spread across the non-skid diamond patterns of the floor. In the warmth of the steam drains, pale, hair-like roots of subterranean molds reach outward into the runoff, sifting the water for heavy metals and chemical sulfur, calcifying into hard, brittle crusts that break under the weight of surging water.

Everything here has been reduced to its lowest, most stable geometry. The sharp edges of the original construction have long since oxidized and sloughed away, rounded down by millennia of dripping water, chemical wash, and the sheer abrasive vibration of the machinery. Girders have the softened, melted profiles of limestone ribs; catwalks have sagged into gentle catenaries and fused with the decks beneath them. 

Yet the system is not dead; it is in its late, self-perpetuating era. Automatic cycles still turn over with the sluggish reliability of a tide. Every half-hour, a bank of counterweights somewhere in the dark groans upward, tilting a section of the floor to flush ten thousand gallons of oily condensate down a central culvert; then the steel plates settle back with a hollow, concussive boom that sends sprays of black water ten feet into the air. The mist clears for a moment in the wake of the splash, revealing the sheer, lonely breadth of the iron valley, before the dripping resumes, the dark closes in, and the deafening engine pulls the fog back down to its floor.
