It sits sixty feet below the paved threshold of the upper city, settled into a sharp-cut rift where the bedrock was blasted out less than two decades ago and never fully cleared. The rift is narrow—perhaps three hundred yards from its northern basalt shear to the bricked bulkheads in the south—but it is built to the brim, packed with the density of an over-sprouted cellar. 

The heat here is immediate and heavy, radiating outward from buried steam conduits, low-pressure manifold vaults, and the natural thermal bleed of the deep stone. It does not circulate. The air hangs at near-fever temperature, thick with the smell of boiled river silt, wet iron oxide, and the sweet, decaying sugars of crushed vegetation. 

Water does not fall here as rain, but it never truly leaves. It manifests as a fine, perpetual film across every surface: greasy droplets gathering on cold copper rivets, fat tears of distilled runoff rolling down the corrugated tin undersides of hanging walkways, and thin, warm rivulets that seep from the mortar joints between engineering bricks. Where the iron flues run hottest, the stone dries to a pale, chalky crust; everywhere else, it glistens.

The architecture is vertically claustrophobic. Four-story worker tenements, thrown together from dark clinker brick and ungalvanized angle iron during the first excavation boom, have had their lower levels drowned in mechanical infrastructure. Cast-iron accumulator tanks, copper heat-exchanger coils, and sprawling networks of grease-packed hydraulic linkages occupy the street-slots, leaving only shoulder-width corridors between trembling machinery and residential brick. The corners and angles are still sharp—the fractures in the rock walls are jagged, unweathered, and raw, their feldspar faces glinting weakly beneath the growth—but the hard edges have been smothered by sheer accumulation.

Over this machinery, the plants have taken absolute, suffocating hold. They are not the thin weeds of abandoned lots, but heavy, thick-ribbed things that thrive on constant heat and industrial vapor. Swollen, wax-skinned liverworts cover the boiler jackets like green leather, insulating the metal until it barely radiates. Pale, rope-like taproots, some as thick as a man’s thigh, lace through the structural lattice of the iron staircases, anchoring themselves directly into the slag-fill below. Broad, spade-shaped leaves, dark emerald beneath and dull purple on their undersides, crowd into the light-wells, overlapping so densely that lower tiers of the settlement exist in a heavy, greenish twilight. 

Light is scarce and amber-cast. A few carbon-arc globes still hum along the higher catwalks, wrapped in protective wire cages that have long since grown a fur of hairy moss. Their glow is diluted by the rising haze, casting long, soft shadows across the choked alleys. Down near the sump level, the only illumination comes from tiny, pressure-resistant indicator lamps—faint red and orange glass pearls peering out from behind mats of wet, fibrous ivy—and the occasional sulfurous flash of an automatic pilot light deep inside a venting chamber.

Despite the concentration of metal and infrastructure, it is nearly silent. The machines do not run with the shrieking tempo of surface factories; they operate on a massive, low-frequency cycle. A hydraulic piston fifty feet tall slides through its greased gland once every twenty minutes, producing a soft, breathless *chuff* and a long sigh of displaced steam that rustles the hanging vines. Condensation taps lazily on iron plates—*plip, plock*—with the irregular rhythm of a cave pool. There is no wind to stir the canopy. 

Animal life is sparse and muted. An occasional blind bristletail, pale as lard, scuttles over the moist brass of an inactive pressure valve; tiny, earless geckos, their skin the color of damp soot, cling motionless to the warm insulation of the steam trunks, waiting hours for the rare, slow-moving gnats that breed in the oil sumps. There are no birds. If people still live in the rooms behind the slatted, green-stained shutters, their lives are conducted in whispers, attuned to the long, slow respiration of the plant-strangled boilers beneath their floors. 

Everything here is creeping toward an equilibrium that feels geological rather than mechanical. The brass gauges are slowly being squeezed out of calibration by the relentless girth of the vines; the iron girders are being calcified by lime-heavy condensation; and the vegetation itself is drinking the engine-heat, transmuting coal-smoke residue and steam into a dense, silent, subterranean jungle that refuses to cool.
