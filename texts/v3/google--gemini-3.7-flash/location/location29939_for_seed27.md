The world beneath is not rock, but the fossilized, continuous wood of an endless arbor that has had nowhere left to grow for a hundred million years.

It sits high in the cold, thin upper stratum, where the atmosphere has long since shed its moisture. There is no rain, no dew, no ice born of water; the cold here is bone-dry and aromatic, smelling of crushed terpenes, desiccated resin, and static electricity. The ground is a floor of tightly fused, petrified-lignin plates—an expanse of ancient, planetary-scale root systems that have grown, died, calcified, and fused into a continent-wide plateau. This floor has been worn smooth by the ceaseless, dry scouring of high-altitude winds, buffed into dull, dark-amber plains that plunge suddenly into abyssal clefts where ancient xylem tubes, wide enough to swallow ships, yawn open to the dark depths below.

Despite the total absence of water, this is an ecosystem of utter, suffocating vegetative biomass. Life here does not drink; it conducts. The flora are anhydrous, metallic-organic hyper-structures whose metabolisms run on atmospheric ionization, deep-core thermal gradients, and pressurized carbon-dioxide currents. Giant, hollow reeds—dense as cast iron and miles tall—stand spaced far apart across the plateau, like solitary spires left behind after a forgotten war. Their surfaces are ribbed with nano-scale silicon lattices, catching the faint, purple twilight that bleeds through the stratospheric haze.

Because the structures are vast and the atmosphere is clear, the scale feels wide open, dominated by massive, lonely silhouettes. Yet the air itself is deafening. 

The silence one might expect from a cold, high plateau is violently dismantled by the respiration of the forest. The great spires inhale and exhale pressurized gases with the acoustic force of industrial turbine exhaust. Sub-bass hums vibrate directly through the fused-root ground as tectonic-scale root fibers adjust their tension, popping with the sharp, explosive crack of breaking iron cables. Between the spires, wind shears through the dry, fibrous canopy-bridges overhead, producing a sustained, multi-tonal shriek that never truly falls still.

Embedded within these mega-flora are the settlements. They are not built upon the wood; they are grown and phase-stitched through it. Using non-baryonic anchor fields and atomic-level self-assembly, the urban clusters suspend themselves between the trunks like hives of smoked glass and folded topology. Geometric buildings—translucent, dark, and hard-edged—are threaded into the living cambium. These cities rely on technology that manipulates the plant mass at a quantum level: local gravity is warped to create walkable vertical districts inside hollow trunk-wells, while acoustic-dampening shields wrap around the residential clusters to carve pockets of sanity out of the roaring wind. Transit between the spires is conducted along shimmering, tensionless light-filaments, along which pods zip silently, dark slivers crossing miles of open, dim sky.

The fauna of this high, dry plateau are swift, hard, and abundant. Without soft tissue or water-based blood, they are creatures of dry chitin, spun quartz, and articulated keratin. Swarms of paper-winged gliders, three meters across and razor-thin, ride the loud pneumatic drafts that blast from the respiratory vents of the reed-spires. They harvest the static charge that builds on the spire hulls, flashing with brief, snapping arcs of violet light as they graze. In the wide, open plazas between the ancient trunks, long-legged cursorial predators with multi-jointed, scissor-like mandibles chase herds of armored litho-striders across the smooth amber floor. Their movements are jerky and impossibly fast, biological mechanisms driven by high-efficiency dry-chemical twitch fibers rather than hydraulics.

Light is a scarce, precious currency. The sun is a pale, violet smudge pinned against an ink-dark sky, casting long, sharp-edged shadows that barely move across the ancient landscape. Most illumination is generated by the plants themselves—cold, bioluminescent pulses of green and amber that ripple up the flanks of the spires in rhythmic waves, tracing the paths of nutrient slurries moving miles beneath the bark.

Everything here is ancient, worn, and deeply settled into its final logic. The trees cannot die because there is nothing left to replace them; the city cannot fall because it is bound to the eternal lattice of the wood; the wind cannot cease because the breathing of the world is mechanical and vast. It is a high, cold, dry monument to life that has abandoned the need for seas, persisting instead as pure structure, humming loudly in the twilight.
