The plateau sits five thousand meters above the sea, but it does not feel like a mountain peak; it feels like the sun has been brought down to rest upon an endless, terraced basin of bleached bone and boiling brine.

The heat is dry until the ground touches the air, at which point it becomes a blinding, shimmering scald. Thin, fierce sunlight cuts through the upper atmosphere without resistance, striking a vast landscape of pale travertine steps, lead-lined sluices, and wide shallow pans of mineral water that stretch for miles across the shelf. There is not a single leaf, spore, or blade of grass anywhere in the basin. The only colors are the fierce white of dried carbonates, the deep rust of oxidized ironworks, and the turquoise tint of hyper-saline pools that simmer under the pressure of the midday glare.

Across this vastness sits a scattered, monumental city—or perhaps an unpopulated industrial apparatus the size of a province. High-arched aqueducts of dark, riveted iron span the basins, carrying steaming runoff between distant distillation bells and silent pumping manifolds. The architecture is sparse and widely spaced: massive, solitary kiln-brick towers stand half a kilometer apart, connected only by submerged walkways and low-pressure steam conduits that snake through the shallows. The urban layout has no cramped alleys; it is an open, geometric grid laid across a drowned limestone plateau, designed to allow boiling sheets of water to wash continuously across the pavement without rising above the ankles.

Water is everywhere, yet it offers no cool relief. It weeps perpetually from the joints of high stone retaining walls, sheets across the broad industrial plazas in quiet, millimeter-deep films, and bubbles vigorously from hundreds of vents in the mineral floor. Because of the altitude and the heat, this water is in constant transition: it condenses against the high iron beams of the refineries, drops in heavy, scalding beads, runs along the gutters, and evaporates back into the white light before it can reach the edge of the plateau.

Despite the heavy presence of machines and the sheer volume of water, the world is nearly soundless. The low air density dampens noise, swallowing it before it can travel. The massive piston-houses operate on slow, balanced counterweights, pulsing with a deep hydraulic rhythm that vibrates in the soles of the feet rather than the ears. Steam does not hiss here; it sighs, escaping through wide, porous ceramic mufflers that turn high-pressure exhausts into soft, trembling exhalations.

In this scalding, mineral-rich soup, life does not sprout—it crawls, wades, and skims. 

The fauna is extraordinarily dense, having evolved entirely around the heat and the inorganic precipitates of the water. Swarms of flat, porcelain-plated arthropods, each the size of a dinner plate, drift in their thousands across the shallow flats, rasping the fresh crusts of lime and copper from the flagstones. Beneath the submerged factory footings, pale glass-eels with blind, sensory snouts weave through the iron lattice, feeding on the thick sheets of chemical grease produced by the silent pumps. 

Above the water, towering on thin, stilted legs made of hollow calcium, move the *skimmers*—creatures three meters tall with vast, fan-like dorsal sails of translucent skin. They step with eerie grace through the boiling channels, their sails flushing pink and amber as they dump body heat into the dry mountain air. They make no vocal sound, only the faint, rhythmic *tink-tink* of their needle-pointed feet tapping against the submerged brickwork. 

In the high, bright air, where the steam meets the mountain wind, long ribbon-like organisms with silvery, reflective hides float motionless on the thermal updrafts. They neither flap nor drift away; they hold position above the condenser chimneys, basking in the ascending plumes of vapor, their undersides harvesting the microscopic mineral dust carried up by the boil.

Everything is in steady, active balance. The great iron siphons pull brine from the deep fractures; the sun cooks it into crust; the herds of white chitinous grazers scour the crust smooth; and the soft, silent overflow washes the clean stone once more. There is no decay, for there is nothing soft enough to rot—only the relentless, bright circulation of water, metal, and heat across an open, empty city that seems to have built itself to house the sun.
