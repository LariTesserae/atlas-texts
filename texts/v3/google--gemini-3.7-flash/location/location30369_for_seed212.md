The noise hits first: not the roar of an ocean or a windstorm, but a dry, fibrous thunder, an uninterrupted cacophony of shearing wood, tearing bark, and the hydraulic shrieking of pressurized sap. It is the sound of millions of tons of vascular tissue growing, rupturing, and self-swallowing at the speed of an engine stroke. 

Down in the low basin, under a sun that glares down with flat, dry brilliance, sits an urban expanse made entirely of green, amber, and pale living wood. There is no stone, no brick, no metal, yet it is undeniably a city. It has boulevards, tiered plazas, multi-story colonnades, viaducts, and sheer spires reaching hundreds of feet into the warm air. But nothing here was constructed; everything is erupting.

The substrate is not soil, but an oceanic mat of compressed, interlocked taproots that heave and buckle like tectonic plates. The ground beneath one’s feet is a living, twitching pavement of lignified plates that continuously grow outward, collide, form sharp pressure-ridges, and then plunge downward into the dark to be recycled by subterranean fungal digestion. Walking—if one could stand the violent vibration—would mean stepping across fresh, razor-sharp splits in green wood that seal shut within minutes with sticky, foaming cambium.

Water does not exist as rivers or lakes here. It is locked entirely within the biology. The air is dry on the skin, yet the basin is perpetually damp from the frantic, pressurized perspiration of the flora. Guttation drops the size of melons form on the undersides of cantilevered leafy canopies, trembling violently under the acoustic roar before shattering onto the thoroughfares, instantly soaked up by thirsty, carpet-like mosses that line the alleys. A warm, sweet, slightly fermented vapor rises in thermal plumes off the rooftops, smelling of crushed stems, boiled resin, and raw chlorophyll.

The architecture is in a state of hyperactive, turbulent renewal. A six-story tenement of woven wicker-wood and dense box-hedges bolts upward in a matter of twenty minutes, its windows perfectly spaced by rhythmic phototropic pulses, its interior partitioned into rooms by translucent pith-membranes. For an hour, it stands rigid, soaking in the sunlight, its hollow chambers humming like organ pipes with internal wind. Then, overloaded by its own hyper-dense xylem production, the base buckles with the crack of artillery fire. The tower folds into an adjacent avenue, not to die, but to be seized instantly by aggressive, rope-thick runner vines that weave through the debris, converting the fallen ruin into an elevated bridge-network before the dust has even settled.

There are no animals. Not an insect crosses the sap-drenched lintels; no birds nest in the frantic eaves. The vegetation has cannibalized every ecological niche. Certain carnivorous brambles cast out looping, whiplike tendrils into the air, fishing for stray leaves shed by the spires above, digesting their own kin in acidic pitcher-wells to harvest nitrogen for the next growth spurt. Other plants mimic the movement of crowds: thickets of jointed bamboo-like stalks march across the broad avenues on ambulatory root-stilts, snapping their joints forward in erratic, collective migrations that mimic the flow of city traffic.

Geometrical rules break down under this biological pressure. The plants do not merely grow into the sky; they grow into each other’s dimensional space, weaving impossible knots where branch becomes trunk, which becomes a hollow archway, which turns inward to feed its own roots in a closed hydraulic loop. Looking down a wide avenue from an elevated ridge, the perspective refuses to settle: towers lean against each other in self-supporting, woven trusses; suspended plazas of interlaced kelp-like tendrils hang between giant, blooming pylons, swaying under the sheer force of sap pumping through their cores.

Everything is sharp, fresh, and raw. The wood has no time to gray, rot, or weather. The edges of the architectural cornices are as crisp as newly sliced celery. Bright yellow resin oozes from fresh fractures, curing in the sun into hard, amber glass that forms the windows and skylights of the lower layers before being swallowed by the next upward surge of shoots.

The deafening howl never drops below a roar—a continuous, screaming friction of cell walls dividing, expanding, and colliding against one another in an endless, feverish drive to claim the light. The basin is an engine fueled by pure photosynthesis, building a metropolis of pulp and leaf that devours and resurrects itself three times between sunrise and dusk, indifferent to anything beyond its own deafening, beautiful, suffocating velocity.
