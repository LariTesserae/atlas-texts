The floor is entirely laid in unglazed, hand-pressed terracotta tiles, each bearing the faint, dusty imprint of a thumb or the weave of coarse burlap used to press the clay before firing. There is no bedrock, no soil, and no puddle of water anywhere; the air is as warm and dry as the inside of a closed kiln hours after the coals have died, smelling faintly of toasted straw, crushed chalk, and dry resin. 

Though everything here is unmistakably made by hand—notched with primitive copper gouges, pegged with dry dowels, lashed with split reed—the space behaves with an impossible, interior botanical vitality. Out of the clean, crisp mortises and fresh saw-kerfs sprout dense, stiff thickets of pith-brush and parchment-ferns. These plants possess no sap; their stems are dry, hollow tubes of layered cellulose that snap like crackers if bent too quickly, yet they unfurl in lush, crowded canopies. Their leaves are papery fans of pale ochre, cinnabar, and bleached bone-white, rasping against one another without dropping a single bead of moisture. They do not drink; they feed on the fine, dry mica dust that drifts up through the floorboards and the slow, radiating warmth of the fired tiles.

The scale is that of an immense, vaulted timber-and-brick atrium, perhaps the size of a village square, but the geometry has folded into itself. A flight of clay-brick steps rises cleanly for eight paces, turns ninety degrees along what should be an open void, and becomes the lintel of an inverted alcove without dropping a single grain of sand. Gravity here is not a single downward pull, but a local agreement between materials: things fall toward whatever surface was most recently struck by a mallet.

Because the construction is young, every edge is sharp. Chisel facets catch the warm, even daylight that filters down from unseen overhead skylights; the wood has not yet grayed, maintaining the honeyed, fibrous tone of freshly split cedar. 

In the tight spaces between structural ribs and dense wicker brambles, fauna thrives in total silence. Segmented, hand-sized beetles with shells of dried, unpainted clay navigate the vertical joists, their legs tipped with tiny copper-like spurs that make faint *tink-tink* sounds as they climb. Flocks of small, feather-tailed lizards, completely dry and scale-less like soft chamois leather, nest inside the hollows of wooden pegs. They move in slow, deliberate darts, browsing on the crisp, crumbly spore-clusters that crust the mortar lines. Occasionally, a silk-spinner the size of a fist pays out a dry, powdery thread between two timber beams, bridging a gap where an impossible corner meets its own reflection.

The silence is thick, cushioned by the sheer density of dry organic matter and porous brick. Aside from the occasional brittle crack of a new fern-blade uncoiling, or the muffled scuttle of a clay-beetle behind a cedar panel, the court remains hushed. 

Deep within the thickets of dry cane, the architecture continues to self-assemble by hand-tool logic: unseen, patient forces chisel fresh dovetails into blank ash-blocks, drawing forth new shelves, new stairs, and fresh crops of dry, leafy rosettes that immediately root into the shavings. Everything is built, everything is growing, and nothing has ever known rain.
