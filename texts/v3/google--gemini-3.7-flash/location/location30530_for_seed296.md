At this altitude, the world has long ceased to be jagged. The peaks are not sharp young horns of thrust rock, but colossal, rounded domes of dark gneiss, worn smooth by three hundred million years of high-altitude scouring. They stand like the backs of sleeping leviathans emerging from a floor of permanent stratus—broad, loaf-like plateaus and towering, fluted monoliths whose sheer sides have been rubbed into soft, undulating gutters by ice and ancient winds.

Between these ancient massifs, wedged into the vertical flutes and resting upon shelves of slick, dark stone, sits an intensely layered city. 

It is cold here, a still, biting chill that never quite freezes, sustained by the great ocean of mist that rises from the unseen depths below. The air carries an unrelenting, fine dampness. It does not often rain from above; instead, the clouds breathe upward against the rock faces. As the thermal currents push dense fog up the granite chutes, the moisture condenses instantly on every cold surface. Every iron rivet, every stone sill, and every hanging cable is permanently beaded with crystal-clear droplets that gather, tremble, and release into a vast, deliberate network of copper runnels and iron troughs.

Because the rock is old and the moisture is endless, the vegetation has claimed the stone with ferocious density. Thick cushions of chartreuse liverwort, leathery mountain-ferns, and dense mats of deep purple dwarf-ivy pad the masonry and choke the gutters. These plants are cold-blooded, slow-growing, and dense as felt, swallowing the sound of the place. They drape over window lintels and wrap around exposed steam pipes, thriving on the warmth that bleeds through the seams of the city’s industrial ironwork.

The architecture is an engine of survival and elevation. Multi-tiered tenements of wet slate and dark timber are bolted directly into the granite, anchored by iron tie-rods thick as a man’s torso. Across the gulfs between the rounded towers, massive steel-truss bridges span the dizzying drops, their lower chords lost in the drifting mist. Counterweighted industrial funiculars, guided by grease-slicked cables, slide up and down the rock faces with heavy, rhythmic clanks, ferrying coal, machinery, and processed peat between the lower terrace factories and the high residential galleries. 

Everywhere, technology is built around the pressure of rising vapor and falling water. Great cast-iron condenser towers bristle with thousands of fine bronze wires, combing water from the clouds to drive silent, subterranean water-wheels before being piped into boiler plants. Heavy iron chimneys vent plumes of pale, scrubbed steam that mingle seamlessly with the passing fog. The industry here is quiet, mechanized, and heavy—the dull, reverberating *thrum-thrum* of air-compressors and the occasional hollow ring of a pneumatic dispatch capsule echoing across a chasm, quickly swallowed by the fog and the moss.

Light is a diffuse, twilight phenomenon. The sun rarely penetrates cleanly; it is filtered through multiple strata of cloud into a soft, pewter luminescence that casts no hard shadows. Lanterns enclosed in thick, domed glass glow with steady, pale gaslight along the tram-ways and staircases, their beams illuminating swirling ribbons of vapor that snake along the stone channels.

Life here moves with an uncanny, quiet efficiency. True animals are rare—a few species of pale, blind salamanders that inhabit the perpetually wet gutters, and broad-winged, silent raptors that glide on the lip of the rising thermals, hunting for nothing visible. Yet the place feels deeply alive through its mechanical and atmospheric cycles. 

The entire valley functions like a single, massive respiration. In the morning, as the low cloud-deck warms, a great river of white vapor surges up the cliffs, setting thousands of condensation-screens singing in a low, harmonious drone. By midday, the water cascades down through the city’s vertical tiers, spinning dynamos, turning water-gates, and filling public cisterns with icy, mineral-rich water. Toward dusk, the updraft pauses, and a breathless, cool stillness descends upon the terraces. In that quiet hour, you can hear only the infinite, tiny patter of millions of condensing droplets striking slate, the slow, rhythmic ticking of cooling cast iron, and the muffled creak of a cable-car hanging motionless over a thousand feet of white air.
