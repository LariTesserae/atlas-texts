There is no light here to define the edges of the space, but the air carries an immediate sense of vast, hollow interiority. It is cold, dry as ground bone, and profoundly parched; any moisture that once drove the sap through these tissues evaporated uncounted ages ago, leaving behind a world built entirely from petrified cellulose, hollowed pith, and brittle, shrieking lignin.

The scale is paradoxical: it is a pocket-world, an enclosed cyst buried within the carcass of some unimaginably immense primordial flora, yet within this enclosed capsule, the physical density drops to nearly nothing. Most of the chamber is sheer, lightless volume—a cavernous, hollow heart spanning hundreds of paces of empty air, held open by the rigid, vaulted rib-works of a dead botanical interior. 

Across this abyss stretches an intricate, primitive metropolis. It is not carved from rock or forged from iron, but lashed, woven, and notched together entirely out of cured reed, split bark, and fibrous timber. Thousands of ancient, multi-tiered dwellings hang suspended in the darkness like dried wasps’ nests, connected to one another and to the distant cavern walls by sweeping webs of braided vine-cables and cantilevered bridges made of bound rushes. It is an urban landscape of staggering complexity, yet built with the logic of simple manual craft: crude square lashings, peg-and-socket joints whittled by flint, and great woven mats that form sagging plazas suspended over the void. No one moves through these streets anymore; there are no animals, no insects scuttling across the wicker pathways, no vermin nesting in the dry chaff. The city is an empty, lightless framework of dead wood, perfectly preserved by total desiccation.

Though the place is entirely devoid of life and weather, it is extraordinarily loud.

The hollow acts as a monumental soundbox. Because the entire environment is an interconnected web of drying fibers held under immense structural strain, it produces a continuous, deafening acoustic presence. The sound is not wind or water, but the mechanical roar of wood under tension. Deep, seismic groans shudder through the suspension cables as the massive outer walls of the botanical capsule shrink by fractions of a millimeter over centuries. Great bass drones hum constantly through the hollow towers, like the sound of giant bowed strings. Occasionally, the acoustic pressure culminates in a sharp, explosive crack—a single ancient cord snapping or a floor-spar splitting down its grain—which ricochets across the empty chamber for long seconds, amplified by the parched, resonant geometry of the wicker roofs and hollow floors.

Beneath the suspension bridges, far down at the rounded base of the chamber, lies the accumulation of epochs: a deep, powdery sediment of dry spore-husks, disintegrated bark, and sawdust worn down by friction. There is no decay here, only mechanical erosion. Over thousands of years, the slow, imperceptible sway of the architecture has ground its own joints into talc-fine botanical dust. 

To move through this space would mean navigating entirely by touch and resonance: feeling the grain of hand-planed burls, the taut vibration of vine-ropes that hum against the palms like live wires, and the dry, freezing draft that rises from the floorless dark below. It is a completely settled place, ancient beyond measure, caught in a patient, lightless cycle of drying, creaking, and vibrating itself to dust in the dark.
