The bedrock here has forgotten how to be sharp. For hundreds of millions of years, water has planed the land down to a broad, weeping pavement of black mudstone and iron-gray shale, tilted so subtly toward the gray ocean that the horizon seems less like a line and more like a gradual dissolving of stone into cold air. There are no hills left, only long, shallow swales where thin sheets of water slide continuously, an inch deep, moving in slow, silent sheets toward the sea.

Above this flat, ancient floor, the sound is staggering. It is not thunder, which breaks and rolls, but a flat, continuous roar that occupies the inner ear until it feels structural, vibrating in the marrow of the teeth. It is the composite noise of millions of hydraulic vents—fissures worn smooth in the shale where the tides and the cold interior runoff collide in darkness, violently expelling air and water through narrow natural pipes. Along the low ridges, the shale is perforated like pumice on a colossal scale, each hole screaming with pressurized vapor, a vast organ of stone played by the weight of a drowned continent.

There is nothing green. No moss clings to the wet ledges, no scrub roots through the gravel. The biology is entirely animal, fungal, and mineral. Where vegetation might elsewhere soften the ground, here the stone is coated in thick, slick films of iron-oxidizing slimes—rust-red, ochre, and cold leaden blue. In the depressions, vast fields of colonial tube-marrow grow: hard, chalky pipes the color of washed bone that rise three inches from the mud, each venting a pale, milky siphon into the moving film of water.

Because the land is flat and the atmosphere perpetually bruised with heavy, low-stratus cloud, the light has no source. It exists as a uniform, cold pewter sheen reflecting off every horizontal surface. The temperature hovers just above freezing; the air is thick with cold mist that does not fall as rain but condenses instantaneously upon any solid surface, coating skin, stone, and bone in a running glaze.

Life is dense, heavy-shelled, and loud beneath the water's skin. Between the whistling vent-holes, millions of flat-bodied scavengers—creatures like broad, segmented plates of slate-colored horn—crawl over one another in shifting drifts. They feed on the sulfur-mats and the drifting filaments of the tube-colonies, their heavy pincers clicking incessantly against the polished floor. In the larger channels, where the water deepens to the waist, slick, blind things the size of seals glide through the currents, their skin pale and rubbery, hunting the shell-crushers with blunt, cartilaginous snouts. They breach occasionally, not for air, but to slap the water with broad, muscular paddles—a dull *thwack* that cuts through the hydraulic roar.

Set far apart on the highest, driest whaleback ridges of smooth stone are the low marks of habitation. They are so flat they are easily mistaken for natural outcrops.

These are low-domed shelters built entirely of dry-stacked slate shingles and the massive, curved ribs of benthic animals dragged up from the tide-edge. There is no timber for beams, no thatch for roofs. The builders have fitted flat slabs of stone together with painstaking patience, caulking the seams with gray tallow and crushed shell-lime. Around each dwelling, the ground is littered with the detritus of a flint-and-bone culture: heavy hammers of river-granite, harpoon points chipped from black chert, and long drying-racks fashioned from tied ribs where strips of pale, fatty flesh hang curing in the salt mist.

A figure moving between these shelters is wrapped heavily in oiled blubber-skins, their face masked against the stinging spray by a visor of thin, scraped horn. They move with a wide, braced gait, accustomed to the slipperiness of the slime-mats and the constant vibration of the floor beneath them. They do not speak; spoken words are swallowed instantly by the vents. Instead, communication happens through the stamping of heavy stone-shod boots against the bedrock, sending sharp, percussive shocks through the stone that can be felt in the soles of the feet hundreds of yards away.

At intervals, the tide pushes inland, not as a wall of surf, but as an inexorable rising of the floor's water-sheet, deepening from inches to feet in the span of minutes. When this happens, the vent-noises shift in pitch, dropping from high, shrill screams to a guttural, earth-shaking thrum. The bone-shelters become islands, isolated in a vast, dim mirror of slate-colored water that stretches to the edge of the sky, vibrating with the pulse of things living beneath the stone.
