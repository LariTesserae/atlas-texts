The plateau sits five miles above the dead lowlands, planed flat by four hundred million years of ceaseless wind. Here, the planet forgot how to make rock; the only bedrock is a cold, petrified peat three miles deep, worn down until its top is as smooth and level as an anvil. Over this expanse, the wet is total and freezing. It does not rain so much as the air itself collapses under its own saturation, condensing into heavy, ice-beaded fog that drenches everything in a matter of seconds.

Yet the plateau is not quiet. It roars with the deafening, rhythmic violence of an unguided engine. 

The vegetation has long ceased to behave merely as flora. It has become a macro-scale industrial anatomy—sprawling, sparse, and colossal. Vast, hollowed trunks of iron-bark cedar, some two hundred yards thick and worn by millennia of friction down to slick, grease-blackened snouts, function as natural pneumatic cylinders. They pump furiously against the chill air. Beneath the wet turf, root systems as thick as subway tunnels act as reciprocating drive-shafts, thrusting back and forth in slow, shuddering strokes that make the standing pools of black water jump and spray.

The landscape is mostly empty space—gray, freezing vistas of bruised fog—punctuated at mile-wide intervals by these titanic vegetal works. Between them, the ground is an ancient, sodden lawn of iron-moss that weeps freezing, viscous fluids: part water, part vegetable tallow, part kerosene. Deep channels have been gouged into the peat by the steady runoff of hydraulic sap, carving rivers of cold, amber fuel that froth over low terraces into the abyss at the plateau’s rim.

There are no buildings here, but the forms mimic the highest escalations of mass production. Immense, ribbed seed-pods, the size of dry docks, hang from bowed, cable-like creepers. Inside their translucent, fibrous hulls, a furious, twitching churn of life is underway. Pods open and close with the hiss of steam valves, venting clouds of warm, ammonia-tinged mist that instantly freeze into falling needles of ice. When they vent, the sound is like a train brake releasing across forty miles of marsh.

Reality fractures along the lines of this mechanization. The boundary between growth and manufacturing has liquefied: leaves do not uncurl, they are stamped out by shivering, hammer-like boughs, emerging with serrations as sharp and precise as rotary saw blades, whirring in the gale until they shear themselves off and skate across the flats. In places where the peat is oldest, the gravity fails to hold its constant; puddles of heavy water lift off the moss in spinning, lens-shaped discs, drifting into the grinding joints of the wood-machinery where they are crushed into cold, white vapor.

Life in the plateau thrives inside this turbulence. Millions of small, blind things—creatures of grease-slick fur and jointed, chitinous legs—nest inside the vibrating exhaust hollows of the iron-bark trees. They move with the hyperactive urgency of grease-monkeys, darting through the spray of pressurized sap to scrape sulfur and solidified fats from the moving bark. In the air above, flocks of flat-winged, membranous flyers circle the high vents, diving into the turbulent updrafts of hot vapor to warm their frozen blood, their cries lost beneath the steady, seismic *thump-thump-thump* of the plateau's subterranean pistons.

Light never properly arrives. The sun is only ever a dull, bruised yellow blister behind miles of wet altitude. In this perpetual half-night, the only sharpness comes from friction: where the immense root-shafts grind against the petrified substrate, cold green sparks jump through the muck, briefly illuminating the vast, empty distances, the steaming vents, and the endless, ancient expanse of living iron grinding itself down to nothing.
