The gorge sits high in the cold notch of a mountain ridge, caught in a permanent cycle of condensation and spill. Bright, white-diffused daylight pours through the upper canopy, illuminating a narrow, vertical world where the boundary between architecture and forest has long ceased to exist. Here, the bedrock is rarely visible; it has been buried under centuries of thick root-mats, clinging liverworts, and peat-sponges that hold the cold highland water like a swollen lung.

It is a settlement built directly inside the living volume of the gorge. The floor is not soil, but a buckled, woven floor of iron-hard taproots polished slick by the passage of thousands of bare feet. Above, rising ten stories into the green-filtered light, the walls of the ravine are tiered with dwellings, walkways, and market platforms lashed directly to the boles of massive, buttressed cedar-relatives and dangling strangler figs. 

The air is brisk, smelling of crushed ferns, woodsmoke, and cold river silt. It carries a heavy, misting spray that condenses instantly on wool coats, timber railings, and the fine down of ferns. Water is the primary infrastructure: hollowed-out log flumes run alongside every footway, tapping mountain springs from the upper rim and shunting water down through the settlement in a clattering, interlocking network of chutes. It pours into bark-lined cisterns, spills over mossy roof-eaves, and drops through floorboards in pale, braided threads to feed the tangled under-layers below.

The energy within the cleft is frantic and crowded. Because space is restricted to the width of the gorge, several thousand inhabitants move in continuous, overlapping vertical traffic. Hand-hewn ladders of notched oak connect platform to platform. Swaying bridges made of braided vine and split-bamboo span the void, bouncing and groaning under the weight of porters hauling wicker panniers loaded with tubers, river-crabs, and wet bundles of bark-cloth. At the major junctions, large wooden capstans—turned by hand and lubricated with rendered animal fat—creak loudly as they haul sling-loads of firewood and stone weights up from the dark, roaring bottom of the chasm.

Life here shares every surface with an overwhelming press of fauna that has integrated entirely into the urban fabric. Swarms of high-altitude swallows and sharp-billed canopy swifts nest by the tens of thousands in the thatch and beneath the boardwalks, darting through the pedestrian lanes in screaming, erratic clouds to snatch insects drawn by the domestic fires. In the bromeliad-choked crooks of the support trees, fat, green-bellied tree frogs cluster in vibrating colonies; their deep, rhythmic croaking forms a continuous baseline roar that echoes off the wet slate cliffs. Long-tailed, coarse-furred arboreal mammals—half-tame, half-feral—scamper along the roof-poles and guy-lines, stealing scraps of dried fish from drying racks or fighting loudly over discarded seed-husks, entirely unbothered by the shouts and swinging brooms of the market-tenders.

There is no stillness. When the wind sweeps over the mountain ridge, the entire settlement moves—the great trees flex with a deep, wooden groan, the suspension bridges tighten and slacken, and sheets of collected rainwater are shaken from the high leaves in sudden, drenching curtains. Below the noise of wind and water, the human presence is loud and physical: the steady, hollow *thock-thock* of wooden mallets beating soaked bark into cloth against log anvils, the shouts of winch-crews coordinating their pulls, the barking of small pack-dogs tethered to the railings, and the sharp hiss of cold water splashing onto clay hearths.

Every construction is simple, repairable, and in a constant state of renewal. Rotted deck-planks are pried up with flat bone chisels and dropped into the lower abyss, instantly replaced by freshly split greenwood that bleeds pale sap onto the moss. Vines are trained into living trusses; awnings of broad, waxy palmate leaves are re-tied with hemp cord as soon as the highland rains batter them thin. The city does not resist the mountain’s cold, sodden weight—it hangs suspended within it, vibrating, loud, and thoroughly alive.
