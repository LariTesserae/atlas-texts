There is no bedrock here, no native dirt or untamed mantle. Beneath every pool, fern root, and basking lizard lies a hand-laid surface: wide, flat terraces of pale terracotta, beaten lime-plaster floors, and endless low parapets constructed of sun-cured, straw-tempered mud brick. The entire visible world is a single, continuous basin of manual masonry, stretching out in wide, generous steps under a white, isotropic glare that offers no sun to look at and casts no shadows beneath anything.

The construction is unmistakably primitive, bearing the immediate traces of fingers, palms, and simple wooden floats. Edges are not eroded; the squared lips of the bricks and the sharp score-marks left by reed combs in the wet plaster are as crisp as if they had set twenty minutes ago. Yet there is no wet mortar drying, no builders at work, and no dust. The world exists in a fixed, perpetual morning-after, locked in a heavy, saturated stasis.

It is fiercely hot—a thick, greenhouse warmth that remains completely uniform from the flat ground to the blinding ceiling of the sky. The air is so laden with moisture that it feels palpable against the skin, yet it does not rain. Instead, the masonry itself weeps. Microscopic pores in the fired clay exude a constant, warm film of clear water that gathers in shallow, millimeter-deep sheens over the brickwork. It slides over the unrounded edges of the terraces and fills wide, shallow cisterns lined with hand-pressed tesserae, never overflowing, never drying down.

Plant life does not struggle against the artificial ground; it is braided directly into it. Fleshy, paddle-leaved succulents and broad, emerald-veined elephant ears sprout from deliberate, thumb-sized perforations molded into the tiles. Great mats of pale green liverwort and star-moss carpet the low risers between levels, their tiny rhizoids anchored in the lime mortar. The foliage is shockingly lush, swollen with water, and deeply green, glistening continuously with beads of condensation that never roll off. Because time does not degrade the landscape, there are no dead leaves, no leaf litter, and no rot. Every stem is at the absolute peak of its turgor, holding its shape with the rigid perfection of blown glass.

The space is broad and sparsely populated by forms. Vast, open courtyards of damp, pinkish brick span distances of hundreds of yards before meeting a single, solitary feature: a low, hand-carved stone plinth, an empty basin surrounded by six-inch retaining walls, or an isolated colonnade of unadorned, sun-baked clay drums. Between these structures, the emptiness is vast and tranquil, a clean expanse of sweating floor reflecting the white radiance above.

Reality bends softly at the margins of these open spaces. The horizontal grid of bricks does not vanish toward a true horizon; rather, the tiling seems to gently warp upward in the distance, displaying distant courtyards and solitary palm-clusters at an impossible tilt, as if the world were the interior of a shallow, handmade bowl whose edges meet an unfeatured white ceiling. Distances fold: a cluster of broad-leafed ginger plants that appears half a mile away can be reached in fifty steady paces across the slick, soundless floor.

Fauna fills the sparse architecture in profound silence. Long-bodied skinks with pale, translucent bellies lie splayed on the warm tiles, their throats pulsing in slow, identical rhythms. Iridescent dragonflies with double-spanned wings hover over the shallow water-sheets, motionless in the air for minutes at a time as if pinned to the heat. Soft-shelled turtles, their carapaces patterned with the same geometric reed-impressions found in the brickwork, rest on the submerged steps of the cisterns. Thousands of small, chalk-white tree frogs sit along the damp lips of the mud-brick walls, their golden eyes wide and unblinking.

Despite the density of animal life, the landscape is almost entirely noiseless. There is no splash when a turtle slides into a pool; the water parts and closes without a slap. The frogs do not croak. The only sound is a faint, universal hiss—scarcely louder than a held breath—produced by billions of tiny water beads continuously forming on warm clay and waxy leaves, an ambient murmur that never rises, never falls, and never resolves into silence.
