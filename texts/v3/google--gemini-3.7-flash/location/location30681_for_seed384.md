The water here is not merely deep; it is thick with the weight of four hundred vertical fathoms of cold brine, pressing down into a drowned, box-canyon basin no larger than a municipal foundry yard. The temperature hovers a fraction of a degree above freezing. At this pressure, the water behaves almost like a hydraulic fluid, deadening vibrations and absorbing the shock of heavy iron so completely that the violence of the machinery becomes an eerie, visual pantomime.

It is a sunken industrial cloister: three tiers of drowned brick tenements, cast-iron catwalks, and heavy pneumatic boiler houses, packed tight against the black stone walls of a sunken basalt trench. The entire district spans perhaps two hundred paces across, but it extends upward and downward in dense, interlocking strata of iron, stone, and silt-caked copper.

Nothing here has stopped moving, though it has been submerged long enough for the sharp corners of the masonry to soften under thick mineral rimes and cold-water calcite deposits. 

Massive, slow-stroke pistons—cast from dark marine bronze that resists the brine—rise and fall within flooded cylinder jackets. They drive vast arrays of looped chain-drives and eccentric cams, which turn gargantuan, low-speed paddle turbines. The purpose of this work is not immediately clear, but it is continuous: the machinery draws in thick, benthic sediment through perforated iron grates, compresses it into dense, rectangular ingots of black mineral silt, and sends the blocks gliding along submerged roller-tracks into flooded vaults below. 

The silence is almost total. A human ear placed against the cold iron would hear only the faintest, low-frequency hum—less a sound than a slow pulse in the jawbone—while water cushions the impact of multi-ton counterweights touching down on lead pads. Bubbles do not form; the pressure simply compresses any outgassing into instantly dissolved microscopic threads.

Light is sparse, dull, and cold. In the upper gantries, where iron pipes cross in dense, bird’s-nest tangles, a few ancient galvanic lanterns continue to burn. Their thick glass housings, frosted with sea-salt crusts, cast a faint, greenish-yellow smudge no further than an arm's reach into the murk. In this dimness, the architecture looms as towering silhouettes: corbelled brick arches, riveted bulkheads with their hatchways permanently dog-latched open, and towering soot-stacks that now vent only lazy, undulating plumes of super-saline, warm-core brine into the freezing surrounding sea.

Ecology in the basin is skeletal and opportunistic. There are no kelp forests or waving fronds; vegetation exists only as vast, velvet-textured carpets of iron-reducing bacteria, which coat the catwalks in shades of bruised purple and ochre. On the slower-moving flywheels, pale colonies of stalked crinoids have anchored themselves, their feather-like arms fanning the sluggish current generated by the wheel’s rotation, feeding on the churned-up detritus.

Animal life is an afterthought, drifting through the gears like stray dust. Occasionally, a pale, scaleless eel—blind and entirely translucent, its spine glowing faintly under the lantern light—will wind its way between the teeth of a six-foot gear, slipping through the mesh just a second before the teeth meet. Long-legged sea spiders, the color of wet chalk, crawl methodically across the valve housings, picking at the microbial mats with needle-like claws.

The atmosphere is one of profound, unhurried purpose. The city-floor is not in ruins; it is operating in a state of post-terrestrial equilibrium. Overhead, thick copper telegraph cables, insulated in gutta-percha and crusted with tube-worms, still twitch and take up tension as submerged hydraulic relays switch on and off in the tenements. Deep within the lower rooms, where the original timber flooring has long since petrified under the salt, heavy iron looms with bronze shuttles continue to weave thick mats of basalt fiber, passing them out through narrow slits into the black silt, slowly laying a woven iron-and-stone carpet across the ocean bed, foot by silent foot.
