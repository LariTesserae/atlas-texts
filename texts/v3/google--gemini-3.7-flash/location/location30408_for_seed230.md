The terrace sits four thousand paces above the valley floor, cut directly into the shelf of a pale slate massif. It is not an expansive plateau, but a tight, deliberate shoulder of rock—no wider than a few city blocks from the sheer rear cliff to the outer drop-off—yet every foot of it has been disciplined by human hands into an organized, vertical settlement.

The air here is thin, bright, and cool. Sunlight falls across the grey-and-cream stone with a clean, unhurried sharpness, casting long, hard-edged shadows that move across the terraces like iron rods. Despite the direct sun, the ambient temperature stays low; stone in the shade remains chilled to the touch, and the heat never sinks deep enough to drive out the dampness that settles each night.

That moisture is quiet and cyclical. It does not fall as heavy rain, but arrives as a high-altitude mist that condenses directly against the rock faces and slate roofs before sunrise. By midday, this moisture has gathered into micro-rivulets that slide down hand-pecked drainage grooves chiseled into the streets. It collects in shallow, square-cut basins set at street corners—stone lips worn smooth by buckets and skins—where it sits clear and mirror-still, shaded by overhanging eaves. 

The architecture is dense and stacked, an urban logic compressed into a tiny footprint. The buildings are constructed entirely of dry-laid metamorphic stone, fitted with remarkable precision without lime or mortar. Larger megalithic blocks form the foundations and retaining terraces, their faces pitted with the rhythmic tooth-marks of copper and bronze chisels; above them, smaller, carefully dressed shale shingles form thick walls and low-pitched roofs. Stepped alleys, only wide enough for two people to pass shoulder-to-shoulder, climb through the tiers of housing, linked by short flights of stone stairs whose centers are dished from centuries of soft-soled foot traffic. Heavy timber lintels, hewn from high-altitude dwarf pine and cured to the color of dry bone, span the doorways, untouched by rot in the dry, cold air.

Yet there is a vast emptiness within this density. The structures stand upright, sound, and orderly, but the population is sparse or still. Doors stand open or are covered with heavy, grease-rubbed leather curtains that hang motionless in the absence of drafts. There is no clutter in the streets—no carts, no beast-pens, no scattered detritus of rapid industry. 

Vegetation is minimal, keeping strictly to the limits imposed by the rock. A pale, crustose lichen, somewhere between sea-green and ash-gray, maps the north faces of the retaining walls. In the joints where stone blocks meet near the drain-gutters, small tufts of stiff, needle-leafed mountain sedge have found root, their pale silver stalks completely dry, vibrating only when the wind comes over the lip of the ridge. No trees grow on the terrace itself; the earth is too thin, confined only to narrow, stone-walled planting troughs that currently hold only settled, dark-grey silt.

Animal life is an occasional, singular presence. A high-altitude chough, soot-black with a yellow bill, hops along the coping stones of an upper parapet, its claws clicking faintly against the slate before it launches into the blue void below. Deep inside the unmortared joints of the walls, small rock lizards, sluggish in the cool shade, press their bellies against the stones that have caught the midday sun.

The soundscape is defined by distance and mass. The drop below the outer wall is immense, muting any sound from the lowlands into a faint, collective murmur that never quite resolves into noise. Within the settlement, the dominant sound is the occasional, resonant *tink* of water dripping from an eave into a full cistern, and the dry, scouring hiss of wind sliding across flat stone surfaces.

Everything here has found its angle of repose. The erosion is ancient and finished; the stone has already shed whatever was weak, and what remains is in permanent balance with the frost and the wind. There is no feeling of decay, nor any sense of impending change. The settlement exists in an enduring, frozen noon—cleanly carved, cool, damp in the seams, and entirely steady against the mountain flank.
