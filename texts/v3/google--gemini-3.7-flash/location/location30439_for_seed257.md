High upon a drowning plateau, inside the sealed belly of an ancient sky-cistern, the water is as warm as blood in a resting vein.

It is a completely enclosed, fabricated volume—the size of a sunken basilica or a flooded amphitheater—perched at an elevation where the air outside, if there were any left to breathe, would be thin and freezing. But here within the stone envelope, insulated by twenty-foot-thick walls of hand-beaten clay and fitted basalt, the water sits undisturbed, heated from some deep, buried flue below the floor.

There is no natural bedrock exposed anywhere. Every surface is made. The floor is stepped with concentric tiers of broad, hand-molded terracotta bricks, their edges smoothed into soft, rounded loaves by centuries of microscopic water-wear. Towering colonnades rise toward a vaulted ceiling lost in the overhead gloom—not turned on lathes, but built from thousands of interlocking, dry-stacked stone cylinders, chiseled out with primitive flint and copper adzes whose faint, rhythmic strike-marks have been softened into faint ripples by the ages.

Not a single leaf, spore, or strand of weed exists in this dark warmth. There is no green; the water has never known sunlight. 

In place of vegetation, the masonry is alive with animals. 

They occupy every square inch of the construction. Pale, skin-blind eels—thick as wrists and longer than a man’s arm—braid themselves into the negative space of the hollow arches, their soft bodies drifting with the lazy convection currents that rise from the floor. The vertical faces of the brick pillars are encrusted with dense, calcified colonies of tube-dwelling worms, their feathery, blood-red gills expanding and retracting in slow unison, sifting the nutrient-thick brine. 

Blind, porcelain-white crabs scuttle across the sunken stairs. They have no eyes, only elongated, jointed antennae that tap constantly against the tiles, testing the lime-rich mortar joints for micro-crustaceans. Fat, translucent mollusks the size of shields glide slowly across the relief carvings on the walls, their muscular feet rasping away the micro-faunal slime, leaving behind clean, pinkish tracks on the gray basalt.

The water itself is dense, heavy with dissolved limestone, slightly alkaline, and nearly black. The only illumination is a rare, sickly ghost-glow: tiny, wandering larval clouds that flash with brief, cold phosphorescence when an eel sweeps its tail through their drift. When the glow flares, it reveals the sheer compression of the space—hundreds of nested niches carved into the walls, originally built perhaps as crypts or storage bins, now packed to overflowing with the coiled bodies of white fish, sleeping in the warmth.

Sound does not travel here in any familiar way. The density of the water and the sheer mass of the surrounding stone swallow all vibration. There is only a profound, velvet silence, broken at long intervals by the dull *tock* of a crab’s shell striking a clay cornice, or the soft, muffled crunch of a shell being crushed between flat, toothless jaws. 

Change occurs across generations. Sometimes a tier of ancient terracotta tiles yields to the endless, gentle water pressure, settling an inch into the soft silt of shed exoskeletons that blankets the lowest floor. Sometimes an eel dies, and within an hour its pale length is covered by a trembling mound of scavengers, leaving behind only a delicate cage of bone that is slowly cemented into the floor by lime-secreting worms. 

The cistern neither drains nor fills. It holds its breath at the top of the world—a dark, hand-built womb of warm brine, endlessly self-consuming, perfectly still, and crowded with soft, sightless life.
