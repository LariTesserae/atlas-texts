The air at the floor of the Great Chasm is bone-cold, completely devoid of moisture, and smells faintly of cured heartwood, powdered talc, and the hot, dry ozone of unshielded dynamos. 

This is a subterranean rift extending several leagues across, yet there is nothing claustrophobic about it. The ceiling is lost somewhere high above in the cool, steady glare of suspended phosphor lattices—broad, industrial light arrays that flood the immense expanse with the clarity of a crisp noon. Below them, the world is open, vast, and astonishingly arid. There are no drips, no condensation on the walls, no slicks of mud or subterranean puddles. Every surface is dry to the touch, velveted with a microscopic dust of shed cellulose.

What passes for terrain here is entirely vegetative, though it bears little resemblance to green, hydrated flora. The floor of the rift is a deep, continuous strata of pale, petrified xylem and compressed root-matter, a dense, ivory-toned mattress of living structural fiber that has self-assembled over the rift’s freshly fractured base. Because there is no water to wear down the edges, the landscape is violently crisp: freshly sprouted spires of hard, hollow reed-timber rise hundreds of meters with razor-clean vertical ribs, and the broken shelves of older vascular growth remain as sharp as newly cleaved flint. It is an embryonic landscape, emerging from deep mineral vents without the softening influence of rain or river.

The plants here do not drink; they breathe dry aerosolized minerals and exude an ultra-dense, self-reinforcing lignin that possesses the tensile strength of cold-rolled steel. Immense, column-like stipes—wide as avenues—arch across the void at long, measured intervals. Between these colossal living pillars lie vast, empty clearings of pale fibrous turf, giving the floor an expansive, almost park-like sparseness despite the overwhelming biomass.

Settlement has fused with this megaflora. Bolted directly into the pale wood are heavy industrial complexes: tiers of dark-lacquered steel housing, high-torque pneumatic elevators, and cantilevered refineries wrapped in copper conduits. The architecture is methodical, layered, and unmistakably urban, organized along the natural growth-lines of the wood. Rail carriages suspended on overhead tension cables travel silently between the trunk-cities, their cars laden with dry, powdered resins, spun mineral fiber, and processed amber harvested from the stipe-cores.

Despite the heavy machinery and the scale of the foundries, the acoustics of the cavern are strangely damped. The porous, cellular nature of the floor and walls drinks sound like a sponge. The thud of a six-ton drop-forge half a mile away arrives only as a dry, muffled heartbeat through the soles of your boots; the whine of a rotary saw peeling structural veneers sounds like someone tearing heavy paper behind a curtain. The cavern is mostly quiet, filled only with the faint, rhythmic *shuck-shuck* of automated dry-sifters and the wind created by the ventilation turbines that keep the chill air circulating through the inhabited boughs.

Life moves through this dry cathedral with deliberate purpose. Pale, many-jointed creatures—somewhere between whip-scorpions and ungulates, sheathed in lightweight, chalk-white chitin—graze on the fibrous margins of the industrial tracks, stripping dry bark with shearing mouthparts. Above them, broad, paper-winged gliders with six-foot spans launch themselves from the urban gantries, riding the cold updrafts generated by the factory heat-sinks, silently patrolling the cavern for the drift of airborne spore-flecks.

The cycle here is not driven by sun or seasonal rain, but by the pulse of deep pneumatic vents and the metabolic surges of the wooden bedrock. Every few hours, the ambient light arrays dim slightly to conserve the city-grids, and the colossal stipes creak in unison—a dry, wooden groan echoing across kilometers of open space—as they expand a few millimeters deeper into the rock, laying down another micron of unyielding, waterless tissue.
