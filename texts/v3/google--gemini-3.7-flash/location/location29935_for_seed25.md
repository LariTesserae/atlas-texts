The sound reaches across the open basin long before any feature of the ground can be identified. It is not the roar of wind or the concussive force of falling water, but a layered, unbroken acoustic mass: the dry clicking of countless articulated carapaces, the wet suction of soft bellies dragging across stone, the rhythmic rasp of mineral-grinding mouthparts, and high, frantic chirps of blind echolocation that knit together into a deafening, white-noise hiss. The noise is physical; it vibrates in the floor of the valley and sets the damp air trembling against the skin.

This is a wide, low depression—a shallow regional basin flanked by ancient, rounded bluffs of black mudstone and weathered slate. The rock has long since settled into soft, worn contours, smoothed over millennia not merely by the intermittent drizzle and heavy night-condensation, but by the relentless, abrasive passage of millions of crawling things. There is no soil here, only polished bedrock, fine mineral silt, and a constant, glistening sheen of cold moisture that collects in the hollows and runs in shallow, sluggish ribbons across the flats.

No green exists in this place. There are no stems, leaves, or photosynthetic crowns. The landscape is entirely animal and mineral. The biological base is non-plant: a pale, grease-like slime of lithotrophic microbes and chemosynthetic crusts that seep from the hairline fractures in the stone, blooming wherever the rock sweats mineral-rich water. 

Upon this thin, slick sustenance thrives a fauna so concentrated that the terrain itself seems to heave.

They carpet the basin in shifting, dense strata. Lowest are the grazers: broad, flattened organisms like segmented shields of dark horn, millimeters thick, possessing no eyes and dozens of spade-like ventral limbs that scrape the micro-crust from the slate. They move in interlocking sheets, grinding against one another with the sound of gravel being turned in a cement mixer. Above them, clambering over their backs in a frantic, ceaseless scramble, are long-limbed scavenger-forms—blind, pale-jointed creatures with whip-like antennae and barbed mandibles, constantly sifting the gaps between the shield-beasts for dead flesh, shed cuticle, and trapped moisture.

The air is cool, heavy, and smells sharply of ammonia, wet slate, and the sour musk of hemolymph. Light is nearly absent—the sky above hangs in a perpetual, thick overcast that permits only the dimmest gray twilight, fading frequently into total, suffocating blackness. In the dark, the landscape does not sleep; without sight, the ecosystem does not recognize day. Activity is a constant, shifting tide.

In the middle distances of the valley, where the ground dips into wide, shallow pans of cold, pooled runoff, the density reaches a critical pitch. Here, soft-bodied siphon-feeders—pale, rubbery sacks the size of boulders, anchored directly into the rock crevices—pump the moisture through their ribbed vents, their heavy, pneumatic exhalations adding a deep, bass percussion to the skittering treble of the swarm. Around them cluster predators: whip-tailed, chitinous wedges that strike with pneumatic speed, puncturing carapaces and draining fluids before being pushed aside or buried by the sheer press of oncoming bodies.

Nothing remains static for long, yet the overall structure never changes. When an animal dies, it is not buried; it is dismantled within minutes, ground down by thousands of blind, rasping limbs until its shell becomes part of the polished grit beneath the living layer. 

The valley functions like an enormous, self-consuming machine made of cold flesh and wet stone. Across miles of open, dim basin, under the steady drip of condensing mist, the living floor ripples and mutters, an ocean of blind persistence grinding itself against the ancient bones of the world.
