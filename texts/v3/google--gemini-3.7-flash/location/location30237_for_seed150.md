A high-altitude cleft, sheared clean by recent tectonic fractures, has been entirely colonized and converted into a vertical, multi-tiered settlement of living wood and pressurized vascular tissue. At this altitude, the air is thin, piercingly cold, and perpetually hung with a creeping, low-lying mist that never quite turns to snow, choosing instead to glaze every exposed surface in a skin of heavy, supercooled dew. 

The scale of the place is contained—a steep mountain amphitheatres no wider than a town square, walled in by sharp, unweathered slate and shale. Yet within this bounded chasm, the density is staggering. There is almost no open air. What would be roads, plazas, tenement blocks, and aqueducts in a human city are here realized entirely through the deliberate, hyper-dense architecture of cold-hardy flora. Iron-hard lignified trunks, thick as ancient pillars, rise in close ranks, their limbs grafted together through generational inosculation to form tiered floor plates, load-bearing arches, and vaulted ceilings.

Water is the primary mover and the governing pulse of the structure. It weeps continuously down the raw slate walls at the perimeter, fed by upland seeps, and is immediately captured by a labyrinth of living biological plumbing. Ribbed, hollowed vines, thick as barrels and insulated by dense coats of oily liverwort, run overhead and underfoot like city mains. They pulse with a low, hydraulic rhythm, routing cold mountain runoff through natural valves, cistern-galls, and woven troughs. Because the temperature hovers just a fraction above freezing, the water moves with a sluggish, viscous density, shedding plumes of condensation into the dim interior courts. 

Light penetrates only in filtered, bruised gradients of slate-gray and chlorophyll-green. The canopy above is an unbroken thatch of needle-bearing boughs and interlocking broadleaves coated in a thick, frost-resistant wax. Below this roof, the interior of the plant-city settles into a perpetual, sub-canopy twilight. To navigate the levels is to climb spiral staircases carved from the living grain of spiral-twisted stems, to walk down corridors whose walls are sheer curtains of woven, fibrous rootlets packed tight with damp peat, and to cross suspended bridges formed from single, continuous tension-roots stretched across the gaps between structural boles.

Despite the architectural organization—streets of uniform height, tiered domestic alcoves hollowed out of swollen trunk-nodes, communal drainage channels lined with slick, filtration-mosses—there is no trace of metal, stone masonry, or tool-work. The growth is the engineering. It is an urban ecology born without a builder, or shaped by hands that used only binding cord and grafting sap to guide the city’s bones into place before letting the plants swallow the scaffolding whole.

Sound is smothered by the sheer volume of organic matter. The cold damp deadens footfalls across the fibrous floorings. What remains is a quiet, continuous environment of small frictions: the heavy, rhythmic *clack-drip* of water overflowing from one suspended leaf-basin into another; the muffled groaning of structural timber flexing against the wind that shears across the ravine high above; and the faint, sibilant hiss of water being drawn up through millions of microscopic capillary paths.

Life here is slow and heavily insulated. The fauna is sparse, consisting mostly of small, cold-blooded specialists adapted to the dark and the damp. Sluggish, pale isopods the size of pebbles graze on the sulfurous fungal blooms that line the lower drainage runnels. In the deeper wall-cavities, where the living wood generates a faint, metabolic warmth, blind tree-frogs with rubbery, translucent skin remain wedged in dormant clusters, their heartbeats slowed to a crawl by the frigid mountain air.

At the base of the cleft, where the young rock floor is littered with sharp, angular rubble that has not yet had time to smooth or soil, the runoff collects in wide, shallow settling pools bordered by thick beds of carnivorous sundews whose glandular hairs are frozen into crystalline beads. From these pools, the excess water overflows through natural sluices in the rock, dropping out of the settlement and tumbling down the sheer mountain face below, lost to the fog.
