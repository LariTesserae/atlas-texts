At this altitude, there is no stone. What presents itself as a mountain range—a sprawling territory of ridges, steep defiles, and wide, wind-raked amphitheaters—is entirely made of compressed, freezing wood. 

The bedrock is heartwood, greyed and iron-hard, thousands of meters thick. Above it, the landscape is in a state of rapid, soundless, industrial-grade convulsion.

The vegetation does not merely cover the terrain; it *is* the terrain, functioning through a strange, unbuilt mechanization. Vast, fibrous cambium-pistons, twenty meters thick and coated in a rind of frosted resin, drive themselves slowly into the floors of the valleys, cycling cold, viscous sap through pressurized capillary tunnels. Girders of petrified vine arch across the ravines, locking together with the precise, interlocking bite of heavy ironwork, only to be sheared apart an hour later when a tectonic heave of new cellular growth tears through the slope. It operates with the unrelenting, scaled output of a foundry, yet there is not a single nail, rivet, or cut timber here. The shapes are biological, but their logic is brute-force engineering: counterweights of dense, swollen seed-pods the size of locomotives, natural steam-venting galls that release cold, atomized vapor to regulate internal hydraulic pressure, and massive sheets of mineral-crusted bark that slide over one another like stamping plates.

Despite this kinetic violence—the constant buckling of slopes, the rapid eruption and collapse of entire botanical buttresses—the silence is near absolute. 

The air is cold, around freezing, and extremely thin. A perpetual, damp mist clings to the low hollows, condensing into heavy, gel-like droplets along the underside of shifting woody plates. This humidity does not rise into clouds but stays glued to the surfaces, lubricating the monstrous friction of the plants. The wood itself is porous and lined with deep, needle-like velvet that drinks sound immediately. When a hundred-ton pylon of pressurized root-matter violently punches through a ridge to vent a pocket of sub-zero sap, it happens without a roar or a crack; there is only a dull, sub-audible shudder felt through the soles of the feet, accompanied by the swift, quiet hiss of escaping moisture.

The light is plain and clear—an even, daylight glare that shows every grain of the striated landscape. There is no warmth in it, but it reveals the sheer scale of the system: miles of interlocking tiers, terraced reservoirs of dark, enzyme-heavy water suspended in the crooks of ribbed branch-systems, and rolling hills that pulse rhythmically as vascular valves the size of barns open and close deep within the mulch. 

The wear on the environment is ancient. Even as the terrain reconfigures itself hourly, the surfaces are polished by centuries of internal shear. The edges of the great wooden cams and shearing leaves are smoothed like river pebbles, ground down by the ceaseless work of their own growth and decay. In the saddles between peaks, beds of dense, peat-like sawdust drift like snow, collecting intermittent drizzles that seep down to recharge the root-pumps below.

Animal life is an afterthought here, scarce and fragile against the scale of the machinery. Occasionally, small, slate-colored isopods, flat as shingles, can be seen clinging to the sides of a rising piston, grazing on the slick sheen of bacterial grease before the wood sinks back into the subterranean churn. They move quickly, avoiding the pinch-points where thousands of tons of living fiber knit together in tight, cold seams. 

Over the course of an afternoon, a valley floor might rise fifty feet on a rising tide of dense, interwoven xylem, forming a high, ribbed plateau that immediately begins to carve itself into cooling flues. The cold preserves the structure even as it transforms, locking the fibrous masses in high tension, a self-generating mountain of quiet, violent labor that never rests and never makes a sound.
