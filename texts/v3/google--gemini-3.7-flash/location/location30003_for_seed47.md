The air at this altitude possesses almost no mass, only velocity. Because there is so little medium to carry a wave, the ongoing destruction of the plateau happens in absolute, crystalline silence.

Below lies nothing visible—only the pale, blue-white haze of the planet’s lower veil, hundreds of leagues beneath the floor of this zone. Up here, the terrain consists of immense, hollowed tectonic plates of dry silicate and matte-black sintered alloys, suspended in a state of perpetual, violent dislocation. These are not mountains rooted in a mantle, but floating crustal slabs the size of archipelagos, held aloft and continually rearranged by a dense, unanchored lattice of metric-expansion fields. 

Though the movement is furious—slabs shearing past one another at hundreds of kilometers an hour, banking into steep, vertiginous climbs, or spinning on invisible gyroscopic axes—the surfaces themselves are ancient beyond reckoning. Every stone, every jutting ridge of exposed basalt or composite foundation, is worn utterly smooth. Millions of years of dry particulate scouring have removed every sharp edge; the rock is rounded, parabolic, buffed to the soft, waxy texture of river jade or sea-glass, though no drop of liquid water has touched it in epochs. 

The light is cold, bright, and unrelenting. Unsoftened by thick atmosphere, the sun cuts hard-edged, geometric shadows that slide across the moving plains. When two continental fragments pass within meters of each other, the shadow of one wipes across the other like a dropped curtain, yet there is no boom of displaced air, no grinding screech—only the visual fact of millions of tons of smoothed mineral sweeping through the light.

Between the drifting masses, the emptiness is nearly complete. Great gulfs of clean, thin space separate one formation from the next. You can look out across fifty miles of absolute void and see only a single, isolated pylon—an unadorned spar of pale ceramic alloy, twenty miles tall, suspended vertically in the clear air, its base anchored to nothing at all. Its surface is pitted by micro-meteors and polished by the high-speed drift of dry dust, gently bleeding a faint, invisible refraction pattern into the sky around it.

Where life exists, it clings to the lee of the moving crags where the shear-stress of the air-slip is neutralized by counter-inertial fields. It is sparse and subdued: cold, graphite-gray lichen-webs that do not grow outward, but etch themselves into the microscopic fissures of the ancient rock, extracting trace elements from the dry mineral matrix. In deep hollows on the undersides of the floating plates, where the ambient temperature settles into a steady, stiff frost, there are occasional patches of glass-stemmed shrubs, dry as straw, which shatter without sound if struck by drifting debris, their seeds dispersing like fine gray sand into the updrafts.

Rarely, something moves with intent rather than momentum. High above the churning floor of slabs, thin creatures with spans larger than sailing ships ride the localized gravity-shears. They are wide, flat, and nearly two-dimensional, constructed of dry, layered cartilage that never needs to hydrate, sustained by the ambient electromagnetic bleed of the old anchors below. They do not flap; they flex their bodies to catch the invisible boundaries between conflicting field-densities, banking silently through the bright, cold sun.

The turbulence is constant, a self-sustaining storm of solid matter that never resolves into a resting state. Huge, smoothed fragments of ancient citadels—structures whose architecture obeys non-Euclidean load balances—are tossed into high, parabolic arcs, tumble for days through the upper stratosphere, and are caught again by the lower field-lines to be swept back into the kinetic mill. 

Yet despite the violence of the churn, the overall feeling is one of petrified stillness. Because the cycle is endless and has run for geological ages, the chaos has the consistency of an ocean current. It is an ancient, polished machine made of continents, turning eternally beneath a clear sky, grinding itself toward a perfection it achieved ten million years ago, making no more sound than dust falling through a beam of light.
