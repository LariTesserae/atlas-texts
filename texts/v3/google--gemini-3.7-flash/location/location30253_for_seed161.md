The place is a throat in the high rock. 

At this altitude, the open world is little more than thin, scouring wind and pale sky, but down inside the spire’s hollow core, the air is caught and held. It is a vertical canyon no wider than a cathedral nave, carved out of an ancient sandstone chimney whose outer walls drop away into mountain valleys so far below they are lost in haze. The rock here has spent uncounted ages dying into shape; every surface is smoothed, scooped, and fluted by millions of years of sand-laden updrafts. There are no jagged fractures left, only undulating, marrow-like curves of deep ochre, rust, and sun-baked umber.

Because the chimney is narrow and sheer, the noon sun never fully reaches the lower levels. The light remains a perpetual, heavy twilight—a warm, amber dusk reflected down through hundreds of cubits of polished stone. The rock retains the heat of the high atmosphere, radiating a dry, constant warmth that makes the air feel thick and static, like the inside of a brick kiln hours after the fire has been raked out.

Water is nearly an abstraction here. There are no streams, no wet seeps, and rain is a rare, violent mist that evaporates before it can pool. Yet the rock face is far from barren. Life has wedged itself into every fluted hollow and horizontal stress-crack. Thick, leathery succulents the color of bruised plums push out from the stone, their skin tough as hide to seal in bitter, viscous jelly. Long, silver-gray taproots, hairy with fine filaments, drape down the cliff faces for dozens of yards, questing for the faint trace of mineral damp trapped deep within the porous stone. Clustered along every ledge are dense mats of bone-sage and thorn-brier, rigid and dry-leaved, which rustle like parchment whenever a draft pulses up from the depths.

Tangled entirely through this vegetation is the settlement. It is not a town of streets, but of tiers, wedges, and brackets. 

The people have built directly into the wind-hollows using flat slabs of dry-stacked siltstone, mortared with a pale, powdered dust and dried plant resin. Hundreds of small, low-ceilinged dwellings cling to the perimeter of the vertical shaft, stacked five and six layers deep, connected by an intricate maze of sun-bleached timber ladders, hemp walkways, and narrow ramps notched straight into the cliff. The wood used is stunted mountain juniper, hard as bone and rubbed dark and greasy by generations of bare palms and climbing feet. There is no iron here; joints are lashed with cured rawhide that has dried drum-tight in the arid heat, and pinned with polished pegs of ibex horn.

Because space is compressed to the rim of this rock chimney, the density of presence is overwhelming. Every ledge holds an activity: grain-rubbers grinding dried starch-roots between smooth river-stones hauled up centuries ago; women braiding tough plant fibers into endless cordage; shallow, communal dung-hearths where smoke rises straight and thin in the windless core, smelling of scorched resin and dry marrow. 

And around this human hive, the animals thrive in astonishing numbers. Thousands of dry-land swifts nest in the stone pockets, their bodies sleek and slate-colored. They leave and return in explosive, coordinated clouds, filling the shaft with a sustained, rushing roar like tumbling shingle. Long-toed cliff lizards, dull orange and completely silent, scuttle over the stone lintels and thatch roofs, snapping at the tiny, armored flies that feed on dried scraps. Fat rock-hyraxes, docile and semi-domesticated, are penned in woven-wicker cages hung out over the abyss, chewing methodically on bundles of dried lichen lowered to them on lines.

The noise within the throat is loud and ceaseless, magnified by the acoustic curve of the ancient sandstone. It is not the sudden crash of machinery, but a continuous, vibrating drone: the shrill screaming of the nesting birds, the dry clatter of thousands of small claws on stone, the hollow *thunk* of wooden mallets shaping hide, and, high above it all, the deep, bass organ-note of the wind shearing across the lip of the chimney, miles above the floor.

Everything moves according to a slow, ancient rhythm. The stone does not fall; it has already fallen as much as it ever will. The dust settles imperceptibly onto the ledges, is swept off into the void, and settles again. Generation after generation lives suspended between the high cold sky and the dead floor below, inhabiting a dry, crowded, amber-lit warmth, perfectly fitted into the hollowed ribs of the mountain.
