The chamber is low-ceilinged and no wider than a railway carriage, buried beneath centuries of dead weight in the foundations of an unremembered processing plant. Its air is clear and perfectly dry, held at a steady, cellar-like chill that smells of slate dust, graphite, and the sharp, alkaline tang of cold zinc.

There is no moisture here—not a slick of condensation on the iron, not a bead of sweat in the mortar seams. Without water, nothing rots. The floor is an apron of cast-iron plates that has been ground down by friction until every seam is flush, every bolt-head worn into a smooth, hemispherical nub, and the metal itself dished into shallow, polished hollows where feet or skids once passed. It has the rounded, final quality of a riverbed where no water has run for ten thousand years; its contours have forgotten their original sharp angles and settled into the easiest, most durable curves.

Through this sunken conduit, light falls from a continuous row of heavy, wire-caged bulkhead lamps mounted along the ceiling. They burn with a hard, white utility glow, clean and unblinking, illuminating a world composed entirely of metal, mineral dust, and living armor.

The place is nearly solid with movement.

The floor, the lower halves of the bulkhead walls, and the broad flanks of dormant reduction-gear housings are entirely carpeted in a surging, dry-bodied fauna. They are creatures of jointed slate and mica-colored plate: broad-backed sow-beetles the size of palms, pencil-thin millepedes with legs like clock-spring brass, and pale, flattened scutigera that flow over the iron with the frictionless glide of spilled mercury. They do not burrow or nest; they simply pack the floor two and three deep, scrambling over one another’s backs with a continuous, deafening clatter.

The sound is immense and rhythmic. It is not the soft hiss of a forest floor, but the harsh, dry roar of gravel shaken in a steel hopper—the collective clicking of millions of chitinous tarsi striking polished metal, amplified by the low ceiling and the resonance of the hollow iron floor plates. Underneath this biological rattle sits a single mechanical note: a great, slow-turning countershaft near the roof, driven by some unseen source deep in the masonry, rotating once every several seconds with a heavy, dry groan of ungreased brass bushings.

Because there are no plants and no liquid water, the ecosystem operates on an aggressive, circular chemistry of its own. The smaller arthropods graze on the microscopic dusting of carbon, rust flakes, and shed exuviae that rain from the swarm above them. The larger ones—segmented, blind predators with heavy, shearing mandibles—lumber through the press like ironclads, plucking up the smaller grazers without stopping, their mandibles cracking dry carapaces with the sharp, popping sound of breaking walnut shells. The dead do not decay; they are turned instantly to dry crumbs by hundreds of picking mouthparts, reduced to fine, gray powder that settles into the floor’s polished hollows, where it acts as a dry lubricant for the next generation's scuttle.

Scattered through this teeming furrow are the remaining bones of the machine: cast-iron valve wheels whose spokes are worn as smooth as river glass by the unending transit of legs; dead electrical switch-boxes with their doors hanging open, packed to the rim with resting, dormant broods that cling together in dry, vibrating knots; and thick lead conduits that snake along the skirting, their surfaces scored with thousands of minute scratching marks where claws have tested the soft metal.

Nothing here is soft. When the mass surges—driven by a sudden vibration in the countershaft or the heavy lunge of an eight-inch mantis-crawler through the middle ranks—the animals hit the iron walls with a hollow *thunk*, sliding and clambering back over one another in a frantic, desiccated cascade.

The cold keeps their metabolism precise and economical. They move with mechanical vigor, their movements sharp and without hesitation, sustained by an endless cycle of scraping, crushing, and shedding in the dry, bright chill. The room is sealed, complete, and perpetually awake—a hidden clockwork pocket where life has discarded green growth and dampness entirely, adapting itself instead to the permanence of iron, dust, and unending daylight.
