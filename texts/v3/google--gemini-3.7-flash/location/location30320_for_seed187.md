The chamber is the hollow interior of a single, colossal root-vault, sixty paces wide and vaulted like a Romanesque nave, sunk deep into a low-lying river terrace. 

Its perimeter is entirely composed of wood—not timber hewn and jointed by carpenters, but a continuous, ancient sleeve of compressed heartwood, dense as basalt and smoothed by centuries of subterranean pressure. Overhead, the arches of taproots knit together to form a soaring, ribbed ceiling. Gaps between the thickest spans let in shafts of cool, unmuddied daylight from the world above, illuminating a vast, mostly empty volume of cool air. 

At the bottom of this wooden vault, half-swallowed by the root-substrate, sits the iron carcass of a low-pressure industrial pump engine. It has been here long enough that the wood and metal have traded properties: iron oxalates have stained the living wood an inky, lustrous blue-black, while the exposed iron crank-arms and balance-beams are coated in an impenetrable skin of dry lichen and dried sap. 

The machine is not a ruin; it is running. Driven by the ceaseless weight of a cool underground head of river water channeled far beneath the root floor, the central flywheel—twelve feet across, cast from pitted pig iron—turns with an immutable, heavy cadence. Its long connecting rods rise and fall through slots in the root-platforms, hissing through packed-hemp glands. 

The sound in the vault is immense. It is not the chaotic clatter of a modern factory, but a steady, deafening acoustic presence: the rhythmic, subterranean *chump-thung* of the piston, the hollow boom of the chamber acting as a resonator, and the wet, rhythmic slap of water being lifted into a timber flume behind the wood walls. The noise is loud enough to vibrate in the teeth, yet it is so regular, so free of variation, that it feels less like an event and more like a permanent atmospheric condition, akin to standing under a waterfall.

Despite the machine’s operation and the total botanical envelopment, the space within is strikingly empty. There are no loose piles of debris, no understory brush, no clutter. The floor is a wide, flat basin of polished, interlocking roots, slick with a cool, intermittent film of condensation, sweeping out into wide, open expanses where nothing grows except a fine, velvet coat of slate-colored liverwort. The dampness is strictly localized: moisture beads upon the cold iron plates and drips in regular, measured cadences into narrow drainage fissures worn smooth into the floor-grain, leaving the wide, central floor clean and bare.

Life in the vault has adapted entirely to the machine's cadence. In the high, dry recesses where the root-arches cross, colonies of barn swallows and small brown bats roost in the hundreds, utterly indifferent to the iron thunder below. They launch themselves into the clear shafts of light, cutting across the hollow air to feed on the gnats and midges that hatch in the steady trickle of the pump's overflow troughs. Along the grease-soaked rocker bearings, blind, pale beetles graze on lubricating tallow and algae, moving in steady files along the bronze journals, undisturbed by the oscillating metal.

The temperature stays fixed at a cellar-cool chill, moderated by the massive living bulk of the wood around it and the steady flow of river-cold water through the iron pipes beneath. Nothing here is breaking down anymore; the destructive phase of rot and rust ended generations ago, having settled into an absolute, petrified equilibrium. The machinery does not overheat; the living vault does not overgrow the clearance of the crank; the air remains clean, washed by the steady mechanical displacement of draft through the ceiling vents. It is a closed, enduring hall where the boundary between engine and root has vanished into an unending, iron-throated stability.
