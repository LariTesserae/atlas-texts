The chamber is about the length of a drydock and half as wide, sunken thirty feet below the surrounding grade so that its upper rim is level with the roots of the world outside, while its floor is a saturated sump of black grease, fibrous peat, and warm seepage.

Nothing here is empty. Space is contested so thoroughly that volume itself feels like an upholstery of wet, layered tissues. The green is not a veneer over stone or steel; it is the load-bearing substance of the place. Tree-scale horsetails and thick, swollen lianas—pale jade and ribbed like firehoses—have grown directly through the spoke-wells of cast-iron flywheels, using the metal as arbors until the iron is less a machine than a rigid skeleton inside a muscle of pulp.

It is warm, holding the steady, humid heat of an engine room wrapped in compost. Water does not fall as rain, but the air produces it constantly through contact: warm mists rise from the floor, hit the cooler iron purlins and the underside of the broad-leaf canopy fifty feet up, and drop back down in fat, rhythmic beads. Every surface weeps. Rust runs down the machine casings in orange syrups, mixing with clear sap that smells faintly of boiled turnip and linseed.

Deep within the tangle, something heavy and mechanical is turning. The sound is loud and profoundly slow—a subterranean *thung-chunk* that repeats every forty or fifty seconds, sending a shudder through the peat mat underfoot. It comes from a horizontal steam engine whose cylinder is buried beneath six feet of dense, spongy liverwort. The brass linkage is still polished bright where it slides back and forth against the friction of tough, hair-like rootlets, shearing them away with each stroke and leaving behind a fresh smear of green juice. The exhaust does not vent into the sky; it is piped directly into a sunken gallery of hollow root-trunks that hiss softly, swelling and relaxing with the pulse of the piston.

There is no soil in the ordinary sense. The ground is a springy mattress of interlocking lignified stems, wet leaf-mold, and discarded iron cogs that have settled into the mat like stones into dough. Walking—if one were to move across it—requires stepping from one swollen knee-joint of root to another, avoiding the dark fissures where lukewarm, iron-black water wells up to the surface. In these pools, thick mats of duckweed are drawn slowly downward into submerged intake grates, pulled by the low, constant draft of some unseen hydraulic circuit.

Light arrives from above in broad, unobstructed shafts, ordinary and neutral, but by the time it reaches the mid-level it has been strained through six or seven tiers of peltate leaves, turning the air the color of river glass. It strikes the copper lubrication cups that still protrude from the moss, illuminating the slow drop of oil as it feeds into joints long since seized by calcified wood.

Animal life is nearly absent. There are no birds in the upper rafters, and the insects are reduced to minute, translucent springtails that coat the surface of the oily pools like dust. Without grazers or scavengers to break the plant matter down quickly, the dead leaves accumulate in wet, intact layers, tanning in the warm water until they form a dense, leathery slate.

The place operates under a patient, self-contained mechanics. The machinery provides the pressure, the slow heat, and the iron-rich water from its deep-driven artesian bores; the vegetation provides the packing, the gaskets, the structural tension, and the fuel. When an iron stay-rod corrodes through and snaps with a muffled report, the liana braided around it simply tightens, taking the strain into its own fibrous heartwood without dropping an inch. 

It has been like this long enough for the iron to learn the grain of the wood, and for the wood to take on the squared-off, channeled geometries of the iron. Nothing is rapidly changing, and nothing is decaying into nothingness; it simply cycles the same warm, iron-stained gallon of water through piston, leaf, and condensate plate, over and over, with the slow, deafening regularity of a clock buried in flesh.
