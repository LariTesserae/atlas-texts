The plateau sits thousands of meters above the low plains, but it has no peaks left to show for it. Epochs of wind and slow chemical weathering have planed the massif into an immense, undulating table of ironstone and chalk-grey granite—a peneplain so old its contours feel less like topography and more like the worn curve of an old tool handle. The horizon runs wide, open, and low, broken only by the gentle rise of smooth, whaleback ridges whose summits were ground down before the current lineages of life were named.

The air up here is surprisingly warm, held in a thermal trap by the massive thermal mass of the dark rock beneath. It carries a faint, constant dampness. Water does not fall in torrents; instead, it settles out of the mid-afternoon mist that creeps over the western lip of the plateau, pearling upon cold surfaces and collecting in thousands of shallow, polished basins etched into the stone. Every depression, some no larger than a palm, others the size of threshing floors, holds a shallow lens of clear, tepid water that neither drains nor rapidly evaporates.

Plant life has surrendered almost the entire territory. There are no trees, no shrubs, only a skin-tight tapestry of dry, orange crustose lichens and small, wiry tufts of grey sedge that wedge themselves into hair-thin fissures. 

Yet the plateau is densely crowded. Where vegetation has stepped back, animal life has filled every cubic foot of the lower air and every square yard of the stone. 

The expanse is covered in a vast, shifting carpet of living things. Millions of heavy, low-slung quadrupeds and shell-backed lung-crawlers occupy the rock, resting shoulder-to-flank, so tightly packed in the low swales that the ground itself is obscured. These are not herds on the move; they are permanent occupants of the stone, living out generation after generation in a slow, almost geologic patience. Their carapaces and thick, hide-covered backs are mottled like the granite, encrusted with the same orange lichens that cling to the boulders. They rarely run or fight. They shift by inches, grazing on the micro-films of moisture, mineral salts, and airborne spore-dust that coat the rocks at dawn.

Suspended in the quiet, warm air just above them are drifting clouds of soft-bodied creatures—translucent, bell-shaped floaters and long, filamented sky-worms that ride the faint convective thermals. They rise and fall across hours, brushing against one another with the dry, papery whisper of shed skin. 

Despite the sheer mass of breathing, digesting, multiplying life, the plateau is deeply quiet. There are no roars, no birdcalls, no territorial shrieks. The soundscape is an unbroken, low-frequency hum: the collective rhythm of millions of respiratory vents opening and closing, the faint rasp of chitin over polished shale, and the wet, rhythmic click of tongues licking water from shallow stone depressions. It sounds like a slow tide moving through dry reeds, heard from a great distance.

Scattered across the plain are traces of old, non-mechanical effort. Long, curving alignments of unmortared stone—never more than knee-high—snake across the ridges for miles, weathered down until they look like natural veins in the rock. Here and there, low circles of stacked shale form shallow corrals or dry basins, their origins lost to time. There are no tools here, no signs of fire or metal, only these simple, ancient stone arrangements that channel the movement of the smaller crawlers and direct the accumulation of dew. Some of the larger creatures rest their heavy chins upon these stones, using them as rub-posts until the rock has taken on an oily, glass-smooth sheen.

Light across the plateau is clear and steady, filtered only slightly by the pale, drifting tiers of floating fauna. In the late afternoon, as the mist begins to condense again on the flanks of the sleeping herds, the whole region takes on a soft, coppery glow. The living carpet and the ancient rock blend entirely: an ocean of slow blood and worn mineral, high above the world, gently breathing through the warm, quiet damp.
