The plateau does not look worn because it has not had time to wear. Its edges are clean, planar shears of unweathered basalt and high-density sintered silicates, meeting at angles that feel more like freshly cut timber or cleaved crystal than ancient mountain rock. This is a newborn continental terrace, lifted and stabilized by deep-mantle structural lattices that hum below human hearing. The scale is oceanic; looking north or west, the horizon drops away into cloud decks that generate their own lower-tier weather systems, while up here, at two thousand meters, the air is cold, thin, and continuously shedding water.

It is a wet cold, hovering just above freezing. Moisture condenses directly out of the pale, bright sky onto every flat surface. Water runs in thin, glassy sheets over the unpitted stone, spilling over millimeter-precise terrace edges into long, silent veils of fall that vanish into the mist below. There is no mud—the geology is too young for soil—only pools of distilled, clear runoff trapped in the wide, geometric depressions of the raw substrate.

Across these immense distances, human or post-human presence is arranged with sparse, absolute precision. These are not dense, tangled metropolises, but an urban framework on a planetary scale: slender transit spires sixty stories tall standing five kilometers apart, connected by suspended conduits that look like single strands of spidersilk against the sky. Foundation slabs the size of small townships sit embedded in the virgin rock, stepped and tiered, awaiting superstructures or simply serving as open, self-draining plazas. The engineering belongs to a physics that treats mass and inertia as soft variables; the pylons do not brace against the wind, they seem to quiet it, their surfaces absorbing kinetic energy and re-radiating it as faint, localized warmth.

Where that warmth bleeds into the cold stone, life has seized the gap with astonishing speed. Because the substrate is chemically fertile and constantly washed in mist, specialized pioneering vegetation has carpeted the drainage seams: pale blue-grey lichens, dense velvet-textured mosses that anchor without root systems, and low, fan-shaped alpine sedges that shudder in the draft. 

And moving through this vast, austere geometry is an immense density of fauna. 

They have arrived along the migration corridors opened by the newly elevated land. Vast herds of heavy-bodied, six-legged ungulates—broad-hoofed, shaggy with oil-rich slate-colored wool—move steadily across the open terraces, their hooves clattering softly like river stones on the unweathered ground. They graze on the rapid-blooming lichen fields, their breath rising in synchronized plumes that drift across the lower plazas. Above them, the sky is layered with bird life: broad-winged high-altitude skimmers riding the cold updrafts, diving between the structural pylons to catch the swarms of pale midges that breed in the condensation pools. Packs of low-slung, white-furred predators track the herds along the perimeter of the urban conduits, using the sharp-edged architectural steps as vantage points and windbreaks.

Despite the abundance of living things, the acoustic landscape is muted. The cold air, the vast open spaces, and the damp sheen over the stone swallow sound. The herds move with a hushed, collective rustle; the wind makes only a low, clean hiss across the unrounded architectural edges. Occasionally, an automated sub-surface stabilizer adjusts deep within the mountain root, sending a single, deep, resonant tone through the rock, like a struck bell under miles of water, but the animals do not startle. They graze through the foundations of the future city as if the impossible stone were simply another kind of ice, patient and enduring beneath the cold white daylight.
