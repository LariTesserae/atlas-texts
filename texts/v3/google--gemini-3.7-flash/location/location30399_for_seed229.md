The shelf sits at an altitude where the air is thin enough to hiss through teeth, yet it is thick with warm, dry friction. It is a hanging terrace—no wider than a city square, cupped inside the hollowed tooth of a massive dry-peak. Far below, the world is lost in a purple-grey heat haze, but here on the ledge, the atmosphere is an amber gloom, lit only by the diffuse glow of a sun smothered behind permanent strata of dry sulfurous dust.

There is no moisture here; not a drop of dew, not a slick of oil. The lushness is entirely dry, a roaring, anhydrous jungle of silica-ferns, calcified plumes, and fibrous, sulfur-rich bracken that feeds directly on the electrostatic charge of the rising wind. 

These plants do not wilt; they shatter or they hum. They stand well-spaced, rooted in deep fissures of ancient, smooth-sculpted granite. Between them, the floor of the terrace is bare, polished pavement stone, swept clean of earth by the updrafts. But where a plant takes hold, it explodes into baroque, voluminous density. A single *comb-tree* might rise twenty feet, its trunk a twisted braid of dry mineral tendons, fanning out into an umbrella of razor-thin mica plates that shiver constantly. Another species, like a mass of woven copper hair, spreads in soft, buoyant mounds the size of haystacks, vibrating so fast in the dry air that its edges blur.

The shelf is deafening. The wind does not merely blow; it shears upward along the vertical cliff-face, roaring like a furnace draft. As it strikes the silica foliage, the plants act as instruments: the mica-combs clatter like millions of dropped needles; the copper-hair mounds produce a deep, droning cello-note; and the hollow, bone-like pods of the *rattle-cane* knock together in wild, arrhythmic percussion.

Through this loud, dim forest, the fauna moves with frantic, jerking speed. They are creatures built of dry horn, chitin, and velvet-dusted scales. Skittering things no larger than a hand dart between the stands of mica-trees, their multiple jointed legs clicking on the smooth stone. They feed on the static charge and the fine, dry pollen that blows off the rattling plumes like smoke. Above, riding the violent updrafts that buffet the edge of the terrace, broad-winged gliders—flat, parchment-bodied things with four eyes set into dry carapaces—tumble and swoop. They do not flap; they pitch and roll violently in the turbulence, snatching at drifting flakes of shed cuticle and mid-air spores before being snatched upward into the dark amber sky.

Human presence here is minimal, manual, and defensive. Tucked into the deep undercut of the mountain wall are three low, dry-stone shelters, built of flat granite slabs wedged together without mortar. They are lashed down with heavy cords braided from the copper-hair plant, tied off to ironwood pegs driven deep into natural cracks in the rock. 

Outside one shelter, a primitive rack made of cured bone holds a set of scraping tools and heavy stone chisels. The inhabitants—if they are here, huddled inside away from the wind—harvest the dry flora for its mineral resonance. Bundles of razor-sharp mica leaves are stacked beneath stone weights, curing into stiff sheets used to deflect the perpetual abrasive grit. Near the cliff’s lip, anchored by stone deadweights, sits a crude wooden frame: a manual winch strung with braided sinew, used to lower flat gathering baskets into the turbulent updrafts to catch the falling molts of the cliff-gliders.

The whole ledge is in a state of hyperactive, mechanical turbulence. Nothing rests. A sudden thermal pulse rushes up the cliff, and instantly an entire grove of mica-combs flexes backward with a collective *crack*, shedding a cloud of shimmering, glass-hard dust. A flock of armored hopper-beasts bursts from the brush, bouncing twenty feet into the air on spring-loaded hind limbs, caught momentarily by the wind before slamming back down to grip the stone with clawed pads. 

The air is warm, smelling faintly of struck flint, crushed chalk, and ozone. In the dim, amber twilight of the high rock, life does not flow or seep; it clicks, vibrates, grinds, and regenerates, entirely indifferent to the absence of water, thriving on the sheer kinetic violence of the height.
