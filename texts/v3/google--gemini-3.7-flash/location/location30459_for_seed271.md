The floor of the sandstone alcove sits three fathoms below the surrounding plateau, reached by a wide, ramp-like slump of weathered red scree that has not shifted in centuries. 

The space has the scale of an unroofed chapel—some forty paces across, enclosed on three sides by curved walls of dense, fine-grained arenite. These walls lean outward slightly as they rise, shielding the floor from direct sunlight while admitting a uniform, tea-colored ambient light reflected down from the higher desert rim. It is permanently warm down here. The stone absorbs the high plateau heat and radiates it inward with steady, unyielding constancy, keeping the pocket of air insulated from wind, chill, and season.

Moisture exists here not as flowing water, but as an ancient, steady breath. Along the deepest curvature of the rear wall, the stone is dark with saturated capillary water that seeps out from hidden bedding planes. It does not run in trickles; it forms flat, glass-like sheets across the rock face, gathering at the base into three shallow, shallow-lipped depressions no deeper than a thumb’s knuckle. In these basins, the water is utterly still, holding a mirror to the dun-colored stone above. The air carries the mineral, slightly alkaline smell of wet clay and warm iron.

Vegetation is disciplined, sparse, and slow. In the seams where the wall meets the floor, thin crusts of slate-gray lichen give way to scattered rosettes of a low, leathery liverwort. Here and there, rooted in a pinch of windblown dust, stands a single, stiff-stemmed rush, its green faded to the color of dry sage. There are wide yards of smooth, sand-dusted floor between each plant. Nothing grows in tangled masses; each living thing has claimed its precise margin of moisture and held it without expanding or retreating for decades.

Against the western curve of the wall lie the only marks of human presence: four low, squared blocks of pale limestone, dragged here from some different stratum long ago. They are not part of an intact structure, but rather the base of a simple bench or a retaining curb meant to catch seepage. Their tool marks have been completely erased by centuries of air and condensation, their edges rounded down until they look almost native to the hollow. Fine red silt has settled in an even collar around their footings. There are no dropped tools, no broken pottery, no soot on the ceiling. The stones simply sit where they were laid, settled into the floor’s permanent grade.

No birds drop below the lip of the canyon. No insects disturb the skin of the shallow pools. The acoustic space is deadened by the soft, porous nature of the sandstone; a dropped pebble would make a dry, contained *clack* that dies instantly against the walls. The only continuous sound is the faint, rhythmic tick of a single drop falling every few minutes from an overhanging lip onto dry sand—a sound so regular and infrequent that it functions as a form of silence.

The landscape is completely at rest. The processes of erosion that carved the hollow have reached a state of near-frictionless equilibrium: the rock sheds only microscopic grains of sand, the water arrives only as fast as the still air can accept its humidity, and the warmth holds the chamber in an unvarying, timeless suspension.
