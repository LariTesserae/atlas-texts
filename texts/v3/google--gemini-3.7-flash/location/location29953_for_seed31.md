High on the fracture-plains, where the mountain crust has sheared upward into sharp, unsoftened edges, the air is warm and dense with moisture. It is a lofty shelf of the world, pushed miles above the lowlands, yet sheltered enough by towering, jagged spires that the temperature remains mild and humid, like a vast green conservatory open to the sky.

The geology here is young. The rock faces are dark, splintered basalt and pale, clean-cleaved granite, showing none of the rounded softness of ancient weather. Their corners are crisp; their faults look as though they snapped open only yesterday. Yet, across every surface, water moves. It does not fall in violent storms, but exists as a perpetual state of condensation and seepage. Veins of water bleed continuously from hairline fractures in the stone, sheeting over flat vertical planes in thin, glassy coats, dripping from overhangs into wide, crystal-clear catchments below.

Spanning across several square miles of this high basin is a settlement of stone—vast in footprint, though generous and empty in its spacing. 

The construction belongs to an organized, urban imagination, but one realized entirely with the simplest tools. Massive blocks of basalt, split along their natural planes with wooden wedges and water, have been dressed by hand and stacked without mortar. There are broad, paved thoroughfares forty paces wide, stepped terraces ascending the raw cliffs, and low, flat-roofed stone halls with heavy lintels. The layout is deliberate, civic, and vast, yet there is tremendous openness between structures. A paved plaza might stretch for two hundred yards, interrupted only by a shallow reflecting channel or a single, meticulously leveled monolith, before meeting the next terrace.

Water dictates every line of the masonry. Hand-carved runnels and stone troughs skirt the foundations of every building, catching the endless mountain weeping and guiding it through a network of open stone aqueducts. The water runs silent and clear over slate-lined gutters, spilling from level to level through shallow, stepped basins that keep the flow from ever gathering violent speed. 

Vegetation grows with lush, warm vigor, but it remains orderly, held in place by the rock and the stone floors. Broad-leaved highland ferns, slick liverworts, and thick, brilliant green mosses choke the seams between the megaliths. Where the open stone terraces end, the ground rises into dense stands of pale-barked, broad-crowned trees that drip constantly from their leaf-tips onto a carpet of wild ginger and mountain reeds. The green is deep and luminous, illuminated by a bright, even daylight that filters through the high, stationary mist.

Despite the urban expanse, the place is profoundly empty of movement. There are no birds wheeling above the sharp peaks; no lizards bask on the warm, wet flagstones; no insects hum among the ferns. The ecosystem is solely botanical and hydrological. 

Because of this absence, the soundscape is reduced to a single, delicate texture: the quiet, polyphonic murmur of dripping water. A drop falling from a slate eave into a cistern; the soft, continuous hush of a thin sheet of water sliding over a vertical granite face; the gentle lapping where an aqueduct meets a courtyard pool. 

Nothing erodes quickly here, and nothing decays. The warm water does not scour the rock; it only washes it clean. The place exists in an enduring, quiet equilibrium—a monumental high-altitude city carved from fresh stone, perpetually washed by warm seeps, suspended in bright mist and absolute stillness.
