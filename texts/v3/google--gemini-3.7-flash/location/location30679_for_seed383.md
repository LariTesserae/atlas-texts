The ceiling of the great hollow does not know darkness. Hundreds of feet overhead, the bedrock is split by wide, continuous seams of pale, milk-white selenite and cold phosphorus that cast an unblinking, surgical glare across the sunken world below. It is the light of a midday winter sky trapped under a billion tons of shale: bright enough to cast hard-edged shadows, yet utterly drained of heat. The air is cool, carrying the dense, mineral freshness of a cellar that has stayed open to the draft of deep vents for ten thousand years.

Intermittent sheets of fine condensation weep from the vault above, drifting down not as rain, but as a silent, suspended fog that settles on every exposed flat. It keeps the stone damp to the touch, silvering the immense expanse of the valley floor with a slick, mica-like sheen.

Despite the subterranean depth, the space opens out for leagues. The ground is an ancient, flattened floor of sedimentary shelf, eroded so completely over geological epochs that its original topography has surrendered to vast, gently tilting flagstone plains. Upon this ancient foundation sits a city, vast and sparse, stretching toward the pale curvature where the cavern walls meet the floor. 

It is an urban landscape of staggering proportions, built entirely without metal, mortar, or machinery. Miles of wide, dead-straight avenues are flanked by low, stepped megaliths and tiered stone compounds constructed of dry-stacked, water-worn basalt and dense gypsum. Every block has been shaped by hand-flaking and abrasive sand; every lintel was dragged on bone-greased rollers and set by the simple physics of lever and counterweight. Because the stone has rested here for so long beneath the weeping ceiling, the architecture itself has aged into the landscape. The corners of the monumental staircases are rounded like river stones; the broad plazas have been rubbed silky and smooth by millions of barefoot crossings and the slow, abrasive lick of moisture-laden winds.

Yet between the grand civic complexes and the tiered residential lattices, there is enormous emptiness. The city does not bunch together; it breathes across open, chalky flats where no buildings stand for hundreds of yards, only low guide-walls and solitary stone cairns guiding the eye from one quiet district to the next. There is no green anywhere—not a blade of moss, not a weed in the joints of the flagstones. The only living things that break the stone are pale, calcified crusts and dry, branching mineral formations that mimic the skeletons of coral.

What should be a petrified graveyard of a civilization is instead gripped by a frantic, boiling dynamism. The city is violently alive, yet it unfolds in total, smothering silence.

The limestone dust and the perpetual veil of moisture act as an acoustic sponge; sound dies within inches of its birth. Thousands of creatures—both the primitive builders and their domesticated beasts—swarm through the avenues in a state of ceaseless, hyperactive kinetic labor. Long lines of gaunt, pale-skinned figures in beaten leather harness dismantle a five-story terrace block by block, passing heavy chunks of shale down human chains with frantic, rhythmic speed, while half a mile away, another crowd is furiously erecting a new amphitheater from the same material. They work with the manic, coordinated urgency of an upturned anthill, constantly remodeling the geography of their stone districts before the moisture can fuse the blocks together.

Beneath their feet, herds of blind, many-legged pack-creatures, their hide like wet slate, surge through the sunken channels, moving dry grain and soft salt cakes without the clatter of a single hoof. In the open plazas, immense flocks of pale, moth-winged gliders drop from the ceiling, catch the cool rising updrafts from the thermal vents, circle three times in tight, frantic spirals, and settle onto the flat roofs, their wings beating in a silent frenzy of displaced air. 

Walls rise, crumble, and are carted away between the hours of a single light-cycle. The layout of entire neighborhoods shifts over the course of a few days, an endless cycle of manual deconstruction and reassembly performed under the glare of the weeping ceiling. 

From the high terraces of the older, untouched quarters, the view across the valley is one of dizzying motion without a single roar: a sprawling, bright, stone-and-water metropolis where millions of hands silently tear down and rebuild an ancient world, their breath rising in cool, faint wisps toward the light that never dims.
