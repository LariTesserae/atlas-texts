The floor has no soil and never had any. It is an endless expanse of hot-rolled zinc plate and stamped lead-alloy grating, stretching under its own manufactured sky for thousands of kilometers in every direction. The seams between the plates are still crisp, the machine-stamped serial numbers sharp enough to cut a thumb, unrounded by centuries of grit, because this world was laid down complete and immediately locked into an absolute, unvarying thermodynamic balance.

It is warm here—a heavy, radiant 32 degrees Celsius that rises steadily from the vast fluid manifolds beneath the metal deck. Because the beneath-floor pipes are hotter than the air, and the air is saturated with moisture, the entire continent exists in a state of perpetual, quiet weeping. Clear, lukewarm water condenses on every overhead beam, every ribbed conduit, every vertical strut of galvanized steel. It gathers into fat, trembling beads that drop without rhythm into the shallow swales and drainage runnels engineered into the floor plates. There are no storms, no sudden squalls, no dry hours. The air simply exhales water against the cold-rolled surfaces, an uninterrupted distillation that keeps every square foot of metal glistening and slick.

There is not a single leaf, spore-pod, or blade of grass across the entire continental shelf. Plant life does not exist here, not even as an evolutionary memory. Instead, the primary chemical synthesis is non-biological: slow galvanic reactions between the weeping copper flanges, the zinc decking, and the warm, mineralized condensate generate a rich, thin smear of synthetic lipid-grease and colloidal sulfur across every surface. 

And upon this grease feeds an ocean of animals.

The fauna does not merely inhabit the machinery; it blankets it like living upholstery. Trillions of soft, blind, pale things carpet the plates. There are segmented organisms the size of a finger, with translucent, pale-ivory carapaces and hundreds of tiny brass-tipped cilia that rasp rhythmically at the condensed grease on the steel. There are broad, flat, disc-shaped grazers that adhere to the vertical bulkheads, pulsing with a slow, hydraulic peristalsis as they filter the warm moisture rolling down the metal. In the shallow, slow-moving gutters, ribbons of fleshy, eyeless eels—white as parsnips, dense as braided rope—undulate over one another in tangled mats yards deep, their gills sifting the warm runoff for chemical flakes. 

They are everywhere, packed shoulder-to-shoulder, tier upon tier, filling every conduit, occupying every cable tray, clustering around the warm exhaust louvers of inactive transformers. They do not fight; they do not flee. Their density is so high that one cannot step without resting a boot on five or six living backs, yet they yield like wet dough, shifting slightly and closing the gap behind with indifferent, steady slowness.

Above this crawling, glistening carpet hangs the infrastructure of illumination: an infinite grid of sealed phosphor tubes suspended sixty meters up from a web of iron trusses. The light is steady, flat, and white-gold, casting sharp-edged shadows through the rising steam. It does not flicker. There is no day and no night; the lamps draw continuous power from the deep thermo-siphons and burn without dimming, baking the upper air and feeding the steady cycle of evaporation and downpour.

Despite the colossal industrial scale and the staggering volume of life, the world is remarkably quiet. The machinery does not hammer or grind; it was balanced at manufacture to turn at a constant, friction-free idle. A low, subterranean vibration—felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard—runs through the decking, humming at a single, unchanging C-sharp. Over this deep note lies only the soft, endless music of water: the *clink* of falling drops striking sheet metal, the muffled trickling through perforated floor plates, and the wet, squelching rustle of a billion soft bellies sliding across wet galvanized iron.

Nothing decays, and nothing builds. When an organism reaches the end of its life, it does not rot in the open air; the warm runoff and the rasping mouths of its kin dissolve it back into the fluid stream within minutes, returning its carbon and minerals to the grease-slick that coats the floor. The metal does not rust; it was alloyed to resist the warm wash forever. 

It is a continent-sized room without walls: a factory floor that manufactures only its own continuation, bathed in warm, bright mist, where billions of identical, perfect machine edges are endlessly bathed in clear water and licked clean by a vast, silent biomass that has never seen the sun.
