The basin is no wider than four hundred paces across, a sudden, sheer-sided sink in the low country where the bedrock has only recently torn open and dropped. Its rim is still sharp enough to cut leather—freshly sheared shale and iron-banded siltstone, their fracture planes dark, clean, and unsoftened by time. 

Down within this tight amphitheater, there is almost no empty space. A settlement has jammed itself into the fracture, occupying every foot of the drop from the gravelly floor to within a few yards of the upper lip. It is not built over the rock so much as wedged between it: thousands of low-roofed, cellular rooms stacked four, five, six tiers high, constructed of raw, hand-split slate slabs bedded in thick, blue-grey clay. The alleys are barely shoulder-wide, running as steep stairways, notched timber ramps, and hollowed-out stone chutes that double as drainage channels. There are no plazas or courtyards; where a roof ends, the threshold of another dwelling begins, each structure stepped back just enough to allow access to the lintel above.

Water is the primary engine of the place. It does not fall as rain, but weeps continuously from the newly exposed strata of the basin walls. Cold, mineral-heavy seepage glazes every exposed face, trickling through the joints of the dry-stacked stone. In the cool air—which hovers at a perpetual, breath-misting chill—this water is constantly depositing pale crusts of lime and silica. The masonry is slowly being petrified into a single, contiguous mass; hand-placed flags from only a few seasons ago are already welded shut by thick, curdled rinds of calcite. 

To manage this, the inhabitants have turned primitive hydraulic craft into a total architecture. Hollowed logs split with flint wedges, clay-daubed troughs, and braided bark gutters crisscross the narrow gaps between dwellings like a dense web of rigging. Every trickle is collected, diverted, and put to work: driving small, undershot balance-scoops that lift bucketfuls of wash-water from tier to tier, or flushing the narrow slop-drains that run beneath the floor slabs. The sound of this motion is constant but muted—an intricate, low-toned percussion of soft splashes, hollow thuds of wooden levers resetting, and the continuous hiss of water sheeting over slick stone.

Plant life is minimal, unable to find purchase on the young rock or choked out by the heavy mineral salts. What little grows is pale and leathery: crusts of orange-brown lichens that trace the dry overhangs, and rubbery, ink-black liverworts that carpet the drip-lines beneath the gutters. In the deeper drainage sumps, long mats of filamentous, pale grey algae sway in the currents like submerged hair, periodically harvested with antler rakes to be dried into brittle, fibrous matting for doorway curtains.

Fauna is similarly sparse. Occasionally, a pale, blind salamander is dislodged from a deep weeping crack in the rock face, washed down into the residential troughs where it scrambles clumsily for a dark crevice before the cold air slows it to stillness. Small, hard-shelled water-beetles drift in the cisterns, grazing on the mineral crusts, but there are no birds nesting along the sharp upper rim; the stone offers no deep weathering pits for nests, and the air within the basin remains dense with cool moisture and the smell of wet silt.

Daylight arrives without drama, filtered evenly through a flat, silver ceiling of low cloud. It catches the wet edges of the slate roofs, turning the crowded village into a stepped mosaic of dull, glinting blades. Down in the narrowest seams, where three or four buildings lean inward to meet the natural wall, the light is reduced to a soft, aqueous twilight, illuminated only by the faint bounce of light off the wet stones.

Movement through the place is quiet, deliberate, and intensely physical. Because iron is scarce and tools are limited to hard river-cobbles, bone chisels, and fire-hardened oak, every surface retains the rough, immediate marks of human labor: the scalloped bite of a stone pick on a door-jamb, the uneven dressing of steps worn slippery by bare, wet feet. There is a sense of rapid, precarious equilibrium—a whole town hurriedly pinning itself into the fresh wound of the earth, racing to guide the water before the water encases them entirely in stone.
