The basin stretches for leagues across a shallow continental depression, flat and wide beneath an unblinking, sunlit sky. It is completely dry. There is no moisture in the soil, no vapor in the air, not a single droplet of dew clinging to the broad, glass-hard surfaces of the growth that fills it. The atmosphere is warm—a dry, baking heat that smells of ozone, cleaved quartz, and sun-cured ceramic—yet the landscape does not look like a desert. 

It looks like a jungle made of solid-state physics.

The ground itself is a young, pristine floor of dark, stepped terraces. It has not been rounded by water or ground down by silt; every edge is a fresh, molecularly perfect shear, like freshly cleaved crystal or newly solidified basaltic mirror. There is no loose sand, no dust. Across this vast floor, an immense, dense forest has colonized the stone, rising in tiered, interlocking canopies that reach thirty or forty feet high.

These plants are lush and crowded, growing so closely together that their crowns weave into a continuous, faceted ceiling. Yet they are entirely anhydrous. Their stalks are not wood, but dense, high-dielectric lattices—semi-translucent columns of vitreous silicate, spun-boron fibers, and layered graphene membranes that drink the brilliant, white-gold sunlight. Their foliage takes the form of razor-thin photovoltaic fronds, broad iridescent plates of doped silicon, and deep emerald ribbons that twist into impossible geometric spirals. Where a branch divides, it does not fork naturally; it splits along crystalline glide planes, multiplying into fractal arrays that seem to violate local volume, packing more surface area into a cubic meter than three-dimensional space should comfortably allow.

Looking into the understory is disorienting. Space within the thickets does not recede along normal lines of perspective. Because the dense, living metamaterials bend light through engineered negative refractive indices, distant clearings appear right before your eyes, suspended like miniature mirages inside the transparent trunks, while objects three feet away seem to float in a deep, sapphire-tinted distance. Shadows do not always follow the angle of the sun; beneath the denser canopies, the light is sorted, rectified, and cast downward in crisp, non-divergent square beams, leaving patches of absolute, geometric darkness right beside areas of brilliant, focused radiance.

Nothing is decaying. Without water, bacteria, or atmospheric moisture, nothing rots. Fallen fronds—shards of sapphire-colored glass and structural metal—lie on the black stone floor where they dropped, as sharp and polished as the day they severed, their internal circuitry slowly dissipating residual quantum charge into the substrate.

The forest is nearly silent. There is no wind to stir it—or rather, the air moves, but the vegetation is too stiff, too structurally rigid to sway. The only sound is a very faint, low-frequency electromagnetic hum, a dry harmonic vibration produced as millions of tons of synthetic chloroplasts convert solar radiation directly into localized, closed-loop magnetic fields. Occasionally, the sudden *tink* of thermal expansion rings out, sharp and pure, echoing across the terraces like a struck tuning fork, before the stillness closes in again.

Life here does not wander. Fauna is extraordinarily sparse, and what exists barely resembles animals. High in the faceted canopy, small, polygonal nodes—scintillating prisms of cold matter—rest motionless on the junctions of the fronds. They do not eat; they are inductive parasites, leeching millivolts from the trees’ power grids, shifting their position only once every few days by sudden, instantaneous topological translations, disappearing from one branch and reappearing on another without crossing the space between. 

Deeper in the basin, where the floor dips lower, the vegetation gives way to vast open glades of pure, unblemished crystal pavement. Here, reality seems to hold its breath. The light remains constant, fixed at an eternal, high-noon intensity. The temperature never fluctuates; the heat radiating from the dark floor perfectly balances the radiative losses to the sky, maintaining a permanent, locked equilibrium. There are no seasons, no weather, no dawn or dusk that brings change.

It is an ecology that has bypassed the messy, fluid cycles of organic matter entirely. The forest stands as a permanent, living computer of unimaginable scale, growing not by cellular division, but by the slow, flawless deposition of atomic layers straight out of the hot, dry air—an immaculate, wild machine flourishing in utter silence.
