There is no ground, only the craft of tension. 

At this altitude, where the atmosphere ought to be thin and cold, it is instead warm, sluggish, and drenched in a perpetually shedding vapor. What holds this space together is an expanse of woven timber and braided bines, engineered so long ago and on such a scale that the architecture has become its own geography. Everything is built; there is no unworked earth, no bare stone, no spontaneous mountain. Yet everything was made with the most rudimentary of means—wedged split-wood, miles of hand-plaited reed-gut, bone trunnels, and the deliberate grafting of young, pliable boughs coaxed across yawning gulfs of air.

The architecture does not fill the space; it encloses nothing and leaves almost everything empty. The scale is oceanic, but the matter is sparse. Miles of hollow, gray-green gloom separate one load-bearing arch from the next. A traveler standing on a path of lashed bark looks out not at a landscape of solid masses, but at lonely, colossal rib-lines of live cedar and ironwood that vault into the haze, arcing through cubic leagues of warm, damp void before anchoring into sister-struts that vanish beyond the curve of the eye.

Water does not behave as a surface feature here. It is an ambient property of the wood. The timbers sweat a cool, viscous lymph that gathers into quivering beads along the underbellies of the bridges, slowly crawling along the grain rather than dropping. Great weeping swellings—calluses where three or four ancient trusses were spliced with hemp-line centuries ago—act as hanging reservoirs. They do not splash; they seep into vast aprons of soft, pale liverwort that coat the spans, keeping everything saturated, slick, and heavy. The air smells of steeped tea, tannin, wet wool, and the faintly sweet rot of living wood that has been kept continuously moist for generations without ever dying.

Light exists only as a soft, sourceless amber-gray twilight. It filters down through countless tiers of open lattice overhead, weakened by miles of clinging mist, casting no distinct shadows. In this half-light, spatial logic unspools. Distances refuse to reconcile with effort: a woven crossing that appears narrow enough to span in forty strides requires an afternoon of steady walking, its weave opening into wider and wider gaps through which only mist and distant, vertical drops can be seen. In places, the bines have grafted themselves into continuous, paradoxical loops—trunks that bend downward, form a self-supporting circle in the air, and rejoin their own crowns, drawing sap through an unbroken circuit of bark without any taproot ever reaching down to an earth that does not exist.

The silence is dense, smothered by the universal upholstery of saturated moss and water-logged fiber. The only sounds are minute and intimate: the faint *tick-tick* of wood expanding as warm drafts drift through the bays, the slow tearing sound of a vine shifting its weight under a layer of slime, and the soft, heavy drop of a condensation bead hitting a broad leaf miles below.

Despite the near-total emptiness of the volume, the structures are alive with a quiet, abundant fauna that has adapted to life on suspension. Pale, soft-padded climbers—creatures with six jointed limbs tipped with curved horn—move along the undersides of the great struts with the slow, deliberate rhythm of tides. Their fur is perpetually wet, matted with tiny green algae. In the gulfs between the timbers, where there is nothing but hundreds of yards of empty air, long-bodied organisms with membranous rib-fins drift on the rising thermals, neither birds nor fish, moving without making a sound, browsing on the airborne spores that drift up from the lower splices.

There is a rhythm to the decay and the holding. Nothing rusts, for there is no iron; nothing crumbles, for there is no masonry. Instead, the world settles in long, creaking sighs as old cordage frays into velvet moss, and new shoots, trained by hands that left no other record, swell to take the load. It is a fabricated wilderness, vast and nearly vacant, breathing quietly at the top of the sky.
