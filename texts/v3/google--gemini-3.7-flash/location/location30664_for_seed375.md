The precinct lies in a shallow, cool depression, bounded on all sides by sheer scarps of unweathered slate that drop straight down into a floor of compressed talc and dry salt-crust. It is small—scarcely wider than four or five city blocks—yet the feeling is of immense, vacated volume. The air does not carry moisture; it is so completely devoid of water that the skin of the eye tightens instantly, and dust does not stick to anything, falling away in dry, microscopic avalanches that make no sound whatsoever.

Despite the total absence of tools, iron, or timber, the space is undeniably a city. It rises in complex, multi-tiered geometries: narrow tenements with stepped rooflines, arched causeways leaping across empty avenues, and colonnaded plazas, all of it constructed from a pale, chalky bone-mineral and brittle grey silicate. There are no mortar lines or tool marks. Instead, the masonry appears extruded, as if a million blind, rhythmic instincts had secreted the settlement overnight from mineral vapor and lime dust, leaving the edges needle-sharp, unrounded by rain or wind.

It is perpetually dim. The light comes not from a sky, but from a faint, cold phosphorescence baked into the upper ceilings of the slate rim—a dull, violet-grey ambient wash that casts long, razor-thin shadows across the white alleys.

Though the streets are vacant, the district is in constant, feverish motion. It is an architecture that lives at the pace of an organism, yet operates without a single audible cue. From the lintels of the highest towers, gossamer-thin struts of dry calcium are continuously spinning outward, bridging alleys in silence, hardening into rigid walkways within minutes, then snapping under their own structural tensions to collapse soundlessly into the powdery troughs below. Entire facades creep sideways on slick, dry mineral faults, adjusting their exposure to the faint light like leaves on an arbor. 

Between these shifting colonnades, vegetation takes hold in sparse, brittle colonies. These are not green plants; they have no sap, no juice, no pulp. They are dry as burned parchment, taking the form of tiered, paper-thin plates of mica-like foliage that vibrate with microscopic shivering to gather static charge from the moving air. In the hollows of the plazas, low hedges of needle-fine graphite quills expand and contract in synchronized, pulsing breaths, sifting the arid atmosphere for floating mineral flakes.

The sound is not merely low; it is entirely suppressed. The dry, porous chalk of the ground and the countless hollow chambers within the buildings act as an acoustic void, absorbing every collision, fracture, and shearing movement before the vibration can travel an inch. When a three-story spire of calcified lace shears off and hits the boulevard, it disintegrates into a billow of white powder with the absolute quiet of falling snow.

Life here is scarce, solitary, and soundless. Occasionally, across an elevated viaduct, a pale, many-jointed scavenger with legs like dry reeds will drift between two towers. It moves with a strange, gliding momentum, its broad, velvet-padded pads distributing its weight across the fresh, fragile crust without leaving a depression or breaking a single edge. It pauses at the corner of an extruded balcony, extends a feather-like appendage to sweep up a drift of newly fallen lime, and vanishes into the dark throat of an unlit stairwell.

The city does not decay; it merely replaces itself faster than it can erode. A street mapped in the dim violet dawn will have closed its gates, grown three new vaulted tiers, and sunken four feet into the chalk basin by the time the dimness settles back to black, leaving only sharp, pristine facets of newly grown stone waiting in the freezing, waterless stillness.
