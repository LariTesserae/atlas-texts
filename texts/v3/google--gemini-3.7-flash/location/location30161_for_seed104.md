The world does not gather its oceans in the basins; it holds them at the summit. 

Miles above the dry, airless lowlands, draped over a continental plateau of worn granite pedestals, lies the Pelagic Shelf: an immense, warm, roofless body of sea that refuses to fall. The water does not tumble off the escarpments into the void below; surface tension and a warped, localized gravity pin the vast, buoyant mass to the highest spines of the planet. Here, the altitude is extreme, yet the pressure within the water is heavy, thick, and womb-warm, somewhere near the temperature of fresh blood.

The light is the bronze-green of deep marsh twilight. Sun filters down from a surface that ripples just fifty fathoms above, broken and diffused by endless drifting canopies of vegetation, turning the water into an amber broth. 

Below the surface, the terrain is not jagged or young. The mountains have been submerged in this high, slow bath for billions of years, sanded down by thermal churn until they are nothing but colossal, rounded humps—smooth as river pebbles the size of nations. There are no cliffs or sharp ravines, only immense, gently curving saddles of polished stone, coated in a thick, velvety fleece of olive-brown bryozoans and ancient, calcified mats of red algae that have grown meters thick, generation over generation, without ever being disturbed by a storm.

Lushness here is suspended in three dimensions. Immense, vascular kelp-trees—neither true seaweeds nor terrestrial trees, but something intermediate and ancient—root into the granite domes. Their trunks are as thick as towers, composed of braided, fibrous water-vessels that pulse rhythmically, drawing dissolved minerals from the bedrock and venting tiny, pearlescent pearls of oxygen into the current. Their crowns do not spread flat; they erupt into massive, tangled spheres of translucent fronds, rust-colored and fleshy, forming a continuous, layered canopy that stretches for thousands of leagues across the submerged highlands.

Between these pillars of vegetable iron, the water is dense with smaller presences. Ribbons of golden water-moss drift in braided rivers of their own making. Vast, porous sponges the shape of hollow amphorae sit anchored to the saddles, filtering the ambient warmth and glowing with a faint, dying ember of yellow bioluminescence. 

The physics of the place are slightly undone. The currents do not follow wind; they are driven by the slow, respiration-like expansion and contraction of the kelp-forests and deep geothermal vents that pepper the high saddles. In places, the water shears into distinct, non-mixing layers: you can drift through a stratum of bitter, salt-dense amber, drop through a paper-thin threshold, and find yourself in a river of crystal-clear, sweet warm water that flows *upward*, climbing a mountain slope like a liquid snake before dispersing into the ceiling above. Trapped air does not always rise; in certain deep hollows, bubbles of pale atmospheric gas sit pinned under stone overhangs, compressed into glassy, immobile globes that have rested there since the world was molten.

Animals are an afterthought here. Once in a great span of leagues, something will drift through the gloom—a pale, translucent thing without eyes, long as an oar, moving with the sluggish, undeliberate ripple of a ribbon dropped in oil. There are no shoals of fish, no rapid predators. The life is overwhelmingly vegetative, fungal, and elemental, busy with the slow chemistry of growth and dissolution.

Every few miles, the seamless organic blanket is broken by a shape too deliberate to be geology. Worn lintels of basalt, cyclopean stairs that lead from one bare granite swell down into a choked forest of bladder-wrack, and singular, towering monoliths rounded by eons of friction into soft, phallic stubs. They are not ruins of a city—they are too sparse, too isolated. They feel instead like navigational markers or anchoring posts driven into the world before the water was summoned to the summits, their inscriptions long ago eaten away by the lick of the warm current, their surfaces now home to small, branching coral-flowers that close at the touch of a shadow.

It is extraordinarily quiet. The density of the liquid deadens all sharp impulse. There is only the deep, sub-audible thrum of the Kelp-trees’ vascular pumping—a pulse felt in the chest rather than heard—and the occasional faint, vitreous *clink* of a mineral crust snapping under the slow expansion of a root. 

The Shelf is never still, but its motion is planetary. Vast rafts of floating turf, miles across, break free from one summit and drift across the submerged plateaus, trailing root-tendrils like the hair of drowned titans, slowly catching on another dome days or decades later, weaving the high ocean together in a perpetual, silent loom.
