The basin is roughly the size of a drydock or a sunken rail yard, bordered on all four sides by sheer retaining walls of dressed granite and bolted zinc plates. At its floor, fifty feet down from the surrounding grade, the air is consistently cool and thick with an unvarying, vaporous chill. It does not rain here, but cold condensation forms continuously upon the cold surfaces: beads of water collect on the underside of cast-iron gantries, roll down copper grounding straps, and drip onto the ballast below with a steady, metronomic cadence that never dries out and never swells into a pool.

There are no weeds between the ties, no moss upon the limestone grout, not even the thinnest film of green slime in the drainage gutters. Life here has no chlorophyll, no leaf, no spore. The landscape is strictly mineral and mechanical, weathered into a long-settled matte gray where the iron has already rusted as deeply as it ever will, and the stone has already shed its loose grit centuries ago.

Across this floor sit three immense, squat industrial converters—hulk-like assemblies of cast flywheels, open gear-trains, and ceramic-collared conduits. They stand widely separated, leaving wide, gravel-paved voids between them. Though there is no human operator, these engines are entirely alive in their rotation. They do not accelerate or slow down; their thick leather belts turn with a relentless, heavy slap, their counterweights lifting and falling in a rhythmic, shuddering thrum that vibrates directly through the soles of one’s boots. 

Yet the machines are not the dominant presence in the yard; they are merely the trellis for something else.

The basin is choked with fauna. It is not an infestation that comes and goes, but a permanent, crushing saturation of animal mass. They are blind, segmented things—somewhere between horseshoe crabs, lungfish, and lead-skinned beetles—each the size of an anvil, layered three and four deep over every available horizontal surface. They cover the ties, crowd the flanks of the converters, and heap against the base of the zinc walls. Their carapaces are the dark, dull gray of wet slate, lubricated by the thin film of condensed water that coats the whole trench. 

Their sound is deafening. Beneath the low, gut-churning rumble of the industrial engines, there is the dry, abrasive roar of millions of chitinous plates rubbing together, accompanied by the wet, rhythmic coughing of their respiration. They draw their sustenance directly from the low-frequency electrical charge leaking from the converter coils and the mineralized tallow weeping from the mechanical journals. They do not fight, they do not migrate, and they do not starve. If one is crushed beneath the slow descent of a counterweight, its shell crumbles without drama, and the others simply crawl over the dry gray paste within minutes, filling the gap instantly.

The light overhead is flat, diffused, and shadowless, like an eternal midday under a canopy of uniform white linen. It catches the slick arcs of the creatures’ backs, giving the floor of the basin the appearance of an agitated, boiling sea of dark metal. Overhead, thick bundles of suspended telegraphy wire sag from iron lattice towers, entirely coated with roosting flocks of small, wingless vermin that cling with triple-jointed claws, their pale throats pulsating in time with the converter dynamos.

Nothing develops here; nothing deteriorates further. The zinc does not corrode past its dull oxide coat; the grease remains fresh yet unreplenished; the millions of shelled bodies maintain an exact, frantic equilibrium of density. The cold mist hangs suspended precisely three inches above the shifting backs of the herd, perpetually stirred by the beat of the piston rods, smelling purely of distilled water, tallow, and cold, polished slag.
