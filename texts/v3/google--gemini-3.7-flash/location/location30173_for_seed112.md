The air is thin, bright, and so free of moisture that it burns the throat like chilled quartz dust. At this altitude, seven miles above any true bedrock, the sun does not warm so much as it bleaches. It casts sharp, black-edged shadows across an unending expanse of dry, skeletal amber and iron-colored timber that stretches to every horizon.

There is no soil and no water here. The flora does not drink; it mines the dry ionic wind and the falling mineral grit of the upper atmosphere, binding silica and atmospheric carbon into an architecture of staggering, hollow scale. Despite the total absence of earth, the vegetation is the entire world. It forms a continental lattice—a superstructure of petrified, fibrous beams, miles-wide truss-roots, and cantilevered struts that bridge yawning chasms of empty air. It is vast, but it is not dense. Between the towering, ribbed columns of the pale lignum trees are gulfs of hundreds of yards, vertical shafts that drop into hazy, turquoise depths where the base of the forest weaves into itself over and over, all the way down to nothing.

The place is deafening. 

It does not sound like a forest; it sounds like an unlubricated shipyard in the middle of a continuous gale. Because these plants are dry, rigid, and heavily mineralized, their joints do not bend—they articulate. Great spherical burls of iron-hard gall act as universal joints; dry, hollowed trunk-segments telescope inside one another with rhythmic, hydraulic groans powered by internal gas pressure and expanding resins. The wind, which blows perpetually at storm speeds across the high plateau of the canopy, drives millions of natural vanes and ratcheted sails. 

Everywhere, the forest is working. Massive, braided tendrils as thick as freight trains act as drive belts, looped across naturally occurring pulleys of worn, hardened heartwood, spinning dry flywheels thirty yards across. When the wind surges, the whole continental shelf of timber shudders into a frenzy of cyclic motion. Massive wooden pistons, housed within the hollow boles of ancient, petrified redwoods, slam back and forth inside their chambers, compressing dry nitrogen and venting it in high-pitched, screaming bursts through rows of resonant flute-knots along their upper branches. 

The light is blinding and pure. It reflects off the polished flanks of the turning timber and flashes against sheets of translucent, resinous film that the canopy extrudes to catch the sun. These films flap and snap with the violence of loose canvas sails, tearing and healing within the space of hours in a continuous, frantic cycle of self-mending. Below them, where the light fractures through the rotating struts and oscillating ribs, the shadows move with the frantic complexity of a thousand clockworks overlapping at different tempos.

Fauna moves through this mechanized arbor with the jittery urgency of things adapted to a world that never stops striking itself. They are dry-bodied creatures, devoid of blood as wet organisms know it—shelled in overlapping plates of mica-tough chitin, moving on articulated limbs tipped with graphite hooks. Flocks of them, narrow and blade-shaped, launch themselves from the piston-exhaust vents, carried aloft on the screaming jets of high-pressure gas. They tumble through the brilliant, cold glare in their thousands, banking around the oscillating spars, scraping edible dry-resin deposits from the hot friction-points of the wooden gears before diving back into the turbulent shelter of the hollow trunks.

Erosion here is not the work of rivers, but of centuries of friction and wind-borne pumice. The surfaces of the oldest arches are polished to the smoothness of turned bone, their grain lines worn into deep, aerodynamic flutes. In places where two colossal branch-trusses have ground against one another for ten thousand years, the wood has turned to pure, vitreous friction-glass, glowing with dull, internal friction-heat even in the freezing air.

There is a terrifying instability to it all. The plants do not grow quietly; they expand by sudden, explosive fractures. A branch three hundred feet long will store the kinetic energy of the wind-swayed canopy for weeks, bending taut like a catapult arm, until a dry wooden wedge at its base gives way with a report like an artillery battery, flinging the limb upward into a new structural alignment. The vibration runs through the entire continent-lattice, a sharp, metallic ring that sets off sympathetic snaps, shifts, and gear-slips across miles of interconnected forest.

Down in the lower voids, where the light scatters into dusty shafts of gold and cold white, the sheer volume of the place resolves into a deep, rhythmic thrum—the heartbeat of a million unyielding wooden engines, grinding their own bones against the sky, self-perpetuating, furiously dry, and vast beyond measure.
