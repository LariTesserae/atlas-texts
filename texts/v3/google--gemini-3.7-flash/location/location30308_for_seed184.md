The basin is no larger than four city blocks, bounded on all sides by the cyclopean retaining walls of an underground foundry vault that long ago filled to the brim. Here, fifty fathoms beneath the surface of the dry world, the water is not cold or hostile; it is bath-warm, heavy with dissolved iron, lime, and mineral grease, circulating in slow, lazy convection cells driven by the slumbering heat of buried boilers far below the pavement.

Everything is stacked upon everything else. It is an urban landscape compressed into a drowned terrarium. Four-story tenements of sodden, salt-cured brick lean toward one another across narrow alleyways, their lower arches choked with silt, their iron fire escapes laced with long curtains of pale, velvet-textured weed that undulate only when a thermal current rises from a basement grate. Above the roofs, a web of cast-iron aqueducts, steam-mains, and gantry rails crisscrosses the dim ceiling of the chamber, so closely packed that looking up is like peering through the exposed skeleton of an enormous clock. 

The vegetation does not resemble the flora of the open sea; it is freshwater swamp-growth that has mutated in the warm, dark, mineralized soup. Thick ribbons of amber eelgrass root directly into the mortar joints between bricks, prying stones apart over decades with patient, swollen tubers. Heavy blankets of liverwort and aquatic moss smother the lampposts, boiler casings, and the rusted hulls of hydraulic tram-cars stranded on their tracks. In the dead-end courts, where the water is thickest and barely moves, the weed forms dense, buoyant mats several feet thick, suspended mid-depth like soft, fibrous floors.

Light is scarce and amber-cast. It does not come from above, but from the slow, steady phosphorescence of sulfur-metabolizing bacterial films coating the high steam pipes, and from the occasional glass bull’s-eye window of a pressurized maintenance chamber where an oil lantern still burns behind three inches of leaded glass. This dimness gives the sunken architecture a soft, dissolved quality: hard iron corners become furry with rust and algae, and the far side of a square is always lost in a tea-colored haze.

Mechanization here has not stopped; it has merely drowned and adapted. The great cast-iron walking beams of the pit-pumps still move, but they move with agonizing, hydraulic slowness, pushing through liquid rather than air. Each downward stroke takes minutes, displacing clouds of fine, red-brown rust that settle like snow across the roofs before the beam begins its silent, creeping return. The water itself lubricates the massive, exposed gearing of the district’s sluice-engines, whose teeth are padded with dense rinds of freshwater sponge. 

Sound travels through this density as a heavy, physical thrum. There are no splashes, no sharp metallic strikes. Instead, a bone-deep, sub-audible vibration hums through the teeth of anyone descending into the basin—the combined resonance of water-wheels turning against the resistance of the deep, the faint groaning of expansion joints in the iron ceiling, and the occasional soft *whump* of a trapped gas pocket dislodging from a submerged chimney to wobble upward toward the vault.

Fauna is sparse, spectral, and indifferent. Occasionally, a school of pale, blind cave-carp, each the length of a man’s forearm and nearly translucent, will drift out of a flooded parlor window, graze peacefully on the moss-furred keys of an upright piano, and weave through the spokes of a motionless flywheel before vanishing into the shadows of the lower tunnels. There are no predators here, only slow grazers and the silent, industrious chemistry of decay and warmth.

The place exists in an eternal, stagnant equilibrium. The iron rusts, but the lack of free oxygen keeps it from crumbling rapidly; the plants grow thick, but the lack of strong light keeps them from choking out the water entirely; the machines run, but only at the speed of the water that resists them. It is a city that has settled into its own deep, warm grave, functioning quietly, endlessly, without need of an open sky.
