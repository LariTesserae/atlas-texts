The heat hits before the space fully reveals itself—a thick, stagnant bath of air somewhere near forty degrees Celsius, tasting of scalded iron, wet slate, and the faint, sweet grease of long-dead machinery. 

It is a sunken chamber, perhaps sixty paces wide and eighty long, cut straight into raw, fractured basalt. The excavation scars are young; the perimeter walls are still violent and angular, marked with the vertical grooves of heavy pneumatic drills and the jagged, stair-stepped bite of dynamic blasting. The stone has had no time to soften. Its edges are razor-sharp, dark, and freshly cleaved, catching the thinnest gleam of what little light exists here.

The illumination is nearly absent. There are no lamps, no sunlit fissures to the sky above. The only visibility comes from a low, maroon-tinged thermal luminescence leaking from the seams of a dead heat exchanger, and the faint, milk-colored phosphor glowing along the undersides of the foliage. In this perpetual dusk, forms resolve slowly: massive silhouettes of iron, long spans of empty gravel, and vast, surging mounds of pale vegetation.

Despite the near-total darkness, the plant life is astonishingly dense, though it grows in deliberate, isolated thickets rather than an unbroken carpet. It has colonised the space along the thermal gradients. Enormous, waxy fronds—thick as shoe leather and entirely devoid of green chlorophyll, shaded instead in ghostly ivories, bruised purples, and translucent amber—sprawl across the floor. They climb over jagged debris piles in heavy, silent cascades. Thick, tuberous runners, swollen with stored moisture, loop around iron conduits and wedge themselves into the fresh fractures of the rock. Without insects or herbivores to browse them, the leaves grow unimpeded to grotesque dimensions, their surfaces glossy with a constant skin of warm condensation.

Between these lush, clotted islands of growth lie wide, empty clearings of crushed stone, undisturbed and desolate. The density of the room is sparse and selective. In one corner stands the black, monolithic bulk of a steam-manifold housing, its cast-iron casing bolted to a slab of unweathered concrete. Two stories high, it sits like a dead altar, its pressure gauges long shattered, its copper pipes crusted in pale green verdigris where hot water once leaked. Across thirty paces of empty shale, another machine rests alone: a massive cylindrical turbine casing, cracked down its spine, with a pale vine thick as a man’s thigh slowly levering the iron plates apart from the inside.

Water exists here not as pools or flowing streams, but as an atmospheric presence. The air is saturated to the verge of rain, yet rain never falls. Instead, moisture gathers on the cold upper stone of the vault, coalescing into fat, heavy droplets that fall at long, measured intervals. 

*Tink.* A drop strikes the hollow curve of a boiler shell. 

A minute passes in total, smothered stillness before another drop hits the broad face of a waxen leaf with a soft, wet *thwack*. 

There are no animals here. No crickets to rasp in the damp heat, no worms turning the rock-dust, no blind salamanders sliding across the gravel. The silence is profound, deadened by the thick vegetation and the sheer mass of the earth overhead. The only sounds belong to the slow mechanics of the room: the imperceptible drip of condensation, the occasional metallic *ping* of cooling structural steel, and the faint, breath-like hiss of thermal air rising along the jagged rock face.

Processes here move on a tectonic clock. The plants do not photosynthesize; they feed on the sulfurous mineral vapors leaking through the blasting fractures and the residual warmth radiating from the industrial infrastructure buried in the floor. Their growth is blind, muscular, and slow, their white roots slowly drinking the grease from machine bearings and anchoring themselves deep into the fissures of the young stone. The room is a closed, suffocating pocket—a mechanical graveyard being quietly, patiently devoured by a pale and silent jungle that needs no sun.
