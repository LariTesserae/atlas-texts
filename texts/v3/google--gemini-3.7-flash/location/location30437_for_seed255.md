The floor is not earth or stone, but an endless, tightly mortared plane of hand-formed terracotta blocks. Each tile bears the unmistakable impression of a palm or heel, pressed into the clay before firing and laid down while the grout was still sticky. There is no natural bed underneath; if you were to pry up one of the blocks, you would find only another layer of tiles below it, cross-hatched and bound with tallow and pulverized shell, descending into a cool, foundationless dark.

The scale is that of an unroofed valley or an immense subterranean basin whose boundaries lie far beyond what little light permits. Above, there is only a low, heavy vault of black air—damp and drafty, smelling of cold kiln-ash and wet pottery. The darkness is thick, nearly total, thinned only by the sporadic, ember-red glow of vent-holes bored into the lower masonry, where slow-burning cakes of compressed peat or fat smolder to cure the newer walls.

Nothing here has aged. The edges of the bricks are razor-sharp; the lime-wash on the rising pillars has not yet chipped or discolored. It is an embryonic world, completely manufactured from the ground up, yet built with the rough, immediate technology of fingers, wooden floats, stone mauls, and rawhide cordage. Everywhere the architecture is packed together at suffocating density: clusters of hollow, unglazed chimneys rise side-by-side like organ pipes, braced against one another by thousands of lashed sapling poles and notched wooden struts. Walkways only two hand-spans wide bridge the gaps between leaning flues. There are no streets, only fissures between hand-stacked towers, where the walls are so close that a traveler must turn sideways, scraping their shoulders against raw, coarse-grained brick.

Water moves through the expanse not as streams, but as a perpetual, creeping condensation. The air is cool—chilled by the vast thermal mass of the clay—and moisture sweats continuously from the porous surfaces. It gathers in shallow, deliberately angled grooves cut into the tiles, trickling downward in thin, glassy braids that vanish into thousands of small, circular drains. Here and there, where the gradient flattens, the water forms skin-deep pools that mirror the faint, orange pinpricks of the kiln-vents. There are no plants—not even lichen has had the centuries required to take hold of the fresh mortar—leaving the entire landscape an unyielding palette of slate-grey, rust-red, and damp buff.

Despite the darkness, the place is violently loud. 

The acoustics of millions of hollow clay chambers amplify every sound into a continuous, roaring drum-tone. High up in the scaffolding, heavy wooden counterweights swing on hemp ropes, thumping rhythmically against guide-timbers to maintain the tension of rising arches. The hiss of moisture hitting the warm draft-chutes is constant, like the breathing of a vast animal drowned under stone. Occasionally, a deep, percussive *clack* rolls across the basin—the sound of an entire section of freshly pinned brickwork settling under its own weight, followed by the dull, cascading rattle of displaced ceramic wedges. 

Activity is everywhere, though sparse of living bodies. Primitive mechanical processes churn without cease: gravity-fed sluices drop weighted clay pestles into grinding troughs; draft-flues suck air with a low, whistling howl; long lines of braided cordage twitch and draw taut across the distances, transferring manual power from unseen cranks deep within the lower tiers. 

Living things are few, pale, and specialized. Blind, soft-bodied arthropods, their carapaces the color of cured dough, crawl along the joints between bricks, feeding on the animal fat bound into the mortar. Occasionally, something larger—a long-legged, leathery thing without eyes, moving with a dry, clicking gait—scuttles across the high rafters, its claws fitted perfectly into the standard-gauge holes left by the builders' dowels. 

The landscape is not waiting for an inhabitant; it is expanding through sheer kinetic inertia. At its edges, where the dark becomes absolute, one can hear the dull, wet slapping of fresh mud being beaten into forms, the scraping of slate blades smoothing wet clay, and the heavy, syncopated thudding of mauls driving wedges into the dark, raising the floor another inch, another yard, out into the empty cold.
