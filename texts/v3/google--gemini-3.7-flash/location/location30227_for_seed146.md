The chamber has no ceiling that can be found by reach or echo, only a rising dispersal of heat into vast, unlit height. Miles down in the dry lithosphere, the rift opened once—snapped clean along shear-planes of feldspar and smoky basalt—and because there has never been a drop of water to roll the edges, every fracture remains as sharp as the second it broke.

The air is warm, thick, and utterly still. It sits at a perpetual body-heat, dry as kiln-air, carrying no current, no drift, no scent of damp earth. In the absence of wind or moisture, there is no erosion to soften the architecture; the landscape is a dense forest of razor-edged mineral slats, vertical slabs of unclouded anhydrite, and cleavage-steps that drop hundreds of feet in sheer, polished planes. You could run a hand down a facet and find it mirror-smooth, then catch the corner and be split to the bone.

Light does not exist here in any optical sense. It is a world of absolute, velvety pitch, yet it is not empty. The density of physical matter and living presence fills the space until it feels crowded. Vast, blade-like crystalline fins crowd against one another like books packed loosely on a shelf, leaning at steep, geometric angles. Between them, the gaps are narrow, warm flues where life thrives without a single leaf, spore, or drop of sap.

The ecology is entirely animal and lithic. Blind, heavy-bodied fauna with carapaces of fused mica and dry chitin crawl through the vertical lattices. They do not drink; their metabolisms run on non-aqueous lipid chains and the slow, piezoelectric charges generated where the rock plates grind in subterranean tension. Long, dry-jointed scavengers with many-jointed limbs tap their way across the chasms, moving with an eerie, weightless deliberation. Clusters of pale, segmented grazers cling to the basalt faces, their mouthparts tipped with hard diamond-grit, methodically rasping the mineral crusts for sulfur compounds and trace metals. Because there is no ambient moisture to lose, their bodies are sealed, smooth, and rattle like dry gourds when they bump against the stone.

The sound of the place is almost purely tactile: a vast, heavy silence punctuated only by the minute, dry clicks of claws on glass-hard stone, the faint *snick-snick* of chitinous shears grazing a quartz rib, and the distant, low-frequency hum of the planet’s crust sitting in static, unchanging equilibrium. Nothing falls. Nothing rots. When an animal dies, its dry husk remains clamped to the rock face for centuries, undisturbed by decay, slowly turning into an empty mineral shell.

Scattered throughout the crystal fins are small, deliberate interventions. These are not grand ruins, but the modest, manual works of a primitive presence adapted to total dark. Notches have been pecked into the razor edges of obsidian blades with hammerstones to create footholds. Between neighboring slabs, thick bridges made of braided sinew and dry rawhide hang taut, anchored into the rock by hammered wedges of dense slate. 

Here and there, in the sheltered angles where three slabs meet to form a wedge-like hollow, there are small hand-built shelters. They are constructed without mortar, made of carefully stacked, dry-cleaved stones arranged to form shallow, insulated pockets. Outside these alcoves, one finds the discarded remnants of tool-making: piles of chipped flint flakes, bone-pointed awls used to punch through tough carapace-hides, and neatly bundled coils of hairless tendon. The beings who made them navigate entirely by temperature gradients and the sound of dry tapping, their tools simple, heavy, and purely functional.

Time does not move here. There are no seasons, no tides, no changes in the air. The temperature never fluctuates by a fraction of a degree. A chipped flake of stone resting on a shelf of anhydrite will remain precisely where it fell, unburied by dust—for there is no wind to stir dust—and unmarred by rust or moss, suspended in a warm, eternal, and bustling dark.
