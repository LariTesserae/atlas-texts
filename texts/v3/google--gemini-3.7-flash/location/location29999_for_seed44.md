The air inside the lower sluice-court has the specific, heavy warmth of a laundry basement that has stood open to a tropical greenhouse for fifty years. Everything here is either made of water, soaked through with water, or constructed to direct the passage of water through iron.

It is a small, sunken district—scarcely three hundred paces from the rusted brick retaining wall on the southern perimeter to the intake culverts on the north. Yet within this pocket, the density of both machinery and plant mass is nearly total. The architecture does not sit beneath the vegetation; the two have formed a single composite material. Huge, multi-tiered banks of cast-iron steam manifold and reciprocating water pumps are bolted directly into the basalt foundation, but their housings have long since disappeared beneath a foot-thick mantle of wet liverworts, weeping strap-ferns, and the dense, fibrous root-mats of aerial figs. 

Every surface is wet to the touch. Water does not merely flow; it weeps from the mortar joints of the high brick walls, condenses along the undersides of suspended copper conduits, and trickles down the fluted iron columns in unbroken, glassy sheets. The floor of the court is a lattice of heavy iron grating set over deep, slow-moving overflow channels. Through the diamond-shaped perforations, black water moves without haste, choked with submerged ribbon-weeds and pale root filaments that sway in the draft of the drainage flow.

The light is the pale, diffused green-gold of an overcast afternoon passing through three distinct tiers of living canopy: first the high, broad umbrellas of cultivated swamp-hardwoods that have rooted in the masonry parapets; beneath them, a dense mid-story of banana-relatives and broad-leaved aroids whose waxy leaves are perpetually polished by falling drops; and finally the fine, dripping lace of mosses hanging from every crossbeam and pressure gauge. The daylight is steady and sufficient, illuminating the fine spray hanging in the middle air without ever creating sharp shadows.

The sound is immense and rhythmic, an unbroken wall of low-frequency work. Three massive, low-pressure walking-beam engines, housed in open-sided brick pavilions along the eastern wall, maintain a slow, unhurried cadence—perhaps eight strokes a minute. Each downward stroke is accompanied by the heavy, lubricated *clack* of brass valve-gear and the deep, percussive *thwump* of displacement pistons moving thousands of gallons through drowned chambers beneath the flagstones. Layered over this mechanical heartbeat is the hiss of pinhole steam leaks venting into cold moss, the drumming of condensed runoff falling from twenty-foot leaves onto corrugated tin roofing, and the continuous, low rush of water descending through stepped weir-boxes. 

There is little life here that moves on legs. The air is too wet and the machinery too deafening for birds or small mammals. Occasionally, a flat, mottled river-frog sits motionless on the warm bronze collar of an eccentric bearing, undisturbed by the slow turn of the shaft beneath its feet, but otherwise the ecology belongs entirely to the kingdom of plants, molds, and the iron that sustains them. 

The relationship between the industrial apparatus and the vegetation is not one of decay or abandonment, but of an old, established equilibrium. The boilers further back in the masonry vaults vent their warm blowdown through subterranean ducts, warming the soil beds to a steady, summer-like heat year-round. In return, the vast root systems stabilize the crumbling Victorian stonework, their living anchors preventing the waterlogged terrace walls from slumping into the central basin. Pipes six inches thick with scale and red-lead paint are bound together by woody lianas that have grown precisely into the contours of the flange-joints, sealing them tighter than gaskets.

At the center of the court stands the primary aerator: a stepped pyramid of circular iron pans, thirty feet across, over which river water is pumped to tumble down through successive tiers. Broad-leaved aquatic plants have completely colonized each basin, their roots trailing over the rims like thick, dripping fringe. As the water falls from tier to tier, it strikes the iron rims with a hollow, bell-like ring, a steady chime lost beneath the heavier, grinding respiration of the pumps below.

Everything moves, but nothing changes rapidly. A single iron valve wheel, four feet across and crusted with lime deposits, is turned half an inch every few months by the slow swelling of a wooden buttress-root wedged behind its spokes. The copper tell-tales on the pressure boards drift lazily within narrow, safe arcs. The court exists in an eternal, soaking afternoon—warm, mechanically loud, and utterly still in its deep, saturated continuity.
