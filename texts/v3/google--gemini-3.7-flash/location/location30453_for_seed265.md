There is no morning here, and no noon; the dark is absolute, a thick, mineral velvet that settles against the skin like warm tallow. It is not the void of space, which is dry and cold, but a wet, crowded blackness, heavy with the weight of an ocean that exists only as vapor and seep. 

The air is hot, hovering near blood temperature, saturated to the point where breathing feels like swallowing a warm, damp cloth. Every surface weeps. Condensation runs down the sheer faces of freshly fractured basalt and cold-rolled steel in thick, continuous braids, pooling in the wide, flat basin that extends for miles in every direction. 

Because the landscape is young—torn open recently by some unremembered tectonic or industrial violence—the terrain has no gentle slopes. The ground is a maze of razor-sharp shale shelves, sudden rectangular drop-offs, and freshly sheared slag. There has been no time for water to round the stones or for silt to soften the edges. If you run your hands along the rocks, they will slice clean through calluses before you feel the pain; if you step down into the black water, your boots strike against the clean, precise corners of submerged iron plate and fresh-fractured stone.

Scattered across this low-lying expanse are the ruins and working engines of a sprawling, half-drowned industry. They do not sit together in a yard, but loom miles apart across the flats, their shapes only known by the way they alter the humidity and the sound of the air around them. Here, a submerged turbine housing the size of a tenement block breathes a deep, sub-audible hum through the silt; three miles to the south, the sharp, boxy silhouette of a coal-cracking tower rises out of a brackish marsh, completely dormant yet vibrating with high-frequency electrostatic tension. Heavy copper cables, as thick around as a man’s torso and insulated with tarred hemp, cross the wet mud in taut, unswerving lines, disappearing into the black brine.

Despite the total lack of light, the basin is not empty. It is suffocatingly full. 

The fauna does not possess eyes; they have no vestigial sockets or clouded lenses, for their lineages have never known light. They are blind, tactile, and abundant beyond measure. Thousands of soft-bellied creatures, slick with a pale, mucous coating, blanket the iron plating in overlapping layers. They feed on the galvanic grease that sweats from the seams of the machines and the rich, warm minerals boiling up from the floor. Beneath the surface of the shallow water, long, ribbon-like animals with velvet skin weave between the submerged girders, their passage marked only by the gentle displace of warm currents against your legs. Blind crawlers with needle-sharp chitinous legs swarm over the fresh rock faces, grazing on thin films of non-photosynthetic slime that smell faintly of kerosene and sweet loam. 

In this dark, reality has begun to fray at the margins where the machinery meets the wet earth. 

Physics here is soft, slow, and unanchored. The water does not always flow toward the lowest elevation; in places, thick sheets of condensate cling to the undersides of suspended iron walkways and creep upward along vertical rivets, pooling at the summits of pillars rather than their bases. Time operates on a sluggish, dampened cycle. A dropped iron bolt sinks through the warm puddle with the agonizing slowness of an object falling through chilled honey, taking several minutes to strike the basalt floor, yet producing a sharp, instantaneous clink the moment your fingers release it.

The machines themselves run on a logic that has drifted away from mechanics. Enormous, five-story flywheels turn in the dark, completing a single, silent revolution over the course of three days. They are driven by no fires and connected to no boilers; their rotation seems maintained by the slow, gravitational breathing of the waterlogged basin itself. Near these engines, spatial distances become elastic. Two iron pillars that stand twenty paces apart when approached from the north may require half an hour of wading through thigh-deep mud to cross between them if approached from the east.

Sound travels strangely through this warm, crowded soup. The landscape is mostly quiet, but it is a dense, muffled quietness, choked by the humidity and the sheer biomass covering every surface. The sudden scream of metal tearing under pressure does not echo; it is swallowed instantly by the wet, fleshy coatings on the rocks. All that remains is a low, continuous acoustic texture: the rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of cooling transformers miles in the distance, the soft, collective rustle of millions of blind legs shifting over wet shale, and the perpetual, patient sound of thick water dripping from sharp edges into the vast, warm dark.
