The floor of the Great Sunk reaches miles out in every direction, held deep below the outer crust of the world. It is an immense, angular trench born of an ancient, clean collapse—a dropped tectonic shelf whose limestone and basalt ramparts still bear the sharp, unpolished facets of fresh fracture. Overhead, the lip of the canyon sits so far above that the daylight never truly reaches the bottom as sun; it arrives only as a flat, shadowless milk-gray wash, cooling into dim violet against the lower talus.

The air is cold and perpetually still, holding at cellar temperature with zero seasonal variation. Because the chasm is so deep and sheltered from wind, the climate does not cycle. It exists in an unyielding, breathless equilibrium. Moisture here does not come from storms or rivers; there are no streams cutting the gravel. Instead, a uniform dampness coats everything. Condensation beads perpetually on the sharp undersides of broken slabs, glistening like oil on the black basalt, weeping downward in silent, micro-droplets that keep the shattered rock surfaces slick and dark. 

Vegetation has barely gained a foothold on the young rubble. What grows is sparse and ground-hugging: a dark, rubbery crust of liverwort pressed into crevices, patches of ghostly pale moss that feed exclusively on the moisture of stone, and isolated, tough-stemmed ferns with fronds as stiff as sheet metal, rooted in pockets of fine silt between the boulders. The floor is largely open, naked rock—miles of pale gray scree, fractured blocks the size of houses, and wide, level pavements of cracked shale that stretch into the dimness.

Yet the emptiness of the terrain is completely overwritten by the life that inhabits it. The chasm is an engine of animal mass. 

The sound is the first thing that presses against the body: an immense, unrelenting, acoustic roar that echoes continuously off the vertical walls. Millions of cave swiftlets and cliff-martins nest in the jagged fissures of the higher rock, their collective chattering, snapping beaks, and frantic wing-beats blending into a heavy, white-noise rumble that sounds like falling water or distant machinery. Lower down, the talus slopes are alive. Long-legged, pale subterranean crickets move in shimmering waves over the damp gravel; blind, armor-plated ground beetles the length of a human hand navigate the block fields in the hundreds of thousands, their hard carapaces clicking against the stone like hail. In every crevice, fat, cool-blooded lizards lie stacked on top of one another in dense, breathing carpets, shifting sluggishly to feed on the endless drift of fallen insects.

The smell is thick, sharp, and mineral, dominated by guano, chitin, wet lime, and cold stone. Life here does not compete for space with vegetation; it covers the geological framework entirely on its own terms.

Tucked into the drier niches beneath overhanging limestone shelves, where the rock has remained sheltered from condensation, there are faint, scattered remnants of primitive human passage: small middens of split mammal bone, fire-cracked river cobbles dragged down from the upper world countless generations ago, and flakes of worked chert discarded on the gravel. On one low basalt face, a line of rough red-ochre handprints remains fixed in the motionless air, utterly untouched by wind or decay, as permanent as the fracture planes that surround them.

Nothing changes here. The light neither brightens nor fully fails, maintaining its perpetual twilight. The temperature never drifts. The roar of wings and clicks remains constant, vibrating through the cold, slick stone, holding the vast subterranean valley in an unchanging, deafening pulse of existence.
