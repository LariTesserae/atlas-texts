The water here is not an intrusion or a flood; it is the sky, the air, and the ground’s medium. It rests on a high, uplifted basalt table thousands of cubits above the earth's dry lowlands—a vast, warm mountain-basin holding an ocean that never drains. Because of the elevation and the geothermal floor beneath, the water is as warm as breath, thick with dissolved silicates, and heavy enough to slow the fall of a dropped stone to a gentle, drifting descent.

Light arrives thin and bruised, filtered through hundreds of feet of green-tinted suspension. It never fully brightens, resting in a perpetual emerald dusk that softens the sharpest edges, though the rock itself is violently young. 

The geology has barely had time to cool. Black, glassy volcanic shelves and jagged needles of obsidian jut upward through the floor, unrounded by sand or surf. There are no smooth pebbles here; every ridge of basalt has the fresh, sheared bite of cooling glass, its fractures still settling with faint, tectonic ticks that vibrate in the chest. Mineral chimneys vent pale ribbons of heat—clear, shimmering columns of water hotter than the rest—that rise slowly toward the distant, unseen surface.

In this warm, dim broth, vegetation grows with quiet ferocity. Broad-leafed vascular ribbons, thick as tanned hide and rooted in the fresh basalt seams, climb upward in dense, tangled curtains. Mats of golden-brown liverwort carpet the terraces, interspersed with fleshy, pale stalks that swell with trapped gases to keep themselves upright. They do not sway with violent currents; the movement of this water is glacial and patient, a slow, convective turnover that rolls through the basin over months. The plants simply lean, recovering their postures across days.

Spread across the terraces of this high submarine valley is an immense, sprawling settlement. It is not a ruin; it is occupied and continually being built, though its tools are made strictly of stone, bone, and braided plant-fiber. 

The architecture takes advantage of the weightlessness of the medium. Long, slender pillars of raw basalt have been pried loose from the floor and lashed together with thick ropes of braided sea-vine to form tall, skeletal frames. Between these uprights, the builders have woven dense lattices of dried reed and kelp-leather, creating layered, multi-tiered dwellings that rise like stacked wicker cages into the dim water. Anchor-stones—heavy nodules of iron-dense lava—hold down floating communal halls made of buoyant timber and inflated kelp bladders. Narrow pathways of rough, unmortared slate span the gaps between volcanic spires, tied together with sinew that swells tight in the wet warmth.

Because metal is unknown or unworkable in the salt-rich heat, everything is bound, pinned, or wedged. Hand-chipped flint wedges secure the joints of the platforms; stone counterweights balance swinging doors that drift open and shut without hinges. The city has the scale of a great mountain metropolis, yet it has the texture of an enormous, submerged basket, anchored firmly to the raw, unweathered peaks below.

Sound travels far in this dense warm water, but it carries little sharp agitation. There is the persistent, low-frequency hum of the geothermal vents, the muffled *clack* of slate ballast adjusting on the ropes, and the long, groaning strain of wet fiber under tension. Voices, when they occur, are swallowed into low thrums and rhythmic taps against stone.

Animal life is sparse and deliberate. There are no schooling fish flashing across the heights. Instead, solitary, translucent creatures—pale siphonophores and slow-paddling, blind isopods the size of shields—drift between the woven walls like wandering lanterns. Occasionally, a heavy, flat-bodied grazer with skin like wet river-slate crawls across the new rock, scraping the young moss with teeth made of mineralized shell.

A fine, gray snow of volcanic ash and organic silt falls constantly through the amber-green gloom, settling on the woven roofs and the broad leaves. Before it can accumulate into soil, the creeping vines incorporate it into their root-mats, binding the ash directly to the stone, building a world that is assembling itself, stitch by stitch, under the heavy silence of the high water.
