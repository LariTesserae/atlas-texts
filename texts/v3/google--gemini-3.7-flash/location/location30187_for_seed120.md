At the absolute apex of an unseen spire—a height where the outside atmosphere thins to a silent, indigo vacuum—there is a single, swollen knuckle of space. It measures barely four paces across. It is an interior that should not possess an interior, a hollow knot tied into the sky, sealed within walls made entirely of hand-coiled mud, boiled hide, and raw wicker.

Inside this pocket, the laws of elevation invert into a sweltering, wet density. It is hot—not with the clean heat of sunlight, but with the suffocating, metabolic warmth of wet compost and freshly baked river-silt. Steam rises constantly from the floor, condensing instantly against the ceiling and weeping back down in fat, heavy drops. Every square inch of the enclosure is man-made, yet nothing within it has had time to weather. The clay walls still bear the deep, sharp impressions of human thumbs, the ridges of skin clearly cast in the drying slip; the wicker lattices are bound with wet rawhide strips that are still translucent, unhardened, smelling of marrow and wood-ash. There is zero erosion here—no grain of mud has yet been rounded by water, no reed split by fatigue. The entire space exists in the first minute after its making, continuously finishing its own birth.

Because there is nowhere else to go, life and matter have packed into a solid, throbbing mass. Thick, pale vines with leaves like scalded cabbage muscle their way between the woven willow ribs, not planted, but seeming to generate directly out of the knotted fiber. They grow visibly, creaking as they swell, their tips oozing a hot, sugary gel that coats the walls in a slick, fragrant sheen. Fern-like fronds with the texture of boiled leather uncurl against the woven ceiling, drinking the dim, amber glow that emanates from nowhere—a faint, sour phosphorescence trapped within the moisture itself. 

The physics within the pocket are coming unstitched. Gravity does not pull straight down toward the planet far below; instead, it pulls outward toward the curved weave of the walls, so that one must brace against a floor that feels simultaneously like an incline and a ceiling. Through tiny gaps in the thatch, where the clay has not yet plugged the seam, you do not see the sky of the world; you see the raw, exposed curve of the stratosphere whistling past at astronomical speed, yet no draft enters. The exterior wind howls like a torn sail—a constant, deafening roar of shear-winds—while inside, the air remains thick as porridge, undisturbed, vibrating in harmony with the roar.

The sound is immense. It is the friction of wood under extreme torque, the rhythmic *thwip-thwip* of flexing bamboo struts, and the wet, sucking noise of setting mud that refuses to stay dry. Water weeps down the thumb-marked walls in sheets, boiling slightly where it meets the lower junctions, pooling in shallow depressions lined with hammered copper foil and smoothed river pebbles that have never touched a riverbed.

Fauna is almost absent, save for things that seem to have been folded into the material during construction: tiny, soft-shelled isopods the color of unbaked dough, moving with mechanical slowness across the wet wicker, chewing the binding-strings and excreting fresh, sticky pitch that instantly cements the joints. 

The heat climbs steadily. The air tastes of wet hair, crushed mint, and green wood roasted over steam. In the center of the pocket, suspended by three braided grass ropes, hangs an unfired earthenware jar the size of a human chest, shivering violently. From its unglazed pores, hot water sweats outward, misting into the room, replenishing the drowned air of an embryonic world that hangs, small and furious, at the edge of space.
