At this altitude, the air is thin but heavy with boiling moisture—a hot, pressurized vapor that smells of bruised pulp, tannin, and green iron. 

There is no rock here, no mineral soil. Everything that rises into the pale daylight is constructed, yet nothing here was ever built with a tool. The terrain is an open, high-altitude expanse of titanic architectural forms engineered purely out of living, vascular wood. Vast, hollow spires—some tapering to needles five hundred meters tall, others boxy and tiered like stepped ziggurats—stand isolated from one another across immense gulfs of misty emptiness. The space between them is broad and dizzying; you look past the sheer, fluted flank of one monument across two miles of open air before the next colossal arcade looms out of the steam.

Because this ecosystem is young and violently unstable, the architecture is constantly erecting, breaking, and remodeling itself. Massive structural beams—ribbed trunks thick as galleon hulls—thrust horizontally out into the void, chasing unseen stress lines. They weave into braided suspension arches, seize each other in knuckle-like root grafts, lock tight under immense biological tension, and then, overloaded by the weight of the water they pump, shear off with the sound of cannon fire. The sound is continuous: the deafening groaning of wood twisting against wood, the deep bass thrum of sap pressurized to bursting, and the sharp, whip-crack echoes of tensile fibers giving way in the fog.

Water defines the entire kinetic life of the place. Siphoned up from miles below through interior taproots the size of mine shafts, scalding water is forced through the walls of every structure. The buildings weep. Clear, hot moisture sweats continuously from the pale green grain of the towers, sheeting down vertical façades in shimmering films and pouring from deliberate, guttered overhangs in roaring, rope-thick cataracts that plunge thousands of feet into the empty mist below. 

Where the water cascades, the architecture responds immediately. Lignin deposits form rapidly along the drainage paths, throwing out razor-sharp fluting, hard-edged fins, and scalloped buttresses designed to catch the flow and channel it into adjoining colonnades. These features have none of the softened, rounded curves of ancient jungles; they are crisp, angular, and freshly grown, their edges as sharp as splintered bamboo. 

Beneath the towering colonnades, open plazas of woven root-thatch span between the main pillars. These wide decks sag under pools of steaming, mineral-rich runoff, their surfaces ribbed with geometric patterns of bark that swell and buckle in real time. Plumes of geothermal steam hiss from purpose-grown vents along the balustrades, whistling like pipe organs as internal valves expand and contract. 

There are no birds, no insects, no scuttling scavengers in the seams of the wood. The architecture is entirely alone with its own ferocious metabolic drive. It builds cathedrals to channel water; the water fractures the cathedrals; the cathedrals seal their fractures with thicker, denser timber, surging upward into the humid sky in an unending, frantic cycle of self-manufacture and self-ruin. The light remains steady and diffuse—a broad, warm glare illuminating the violent, beautiful geometry of a city that grows its own bones, drowns them, and rebuilds them before they can fall.
