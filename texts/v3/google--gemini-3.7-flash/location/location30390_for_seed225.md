The floor of the sun-well lies forty paces beneath the scrub-plain, accessible only where a smooth, calcrete lip has sloughed away over millennia to leave a ramp of packed white silt. 

It is a single, vaulted hollow, roughly the span of a quiet chapel, but stripped of all weight. The air inside is vast because there is almost nothing in it. Overhead, the ceiling is gone entirely—punched out in some distant epoch—leaving an aperture that funnels an excessive, bleached daylight down into the bowl. The light here is not diffuse; it is a dense, direct brilliance that bounces off the chalk-white walls until the entire volume hums with a warm, shadowless glare.

Despite the brightness, the air has the thick, sheltering warmth of an unventilated greenhouse. It smells faintly of slaked lime, warm dust, and the sharp mineral edge of condensation. 

Moisture exists here not as flowing water, but as an intermittent skin. Along the northern overhang, where the rock curves inward away from the direct sun, the stone sweats. Drops form along micro-fissures in the travertine, swell to heavy, mercury-bright beads, and fall thirty feet in silence. They strike the floor where they have drilled shallow, smooth-sided cups into the stone. Each cup holds a thimble of lukewarm, mineral-saturated water that never quite dries before the next drop arrives, yet never overflows its rim. 

Vegetation is minimal, reduced to things that have learned to survive on raw glare and dew. A pale, tendon-like succulent creeps across the floor, its segments flattened so tightly to the rock they resemble dried plaster casts. In the higher niches, dry filaments of gray moss hang down like unraveled twine, motionless in the dead calm.

Yet the open air of the hollow is not deserted; it belongs to a slow, strangely numerous fauna. 

Pale, disc-shaped insects with paper-thin carapaces hang suspended in the light beams. They do not buzz or dart; they drift on faint thermal currents rising from the heated floor, steering with fine, hair-like antennae that catch the light like spun glass. On the lower rock shelves, small, flat-skulled reptiles—blind, or nearly so, with skin the color of milk quartz—lie pressed against the warmth. They exist in high numbers, dozens of them lining the subtle ledges, utterly still, their throats pulsing with slow, shallow breaths. Occasionally, one will shift its footing by an inch, its claws producing a faint, dry scrape against the limestone that echoes softly around the chamber and then dies.

In the southern curve of the wall, where the rock is shaded for half the day, there are faint marks of simple handiwork, ancient and unhurried. A set of shallow footholds has been pecked into the stone using a harder river cobble; the impacts are weathered, their rough edges rounded over by a thin glaze of calcium carbonate. Near the floor rests a hollowed sandstone block, its depression smoothed to a dull polish from long-forgotten grinding, surrounded by a handful of split bone awls that have petrified where they were dropped. No one lives here now, but the hollow retains their presence without disturbance, sealed in its own slow rhythm.

Time inside the well does not rush. The circle of white light glides across the pale floor through the middle hours of the day, heating the stone, drawing up moisture, and letting it settle again as the sun tilts past the rim. The insects hover without landing; the reptiles do not hunt, as if sustained purely by the radiance and the damp stone; and the single drops continue their patient, rhythmic descent from the overhang into the floor, striking their little basins with a faint, musical tap that marks the hours.
