The wind arrives before anything else, not as a breeze but as a solid, roaring wall of cold air funneled through a thousand fluted stone apertures.

This is the Upper Shelf of Oor—a hanging terrace pinned five thousand meters up against the sheared shoulder of a dead metamorphic massif. It is not large; the entire basin spans no more than four or five hundred paces across, a contained mountain amphitheater whose outer edge drops away into sheer blue void. Yet inside this cradle lies a completely organized, hollowed city that appears not to have been built with tools, but settled into existence so long ago that time has erased every seam, corner, and chisel mark.

The architecture is urban in scale and arrangement, but primordial in substance. Great multi-tiered tenements, street-canyons, and terraced squares rise out of the bedrock like a calcified hive, but they have been ground down by millions of years of glacial runoff, freezing mist, and scouring gales. Every threshold is rounded like river jade; every staircase has dissolved into a smooth, undulating ramp. The walls are thick, sweeping membranes of dark granite-gneiss, polished to a dull, greasy luster. Entire avenues run between four-story facades whose windows are wide, ovoid bellies of emptiness, looking out onto vast tracts of hollow air. 

There is an intense, cavernous vacancy here. For all the density of the grid—the intersecting plazas, the raised viaducts spanning empty courtyards—the place is utterly unoccupied by mass. The chambers are cavern-deep and stripped of clutter, letting the brilliant, high-altitude sun plunge straight through stacked rows of oval skylights down to the wet flagged floors. The light has that knife-edge clarity of extreme elevation: dazzling, shadow-sharp, revealing every micro-fracture and velvety patch of green on the stone.

The atmosphere is biting, near-freezing, yet soaked through with intermittent moisture. Clouds do not merely pass overhead; they charge across the lip of the shelf in ragged, low-slung herds, breaking against the stone towers. Within seconds, a dry, sunlit colonnade is swallowed by dense, freezing fog. The mist condenses instantly upon the cold rock, sheet-glazing the walls with a skin of water that races down pre-carved gutters, pooling in wide, shallow basins before draining over the precipice in long, wavering threads of spray.

Wherever water clings, life responds with lush, low-slung ferocity. Spongy mats of liverwort, deep emerald peat-mosses, and cold-adapted saxifrage carpet the lower thirds of the buildings. They line the interior floors of the grand halls like thick, wet rugs, drinking the condensation and releasing a sharp, vegetal scent that smells of crushed iron and wet loam. In the sheltered lees of the towers, tiny pale orchids with translucent petals shiver in the turbulence, rooted directly into the hair-cracks of the granite.

The sound is immense and ceaseless. The city is essentially a gigantic, self-playing pneumatic organ. The prevailing mountain westerlies strike the outer facade and split into thousands of high-velocity jets, whistling through the smoothed doorframes, booming within the barrel-vaulted cellars, and producing a deep, chest-rattling drone that vibrates through the soles of one’s feet. When a gust peaks, the entire terrace hums like a tuning fork, accompanied by the frantic, rhythmic slap of falling water and the clatter of loose slate pellets skimming across the courtyards.

Animal life is sparse, existing only at the margins of this roaring theater. Occasionally, a single high-altitude scavenger—a kite-legged, featherless avian with a wingspan like a cedar plank—will bank hard over the roofless plazas, ride the thermal venting out of a chimney, and vanish over the abyss without ever touching down. Deep within the moss beds, small, blind isopods with glass-like carapaces graze the algae in total stillness, indifferent to the acoustic storm above them.

Everything is in motion even though nothing has changed here for an epoch. The cycle repeats by the minute: the sudden blinding flare of the sun drying the slick granite; the immediate roar of incoming cloud-packs; the flash-drench of ice-cold condensate feeding the green carpets; and the perpetual, deafening chord of the wind working the empty city’s throat. It is an end-state landscape—a place that has finished eroding, finished settling, and now simply breathes, cold and lucid, high above the world.
