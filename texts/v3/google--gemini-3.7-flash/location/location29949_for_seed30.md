At the shelf-edge of a high, hanging plateau, the rock has no memory of water, though water coats every millimeter of it. 

The stone here is brand-new. It does not look quarried or tumbled; it looks *spawned*, extruded out of the bedrock’s quantum grain in mathematically flawless cleavage planes. There are no softened corners, no ruts where feet or currents have worn a groove. Basaltic plates meet at crisp, micro-meter angles, their surfaces sheer and mirror-dark, unmarred by the slow scuff of deep time. The geology is embryonic, caught in the middle of a continuous birth: every few minutes, a low structural thrum shudders through the floor, and a hairline fracture opens with the dry *clack* of splitting flint, instantly sealing itself as a new tier of obsidian-like terracing rises half an inch above the last.

The air is warm—a thick, greenhouse warmth that smells of ozone, hot quartz, and distillates. It hangs heavily in the dim, violet-tinted dusk that never quite deepens to night nor opens into sun. A steady, warm condensation sweats out of the saturated air, weeping down the vertical planes of the unweathered stone in wide, unbroken sheets. Because the rock has no micro-pores or weathered roughness to cling to, the water moves fast, cascading over razor-sharp steps with a constant, heavy hiss that fills the enclosed basin.

Scattered across the floor of this basin—an amphitheater no larger than a cathedral yard—stand several tall, slender pylons. They are made of a dense, non-reflective material that current physics has no vocabulary for: matter that seems to exist in a state of suspended torsion, neither metal nor ceramic, twisting subtly as if compensating for forces occurring elsewhere in the galaxy. These are not ruins; they are active fixtures of a silent, incomprehensible engineering project. Around their bases, the air bends like heat shimmer, and the weeping water is repelled by a two-inch perimeter of pure vacuum, falling away in perfect, suspended beads that roll down invisible incline planes.

Despite the sparsity of plant life—confined only to sparse, hair-thin filaments of colorless glass-moss that trail from the sheerest joints—the terrace is violently alive.

Fauna fills the space, thriving on the raw electromagnetic waste of the pylons and the sudden, mineral-rich runoff. Translucent, multi-jointed organisms—the size of sand crabs but moving with the fluid snap of shrimp—scuttle by the hundreds over the damp, polished slopes. They do not feed on vegetation, but graze the razor-thin film of ionized vapor clinging to the new rock, their hard carapaces clicking in a loud, rhythmic chorus that competes with the roar of falling water. 

Above them, in the warm, dim air, blind ribbon-things ride the thermal updrafts generated by the pylons’ exotic heat sinks. They have no wings, only broad, shimmering membranes that ripple in peristaltic waves, snapping their tails with loud, wet cracks as they dart to intercept floating clusters of spore-like, self-illuminating midges. 

The environment operates at a restless, accelerated clip. There is no stillness here: moisture condenses in seconds, sheets over zero-friction basalt, strikes a freshly extruded ledge, and drains into deep, singing vents, while the pylons pulse in slow, deep sub-frequencies that make the teeth ache. The space is vast enough to feel solitary, but bounded on all sides by towering, sheer curtains of raw, uncarved stone that climb upward into the dense, warm mist—a pocket of frantic, high-altitude generation where the world is being manufactured faster than the elements can break it down.
