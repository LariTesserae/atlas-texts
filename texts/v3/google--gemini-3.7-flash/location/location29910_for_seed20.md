The water does not fall from above; it occupies the high altitude as a violent, continuous body, suspended in a pocket of broken gravity between three razor-sharp needles of newly cleaved basalt. This is an upland ocean compressed into the volume of a single, colossal cathedral nave, perched thousands of feet above the lowlands, boiling with warm, deafening turbulence.

The rock is brutally young. The spires of black stone have not been polished by the torrent; their edges are fresh, fractured glass, snapping under the hydraulic pressure with dull, percussive cracks that join the greater noise. Great blocks of obsidian and raw, cooling crust stand vertically in the murk, their surfaces jagged enough to sever bone at a touch. Between these teeth of stone, the brine churns in a closed, frenzied loop—not rushing down the mountain, but boiling inward, folding over itself in massive, twisting cataracts that rip upward toward a drowned ceiling before crashing back down through the center of the chamber.

The sound is not an ambient background; it is a physical mass. It is a blinding, continuous 120-decibel roar of cavitation, hydraulic shock, and the scream of trapped gases shearing against submerged cliffs. The water is so saturated with pressure waves that vision blurs from the vibration of the eyeball itself; every surface shudders, and the liquid hums like the inside of a struck bell that never decays. 

It is profoundly dim, lit only by the faint, diffused twilight that filters down from the storm-choked sky above the liquid dome, and by sudden, violent flashes of triboluminescence—sparks of cold blue light born deep in the stone whenever the basalt fractures under the churning weight. In these flashes, the scale reveals itself: a space no larger than a few hundred paces across, yet vast in its emptiness. There is immense negative space between the walls, filled only with the violent, opaque green-black of the water and clouds of volcanic grit.

The water itself is thick and body-warm, nearly blood-temperature, dense with dissolved iron and bitter alkalis. It feels heavier than ordinary sea, dragging with the force of wet cement yet tearing past with the speed of a gale. Gravity here does not agree with down; buoyant things are dragged toward the churning center of the vortex, while dense chunks of basalt drift sluggishly upward, caught in thermal upwellings that tear them toward the apex before hurling them into the dark.

Signs of hands are few and baffling. Bolted into the sheer faces of the young cliffs are massive, crude lintels of dressed granite—stone that does not belong to this peak, dragged here before the flood-rupture and lashed to the basalt with thick cords of braided animal hide and crude bronze pins. These lintels are being torn apart. They form no roofs, only skeletal frames and blind doorways that open onto nothing, shuddering against the liquid gale. They look less like dwellings and more like anchors, or the foundations of an altar built on dry, cold heights just hours before the warm sea tore through the sky and drowned the summit.

Life is nearly absent, unable to withstand the shearing forces, save for what is tough, blind, and strange. Clinging to the lee side of the fractured basalt are patches of rubbery, liver-colored lichen, thick as shoe leather, that drink the dissolved minerals and clamp tight against the rock. Occasionally, through the roaring gloom, something long and pale sweeps through the upper cataracts: rib-caged, skinless ribbons of muscle without fins or eyes, propelled solely by the violent contours of the currents, tumbling end over end through the foam, entirely deaf to the roar that fills the sky.

Here, the world is caught in an unfinished transformation. The mountain peak has become an abyss, the air has become an ocean, and the warm, dark deluge continues to hammer the fresh stone into splinters, roaring endlessly into its own self-contained night.
