At this altitude, the sun does not warm so much as it exposes. The light is flat, hard, and mercilessly bright, striking off billions of freshly milled steel surfaces with a glare that leaves no room for soft shadows. 

There is no earth here. Below the boots, below the foundations, below the lowest hanging ductwork, there is only more construction. The continent—if it can be called that—is an open-lattice megastructure of heavy industrial plate, cold-rolled I-beams, zinc-plated catwalks, and pressurized ceramic conduits that stretch into the horizon for hundreds of leagues. Because it is young, none of it has softened. The sheared edges of the girders are still razor-sharp, bearing the iridescent temper-colors of the torches that cut them; the stamped serial numbers across the steel bulkheads are crisp, unpitted by rust, still slick with a micro-thin sheen of clear machine grease.

Despite the scale and the machinery, it is astonishingly quiet.

The air is thin, cold, and heavy with a creeping, condensation-fed dampness. Mist does not rise from soil; it precipitates directly from the chill metal itself. Droplets bead on the undersides of infinite overhanging walkways, gathering on rivets until their weight pulls them loose, falling in endless, soundless lines through miles of open, geometric air. Sound does not carry far; the acoustic density of the latticework swallows vibration like velvet. The massive, house-sized counterweights and slow-revolving flywheel assemblies that span the chasms operate in near-total silence, their bronze bushings bathed in steady oil that muffles every groan of leverage into a faint, sub-audible vibration felt only through the soles of the feet.

Then the architecture forgets itself.

In the middle distances, the industrial logic fractures into impossible recursions. A row of vertical cooling stacks, perfectly straight and bolted with four-inch studs, vents cold vapor upward, only for the plume to drift directly *into* the base of the stack below it. A gantry bridge fifty meters wide extends cleanly toward the horizon, its perspective narrowing normally until it abruptly connects to its own underside at a right angle, carrying a slow-moving train of empty hopper cars that travel simultaneously upside down and right-side up without ever leaving the same plane of gravity. Where two massive structural trusses meet, they sometimes do not touch, leaving a five-foot gap of empty air across which metal filings hang suspended in rigid, crystalline arches, bridging the physical stress through a fold in local space. 

Yet life has claimed these cold, bright geometries with startling speed and abundance.

Because the dampness is constant, a pale, wire-like flora has taken root in the seams where metal sheets are bolted together. It is not true moss, but a dry, fibrous filament—like spun copper wool—that drinks the condensation and traps the airborne grease. 

Woven through this sparse, metallic carpet is an overwhelming wealth of fauna. Millions of small, cold-blooded things thrive in the infinite density of the frames. Swarms of flat-bodied carapace-beetles, their shells mirror-polished and reflecting the blinding sky, crawl along the vertical flanges in shifting, synchronized sheets. They make no sound, scraping microscopic algae from the damp zinc. Above them, nesting in the hollow box-girders and the open throats of silent turbine housings, are vast flocks of ribbon-winged birds. They have no feathers, only pale, taut membranes stretched over needle-thin bones of cartilage. They launch themselves into the glare by the thousands, circling through the recursive loops of the iron sky, diving through gaps in the architecture where the wind blows sideways, snapping up midges that breed in the standing water of drainage channels.

From a high observation platform, the view is dizzying in its openness. You can look out across a valley framed entirely by cathedral-sized pistons, five miles across, descending step by step down an artificial slope of terraced machinery. Far in the distance, through the crisp, unpolluted atmosphere, an entire quadrant of the superstructure can be seen slowly, imperceptibly shifting—a million tons of alloy sliding on grease-ways to accommodate a thermal cycle that takes months to complete.

The air smells of wet iron, cold hydraulic oil, and the sharp, clean ozone of distant, ungrounded static. Water drips everywhere—a steady, rhythmic, delicate patter against hollow plating—while overhead, the glare burns through a sky so pale it seems made of the very same metal as the ground.
