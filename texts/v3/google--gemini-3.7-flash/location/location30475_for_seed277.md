At this altitude, there is no true horizon, only an immeasurable shelf of iron and basalt that stretches into darkness. The atmosphere here is thick and warm, trapped under a permanent ceiling of low-hanging, oil-tinged clouds that seal in the heat rising from below. It smells faintly of boiled brass, wet rust, and sweet, decaying silt.

The land does not feel built so much as forged and then abandoned to geological time. There are no walls, no roads, no human proportions to anything. Instead, the terrain consists of continent-spanning plates—sheets of cold-rolled slag and cast iron tens of miles thick, their edges rounded and softened by eons of steady chemical weathering. In the blackness, these plates heave with a movement so slow it feels like continental drift, though beneath your feet you can register the rhythmic, sub-audible thrum of colossal mechanics: planetary shafts turning in baths of thick grease deep in the crust, venting quiet plumes of warm, humid steam into the night.

Because of this constant venting, the air is never dry. Moisture gathers along the cold lips of the iron terraces, beading in the dark until it breaks and runs in narrow, tepid rivulets down deep drainage gullies. These streams do not lead to an ocean; they gather in shallow, warm pans that puddle across the plateaus, evaporating back into the gloom before they can carve canyons. The mud in these flats is magnetic, heavy, and black, clinging to everything like wet graphite.

Vegetation is sparse, appearing only where the metal has rusted down into coarse, mineral-rich grit. In these shallow soils, clusters of wiry, leafless scrub grow in long, straight rows over buried heat-conduits. Their stems are stiff as fencing wire, tipped with pale, bulbous nodules that slowly sweat water back into the air. Elsewhere, across the sweeping flatlands between the steam-vents, the iron is bare save for a dull, velvety coat of rust-colored moss that feeds directly on the ambient sulfur and dampness.

Despite the darkness and the scarcity of plants, the plateau is alive. The warmth and moisture sustain a vast, blind ecology. 

Things move continuously through the vast spaces. Blind, long-legged grazers with hide like cured leather pick their way over the rivets and expansion joints, lowering spade-shaped snouts to scrape the moss from the iron. In the air above, kept aloft by the steady thermal updrafts of the exhaust fields, wide-winged gliders drift in wide, silent circles. They have no eyes; their heads are dominated by sensory pits that map the electrical currents radiating from the ground. When they drop, they fall like wet cloaks over the smaller crawling things that nest in the labyrinthine under-ribs of the plates.

It is surprisingly quiet for a machine-world. The sheer scale of the landscape swallows sound. The grinding of gears miles below registers only as a dull chest-vibration; the hiss of venting steam is muffled by the velvet lichen and the warm density of the fog. The most distinct sounds are local: the soft *clack-clack* of claws crossing rusted sheet-metal, the sudden splash of a pool disturbed by a drinking animal, and the rhythmic, heavy drip of condensation falling from iron overhangs into the dark below.

Occasionally, the system undergoes an adjustment. A low horn-like resonance will vibrate through the floor, and a plate the size of a mountain range will smoothly tilt three degrees, shedding thousands of gallons of gathered condensate in a wide, cascading sheet that falls for minutes into the black trenches below. The animals do not panic; they lean into the shift, adjusting their footing on the grooved metal, waiting in the warm dark while the water clears and the plateau settles into its next century of quiet motion.
