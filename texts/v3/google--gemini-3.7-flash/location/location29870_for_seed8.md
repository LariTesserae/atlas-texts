The land has forgotten how to be tall. What remains is a world worn down to its deepest bedrock—a vast, sunlit peneplain where millions of years of rainfall have dissolved every mountain into smooth, sprawling shields of ancient pink granite and gray gneiss. The elevation barely registers above the horizon; the whole region is a floor of leveled stone, tilted so imperceptibly that water does not rush anywhere, but simply rests, creeps, and spills across miles of polished surface in sheets only a few inches deep.

The air is thick, warm, and constantly laden with vapor that rises from the shallow expanse under a broad, high sun. The light is generous and direct, bouncing off endless skins of still water to double the sky below. In every direction, the open space stretches out with an exhausting calm—an immense, flat basin where distance is measured only by the pale heat-shimmer and the occasional solitary silhouette of a low, rounded hummock of stone that has resisted the long weathering.

Vegetation here does not crowd. The bedrock provides little purchase for deep roots, and the soil is mostly a fine, mineral silt gathered in joints and shallow depressions. Consequently, the plant life is spaced with a quiet, deliberate geometry. Tufts of wiry, pale-gold sedges rise out of isolated fissures. Here and there, a solitary scrub tree—broad-trunked, stunted, with leaves like thick green coins—anchors itself directly into a fracture, drinking from the perpetual damp beneath the stone. Vibrant rust-colored and olive crusts of algae carpet the submerged flats, bubbling slowly in the warmth, while pale, dry lichens claim the dry knuckles of granite that rise an inch or two above the waterline. There is vast breathing room between every living plant, leaving the landscape dominated by stone, water, and open air.

Yet, despite the sparse plant cover and the immense stillness of the ground, the landscape is intensely loud.

The noise is entirely biological, an unbroken, ringing chorus sustained by millions of small, thriving lives. The warm, sun-baked shallows are an ideal cradle: thousands of species of tiny, pale-skinned frogs rest in the film of water, their vocal sacs swelling like pearl beads, producing a rhythmic, chattering roar that rolls across the flats like distant thunder. Insects rise in shimmering, translucent clouds over the warmest pools—dragonflies with dark, heavy wings, and dense swarms of midges whose combined vibrations generate an electric drone that fills the mid-tones of the air. Long-legged wading birds, heavy-bodied and archaic in form, pace slowly through the reflections, occasionally snapping their broad beaks together with sharp, wooden clacks that echo off the wide stone floor.

Beneath the sound, the physical reality is profoundly slow. Currents move at the pace of a slow walk or not at all. Water drains not through canyons or gullies, but by seeping across vast aprons of quartz sand, collecting in shallow, saucer-like pans, and evaporating back into the warm atmosphere to fall again as evening mist. Sediment settles over centuries into micro-layers of pink feldspar dust. 

It is a landscape that has already survived all its catastrophes. The violent work of uplift, faulting, and carving is long finished. What is left is this luminous, open floor—warm, soaked to its skin, vibrating with the loud, persistent vitality of creatures that need nothing more than an inch of sunlit water over primordial stone.
