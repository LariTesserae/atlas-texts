The drowned cordillera sits in perpetual, freezing brine, its highest crags held four hundred fathoms below any sky. Here, high elevation does not mean thin air, but a crushing hydraulic mass that leans hard against the ridges. The water is near-freezing, heavy with mineral salts and drifting particulate, a dense and viscous dark that presses into every crevice of the granite peaks. 

Though darkness is near-total, the landscape is not still. Sound travels here with violent clarity. It is loud—a vast, percussive symphony of hydraulic friction. Cold currents shear across the saddlebacks and cols like subterranean gales, producing deep, resonant tones that vibrate through the rock. Stalks of towering, iron-ribbed kelp and thickets of siphon-reeds whip and clatter against one another, their hollow chambers snapping like breaking bone under the shifting pressure waves.

Because there are no creatures—no fish to graze the fronds, no worms to bore into the silt, no shells clinging to the stones—the vegetation has claimed the whole dynamic cycle of the plateau. It grows with architectural, deliberate vigor. Great forests of black siphon-weed rise from the summits, rooted deeply into old fractures. These plants do not rely on sunlight; they feed on the heavy chemical broth welling up from the deep faults, cycling pressurized fluid through thick, translucent stems that thrum with inner circulation. They grow wide and sparse, leaving long, open valleys of gray silt between stands of monumental, coiled flora. Some stalks extend upward fifty yards before ballooning into massive, fluid-filled bulbs that act as ballast, swaying in wide, synchronized arcs with the passing swells.

Every few hours, a cold pulse passes over the range—an underwater weather front driven by temperature differentials far below. When it hits, the forest responds mechanically. Seed-pods the size of boulders split along pre-stressed seams, popping open with loud, concussive thuds that send shockwaves ringing through the valleys. Tendrils snap taut; leaves of tough, leathery cellulose curl inward to shed the heavy sediment, billowing like sails before unfurling again in the slack water.

Scattered across this submerged topography are the marks of ancient, manual labor. They are not cities, but sparse, solitary workings of stone and sinew that have long found equilibrium with the sea. 

On the high saddles, where the current is most fierce, stand low, curved breakwaters of unmortared granite. The stones are huge, hand-split with primitive wedges and fitted edge-to-edge to deflect the scouring brine. Anchored to these walls with rotted lengths of twisted vegetable fiber are ballast-weights—smooth, notched river stones brought up or dropped down from some forgotten era, grooved to hold ropes that vanished centuries ago. 

Further down the lee slope, half-buried under settled silt, a solitary stone chamber rests against an outcropping. Its lintel is a single slab of basalt, carved with rough, shallow gouges made by flint or soft copper chisels. Inside, there are no bones or relics, only thick drifts of pale, lime-rich sediment and a cluster of hollow reed-tubes bound with braided bark, preserved perfectly by the freezing, oxygen-poor water. 

The water here never clears entirely. A constant snow of dead vegetal matter and mineral snowdrifts falls from the heights, drifting through the dark between the great black stems. The stone of the peaks is rounded, polished smooth by eons of friction against coarse suspended sands, its forms established, settled, and utterly patient. The mountains hum with the weight of the sea above them, vibrating in the dark as the black forests thrash, flex, and endure.
