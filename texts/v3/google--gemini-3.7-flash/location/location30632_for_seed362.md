The floor is not earth, but a continuous expanse of cold-rolled boiler plate and zinc-cast tessellations that stretch unbroken to every horizon. It sits low in the crust of whatever shell holds it, a sunken continental plain where the atmospheric pressure is dense, dry, and chilled to the temperature of cellar stone. There is no moisture here—not in the air, not in the sap, not in the dust. What circulates through this world is not water, but a thin, clear dielectric fluid with the viscosity of ether, carrying suspended copper salts and dry silicates through vessels that are half-xylem, half-pneumatic tubing.

Above, the sky is a ceiling of sheer, blinding illumination: a continuous, stutter-free daylight cast by megawatt phosphor panels miles overhead, radiating a harsh, cold glare that casts razor-sharp shadows without generating heat. In this brilliant white silence, the vegetation has colonized every rivet, beam, and turbine.

The flora is absolute, dry, and architectural. It does not rot; it cures. Massive, square-profiled trunks of dense, bleached boxwood rise out of cast-iron intake vents, their grains aligned perfectly with the millwork below. These trees have no soft pulp. Their heartwood is cross-laminated and hard as bakelite, layered in paper-thin veneers of dry cellulose that click like playing cards when the chilled draft shifts through them. From their branches hang curtains of dry, foil-like fronds—leaves stamped out with the precision of punch-press gaskets, silver on the underside, brittle and chalk-white on the top. They photosynthesize not by splitting water, but by harvesting the ambient static charge and the fierce, steady phosphor glare, converting high-voltage radiation directly into dry carbohydrate lattices.

Between the trunks, the undergrowth is a dense, interlocking weave of lacquered reed-work, corrugated fiber-ferns, and brittle brambles composed of drawn copper wire clad in corky cambium. Everything grows along the logic of manufacture. Roots do not meander; they track conduit channels, sleeve themselves neatly through conduit pipes, and tap into ancient, ungrounded busbars to draw current. A hedge will grow as a solid, milled baffle thirty feet high, its surface a self-assembling lattice of honeycomb cells, entirely devoid of dampness, smelling faintly of heated transformer oil, resin, and dry paper pulp.

Reality slips at the junction points where the plant tissue meets the structural iron. Because there is no organic decay, the boundary between what was forged and what was germinated has dissolved over centuries. Iron beams have sprouted knots and concentric growth rings; wooden struts terminate in functional ball-and-socket joints made of hardened heartwood that lubricates itself with micro-graphite powder. In some sectors, the perspective refuses to settle: aisles of interlocking wooden scaffolding appear to recede for leagues, yet a single dry leaf drifting down from their upper tiers will land immediately at the viewer’s feet, having crossed miles of optical depth without traversing physical distance.

Movement here is constant but hushed. The canopy is engaged in a vast, dry metabolism. Massive counterweighted boughs, sixty yards long, slowly pivot on dry lignin hinges, resetting their leaf arrays to face different sectors of the ceiling light like automated solar trackers. Great piston-like stems of giant dry-bamboo plunge systematically into floor hatches, pumping desiccated spore-dust and dry lubricants through the sub-floor channels before withdrawing with a soft, velvet sigh. It is an ecosystem in perpetual motion—ratchets clicking, dry fibrous tendons contracting, boughs flexing under immense mechanical tension—yet the sound it produces is almost entirely muffled by the infinite padding of dry leaves, dead air, and sawdust. The ambient noise never rises above a faint, electrostatic whisper.

Fauna is nearly nonexistent, and what lives here is barely distinct from the architecture or the brush. Occasionally, something with the dry, multi-jointed gait of an oversized lathe-cut mantis will emerge from a grove of square timber. These creatures are constructed of the same dried hornbeam, steel rod, and mica-shale as the undergrowth; they do not eat in a conventional sense, but browse on the cold lead solder of exposed junction boxes, their movements jerky, stop-motion, and utterly silent. When they pause, their limbs lock into place with the mechanical certainty of a set brake, disappearing instantly into the dense forest of framework and foliage.

The ground stays level, low, and infinite. Looking across the expanse under the frozen brilliance of the phosphor sky, the eye travels across endless tiers of dry, chalky-green terraces, boxwood towers, and churning wooden transmissions, all perfectly dry, all functioning with the cold, rhythmic indifference of a clockwork forest that has forgotten its makers and needs no rain.
