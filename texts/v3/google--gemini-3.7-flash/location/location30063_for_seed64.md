Tucked into a sheer, vertical fracture near the pinnacle of a frozen needle of slate—miles above the cloud deck, where the atmosphere thins to an icy, anhydrous rasp—lies a sealed pocket no larger than the hold of a small cutter. 

It is completely packed. There is no empty air here, only a dense, interlocking weave of anhydrous flora grown over, through, and around the heavy cast-iron carcasses of industrial machinery.

The vegetation here has never tasted water. Its sap is a cold, heavy aromatic ester, viscous as lubricating oil, moving through vascular networks of hair-fine glass and spun sulfur. Thick, dry fronds the color of bruised graphite crowd against liver-colored mosses that feel like raw felt. Velvet-tufted growths, completely dry to the touch, spill from every rock seam, carpet the iron footplates, and mat together until the floor is a yielding, fibrous floorboard several feet thick. Fern-like structures with segmented, mica-sheathed blades overlap in layered shingles, so densely clustered that one cannot part them without snapping dozens of brittle, powdery stems. 

Wedged into the narrow throat of this cleft are the soot-blackened flanks of stationary industrial engines—reciprocating steam-less compressors, heavy iron linkages, and copper-ribbed dynamo housings bolted directly into the ancient, weather-smoothed granite. These machines are not dead, nor are they breaking down. They operate in an absolute, self-contained equilibrium. Driven by deep geothermal heat-pipes that pierce the mountain's core, their counterweights rotate and their heavy steel rocker arms nod in a relentless, immovable rhythm. 

The sound is continuous and massive: a deafening, rhythmic thrumming that shakes the teeth in one’s skull. It is the low, percussive *clack-whir-thump* of dry gear trains meshing under heavy loads, combined with the continuous roar of pneumatic pressure circulating through a closed loop of copper tubing. Because the air is bone-dry and cold, the noise does not echo with wet resonance; it hits like a solid, vibration-heavy weight, deadened by the suffocating mass of dry moss and dry leaf-litter.

Light exists only in the lower registers. A dull, copper-red twilight filters down from the narrow fissure above, where the sky is an empty, dark indigo, but inside the pocket, illumination comes from tiny, mica-shielded pilot burners and faint, static electrical discharges crawling across the fuzzy margins of the oil-ferns. The glow picks out the gleam of grease, the gray dusting of spores, and the polished steel surfaces where dry leaves brush continuously against spinning flywheels without catching or tearing.

Everything has achieved a permanent state of balance. The iron does not rust; there is not a drop of moisture to oxidize it, only a microscopic drift of dry, waxy pollen that coats the metal like a fine preservative. The plants do not wither, sprout, or drop seed; their crystalline cell walls are permanent, sustained by the steady convective draft and the friction-heat of the bearings. No beetles burrow in the fibrous peat; no mold blooms in the shadows. 

It is a crowded, roaring closet of green-black iron and gray-purple foliage, perched at the top of the world, vibrating forever in the dim, dry cold.
