The air in the Sub-Basin Sump is as thick and heavy as wet wool, suspended at a constant, fevered ninety degrees. It is an enclosed municipal terrace—perhaps three city blocks wide and sunk forty feet below the pavement grade of the surrounding district—where the low-pressure runoff and thermal exhaust of an entire manufacturing quarter pool before draining away. Overhead, the sky is completely occluded by a lattice of iron-truss skyways, dripping cat-walks, and immense corrugated baffling plates that catch the rising heat and drop it back down as an uninterrupted, scalding drizzle.

Everything here weeps. The walls are cast-iron revetments and slab concrete, black with moisture and furred with heavy sheets of liverwort and aquatic moss. Water does not sit still; it is pumped, sloshed, channeled, and atomized. It gushes in thick, rust-tinted sheets from rectangular sluices set high in the masonry, tumbling into wide, brick-lined holding basins that take up the center of the terrace. From beneath the drowned floorplates comes a continuous, shuddering mechanical pulse—the subterranean stroke of displacement pumps working against the river’s back-pressure. The sound is immense, filling the ribcage: the deep, cyclical *thud-clack* of brass valving, the rushing roar of tons of warm water spilling over weirs, and the high, sharp hiss of steam purging from overhead pressure-relief elbows every few minutes.

Despite the heavy, organized engineering, the basin is remarkably empty of human motion or animal life. Not a bird nests in the overhead ironwork; no fish move in the warm, mineral-slicked pools, where the pH shifts unpredictably between vinegar-sharp and chalky alkaline. Instead, the void has been claimed entirely by a luxuriant, aggressive flora that feeds directly on the heat and the chemical wash.

Great elephant-ear aroids and enormous ribbed ferns jut horizontally from the mortar seams between stone blocks. Their leaves, some ten feet across, tremble under the steady percussion of falling condensate, glistening with a slick, oil-sheened sheen. Thick, fibrous vines the color of wet hemp climb the copper conduit bundles, wrapping the hot pipes in pale green, fleshy sleeves. These plants grow at a speed that is almost visible; their roots search out the sweating seams of the pipework, prying apart lagging and drinking the hot condensation straight from the bronze fittings. 

The light is a perpetual industrial gloom. Daylight filters down only as a bruised, greenish shadow through the dense tangle of upper girders and wet leaves. What illumination exists comes from occasional cage-protected filament globes, glowing a dull, sodden amber, and the sporadic, brilliant flare of open pilot burners along the gas-relief conduits. These lights do not pierce the mist so much as create localized halos of boiling yellow and white vapor, leaving vast expanses of the floor in dim silhouette.

Between the massive, solitary monoliths of the pump-houses and the elevated valve towers, there is a surprising amount of open water-space. The flooded streets are wide, straight channels of grey-green liquid, flanked by empty sidewalks of raised diamond-plate steel, long oxidized into shades of plum and ochre. There is a strange, steady equilibrium to the decay: the metal does not collapse, because the constant flow of lime-rich hot water coats everything in a hard, protective mineral crust—a stone skin growing over iron skeletons.

Every quarter-hour, a major intake cycle initiates somewhere in the dark foundations. The water level in the sunken plazas drops three feet with a sucking, resonant groan that echoes off the enclosing walls, exposing stepped brick channels choked with pale, waterlogged roots. Then, with a concussive thump of pneumatic rams, the backwash returns, foaming white and hot, surging back up the stairways to lap once more against the thresholds of the bolted, windowless control-bunkers. The ferns bend flat under the surge, right themselves as it recedes, and continue to transpire into the loud, dripping dark.
