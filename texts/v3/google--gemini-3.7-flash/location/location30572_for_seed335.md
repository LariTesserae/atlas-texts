A sudden, jagged rift opens beneath the heavy foundations of the works—a cold, hollow pocket of raw stone where the bedrock fractured clean and never got filled.

The scale of the place is small, an intimate void dropped forty feet below the lowest floor of the works, but inside it feels sparse and hollow. It is mostly air, water, and sheer, razor-sharp faces of dark basalt that broke away recently and haven't had the time to round down. The rock edges are still sharp enough to bite through leather. Between these black, angular walls, the space is surprisingly empty: no catwalks, no brick reinforcement, no handrails. Only a few heavy, frost-rimed cast-iron intake pipes breach the ceiling, shuddering in their sleeves, plunging straight down toward a black sump pool at the bottom.

Everything here is cold, raw, and fiercely wet. The air sits at just a few degrees above freezing, dense with a clinging, particulate fog made of condensed steam and pulverized cooling-water. Water weeps constantly from the ceiling joints and sheets down the newly sheared stone in thin, glass-like films. It gathers on mossy ledges, falls in heavy, rhythmic drops, and drains through the deep gravel floor with a hollow, churning gurgle. 

The noise is immense and all-enclosing. From above comes the deafening, rhythmic thrum of scaled industry—the low-frequency sledgehammer pulse of stamp mills, the hydraulic whine of pressure pumps, and the continuous, resonant groan of steel under load vibrating straight through the bedrock. Every drop of water hits the stone in a space that is already ringing like the inside of an iron bell.

Despite the cold and the dark, plant life has taken hold with startling speed. Wherever the stone offers a horizontal notch, thick mats of cold-tolerant liverworts, water-logged ferns, and pale, sponge-like mosses thrive under the persistent mechanical drench. They grow in vibrant, lime-green and bruised-purple clumps, fed by the constant wash of aerated, mineral-rich runoff. Long, fibrous root-veils hang free in the empty air, drinking the fog.

The light is dim and indirect, filtering down through narrow grated drains and floor-hatches high above. It arrives in muted, amber-and-slate shafts, illuminating the churning mist and catching the slick bodies of the animals that inhabit the drop. 

Fauna thrives here in chaotic, high-turnover cycles. Large, flat-bodied salamanders, pale as parsnips and nearly blind, clamber across the moss cushions, hunting water-striders and thick-shelled caddisfly larvae that cling to the submerged iron intake pipes. In the sump pool, where water is kept in constant, violent circulation by the sucking pipes, shoals of small, blunt-headed minnows dart through the turbulence, feeding on grease flakes, microbial flakes, and wind-drifted chaff shaken loose from the machinery floors above. Little white-bellied dippers—small birds that slipped in through the exhaust vents—flit between the jagged rock ledges, diving into the frigid, churning plunge-pools without hesitation, their sharp calls completely swallowed by the thunder of the engines.

The place never rests. Pressure pulses through the rock every few seconds, shifting the condensation paths, sending sudden sheets of icy runoff splashing across the ferns. It is a fresh, accidental biome, entirely wild and self-contained, thriving in the cold, wet shadow of an engine it does not know exists.
