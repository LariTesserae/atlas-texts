It exists entirely inside the hollow, fist-sized cavity of a dead pelagic shell, wedged into the silt three thousand meters beneath the thermal ceiling. To look into it from outside would reveal nothing more than a scarred nodule of black calcium; to be within it is to stand on the terrace of a soundless, monumental metropolis, built at the scale of a thimble, yet containing the infrastructural density of an empire.

The water here is heavy, near-freezing, and entirely stagnant in terms of currents, though it shivers constantly with the wake of minute movements. It is cold enough that water behaves almost like a solid medium, viscous and dense, holding every suspended particle in a slow-motion drift. There is no sound at all. The pressure has crushed vibration down to a non-entity; collisions happen in utter, absolute silence. When a colonnade fractures or an archway collapses under the weight of newly laid mortar, it does so like salt dissolving in oil, without a single click.

The architecture is urban, layered, and strictly municipal, yet entirely devoid of tool-marks or metallurgy. It is a city constructed of fresh, embryonic biological masonry: cured mucus, calcified regurgitations, and interlocking combs of translucent, bone-cold chitin. Because it is freshly founded—scarcely hours or days into its expansion—the edges of the buildings are soft and raw. The facades have not yet weathered or eroded; they are still weeping their setting-gels. Scaffolding made of stiffened cilia crisscrosses the narrow vertical alleys, supporting soaring tenements that rise forty or fifty tiers within the span of three inches.

A pale, sourceless twilight permeates the chambers, filtered down through the semi-translucent shell walls and augmented by the faint, cold luminescence of blue-green fluid weeping from municipal conduits. These conduits run like aqueducts along the eaves of the high quarters, carrying dense, nutrient-heavy brine from chamber to chamber. 

The density of life is suffocating. Millions of millimeter-long architects—blind, pale-limbed arthropods and soft-bodied siphonophores—move along the causeways in strict, relentless patterns. They do not resemble wild fauna so much as a populace caught in the middle of a frantic, generational mobilization. Thousands are clustered on the newly raised lintels of civic domes, their mandibles kneading wet lime into geometric bricks; others haul long chains of silica needles through the thoroughfares, weaving them into the foundation of new bridges that span the stagnant rifts between shell-ribs.

There is almost no plant life—only a sparse, velvety frost of chemotrophic fungal threads that coats the lowest gutters, feeding on the runoff of the construction. These threads do not wave; they stand stiff and brittle in the cold, acting as biological pavements along which thousands of tiny, many-jointed porters scurry. 

The dynamism of the place is constant, silent, and frantic. While one quarter is being raised, an adjacent block is being hollowed out by specialized dissolving-castes whose excretions melt away obsolete chitin to make way for broader avenues. Predation occurs as a civic function: specialized scavengers move through the lower thoroughfares, enveloping dead builders before their carcasses can foul the water, incorporating their husks directly into the wet masonry of the rising city walls.

The air—or rather, the pocket of brine within the shell—tastes of dissolved lime, bitter proteins, and intense cold. Every cubic millimeter is occupied: by a spire, a scaffold, a suspended bridge, or a body in transit. It is a place of terrible, organized industry, growing blindly in the dark, building a vast and intricate civil order that will never know light, heat, or sound.
