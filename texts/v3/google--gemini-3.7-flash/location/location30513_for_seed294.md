The water here is not an ocean with a floor of sand and silt; it is a high, drowned plateau whose entire ground was laid down by hands or instincts that left no other record. Millions of square leagues of fitted, mortarless masonry—slabs of dense, iron-gray basalt and calcified shell-rock the size of houses—form the only substrate. There is no natural earth beneath them; if one were to peel back the blocks, there would only be more courses of stone descending into the black. 

Yet despite being entirely constructed, there is no machinery, no metal, no conduit, no spark of artifice that survived. It has been submerged in freezing, high-altitude water for so long that the millions of sharp edges have been rounded down into smooth, undulating saddles and domes. The megaliths have been worn by an eternity of scouring into the shapes of river-smoothed bones, ancient and heavy, with wide, empty avenues of deep water stretching out between them into the cold twilight.

The light at this depth is a permanent, bruised indigo, filtered down through hundreds of fathoms of mountain water. It is dim, offering only silhouettes at fifty paces, but the water itself is violently clear, free of green algae or organic murk. There is no vegetation whatsoever—no kelp, no sea-grass, no velvet moss clinging to the stone. The masonry is scoured completely clean, polished to a dull sheen by the frantic motion of the liquid.

Above and across these vast, sparse expanses of stone, the dynamics are relentless and chaotic. While the soundscape remains muffled—reduced to low-frequency hydraulic groans, deep sub-bass thuds where currents clash against sunken pylons, and the faint, constant hiss of micro-eddies—the physical force of the water is in perpetual upheaval. Invisible, ice-cold rivers carve through the open volume, colliding at unpredictable angles to form vertical shears, violent downward drafts, and spinning vortices that can seize an expanse of stone and sweep it bare in seconds. Slabs weighing fifty tons, loosened by eons of erosion, occasionally lurch and tumble down the sweeping inclines, tumbling silently into the dimness with only a dull shockwave to mark their passage.

Because there are no plants to anchor the food chain, the ecology is driven entirely by the turbulent drift. The cold water is thick with pale, non-photosynthetic life that feeds on deep detritus and mineral snow carried by the down-drafts. Vast, loose shoals of blind, translucent rib-fish sweep across the vistas, their bodies flashing pale white as they bank hard to navigate the violent cross-currents. Clinging to the lee side of the ancient stone blocks—where the tearing current cannot shear them off—are thick, shuddering colonies of pale, jointed scavengers: porcelain-white crabs with limbs like needles, rubbery multi-armed brittle-stars that interlock their limbs into living nets to strain the freezing drift, and great, solitary cephalopods, chalk-skinned and heavy-mantled, that anchor themselves into the deep grooves between the masonry blocks and wait for the currents to deliver their prey.

Looking outward across the high terrace, the vista opens into vast, empty spans of blue-gray gloom. Every few hundred yards, a solitary megalithic arch or a cluster of massive, worn pillars rises toward the surface, standing like the vertebrae of a leviathan against the moving void. The architecture feels deliberate, solemn, and hopelessly distant from its original purpose, now serving only as hydro-dynamic obstacles around which the freezing currents tear, foam without air, and spin away into the dark.
