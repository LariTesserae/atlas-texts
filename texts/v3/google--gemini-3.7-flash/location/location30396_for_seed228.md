The vault sits three hundred meters beneath the surface cap, hollowed out to the dimensions of an inverted cathedral, its ceiling lost in a blinding, self-luminous vapor that never settles.

It is violently, continuously bright. The light does not come from fixtures, but from the air itself: a persistent, ionized brilliance caused by a set of dormant field-stators anchored into the deep bedrock. They hum with a deafening, sub-audible pressure that rattles the marrow, over which lies the roaring, chaotic smash of falling water. 

Water enters from everywhere above—not as delicate seeps, but in broad, heavy sheets that peel off the lip of the overhead basalt and crash into the lower floor. The chamber is hot, hovering at the temperature of a fresh fever, thick with an aerosol of mineral-heavy spray that coats everything in seconds. Where the water hits the floor, it does not pool peacefully; it is caught in the invisible, erratic torque of the field-lines. Torrents of water are violently sheared sideways, whipped into violent standing waves, flung upward in sheets that turn to luminous dust before splashing back against the worn walls.

The stone here has lived with this violence for centuries. The rock is a dark, iron-rich aggregate, but it has been eroded into strange, parabolic curves—deep, polished flutes and swooping flumes that look sculpted by a lathe rather than natural drainage. The edges are entirely rounded, smooth as wet bone. Embedded in this sculpted stone are the sparse remains of the mechanism: ribs of a dense, pale, non-metallic composite that refuse to weather, protruding like the clean-picked vertebrae of a prehistoric leviathan. These structural remnants do not rust; instead, they hum in sympathetic vibration with the water, vibrating with enough amplitude that droplets striking them atomize with sharp, snapping cracks.

Vegetation exists only in the marginal zones of slightly lesser violence. Thick, rubbery sheets of a cadmium-yellow lichen-analog cling to the leeward sides of the composite ribs, anchored so hard into the micro-fissures that the spray cannot tear them loose. In the deep hollows beneath the polished stone flumes, where water circulates in boiling, aerated whirlpools, thin ropes of an oily, black weed whip back and forth, feeding on the excess charge in the water. 

There is little life here that moves on its own. Occasionally, across the high, wet shelves where the spray turns into a steady, warm run-off, pale, many-jointed things—blind, heavily shelled isopods the size of a hand—drag themselves through the slime, wedging their flat bodies into stone crevices the moment the chamber’s magnetic pulse shifts pitch.

The scale of the place is contained—perhaps eighty meters from the eastern terrace to the terminal drop where the water escapes into the lower crust—yet the sensory density makes it feel bottomless. It is a closed system of pure kinetic release: water converted to mist, mist charged with light, light breaking on stone that has been beaten into perfection by an engine that has forgotten what it was built to power. The noise is total, a white wall of sound that obliterates thought, leaving only the rhythm of the water tearing itself apart against the bright, impossible heat of the floor.
