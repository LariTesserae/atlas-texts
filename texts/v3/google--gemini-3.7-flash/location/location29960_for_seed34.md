The plateau sits at an elevation where the sky feels thinned and immediate, a shelf of granite so wide that its edges disappear into their own localized weather systems. Spanning this entire high expanse is a city of stacked, dry-laid stone—not a ruin, but a massive, continuous settlement of low-slung dwellings, tiered causeways, and tightly baffled courtyards that stretches over hundreds of square kilometers, unbroken by open country.

The air is cool, carrying the clean, sharp bite of altitude, but it is perpetually saturated. Great banks of cumulus and hill-fog roll across the rooftops on an hourly cycle. When the cloud deck lowers, the masonry drinks the vapor until it is black with moisture; when the cloud lifts, bright, raw sunlight hits the wet flagstones, turning the entire vast basin into a shimmering field of reflected light. Water does not pool statically here; it is constantly in transit. Every roof is slightly pitched, every terrace carved with narrow, shallow troughs that guide the condensation downward. 

The sound across leagues of stone is extraordinarily quiet. The sheer scale of the architecture swallows echoes, dampening the environment into a soft, steady murmur of draining water: the faint hiss of moisture running down sheer vertical blocks, the rhythmic drop-drop into recessed cisterns, the quiet slip of rills through street gutters. There are no engines, no clatter of metal, no hum of power. The entire urban fabric relies on gravity, weight, and the precise fitting of megalithic granite and slate. 

Because of the altitude and the cold, dense stone, plant life is sparse and disciplined. It does not overgrow the city. Instead, it exists in the joints: black and sulfur-yellow crustose lichens adhering to the north faces of walls, thin lines of dark liverwort in the seams of drainage channels, and occasional tufts of stiff, wire-like sedge rooted in the silt traps of unused alleys. There is no canopy, no brush, no true soil—only stone, water, and the slow, cold biology that can anchor itself to bare rock.

The layout is intensely dense. The buildings are pressed together with minimal gap, sharing thick party walls designed to resist the high winds of the plateau. Narrow stair-streets, barely wide enough for two people to pass, plunge downward through successive terraces, linked by lintels and heavy stone buttresses that bridge the alleys overhead. From a high vantage, the metropolis resembles a fractured geological formation as much as an inhabited place—a labyrinth of flat roofs, stepped parapets, and open collection basins that mirror the sky.

Fauna is sparse, adapted to the wet stone and silence. Now and then, a single broad-winged bird—dull gray and silent—drifts out of a cloud pocket, riding the updraft along the outer terrace wall before disappearing into the upper mist. Small, translucent-skinned isopods navigate the film of water on the shadowed sides of the cisterns, grazing on the film of algae that coats the stone beneath the water line.

The landscape is in constant, quiet motion driven by the mountain’s atmosphere. Morning brings a sharp drop in temperature that precipitates thick dew, turning every wall into a weeping surface. By midday, intense, unfiltered sunlight burns through, setting the streets steaming with rising vapor while the deeper runnels continue their cold, gravity-fed descent toward the distant, unseen drop-offs at the plateau’s rim. It is an enormous, enduring world of wet rock and quiet air, balanced between the permanence of stone and the restless movement of mountain weather.
