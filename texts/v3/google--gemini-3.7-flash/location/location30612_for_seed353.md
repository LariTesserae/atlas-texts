The air does not hold moisture; it holds friction. It is a dry, choking heat, hovering just below the flashpoint of scorched wood, trapped inside a vast high-altitude terrace or sealed industrial mezzanine that stretches beyond sight into thick, suffocating dark. 

There is no sky, only the black underbelly of ancient iron ceilings miles above, hung with sagging gantry networks, cable bundles thick as tree trunks, and dormant exhaust flues. Beneath this ceiling, the terrain is entirely engineered—and entirely choked with living carbon.

This is an ecosystem of pure, waterless botany, thriving directly upon and within an endless industrial carcass. The plants here do not drink; they conduct. Their tissues are woven from dense silicate threads, braided graphitic fibers, and dry, crystalline resins that metabolize the relentless ambient heat and the low-frequency electrical leakage of the steel frame. They form a continuous, wall-to-wall matting—a solid carpet of ironwood, wire-grass, and petrified vascular brambles that covers every square inch of cast-iron flooring, filling the machine sumps and swallowing the catwalks.

In the near-total darkness, vision fails within a few yards, finding only the faint, dull infrared bloom of superheated engine blocks and the rare, ember-red friction-scars where shifting branches grind against spinning drive-shafts. 

Despite the darkness and the absence of any breathing creature, the landscape is in a state of frantic, violent turbulence. The growth cycle does not take seasons; it takes minutes. Dry, fibrous tendrils—hard and splintered like tempered wicker—whip and twist out of the joints of steel pressure-valves. They swell as they absorb the furnace heat radiating from deep underground turbines, lengthening in aggressive, jerky thrusts. They knot into massive, load-bearing trunks within hours, forcing their way between hydraulic pistons and split-casing gears, wedging plates apart with dry, mechanical leverage.

Because there is no water to provide turgor pressure, the plants rely on internal tension, like coiled clock springs. When a branch grows too thick or absorbs too much heat, it does not rot; it carbonizes, calcifies, and fractures with a muffled, heavy *thwack* that shudders through the iron floorboards. Whole thickets of black, needle-leafed scrub collapse into piles of hot, aromatic charcoal, instantly crushed beneath the next surging wave of dry creepers.

The soundscape is strangely muted, swallowed by the sheer density of the vegetation. The enormous churn of this habitat produces no loud echoes—only a dry, restless rustling, like billions of broomstraws dragged across rough iron, punctuated by the rhythmic, low-frequency thrum of buried machinery and the occasional snap of stress-fractured timber. 

Everything has been worn smooth by unimaginable age. The metal beneath the root-systems is pitted and rounded by eons of friction; the non-functional cranes and collapsed ore-chutes have been ground down by the ceaseless climb-and-die cycle of the graphitic vines into gentle, mounded hummocks of compressed charcoal dust. There are no sharp, newly broken machine edges; the plants have filed them down with silicate bark.

You can trace the contours of ancient factory floors—high-elevation assembly bays, rail switchyards suspended thousands of feet above the unseen planet core—only by the way the flora arranges itself. Over the buried dynamos, where the heat reaches searing levels, the plant mass thickens into impenetrable, blistered domes of black amber and iron-bark. In the drafty, slightly cooler gaps between the structural pylons, the growth flattens into tight, abrasive turf made of billion-strand copper-moss that hums with static charge.

Nothing rots. Nothing dampens. The air smells intensely of toasted linseed, ozone, dry graphite, and the hot, metallic breath of an engine that has run without oil for ten thousand years.

As you move deeper into the dark expanse, the scale of the mechanical-botanical war reveals its ancient equilibrium. The machines cannot die because their thermal cores are inexhaustible; the plants cannot be starved because the heat is their light and their rain. The two have braided into a single continuous substrate, tearing each other apart and rebuilding each other in the pitch-black, dry, baking silence of the high shelves.
