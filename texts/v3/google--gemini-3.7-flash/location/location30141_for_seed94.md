The high plateaus do not begin where the world ends, but where its weight changes. At five thousand meters, where the atmosphere ought to thin into barren gravel and blue chill, the air is instead thick with cold, pressurized steam. A continuous ocean of cloud catches against the ragged basalt ramparts of the Great Sump-Chains, piling up until the mountain valleys become reservoirs of pure, cool vapor.

It is an ancient, saturated massif of colossal proportions. The rock itself has long since softened into rounded, elephantine shoulders, carved out by eons of dripping water into smooth-lipped flutes and amphitheaters. But beneath the emerald fleece of wet peat and club-mosses lies an underlying skeleton of iron. 

Centuries or epochs ago, this vast uplift was fitted with deep-bore industrial conduits: massive cast-iron cyclinders the size of cathedral naves, reciprocating balance-beams three miles long, and riveted zinc aqueducts that span the gorges like fallen bridges. No one lives here to tend them; there are no roads, no towns, no footprints. Yet the machines still work, driven by deep geothermal pressures and the sheer hydrostatic head of the mountain itself. They do not roar; they breathe with an immense, muffled patience. A brass valve forty feet across vents a feather of tepid mist with the sound of a soft intake of breath; three valleys away, a submerged iron piston settles downward through a shaft of black water, causing the bog beneath one’s feet to tremble slightly, a low vibration felt in the knees rather than heard in the ears.

Everything is packed to suffocation with life that has adapted to cold, unending moisture. Mosses six feet deep cushion every surface, soaking up water until the hillsides weep from every fold. Dwarf junipers, twisted by ancient winds, are entirely clad in hanging curtains of pale green usnea that brush against the ferns below. There is no empty space. If an iron pipe emerges from the cliff face, its corroded seams are immediately packed with tiny, fleshy stonecrops, liverworts, and violet-leafed saxifrages that drink the grease-rich condensation weeping from the joints.

Water moves everywhere, but quietly. It runs under the moss in subterranean veins; it trickles through the flanged joints of steam-engines; it hangs in the air as a fine, cool drift that settles on the eyelashes without ever breaking into a distinct downpour. Because the moss is so deep and the air so saturated, the ambient sound is extraordinarily dampened. The world has the acoustic quality of a vast, carpeted hall. The crash of a distant water-fall sounds only like dry paper tearing; the slow, cyclical *thrum-chug* of a steam-accumulator deep within a granite rib is reduced to a rhythmic pulse, like the heartbeat of an animal sleeping beneath the soil.

The fauna here have evolved in the quiet pockets between the iron and the mist. Slender, long-toed deer with slate-colored, water-repellent fleeces pick their way across the zinc pipelines, grazing on the rich aquatic blooms that grow in the heated run-off gutters. In the warm condensation pools around the cylinder heads, pale mountain salamanders—some the length of an arm—bask in the mild water, their gills waving like crimson ferns. Flocks of short-winged, heavy-bodied birds with down as thick as beaver pelt nest inside the dry exhaust chimneys, their low, hollow whistles echoing through the piping like flutes played through wet felt.

Light falls through this high world with a pale, silver clarity. The sun rarely appears as a distinct disk, but the clouds themselves are radiant, holding the daylight and diffusing it so evenly that there are almost no shadows. Everything—the glistening wet ferns, the dark oiled iron of exposed flywheels, the white spray of trickling water—is rendered with a crisp, cool definition.

It is an ecosystem locked in a perpetual, self-sustaining loop. The machines pump subterranean warmth and water to the heights; the mountain cools it into clouds; the clouds rain down into the peat bogs; the peat filters the water back into the deep iron intake-wells. Nature and archaic metallurgy have achieved a seamless, unhurried equilibrium, high above the dry lowlands, humming quietly in the cold mist.
