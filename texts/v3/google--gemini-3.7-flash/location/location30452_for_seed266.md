At this altitude, the air does not bite; it bakes. It is an elevated world of bleached chalk plateaus and red-iron mesas rising miles into a pale, dry sky, where the heat is retained by the stone long into the thin nights. There is no moisture to speak of—no clouds form, no dew gathers on the iron railings, and the occasional dust-devils carry only fine, talc-dry silt and shavings of mica.

The land is carved into colossal vertical geometries. Canyon mouths open five miles wide and drop out of sight into heat-shimmering abysses, their sheer faces smoothed by eons of steady, dry winds. These are old rocks, settled into their shapes long before the engines were anchored to them. Across the vast gaps between the massifs, horizon lines run straight and uninterrupted for hundreds of leagues, broken only by the occasional needle-spire or the massive, solitary silhouette of an extraction town clinging to a cliff face like a cluster of hard-shelled barnacles.

These settlements are sparse, separated by hours of empty cable-transit across the gulfs. They are built of bolted cast-iron, dry-laid shale, and dense yellow brass that has tarnished into a matte velvet. Heavy industrial architecture defines them: tiered retorts, counterweighted lift-towers taller than cathedrals, and wide, open-mouthed smelters that process the dry mineral veins of the plateau. Great iron pipes, three men high, run along the rock faces, carrying pressurized gasses—heavy, sweet-smelling hydrocarbons and sulfurous vapors harvested from deep volcanic vents within the mesa roots. These gasses drive the slow, piston-driven heartbeat of the settlements. Flywheels the size of houses turn with a steady, hushed rhythm, turning once every few minutes, their motion so deep and measured that it feels less like machinery and more like the shifting of tectonic mass.

Despite the total absence of greenery—no moss, no scrub, no dry lichen—the cliffs and the air between them are thick with life. It is an ecology built entirely without water, sustained instead by the chemical currents of the vent-plumes and the dense dusting of organic mineral salts that coats the rock.

Herds of pale, many-jointed lithovores, their shells resembling cracked porcelain, move across the sheer vertical walls on padded, pneumatic limbs. They scrape the sulfur crusts with hardened keratin rasps, their movements unhurried, almost meditative. Above them, drifting on the massive thermal updrafts generated by the sun-baked canyon floors, glide creatures vast as ships. They have no feathers; their lifting surfaces are translucent sheets of amber-colored membrane stretched over hollow, silicate-dense bone. They steer with heavy, articulating tail-fans, banking through the empty air without a single wingbeat for days at a time, sifting the hot updrafts for drifting spore-pellets and airborne mineral flocs.

In the shadows beneath the industrial gantries, smaller things flourish: scurrying dry-beetles with shells like polished hematite, blind glass-wasps that construct brittle, hexagonal hives out of regurgitated quartz sand, and serpentine leapers that hunt along the cable-runs, their bodies dry and cool to the touch.

Sound here is muted, swallowed by the sheer scale of the space and the thinness of the warm atmosphere. The immense foundries do not screech; they sigh. You hear the far-off, rhythmic *clack-thrum* of a cable-car pulley miles away, the dry rattle of shale dislodged by a grazing herd on a ledge half a league distant, and the ceaseless, low whistling of the warm wind through the lattice-work of iron bridges. 

Everything moves on long arcs of time. A cable bucket laden with copper ore takes half a day to traverse a single span between two peaks. The sun tracks slowly across the pale, unclouded sky, casting long, sharp-edged shadows from the industrial stacks across miles of empty stone. The heat rises in steady, shimmering waves, and the city hums its low, mechanical drone, anchored immovably between the stone and the sun.
