High up in the structural dead-space behind the number four calcining flues, three hundred feet above the valley floor, the air is thick, dry, and ninety-five degrees Celsius. It is a pocket no wider than an armspan in most directions, wedged between raw, unshaped slag-curtains and the vast, blind flank of an industrial exhaust manifold. 

There is no rain here, and no moisture has touched these surfaces since the furnace banks were lit decades ago. Any water that drifts toward the flue intake is instantly atomized into dry superheated vapor and carried away. What remains is a world of bone-dry mineral powder, scorched scale, and unweathered iron casting. The edges of the steel reinforcement ribs and the basalt blocks are needle-sharp; they have suffered no wind-borne sand, no frost-shattering, no chemical decay from rainwater. They exist in an embryonic, unsoftened state, precisely as they were poured and fractured, their corners pristine beneath a velvet coat of soot.

The heat is immense and constant, radiating from the steel skin of the flue with an unrelenting, iron-smelling force. It pushes against the chest like a physical weight. Yet this pocket is neither dead nor empty. It is packed nearly to bursting with a dense, dry ecology that has turned the chimney’s radiant envelope into a crowded sanctuary.

Every square inch of the basalt shelf and the pitted steel bracing is occupied. Thick mats of blind, flat-bodied dermestid beetles and high-temperature fire-brats overlap three and four deep, their brittle, chitinous bodies clicking against one another in an endless, slow-motion press. They do not scurry; the environment is too crowded for speed. Instead, they shift like dry grain in a hopper, a living, rustling carpet that climbs up the perpendicular ribs of the furnace skin. They graze on the microscopic crusts of dry sulfur, sublimated zinc vapor, and the endless fallout of their own shed exoskeletons, which dry to ash within minutes of being cast off.

Above them, tucked into the narrow crevices where the expansion joints meet the raw rock, long-legged pseudo-scorpions and pale, desiccated geckoes cluster in tight, communal knots. Their skin is paper-dry and tough as parchment, lacking any sweat glands or mucous membranes. They breathe the scorching air through spiracles fringed with fine dust-filters, conserving every fraction of internal lipid. When they move, they step carefully over the shifting floor of beetles, plucking out the slowest individuals with deliberate, heat-dulled efficiency. 

The sound in the chamber is colossal, though it rarely varies. The industrial flue behind the plates is a column of rushing fire and forced-air draft, producing a deep, continuous sub-bass drone that vibrates the marrow of the bone and sets the dry carapace plates of the swarm into micro-vibration. Layered over this mechanical roar is the dry, papery friction of millions of tiny legs scraping across cooled slag—a sound like autumn leaves being continuously ground into powder beneath a heavy stone.

Light scarcely penetrates this high junction. A weak, orange-red luminescence leaks through the microscopic fissures around the bolt-housings, casting long, sharp-edged shadows across the huddled fauna. It is a gloom lit mostly by the dull, infrared throb of the machinery itself. 

Change here is measured in long, unhurried arcs. The heat never drops; the furnace runs continuously on seasonal refining cycles, maintaining an unbroken thermal plateau for months at a time. The air currents are sluggish, trapped in this blind vertical slit, moving only by the faintest convection off the furnace wall. Generation upon generation of chitinous life is born, hardens, feeds on mineral dust, and crumbles to fine grey powder without ever leaving this three-meter corridor of hot metal and raw, unsoftened stone.
