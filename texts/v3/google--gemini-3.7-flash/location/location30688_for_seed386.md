At this altitude, where the air should be thin and frigid, the heat instead rises in vast, wet thermal columns that carry the weight of an unbroken greenhouse. The peaks are colossal: sheer, fluted columns of black basalt and ancient oxidized ironstone that rise thousands of meters above hidden valley floors, their summits piercing through layers of boiling mist into a harsh, bright tropical sky. 

The heat is not merely solar; it is metabolic, driven from the deep interior of the range. Wedged into the chasms between the stone towers are the remains of a vast, unremembered industrial anatomy—not structures or dwellings, but colossal mechanical organs that have become part of the mountain’s tectonic function. Giant cast-iron cylinders, wide as amphitheaters and thick with orange scale, are embedded directly into the vertical cliff faces. Massive counterweights and articulated iron struts, long ago fused by mineral crusts and wet rust, span the gorges like fossilized bridges. Deep within the rock, these ancient assemblies still breathe: every few minutes, a low, shuddering stroke resonates through the stone, releasing plumes of clean, scalding vapor from vents lined with manganese bloom.

Because of this constant mechanical exhalation, water exists everywhere in active transit. It weeps from the hanging joints of the rock, sheets across the flat iron plates of long-dead boilers, and condenses on every leaf until the entire landscape sounds like a soft, perpetual percolation. The air is so thick with moisture that distant spires do not fade into blue haze, but into a luminous, shimmering white where the bright midday light scatters through atomized water.

Vegetation has swallowed the vertical world with aggressive, heavy growth. Tree ferns with trunks as thick as millstones anchor themselves into the seams between stone and rusted plate, their fronds spreading five paces wide to catch both the sun and the rising steam. Enormous climbing arums, with leaves waxy and split like ribs, wind around iron drive-shafts and tectonic rivets, their aerial roots dripping meters down into the open air before disappearing into the mist below. Hanging bogs of sphagnum and thick liverwort cling directly to the perpendicular basalt, soaked through like saturated sponges, occasionally tearing away under their own wet weight to plummet in silent, green avalanches into the unseen depths.

Despite the monumental scale and the persistent, low-frequency hum of the deep hydraulic mechanisms, the mountain range is remarkably quiet. The falling water muffles sound, deadening the calls of the wildlife into short, isolated notes. Pale, broad-winged gliders—reptilian or avian, their skin slick with water—drift between the towers without flapping, riding the hot updrafts that boil out of the iron flues. In the moss cushions along the cliff ledges, small, blind crabs with translucent carapaces pick through the iron-rich silt, feeding on the sulfurous algae that thrives wherever a steam seam touches the stone.

The rock itself shows the deep balance of long weathering. The iron elements are not fresh; their edges are rounded by centuries of condensation, their surfaces stained in brilliant streaks of ochre, burnt umber, and copper turquoise that run down the dark volcanic stone like dried pigments. The water has carved precise, fluted channels through both metal and rock alike, treating the industrial slag and the native basalt as a single, unified geology.

From the high ledges, the view opens across miles of these wet, steaming pinnacles. They stand like the pillars of an unroofed temple, connected only by drifting clouds, sagging webs of lianas, and the immense, iron bones of an engine that has forgotten its purpose, now serving only to pump heat and water into a sky that never dries.
