The water here is not an empty medium, but a dense, grinding paste of deep cold and suspended mineral mass. Miles beneath the surface, where sunlight was extinguished countless ages ago, an immense benthic basin spreads out—an ancient plain of basalt that has been ground down by eons of unrelenting hydro-friction until every ridge, peak, and spire has been smoothed into flat, dull-shouldered pavements.

It is a world packed entirely to its margins. The water column does not clear; instead, it is permanently saturated with a chaotic, violent blizzard of pulverized shell, dense silt, black sand, and scouring gravel. This is a perpetual turbidity storm—a deep-ocean tempest that never breaks the surface, driven by immense internal waves and cold thermal cataracts plunging down from polar shelves. The fluid is thick, heavy with salt and particulate, possessing the dragging inertia of wet concrete yet moving with vicious, swirling speed. Eddies the size of cathedrals spin and collide in the dark, shearing against one another, lifting tons of sediment from the floor only to slam it down elsewhere in roaring, fluid avalanches.

Despite the ferocious kinetic energy, the sound is smothered into near-silence. The crushing hydrostatic pressure deadens acoustic sharpness; there are no crashes or clatters, only an all-encompassing, bone-vibrating infrasound—a rhythmic, subterranean thrum that is felt directly as a vibration in the gut and marrow rather than heard by the ear. 

The light is virtually non-existent, lingering at the threshold of absolute blindness. What little visibility occurs comes from the brief, cold spark of disturbed dinoflagellates and pressure-stressed bioluminescent bacteria caught in the churning slurry. These pale, greenish-blue sparks flare and vanish in fractions of a second, briefly outlining the rushing currents like smoke caught in a draft, revealing the sheer physical density of the moving water.

Ecology in this violent expanse is sparse, specialized, and built to endure grinding abrasion. Nothing tall can survive. There are no kelp forests or branching corals; any structure extending more than an inch into the churning current is sheared away. Life exists as a thin, tough veneer cemented into the micro-fissures of the smoothed bedrock: encrusting black sponges as hard as vulcanized rubber, flat calcified tube-worms whose shells are polished smooth by the passing gravel, and translucent, armored isopods that grip the stone with recurved hooks, feeding on the rich organic drift whipped along by the storm. Occasionally, a pale, blind grenadier fish sweeps through the turbulence, muscular and eel-like, navigating the chaotic vortices by lateral-line sensation alone, its scales thick and mucus-coated to shed the scouring grit.

Scattered across the scour-channels are the rare, heavy relics of a world long forgotten above: dense river-stones grooved with ancient tool-marks, basalt sinkers once lashed with twisted gut to primitive nets, dropped into the sea millennia ago. They have tumbled down the continental slopes into this deep basin, rolling endlessly along the bedrock until they are as round and smooth as cannonballs, their carved notches nearly erased by the tireless polishing of the deep-sea gale.

The place exists in a state of hyperactive, perpetual equilibrium. Nothing settles, yet nothing is destroyed completely. It is an ocean floor reduced to its most durable, primal components, endlessly reshuffled in the freezing, lightless churn of a storm that has outlived epochs.
