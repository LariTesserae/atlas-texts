The water is cold enough to ache in the marrow, yet it remains completely liquid, held just above freezing by a merciless, unyielding pressure. It sits atop a vast planetary uplift—an oceanic plateau raised miles above the world’s true baseline, where the atmosphere ends abruptly at a mirror-flat surface, allowing a clean, blinding daylight to drive straight down through miles of flawless, unclouded brine. 

Because there is virtually no sediment, no soil, and no suspended drift of dead plankton, the visibility is immense. One can look horizontally through the turquoise-white volume for dozens of miles before the light finally dissolves into deep prussian blue. In all that sheer, transparent expanse, there is almost nothing to break the sightlines. It is an ocean that feels like a void.

Rising from the depths are the crowns of young tectonic massifs—jagged, knife-edged ridges of black basalt and crystalline granite that have known no rain, no river channels, and no life to soften them. Their edges are freshly cleaved, razor-sharp fractures dating to an instantaneous geologic uplift. No moss, kelp, or lichen coats these stones. They are utterly barren, clean-shorn mineral faces plunging straight down into abyss-trenches that drop out of reach of the light. 

Yet the silence one expects of such an empty blue void does not exist. The water is deafening. 

Sound travels through this hyper-dense, frigid medium with brutal efficiency, carrying the collective, unending roar of heavy industry across thousands of square leagues. Miles apart, anchored directly into the raw basalt crags by iron pylons thicker than redwood trunks, sit lone industrial stations. These are monolithic, riveted caissons of black iron and zinc-plated alloy, housing deep-cycle compression pumps, submerged rotary turbines, and electrified intake manifolds. They do not whistle or hiss; they hammer. The cavitation of their gargantuan bronze impellers creates a continuous, earth-shaking thrum—a low-frequency concussive vibration that presses against the skull from every direction at once. When a pressure valve cycles thirty miles away, it rings through the water column like an iron bell struck with a sledgehammer.

These machines serve no visible masters nearby. They are solitary, separated by leagues of empty blue water, connected only by thick, sagging cables that trace catenary arcs across the chasms. They operate in a state of rigid equilibrium. There are no shifts in output, no day-and-night cycles of power, no seasonal shutdowns. The turbines have turned at the exact same RPM for centuries, maintaining an unvarying current that holds the great basin at its precise, frozen balance.

Despite the total lack of plant life, the water is not lifeless. Fauna thrives here, adapted to an ecosystem built entirely on mineral-rich thermal discharge and electrical micro-currents. Great schools of translucent, armor-plated chordates—fish with ribcages of flexible horn and broad, mirror-scaled flanks—drift through the open water between the mountain peaks. They do not swim with haste; they glide on broad, motionless pectoral fins, their bodies acting as acoustic resonators for the industrial thrum that fills the sea.

Higher up, near the bright, flat surface where the sky is visible as an unbroken ceiling of pale white, vast colonial siphonophores drift. They stretch for hundreds of feet: delicate, segmented chains of pale pink and silver gelatin, devoid of chlorophyl, feeding entirely on the sheer abundance of blind, iron-shelled crustaceans that graze upon the machinery hulls. 

Occasionally, one of the great solitary scavengers passes through the open void—creatures with vast, barrel-shaped ribcages of uncalcified cartilage and wide, milky eyes that require no darkness to see. They navigate along the submerged acoustic corridors, steered by the acoustic map created by the deafening turbines. They swim near the machine stations, nudging against the humming iron plates to scrape off the crusts of zinc oxide and mineral salts that form around the electrical grounding nodes.

Everything here is permanent, bright, and loud. The light never dims; the cold never deepens into ice; the water never clouds; the engines never fail. It is a world of pure, suspended suspension—a high-altitude ocean of knife-sharp rock and deafening iron, where life drifts through vast desert-volumes of water, perfectly at home inside a perpetual, daylight roar.
