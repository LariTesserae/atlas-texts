At seven thousand meters above the unseen sea, the air should be thin and freezing, but here it is thick as broth, hot as a greenhouse, and impossibly sweet with the scent of wet mulch and crushed pulp. 

This is the Upper Sump: an immense, sprawling archipelago of sandstone needles and colossal fluted massifs that pierce through the low clouds and keep climbing until their tips touch the deep, black-violet rim of the mesosphere. By every law of planetary physics, these peaks should be barren, scoured by dry jet streams. Instead, they are drowning in warmth. Invisible gravitational compression fields—tethered by faint, monolithic toruses of seamless, gunmetal-dark alloy clamped around the midsections of the spires—hold a localized, hyper-dense atmosphere pinned to the stone. The pressure here matches a sea-level swamp, and the heat rising from the deep geothermal veins within the spires turns the heights into a vertical rainforest suspended in the sky.

The scale is vast and yawning. Looking out from any ledge, the view does not drop down to a floor; the base of the world is lost thousands of feet below in a perpetual floor of golden, sunlit steam. What exists are columns—hundreds of them, some as wide as whole towns, others slender as lances—standing in an immense, sun-drenched void.

Water is everywhere, but it moves with an altered, lingering weight. The mist never quite leaves; it coats the stone in a continuous, glittering skin that weeps downward in slow, fat rivulets. In certain zones, where the containment field’s field-lines warp, waterfalls do not plunge—they spill over cliff edges and break into slow-motion curtains of heavy, pea-sized droplets that drift horizontally across the chasms, drawn by thermal drafts until they collide with an opposing cliff and nourish another wall of moss. 

The stone itself is ancient and settled. Long, rhythmic cycles of condensation and plant-acid have carved gentle, fluted gullies into the dark red rock. Massive root-nets have knit the cliff faces together, their grey-brown bark thicker than cathedral pillars, anchoring forests that grow perpendicular to the stone. Great, saucer-leafed bromeliads cling to vertical overhangs, each reservoir holding dozens of gallons of boiling rainwater where pale, blind crustaceans paddle among the algae. Higher up, closer to the sunlit crowns of the spires, dense groves of broadleaf ironwood and hanging curtains of iridescent, blue-veined lichen form dense, multi-tiered canopies that hum with life.

And the sound is relentless. It is not the roar of wind, but the deafening, layered chorus of an impossibly dense biomass. Billions of micro-cicadas inhabit the moss cushions, vibrating at frequencies that make the moisture on one's skin tingle. Through this continuous high-pitched sizzle comes the deep, hollow booming of bladder-throated quadrupeds that cling to the underbellies of the spires with six-toed suction pads, calling across the five-mile voids to potential mates. Their calls roll through the warm canyons of empty air like slow thunder, reverberating against the wet stone.

Occasionally, high overhead, where the green canopy breaks and the sky darkens into the purple edge of space, one can see the faint, shimmering lensing of the world’s quiet machinery. It does not smoke or flicker; it simply keeps the air where it is. Small, ancient observation hulls—no larger than fishing boats, overgrown with trailing ivy and sealed in glass that has turned amber with age—hang utterly motionless between the spires, long abandoned by whoever tuned this biome, suspended on dormant buoyancy nodes.

Below them, iridescent gliding rays with wings of stretched, vascular skin bank lazily through the warm thermals, drifting from one spire’s forest to the next. They glide through the horizontal rains, disturbing plumes of pollen that hang in the gold light like dust motes in a cathedral. 

Nothing changes quickly here. The heat is steady, day and night, held by the thermal inertia of the compressed air and the deep warmth of the stone. The forests drink the infinite mist, grow fat, drop their leaves into the abyss to feed the lower roots, and slowly, over centuries, crack the rock a millimeter deeper, perfectly balanced in an engineered infinity.
