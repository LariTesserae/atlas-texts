The basin sits under a flat, pale ceiling of cloud that neither breaks nor darkens. It keeps the air trapped, warm and thick as breath, holding the temperature at a steady, oppressive tepidness that never cools. Everything is wet, not from driving rain, but from a constant, ambient perspiration; moisture condenses continuously on every surface, sliding down raw faces of pale, sharp rock to gather in shallow, interlocking pools that cover the valley floor.

There is no soil here, and nothing resembling a tree or a leaf. The ground is a freshly precipitated floor of calcium crust, chalky silts, and brittle shale, unsoftened by roots and unrounded by age. The edges of the stone are razor-sharp and embryonic, looking as though they were shattered or laid down only days ago, though they have existed in this exact arrangement indefinitely. There are no seasons to scour them, no freeze-thaw cycles to split them, and no wind strong enough to grind them down. 

Across this white, mineral basin stretches a city of immense proportions and primitive architecture. It is built entirely of unworked, hand-fitted stone slabs, baked mud, thick shell-lime mortar, and the massive, curving ribs of wetland beasts. The structures do not rise high—rarely more than two or three low-ceilinged levels—but they are packed so tightly together that the settlement functions as a single, porous organism. Alleys are little more than narrow, wet crevices between sheer mud-and-stone walls, roofed over with slate to keep out the heavy, dripping mist. Because there is no timber, lintels and roof beams are made of stacked flagstones or vast, pale animal bones lashed together with rawhide strips that remain perpetually slick and supple in the damp air.

The population is dense, moving along raised causeways of piled rock above the shallow, warm water. Their tools are simple and heavy: adzes of flaked black chert, hammers of dense river-cobble, scrapers made of split bone. Yet despite the simplicity of their gear, the scale of construction is regional. The low, labyrinthine sprawl extends for miles across the basin, terrace after terrace stepping down toward a wide, slow-moving sheet of warm, mineral-rich water that never rises and never drains away.

The noise is total and deafening. It is a solid, physical pressure that fills the ears until the vibration is felt in the teeth and ribcage. Part of it is the human work—the unending, rhythmic *tock-tock-tock* of thousands of stone hammers chipping at raw shale, the sloshing of millions of footsteps through the flooded lanes, the low shout of voices channeled through narrow stone corridors. But the greater part belongs to the fauna. 

Without plants to anchor the food chain, the warm, shallow water is thick with animal life feeding on mineral slimes, benthic crusts, and each other. Millions of pale, heavy-shelled crustaceans, some as small as pebbles, others broad as shields, crowd the shallows, their hard legs and mandibles clattering against the rock bed in an unending, gravelly roar. Amphibious, thick-skinned quadrupeds crawl in dense packs across the mud banks and between the foundations of the stone huts, wheezing and bellowing through wide, throat-sacs to establish territory. Vast clouds of blind, soft-bodied midges churn through the warm mist, their collective wing-hum merging with the clatter of the crabs and the percussive industry of the stoneworkers into a single, uniform wall of sound that drowns out individual thought.

Light filters through the cloud layer evenly, casting no hard shadows. It is a dim, pearlescent glow that reflects off the sheen of water covering the rocks, the slick backs of the mud-dwellers, and the wet, tallow-rubbed hides stretched over doorways. There is no sunrise to anticipate and no nightfall to fear; the luminous grey overhead remains fixed, sustaining a perpetual, warm noon-twilight. 

In this basin, the world has arrived at a finished state without ever having aged. The mud does not dry, the sharp fractures of the stone do not dull, the water never recedes, and the deafening chorus of stone and shell never drops by a single decibel. It is a vast, crowded engine of mud, bone, and life, completely enclosed within its own unchanging warmth.
