The stone here is brand new, so recently broken from the crust that its fracture faces are still sharp enough to shear wool. There are no smoothed corners, no gravel aprons softened by centuries of wash, no rounded boulders in the bottoms. The rock is a pale, blue-tinted diorite, split along clean, glassy planes that rise like the ribs of a cathedral spanning only a few hundred yards across. It is an elevated pocket—a high mountain cleft held between three sheer, upward-thrusting blades of stone, isolated from the world below by sheer altitude, open to the cold, pure brilliance of an unclouded upper sky.

Despite the raw, un-weathered youth of the rock, it is thoroughly inhabited by architecture. 

A vertical settlement clings to the sharp geometry of the chasm: tiers of dry-stacked masonry, immense timber bracing lashed with treated sinew and boiled bark, and hanging walkways of split cedar that step upward through the void. It has the layered density of a mountain city—terraces, cisterns, drying racks, lintels, and interlocking causeways—yet the heart of the space is hollow. The buildings do not fill the cleft; they frame it, skirting the perimeter walls in steep, stacked arcades, leaving the central drop wide, empty, and yawning. One can look across three hundred paces of thin, cold air from a high kitchen platform and see the chisel-marks on the opposite wall, naked and distinct under the sharp daylight.

Water moves through the site constantly, though there is no single river. It seeps directly out of the fresh seams in the rock—ice-cold, mineral-rich, condensing out of the high-altitude mists that brush the peaks and weeping through the stone’s micro-fractures. The walls are perpetually slicked in a shining, moving skin of water that spills over the chasm’s faceted ledges in thin sheets. 

This moisture feeds an explosion of low, cold-adapted greenery. Wherever the rock offers a horizontal terrace or a timber beam has caught silt, the vegetation takes hold with astonishing vigor. Thick cushions of emerald liverwort, fat-leafed alpine succulents, and curtains of pale hanging moss drape over the hand-hewn stone balustrades. Ferns with black, wiry stems sprout directly from the joints between cyclopean blocks. The lushness is damp, heavy, and quiet against the rock, smelling of crushed parsley, wet slate, and raw sap.

Yet the place is far from quiet. The acoustic landscape is immense and deafening. 

The geometry of the cleft acts as an acoustic horn. The constant drip and sheet-flow of water does not patter; it collides with the unsoftened, glassy floor hundreds of feet below, compounding into a continuous, percussive roar. Gusts of high wind shear across the upper needles of stone, catching in vertical flutes and hollow masonry conduits to produce a deep, droning resonance that vibrates in the teeth. Wood creaks under the tension of counterweighted lift-platforms; water sluices through hollowed log-flumes with hollow clatter. The noise is steady, rhythmic, and vast, making spoken conversation impossible without leaning close to an ear.

Animal life is sparse and cautious. There are no birds nesting in the high eaves; the air churns too violently in the chasm mouth. Occasionally, a heavy, velvet-bodied moth will drift down through the light, or a solitary, pale-skinned skink will pause on a sunlit beam to drink from a droplet before retreating into the dry-stacked stone. The humans who built this place move with the deliberate, slow economy of people who work at high elevations, hauling dressed stone with wooden winches, tending the terraced moss-gardens, and checking the tension of the hemp guy-wires that keep the settlement pinned against the embryonic mountain.

Nothing here is finished. The mountainside feels as though it cooled and split only yesterday, and the city upon it is being raised by hand, block by heavy block, in the bright, deafening, cold air between the cliffs.
