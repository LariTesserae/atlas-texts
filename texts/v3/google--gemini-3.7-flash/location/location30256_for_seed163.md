The basin sits at eight thousand feet, caught between raw, jagged crests of freshly thrust granite that have not yet had time to soften or round. But the rock itself is almost entirely hidden beneath a profound, unbroken mattress of living plant matter. It is a world of high-altitude bog-tundra—not fragile, but massive, thick, and suffocatingly complete. Deep cushion-plants, pale sphagnum, liverworts, and tough, fibrous sedges form a continuous, springy mantle two to three meters deep over the fractured bedrock. It squelches and heaves underfoot, an immense green skin holding millions of tons of cold water in place through capillary grip alone.

Above this mantle, the light is ferocious. The air at this altitude is thin, dry, and clean, allowing the sun to strike down with an unmitigated, white-hot brilliance, even though the ambient temperature barely climbs above nine degrees Celsius. The light catches the continuous sheen of surface moisture, transforming the whole valley into a field of blinding, silver-green glare. Every blade of sedge and every bead of condensation on the dwarf shrubs acts as a minute prism; looking across the wide, sparse floor of the basin requires squinting against a radiant blanket of reflections.

What dominates the basin before anything else is the sound. It is nearly deafening—a relentless, colossal roar composed of two distinct forces locked together. 

The first is the water. The basin is an enormous sponge tilted slightly toward a southern notch, shedding constant run-off through thousands of concealed rills beneath the moss, which converge into roaring, unchanneled cascades that spill over raw bedrock scarps. The second is industrial. Scattered across the basin at great distances from one another—hundreds of meters of empty, glowing peat between them—are heavy, unpainted industrial extraction and pumping facilities. 

These are not delicate weather stations, but brutish installations of cast iron, riveted steel, and galvanized pipework, anchored straight into the hidden stone with thick vibration-dampening pilings. They operate under extreme hydraulic pressure, processing the mountain runoff to drive high-head turbines or compress air for subterranean operations down the range. Enormous pressure-relief cowls vent superheated steam and pulverized mist into the cold air with a shriek that combines with the low, thudding rhythm of multi-cylinder pumping engines. The acoustic profile is absolute; it fills the skull, obliterating the sound of wind or voice, vibrating in the chest cavity like a physical pressure.

Between these installations, the space is wide and largely empty. There are no roads—only elevated catwalks made of heavy steel grating, rusted orange by the continuous damp, running in long, solitary lines across the moss. These walkways are slick with slime and spray. Where pipes run across the terrain, the vegetation has simply climbed over them, enclosing iron conduits in thick sleeves of dripping bryophytes. 

The water here is everywhere: bubbling up from pressurised pockets beneath the peat, weeping from cut banks of raw, unweathered gravel, and hanging in an omnipresent, sun-drenched drift of fine spray thrown up by both the natural waterfalls and the industrial exhausts. It is ice-cold, smelling faintly of tannin, wet iron, and mineral dust from the young, freshly fractured stone below. 

Life beyond the plants is minimal. The noise and the sheer, driving damp keep birds away; there are no herds, no small mammals skittering through the moss. Occasionally, tiny translucent midge larvae can be seen in the clearest, calmest pools between the cushion-plants, but otherwise the ecosystem is a closed loop of cold growth, decay, and mechanical throughput. 

Everything happens at a slow, relentless crawl. The iron rusts slowly in the cold; the peat deepens millimeter by millimeter over decades; the jagged fault-lines beneath the sod settle with imperceptible, decade-long groans. Yet the surface remains in a state of high-energy stasis: sun blazing down out of a deep cobalt sky, mist churning in radiant plumes, water gushing through steel and moss, and the vast, industrial roar echoing off the naked peaks that ring the horizon.
