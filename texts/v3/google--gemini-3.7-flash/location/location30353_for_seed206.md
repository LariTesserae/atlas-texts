This is a sump-chamber, no larger than a small foundry yard, suspended within the root-mass of an ancient vegetative engine. 

The space is entirely enclosed by living tissue, yet inside the pocket there is a surprising, vault-like emptiness. Immense structural staves—lignified root-trunks the width of shipping containers—curve upward from a dark, churning floor to form a low, ribbed ceiling. Between these towering structural ribs, the air is open, warm, and charged with moisture. It is a wet warmth, thick with the smell of boiling starch, ozone, and bruised pulp.

There is no stone here, no poured concrete, no iron framework, yet the chamber operates with the brutal, scaled momentum of heavy industry. The technology is botanical, grown rather than assembled, and it has operated in this exact equilibrium for so long that every fiber has settled into its workload. 

High up where the ribs converge, translucent amber xylem tubes—thick as boilers and pressurized to hundreds of atmospheres—carry scalding, copper-tinted sap across the expanse. Where these conduits narrow into valve-like nodules, they hiss with a deafening, continuous exhaust, venting plumes of pale steam that instantly condense on the upper walls and run down in steady, thick sheets. The sound inside the chamber is overwhelming: a deep, rhythmic thrumming of hydraulic vegetable hearts, the steady roar of pressurized fluid rushing through fibrous pipes, and the sharp, rhythmic *clack-thud* of woody intake gates opening and closing in mechanical cadence.

The floor is a broad, shallow basin of black, fiber-rich slurry, slowly circulating in a lazy vortex. It never rises and it never drains; the intake equals the output with perfect, stagnant precision. Rising out of this slurry at measured intervals are three massive, vertical pistons formed of densely compressed cork and petrified vine. They do not turn or pivot; they stroke up and down against the wet root-sleeves with a slow, grinding friction, generating a steady galvanic current that arcs between hanging, wire-like tendrils in faint, continuous blue sparks. 

Because the chamber is sparsely occupied by these few massive elements, the sightlines are clear through the steam. The light is ordinary, diffuse, and unchanging—a flat, daylight-bright radiance emitted by broad pads of bioluminescent fungal skin that line the recessed alcoves between the staves. There are no shadows that move; the illumination is as fixed as the hum of the dynamos.

Life here has adapted entirely to the noise and the power. Across the wet, ribbed architecture, thousands of heavy-bodied, chitinous crawlers navigate the vibrating wood. They are broad and flat, like bronze shields, grazing on the scorched film of sugar that bakes onto the outside of the steam conduits. In the lower air, between the steady arcs of the electrical tendrils, dense clouds of pale, blind midges hover in the rising thermals, undisturbed by the mechanical roar. Small, pale-skinned skinks with suction-tipped toes dart along the wet margins of the slurry, snapping at the flies, their bodies vibrating in resonance with the 60-cycle electrical buzz that saturates the wood.

Nothing in this pocket grows wild or untamed, though everything is alive. The growth has reached its limit; new cell production is precisely offset by the sloughing of dead bark into the central basin. The heat does not fluctuate by a fraction of a degree. The steam never thickens beyond the point where the far wall is obscured, and it never clears. It is an engine that grew itself into being, stabilized its own fuel and output, and now sits in the dark lowlands of the world, cycling its loud, wet, solitary work without beginning or end.
