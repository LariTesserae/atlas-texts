The floor of the trench sits hundreds of feet below the surrounding country, a vast, flat-bottomed fracture dropped clean between parallel scarps of freshly sheared black rock. The fracture is young; the basalt faces have not yet sloughed their sharp edges, and the scree at their bases lies in raw, razor-sided blocks, unrounded by centuries of weather. Cold air, dense and heavy, sinks into this depression and remains there, trapped between the walls in a permanent, pooling chill that hovers just above freezing.

Across this wide sunken valley, the earth is invisible, completely buried beneath a thick, unbroken floor of cold-climate vegetation. It is not a canopy, but a continuous living mattress several feet deep: dense, fibrous carpets of pale peat-mosses, tough liverworts, and rubbery, gray-green cushions of reindeer lichen that have meshed together with the creeping, woody stems of dwarf arctic willow and sub-surface sedges. Walking upon it—if one could cross the immense, open stretches of the trench—would feel like stepping on frozen wool. The plant mass absorbs and conceals the jagged floor beneath it, bridging the deep crevices in the rock with tightly woven root-tangles and dense mats of dormant foliage.

Where moisture exists, it clings as cold condensation. Droplets bead across the waxy cuticles of the plants and accumulate on the undersides of exposed rock overhangs, dripping at long, irregular intervals onto the moss below. In the low temperatures, this dampness rarely evaporates; it sits in the hollows, forming shallow, icy pools between the hummocks of lichen, or freezes into delicate crusts of rime on the windward surfaces.

What dominates the chasm, drowning out the vastness of the space, is sound. 

From several miles down the rift, where the trench narrows into industrial intake zones, massive subterranean ventilation shafts and turbine arrays draw and force air through the cut. The acoustic energy is colossal. It does not arrive as a distant hum, but as a continuous, deafening mechanical roar—a low-frequency thunder that vibrates through the rock walls and shakes the condensation from the moss. The sheer volume flattens perception; it presses against the chest and vibrates in the teeth, a steady, unyielding pressure generated by banks of multi-ton steel fans and pneumatic compressors housed deep in the rock. The sound fills the empty miles of the trench like water filling a reservoir.

Scattered across this immense, loud emptiness are the isolated remnants and nodes of heavy industry. They do not form a town or a continuous facility, but stand alone, separated by hundreds of yards of silent, green-gray plant cover. A solitary steel gantry, bolted directly into the shear face of the eastern cliff, supports a bundle of thick, lagged pipes running vertically into the gloom above. Out in the center of the moss floor sits a single, low-profile compressor bunker made of corrugated iron and cast concrete, anchored with heavy guy-wires. High-pressure steam or cold exhaust periodically vents from its roof stack with a sharp, pneumatic hiss that is instantly swallowed by the overwhelming drone of the primary airworks. 

The lighting is poor and heavily diluted by the cold mist that forms near the floor. Suspended from long steel cables spanning the narrower sections of the rift, a few widely spaced sodium-vapor fixtures cast pools of flat, amber light onto the vegetation below. Beyond these yellow discs, the landscape fades quickly into an ash-gray twilight, where the towering wall of the far cliff is visible only as a vast, dark occlusion against the dim sky.

Animal life is sparse and strictly peripheral. Occasionally, small, heavy-bodied cave moths with pale, fringed wings cling motionless to the cold flanges of the steel beams, indifferent to the violent vibration of the metal. In the deep hollows beneath the root-mats, minute, cold-hardened springtails crawl through the icy humus, living out long, slow lifecycles in the damp dark.

Everything in the trench moves at the speed of the cold. The plants grow by microscopic increments through the perpetual damp; the machinery runs on unvarying, governed cycles, burning fuel or drawing current with monotonous precision; the rock walls shed a single stone every few months. The environment is severe, raw, and rigorously mechanical, a massive subterranean hallway upholstered in living frost-growth and filled from wall to wall with an enormous, industrial roar.
