Inside the upper mass of the ridge, sealed within an unopened suture of the mountain where the rock has never felt wind or weather, lies a pocket no wider than a long hall and barely twice the height of a person. It is suspended thousands of feet above the surrounding lowlands, yet entirely inward-facing, blind, and pitch-black. 

The heat here is steady and blood-thick, rising from deep fractures below to pool in this vaulted hollow. The atmosphere is saturated to its absolute limit; warm moisture does not merely float in the air, it precipitates on every available surface simultaneously. The air is so dense with vapor that breathing feels like drinking warm fog. Water weeps down every facet, not in sudden rushes, but in a continuous, unbroken film that sheets over the rock and drips in heavy, rhythmic cadences from a thousand low points.

Because this pocket has been shielded from frost, sun, and running torrents, the geology possesses no softness or wear. The surfaces are embryonic, freshly accreted from the mineral-heavy condensate. The walls are not worn stone but an interlocking jungle of raw, needle-sharp aragonite needles, thick crusts of blooming calcite, and jagged shelves of raw silicate that have never been blunted by friction. The chamber is extremely crowded—a dense forest of stone teeth, vertical spires, and crisscrossing mineral bridges that leave only narrow, winding gaps through which a body must squeeze sideways. Everything is sharp, crystalline, and untouched by decay.

In the total absence of light, life exists only as pale, tactile presences clinging to the rock. Mats of thick, white bacterial slime drape like heavy wet silk over the mineral spurs, feeding on the warm sulfurous wash. In places, pale tendrils of cave-adapted molds and rootless, subterranean thallophytes form spongy, waterlogged cushions that squelch under the slightest pressure. Fauna is nearly absent, reduced to the occasional slow-moving, blind bristletail or pale, shell-less gastropod navigating the flooded crystal lattices with translucent, quivering antennae.

Yet, despite the absolute isolation, there are marks of ancient, manual intrusion. wedged tightly into the crystalline bottlenecks are short, notched logs of petrified heartwood—primitive climbing rungs, placed by hand generations ago and now entirely coated in a gleaming, glass-like layer of transparent flowstone. In one dead-end crevice, several carefully stacked slabs of raw shale form a low, crude altar or marker, lashed together with split-bark cordage that has hardened under mineral drip into solid rock. Whoever entered this high furnace of a room did so with simple stone and wood, leaving a few sparse markers before the narrow crawlway behind them choked shut with rapid mineral growth.

The sound within the pocket is quiet, dull, and invariant. There are no drafts, no echoes of the outside world, no thunder from the peaks above. There is only the low, ceaseless *tink-tink-plat* of dense mineral water falling into shallow, rimstone pools, and the faint, steady hiss of steam condensing into liquid on the razor-sharp ceiling. Nothing here changes; the temperature does not fluctuate by a fraction of a degree between seasons. It remains a hot, drowned, crystalline lung held in perfect, dark equilibrium inside the summit.
