At this altitude, the heat does not come from the sun, which remains a dull, bruised smudge behind a ceiling of amber-black mist. The heat rises from beneath the flagstones and the iron decks, driven upward through miles of high-montane basalt by deep planetary friction, turning the thin mountain air into a scalding, breathless vapor.

The city sprawls across a high shelf between dead crags—a metropolis built at the scale of an empire, yet constructed almost entirely out of open space. Its avenues are hundreds of paces wide, paved with thick, vitrified slabs that radiate warmth like kiln floors. Colossal iron arches, webbed with riveted trusses and oxidized to a dark, saturated plum color, leap across the void to support nothing but further tiers of empty viaducts. There are no walls to many of the buildings; they are immense, skeletal frameworks of blackened cast iron and porous volcanic stone, rising dozens of stories into the wet gloom, enclosing vast volumes of hot, circulating fog.

Water is everywhere, but it never cools. It arrives as condensation, beading violently upon every cold-cast beam and machined flange, running down vertical struts in unbroken, shimmering braids. It gathers in shallow, scalding sheets across the plazas, flowing slowly toward wide, grilled sluices that disappear into the foundations. The stones do not dry. They weep with a mineral-rich sweat that leaves crusts of pale orange and pale yellow salts where the runoffs pool and evaporate, building delicate, brittle terraces along the curbs.

There is no green anywhere. Plant life never took hold in this sulfurous, superheated atmosphere; instead, biology here is primordial and chemical. The underbellies of the iron bridges are furred with thick, rubbery mats of black and ochre slime molds that pulse minutely with the passage of warm drafts. In the corners of abandoned turbine halls, where the boiling mist collects into stagnant lagoons, colonies of lithotrophic microbes form dense, velvet-textured mantles that range from bruised purple to an intense, metallic copper. 

The silence is vast, given weight by the colossal proportions of the empty streets. What sound exists is heavy, dampened by the thick humidity and swallowed by the sheer emptiness between structures. A steady, rhythmic *tock... tock... tock* echoes from somewhere miles away—the slow, automated stroke of an unmonitored steam siphon clearing its cylinder. High above, the ironwork expands and contracts in long, groaning sighs as thermal drafts roll through the girders. The constant falling of hot droplets onto metal creates a soft, continuous hiss, like a distant rain that never hits dry earth.

Movement belongs mostly to the infrastructure. Deep within the recesses of the open-air arcades, massive flywheels, balanced on jeweled bearings and driven by rising convection, turn with an eerie, frictionless momentum, making a full revolution every few minutes. Counterweighted sluice gates lift and drop on silent oil-damped pistons, managing the eternal overflow of boiling condensate across the terraces.

Very rarely, something alive moves through the mist. They are pale, low-slung organisms with jointed carapaces made of layered calcite, moving with deliberate, glacial slowness across the slick iron. They have no eyes; their front limbs end in wide, rasped spatulas with which they scrape the bacterial scum from the rivets, leaving clean, dark tracks through the orange film. When the steam thickens, they clamp themselves flush to the warm metal and go motionless, indistinguishable from the bolted plates beneath them.

The city sits in a state of long-established equilibrium. Its rust is stable, sealed under layers of mineral wax and condensation; its masonry has been smoothed by centuries of hot runoff into soft, rounded gutters. It is an urban landscape stripped of habitation, functioning purely as a thermodynamic apparatus—a high, dim shelf where heat and water endlessly circulate through empty rooms, slowly encrusting the bones of an industrial age in lime, iron oxide, and sulfur.
