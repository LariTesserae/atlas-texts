Far below the arid crust of the upper world lies the Grand Sump: a subterranean valley several miles wide and twice as long, carved out of deep, dead limestone during an era so distant that the geological memory of whatever carved it has completely vanished. 

There is no running water here. The air is bone-dry and cool, hovering at a constant, cellar-like chill that preserves rather than decays. A fine, pale dust of pulverized chalk and dry spore-hulls coats every horizontal surface, cushioning footsteps with a faint, velvet puff. 

What fills the immense vault is not a void, but a vast, silent skeleton of a city—now utterly drowned in dry life. 

The architecture belongs to a pre-tool era of monumental stonework, where tiered towers, broad viaducts, and cyclopean colonnades were hewn directly from the valley floor and walls. Over uncounted millennia, the sharp edges of this metropolis have been planed away by the steady, patient circulation of subterranean air currents. Stairs have softened into smooth, sloping ramps; lintels sag like weathered dunes; grand plazas have settled into shallow, polished basins. The built density is high—organized street grids and vast multi-tiered tenements march across the floor and climb the cavern’s rising flanks—yet the spaces between these districts feel cavernous, broad, and profoundly empty, like monumental avenues built for giants who never arrived.

Despite the absolute lack of moisture, vegetation carpets the ruins in thick, silvery-green blankets. These are not mosses of wet caves, but ancient xerophytic cryptogams: brittle, felt-like crusts of gray scale-moss, dry papery tapestries of resurrection fern that never unfurl, and thick, tangled briars of wire-weed that anchor directly into the porous stone. They extract trace chemicals from the rising drafts and the dust itself, forming a dry, spongy understory that covers roofs, fills sunken courtyards, and spills over the parapets of ancient aqueducts that never carried liquid.

The light is minimal—a perpetual, heavy twilight. Faint, cool bioluminescence from patches of powdery shelf-fungus provides just enough luminescence to sketch out the monumental shapes in the gloom: the charcoal outlines of square towers, the pale white of ancient plazas, the dark ribs of arches spanning the gloom overhead.

What dominates the Sump, shattering the visual stillness, is sound. 

The valley is an echo chamber of terrifying efficiency, and it is overwhelmingly, deafeningly loud. The noise does not come from machines or storms, but from the astronomical density of dry fauna that has colonized the stone. Tens of millions of pale, armored crickets, each the size of a human palm, scrape their legs in synchronized stridulation that vibrates through the rock and hits the chest like a physical blow. Above them, nesting in the millions of eroded windows and friezes, flocks of blind, featherless cave-swallows churn the air, their dry, leathery wings producing a continuous, thundering roar like a freight train passing through a tunnel. Added to this is the dry, rhythmic clicking of millions of beetles crawling over the stone masonry and the steady hiss of dislodged chalk dust drifting down tiered facades. 

The acoustic volume is so intense it swallows individual noises whole, turning every click, rustle, and wing-beat into an unceasing, white-noise deluge that reverberates off the high, arching ceiling miles above.

Yet beneath this roar, the landscape’s dynamic is glacial. There are no sudden seasons, no floods, no collapses. The temperature does not shift; the light does not brighten or fade. The winged colonies rise, cycle through the vault, and roost in rhythms measured by internal biological clocks, not the sun. Dust falls grain by grain, coating the carapaces of dead insects that dry into hollow husks before blowing into the crevices of buildings that have stood for eons, slowly, patiently becoming the rock they were carved from.
