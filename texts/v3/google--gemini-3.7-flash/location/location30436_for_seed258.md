The horizon here does not belong to stone or soil, because neither exists in this place. Everything across the breadth of this high, warm shelf is fabricated from living vascular tissue, yet none of it was left to grow at random. It has been extruded, braided, and tensioned with absolute geometric intention: a regional expanse of pale-barked arches, colossal fluted pylons, and suspended viaducts that span miles of warm, open air. 

Despite the total dominance of plant matter, there is no forest floor, no accumulation of humus, and no decay. The edges of the timber-structures are sharp and embryonic, as smooth and unmarred as fresh-poured wax or the rind of a melon picked moments ago. There are no scars of weathering or rot; the system exists in a fixed, roaring equilibrium, maintaining its crisp, just-finished edges against the torrents that course through it.

The sound is immense and invariant. It is the roar of millions of gallons of water moving through high-pressure biological plumbing, mixed with the hiss of warm, pressurized mist spraying from millions of pores across the structural members. Water does not fall as rain here; it is pumped from deep interior conduits through the heartwood, jetting out of perfectly circular sphincter-vents along the flanks of the great aqueducts, tumbling in unbroken, white curtains across the empty chasms between towers. The water is clear, lukewarm, and slightly viscous, carrying a faint scent of green starch and crushed reeds.

The scale of the place is vast and uncrowded. The structures do not knot together into a dense jungle; rather, they rise as solitary, monumental presences separated by hundreds of yards of empty, mist-laden sky. A single column may be thirty stories thick—a fluted bundle of petrified-fiber tubes wrapped in a tight, fibrous skin of glistening, lime-colored cambium—rising sheer into the bright daylight before branching into four hollow struts that carry a canal toward the distant horizon. Between these mega-structures, the air is cavernous and open, lit by an even, pervasive sun that makes the weeping walls of the pylons glisten like oiled glass.

There are no tools here, no signs of metal, masonry, or combustion. The joints where massive lintels meet vertical supports are seamless cellular grafts, swollen slightly with structural lignification, bound together by the natural hydraulic tension of the organism itself. Broad causeways, wide enough for armies to march abreast, stretch out into the distance, completely vacant, their surfaces textured like woven rush but hard as kiln-fired tile. Every few hundred paces along these causeways, small, sunken catch-basins drain excess moisture back into the internal circulatory network through pulsating, fibrous grates.

Fauna is extremely sparse. Occasionally, high in the sunlit spray where an aqueduct vents its runoff into the void, one can spot a lone scavenger: a broad, pale-bodied creature with translucent paddles, neither fully insect nor vertebrate, clinging to the wet bark to filter the thick nutrient-sap weeping from the seam. It does not scuttle or hunt; it remains glued in place for hours, practically a statue against the pale green wood, vibrating gently in sympathy with the deep hydraulic hum of the column beneath it.

Because the erosion rate is held at near zero by the self-repairing perfection of the plant-flesh, nothing crumbles, silts over, or deepens. The waterfalls plunge thousands of feet into empty troughs of polished root-wood without wearing a single groove into the basin floors. The air stays steadily warm, heavy with moisture yet moving in wide, lazy drafts created by the thermal mass of the wet timber. 

Looking across the region, the view is one of majestic, unpeopled order. Miles of towering, pale-green arches stand over vast voids of clear air, endlessly weeping bright water into the chasm below, locked forever in the first morning of their completion.
