High on the worn shoulders of the old plateau, where the mountains have long since ground themselves down into rolling, dust-scoured tablelands, there is a fissure that does not open to the sky. It is a long, horizontal hollow—a collapsed bedding plane softened by eons of dry air until its edges are as rounded and smooth as river stones, though no river has run here for ages.

Inside, the light is reduced to an amber, perpetual half-shade. The sun never strikes directly into the throat of the shelf; instead, it catches the white dust of the outer flats and bounces inward, diffuse and warm. The air inside stays thick, dry, and baked, trapped in a pocket that smells intensely of resin, guano, crushed seeds, and the warm musk of thousands of warm-blooded bodies.

Despite the near-total lack of free-standing water, the shelf is choked with growth. Life here does not drink; it hoards. The rock walls are carpeted in thick, felt-like cushions of silver-green xerophytic moss that draw moisture purely from the faint, baking updrafts that rise off the salt-pans far below. Rope-thick vines, gray-skinned and leafless, crisscross the gap from ceiling to floor, taut as rigging. Where they knot together, dense bouquets of fibrous, stiff-petaled air-plants cluster like sea anemones, their surfaces coated in a protective, waxy varnish that catches the dim light with a dull sheen.

Suspended within this dry thicket, filling every terrace and alcove of the ancient stone, is a dense, tiered town. 

It is constructed entirely by hand from what the rock and brush yield. Platforms of bundled ironwood reeds and woven vine-bark are slung between the living pillars of the cavern. Thousands of small, round huts—plastered with a mortar made of dust, mastic sap, and crushed beetle-casings—are stacked atop one another against the inward-sloping back wall. Narrow gangways made of split timber and bound with twisted hair span the dizzying drops between layers. Ladders carved from single lengths of notched taproot connect one level to the next. Everything is built close, shoulder-to-shoulder, utilizing every cubic foot of the pocket, so that climbing through it feels like moving through the inside of a massive, inhabited hive.

The sheer volume of life within this hidden hollow is deafening. 

The cavern floor is lost beneath the bustling lower tiers, where thousands of small, scuttling creatures churn through the detritus. Compact, fat-tailed rodents with coats like dry grass dart across the timber bridges, entirely indifferent to the human feet stepping over them. Above, the ceiling is a shifting, chittering ceiling of roosts: swift-winged cave sparrows, flat-bodied canyon swifts, and broad-eared micro-bats that nest together in tangled communal clumps, taking advantage of the warmth. Their chatter, combined with the dry rasp of millions of dust-beetles grinding through seed hulls in the dark corners, creates a continuous, vibrating roar that echoes off the smooth limestone.

Because there are no streams or rains to bring life to a halt or dictate seasons, the pace of the place is relentlessly steady. Work never stops; it only shifts rhythm. In the semi-darkness of the mid-tiers, people in dyed hide aprons and bone jewelry sit along the narrow ledges, scraping thick, amber gums from harvested root-burls using flakes of chipped flint. Others beat dried seed-pods with wooden flails, raising clouds of fragrant, golden chaff that drifts through the air like pollen before settling on the backs of the lizards sunning themselves along the outer lip. 

Predation and scavenging happen in plain sight, woven directly into the town’s routine. Children flick bone hooks into the vine thickets to haul in fat-bodied locusts, while above their heads, long, whip-tailed hunting lizards traverse the woven underbellies of the houses, snapping up beetles and mice that stray too close to the edge. Nothing is wasted; meat is cured in the warm, desiccating drafts, bones are honed into needles and structural pins, and the hard, enameled wings of burrowing insects are stitched by the hundreds into scale-like cloaks that rattle softly with every step.

There is no sense of youth to this place. The stone itself has been rubbed smooth by untold generations of feet and paws passing over the same sills. The timber foundations of the lowest houses have darkened to the color of horn, petrified by centuries of sap-smoke and animal tallow. It is an entirely self-contained world of dust, friction, and dense vitality, perched thousands of feet above the barren lowlands, humming quietly in the dark warmth of its ancient, hollow stone.
