At this altitude, the air is thin, cold, and entirely devoid of humidity. It does not carry the scent of rain, sap, or loam; instead, it smells of pulverized talc, cold slate, and the dry, sweet musk of keratin that has never known rot.

The terrain consists of the *Thimble-Planes*—a network of planetary-scale pillars and broad, flat-topped massifs that rise far above any lower atmospheric layer. Over billions of years, whatever turbulence once shaped these heights has run its course, grinding the rock down to a final, permanent geometry. The edges of the precipices are not jagged, but worn into broad, soft bevels, like mammoth bones tumbled in an ancient, vanished surf. The ground is a solid pavement of pale, non-porous siltstone, swept clear of loose gravel and settled into an eternal, unyielding equilibrium.

Between these pillars, the sky is wide, open, and pale, lit by a steady, neutral sunlight that neither warms nor scorches. The light does not seem to change angle; it casts fixed, sharp-edged shadows that have occupied the exact same square inches of stone for centuries.

What dominates the expanse is not the void, but the crowd.

The fauna here is saturated to the point of structural impossibility. Millions upon millions of living forms occupy the air, the rock, and the spaces between, yet nothing stirs. They do not migrate, feed, or struggle. They exist in an absolute, waking stasis—a density of life so thick that in many places the stone cannot be seen beneath the interlocking layers of bodies.

They are creatures of dry biology. There is no blood beneath their hides, only packed chalky marrow, fibrous silicates, and cool, static tissue. Great, six-winged ungulates, their flanks touching flank, stand upon the smooth rock faces in rows that stretch for miles. Suspended in the air beside the cliffs, long-bodied serpents with coats of dense, dry down hang motionless at varying elevations, unaffected by gravity, held in the geometric grid of a reality that has forgotten how to let things fall. Above them, thousands of broad, flat-faced flyers hover with outstretched, leathery membranes, overlapping like shingles on a vast, suspended roof. 

Their eyes are open—round, dark, and clear as polished obsidian. They do not blink, because there is no moisture to replenish, and their pupils remain fixed on whatever horizon they caught sight of when the stillness took hold. 

Because of the sheer mass of fur, skin, and down, the world is radically silent. The millions of bodies act as an acoustic baffle of extraordinary power. A spoken word travels only a few paces before its vibrations are absorbed by the soft, unmoving hides of the beasts. There is no wind to whistle through the pinnacles; the only sound is the quiet, dry friction of an occasional loose hair drifting down against a pelt, or the faint, internal ticking of cooling mineral within the plateau’s core.

Tucked into this breathing, motionless tapestry are the sparse marks of human or pre-human hands. 

Small, primitive shelters made of dry-stacked river-stone—carried up from depths that no longer exist—are built directly against the ribs and haunches of the standing megafauna. Because the beasts never shift their weight, a builder can treat a colossal leg as a load-bearing pillar. Cords plaited from coarse, shed under-wool lash simple wooden poles across the backs of two sleeping colossi to form a sturdy lintel. Small niches have been scraped into the chalky rock with hand-held flints, holding small offerings of chipped chert, polished knuckle-bones, and dried, gray lichen that has not grown a millimeter in an age.

Between two massifs, a traveler can cross an abyss thousands of fathoms deep simply by walking across the backs of the floating serpents. The hides yield slightly underfoot—cool, firm, and dry as cured leather—before returning to their exact form. The creatures do not react to the weight; their respiration is imperceptible, their warmth steady and subdued, suspended between life and mineral permanence.

Here, time has achieved its terminal density. The sparse, brittle lichen on the rock faces does not advance; the primitive bone anchors hammered into the beasts’ calcified horns do not loosen; and the millions of dry, cool hearts beat at intervals so far apart that they belong to the movement of continents rather than the rhythm of life.
