The air inside the hollow is heavy and warm, tasting of wet limestone, lime paste, and ancient silt. It is a sunken space, an amphitheater of dense, weeping rock no more than sixty paces from one curved wall to the other, ceilinged by a low vault where condensation gathers into fat, heavy beads that fall at measured intervals.

There is no soil here, and nothing green has ever taken root. The surfaces are entirely mineral: bulbous, shelf-like terraces of travertine and grey dolomite that step down toward the center in a crowded, overlapping maze. The architecture of the stone is round and swollen, smoothed by centuries of warm, mineral-rich seepage that films every surface with a skin of water no thicker than an oil sheen. The floor is packed so tightly with these rimstone pools and calcified domes that there is barely room to walk without stepping from one rounded lip to the next. The pools are shallow—scarcely deeper than a hand’s span—filled with water that is perfectly clear, warm to the touch, and dead calm.

Dim light enters from above, filtering through a narrow horizontal cleft where the ceiling meets the outer slope. By the time it reaches the floor, the light has lost all direction, scattered by the fine mist of evaporation into a uniform, leaden twilight that casts almost no shadows. It gleams faintly on the wet curves of the stone, catching the fine, sandpaper grit of calcite crystals forming along the waterlines.

Tucked into this natural plumbing are the quiet traces of long-standing human presence. They are spare, utilitarian interventions made of the same stone: small weir-dams constructed of knapped slabs wedged into the drainage notches between pools to hold the water deeper; hand-smoothed depressions in the travertine where someone sat for hours in the warm bath; a pair of heavy, oval river stones resting on a ledge, their ends bruised and pitted from pounding mineral crusts. In one of the drier recesses, three stone adzes lie half-buried in a thin crust of lime that has slowly cemented them to the rock floor, their flaked chert edges still sharp beneath the clear mineral glaze.

Life is sparse and pale. In the lower basins, where the overflow trickles sluggishly through hair-thin channels, tiny blind amphipods drift like flecks of ash across the silt-free bottoms. A species of flat, shell-less mollusk, no larger than a thumbnail and the color of wet parchment, clings motionless to the underside of the stone shelves, grazing on the imperceptible bacterial slick that coats the submersed rock.

The space is almost silent. The only sound is the acoustic drop-and-plink of water falling into water, rhythmic and muffled by the soft, waterlogged density of the basin. There is no wind. The temperature does not drift with the seasons above; the subterranean heat and the perpetual seepage hold the chamber in a steady, unvarying equilibrium. A stone knocked into one of the pools ten years ago rests precisely where it fell, already acquiring the rounded, pale crust that will eventually fuse it to the basin floor.
