A high, hanging terrace sits cupped in the side of a pale stone mountain. It is small—perhaps fifty paces from its moss-wrapped rear wall to the smooth, plunging lip that looks out into the sunlit void—yet it feels like an interior chamber. The air here does not belong to the altitude; it is dense, greenhouse-warm, and weighted with moisture that rises steadily from deep fissures in the limestone floor.

The rock is old and thoroughly resolved. Millennia of warm seepage have softened every corner into parabolic swells, melting-wax ledges, and broad, shallow basins that overlap like the scales of a carp. A continuous, glass-thin skin of warm water slides over this floor, moving so uniformly that it creates no ripples, only a trembling glaze that mirrors the sky. 

Despite the lushness of the life that grows here, there is great emptiness between each form. There is no tangle or underbrush. Instead, the floor is an expanse of pale, wet stone, punctuated at wide, deliberate intervals by singular, massive botanical forms. 

Each plant stands alone in its territory of wet rock. Some are thick-limbed, resembling swollen, jade-colored coral, their surfaces covered in a velvet skin that repels water in tiny, mercury-like beads. Others are great, solitary vessels: broad, fleshy leaves fused into seamless cups the width of a wagon wheel, brimful of warm rainwater that has filtered down through the upper cliffs. These plants do not rely on insects or wind to reproduce; they propagate through thick, subterranean rhizomes that pulse invisibly beneath the stone, or by slowly dropping heavy, buoyant bulbs directly into the stone channels.

The silence is absolute, save for the lowest register of physical weight: the rare, soft *plip* of a drop releasing from the tip of an overhanging leaf, or the faint, thermal hiss of vapor escaping a crack in the rock. There are no birds in the upper air, no insects darting over the pools, no worms turning the wet silt in the crevices. The leaves have never been notched by a caterpillar; the water's surface is never broken by a water-strider. The place has the immaculate, suspended quiet of a museum vault, operating entirely through vascular hydraulics and sunlight.

Set into the floor near the terrace’s western curve are the only marks of hand: a series of shallow, linear grooves scored into the soft travertine. They were cut with stone tools so long ago that their edges have been rounded by the water until they appear almost organic, yet their geometry is purposeful. They gently herd the thin sheet of run-off toward a single, hollowed stone bowl. In the corner of the basin rests an ancient hand-adze made of dark river basalt, worn smooth by human palms that have not touched it for centuries. It lies half-submerged in clear water, encased in a micro-crust of pale calcite, perfectly preserved in the steady, unchanging heat.

The light is broad and clean. It strikes the wet floor and turns the whole shelf into a shallow reflector, illuminating the undersides of the great succulents with an amber glow. Time here moves only by the slow accretion of mineral crusts and the patient, millimeter-by-millimeter thickening of the fleshy stems. Nothing rots, nothing rushes; the terrace simply holds its breath in the sun, wet, warm, and utterly vacant.
