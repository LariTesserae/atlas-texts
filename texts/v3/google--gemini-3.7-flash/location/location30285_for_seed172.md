It exists inside the upper expansion manifold of an uncrewed cryogenic fractionation stack—a hollow, sealed chamber hoisted kilometers into the thin, frozen upper atmosphere, though here inside the metal envelope, the outside world is entirely absent. 

The chamber is small, perhaps thirty paces across and towering twice that in height, framed entirely in passivated titanium plates and milled extruded aluminum. There is no dirt, no bedrock, no stone; every surface is a product of high-precision machining, its edges so crisp and un-eroded they retain the micrometer-fine swirl patterns of the cutting heads. Nothing has weathered. Nothing has softened. The joints between the bulkheads are sealed with bone-white ceramic gaskets that have never known moisture.

The cold here is absolute and dry. It is not the damp chill of winter, but a vacuum-pure thermodynamic basement, hovering just above the condensation point of nitrogen. Without moisture, there is no ice, no rime, no fog. The atmosphere is an inert mix of dried noble gases under mild positive pressure, perfectly transparent and devoid of scent.

Yet the space is not entirely barren of growth. Sprouting from the narrow expansion seams between the floor decking are sparse, dry clusters of synthetic pseudo-flora. They are not biological in any traditional sense, but self-propagating crystalline whiskers—capillary growths of organosilicon polymers and microscopic copper threads that have wicked out of the dialectic lubricants sealed beneath the floor. They grow like calcified reeds or dried, brittle bracken: stiff, branching stalks of pale amber and spun metal, no thicker than wire, entirely motionless in the draft-free room. They do not bend; they are brittle as glass. They stand isolated from one another in wide, clean clearings of gray industrial plating, spaced out as if respecting some rigid geometric boundary condition.

Above, bank upon bank of bare phosphor-tube fixtures flood the pocket with an unyielding, high-lux white glare. The light is flat, surgical, and relentless, casting sharp-edged, high-contrast shadows across the deck plates. There is no flicker, no cycling of day or night, no shift in hue. Every bolt head, every laser-etched serial code on the structural ribs stands out with hyper-real clarity.

What defines the space above all else is the acoustic violence.

The sound does not come from within the chamber, but passes through its rigid bones from the colossal liquefaction turbines and multi-megawatt electromagnetic compressors mounted in the adjoining hulls. It is a deafening, monolithic wall of sound—a sub-bass roar intertwined with a screaming 400-hertz mechanical drone so loud that the air itself feels dense and viscous against the eardrum. It is loud enough to blur vision and vibrate the marrow of a bone, yet it is utterly uniform. It never surges, never stutters, never decays. It is a permanent acoustic plateau, an unchanging column of sheer physical force held at maximum amplitude.

Because the acoustic vibration is so evenly distributed across the entire structure, nothing rattles. The wire-stalks do not sway. The air does not ripple. The landscape remains completely, unnervingly still beneath the crush of the sound. 

High up on the vertical bulkheads, structural ladders lead to blind access hatches that have been torqued shut and wire-locked. Thick, jacketed high-voltage conduits run in clean, parallel groupings, vanishing into insulated ceiling sleeves. Between these massive arteries of power, the sparse copper-whisker bushes stand in small, frozen groves, entirely unbothered by the noise, the cold, or the dry glare, existing in a state of perfectly balanced, mechanized eternity.
