The chamber is hung high in the sky, miles above any ground, suspended within the summit trusses of an atmospheric tower whose exterior struts groan against the upper winds. Inside the sealed perimeter of the containment vault, however, the air has been entirely displaced. The room—perhaps sixty paces across, vaulted like a foundry floor or the engine room of a dreadnought—is brim-full of dense, crystal-clear, heated water. 

There is no natural earth here. Every surface is machined: thick plates of galvanized steel, cold-rolled copper baffles, extruded bronze conduits, and interlocking alloy tiles. The metal is astonishingly new. The milling marks—the concentric scallops left by high-speed cutting heads—are still crisp under the water, catching the bright, uniform glare of sodium-mercury tubes sealed in heavy glass housings along the ribbing. Nothing has oxidized; there is no rust, no lime scale, no softening of the edges. The corners of the steel bulkheads remain razor-sharp, cutting cleanly through the liquid.

And yet, it is completely choked with plant life.

Because the water is maintained at an exact, blood-warm temperature by arrays of internal heating coils and submerged copper radiators, the flora has exploded across the machinery with violent speed, occupying every cubic foot of space. It is not wild vegetation, but something systematically vigorous. Dense, thick-stemmed vascular weeds with jade-colored leaves anchor their tendrils directly into the drilled drain-holes and around the flanges of hydraulic pistons. Thick ribbons of translucent, wax-skinned kelp rise vertically, held aloft by pea-sized internal flotation bulbs, weaving themselves between vertical bundles of high-voltage conduits. 

The density is near absolute. There is almost no open water left to swim through; to move here would mean parting a solid, yielding wall of broad leaves, fibrous stalks, and spongy, hyper-oxygenated moss that coats the metal decking in a carpet four inches deep. The leaves press flat against the thick borosilicate observation windows, through which the blinding white expanse of the high-altitude cloud deck can be seen stretching toward the horizon outside.

The light is steady, sterile, and abundant. It illuminates a world of total stillness. 

There are no fish. There are no snails, no water-beetles, no microscopic daphnia drifting in the currents, not even the thinnest ribbon of larval life. The water was sterilized before the chamber was sealed and the botanical seeds introduced, leaving the vegetation to grow in uncanny, solitary purity. Without herbivores to graze them or storms to tear them, the fronds grow massive, structural, and unmarred. Broad, plate-like leaves the size of iron shields stack one above the other in vertical tiers, like green slate shingles covering the faces of giant, dormant turbine housings.

Sound barely travels, smothered instantly by the sheer mass of vegetation and water. The only audio is a subterranean, bone-conducted thrum: the ultra-low cycle of electric pumps far below the floor plates, and the faint, rhythmic *clack-hiss* of a hydraulic relay tripping once every twenty minutes behind a brass access hatch. When the water shifts, it moves with the slow, heavy grace of oil, lazily bending the kelp forest by a few inches over the course of an hour before the current settles back to zero. 

Oxygen bubbles, produced in vast quantities by the sunlit leaves, do not rush to the surface; the water’s pressure and slight synthetic viscosity hold them captive. Millions of tiny, perfect silver spheres cling to the sharp edges of the steel framework, pinned against unpainted iron seams and the hair-covered stems of the plants, sparkling steadily in the mechanical light like a dusting of frozen mercury.
