The plateau sits so high that the atmosphere has thinned to an absolute, frictionless dry slate, yet it does not freeze. Instead, a pervasive, unmoving warmth radiates upward from the bedrock—a dark, baked heat held by miles of iron-rich basalt and dense cast masonry. There is no sky to speak of, only a vast, starless ceiling of pitch, an overhead void into which heat bleeds with agonizing slowness, perfectly counterbalanced by the deep thermal currents rising from below.

Across this high expanse lies an ancient, sparse metropolis of heavy industry, long settled into an immutable equilibrium. 

The city is not ruined; rather, it has been ground down to its truest, most enduring profile. Billions of hours of imperceptible, dry dust-drift have buffed away every sharp angle, bolt-head, and sharp flue-rim. Massive cast-iron smelting halls, cylindrical gasometers, and tiered rail viaducts look less like assembled architecture and more like basalt monoliths sculpted by endless time. Metal and stone have cross-migrated under immense age: the iron has oxidized into a dry, velvety purple-black that sheds no scale, while the stone lintels have absorbed traces of lubricating oils and heavy grease until they shine with a faint, greasy luster in the darkness.

Light does not exist here in any optical sense, save for the faintest, infrequent cherry-red dullness deep within the throats of thermal sumps. The landscape is entirely legible, however, through its thermal relief. If one could see the heat, the plateau would reveal itself as an orderly, open grid of cooling blocks and dormant manifold districts, each separated by wide, empty boulevards of powdered shale. Gaps of several hundred yards stretch between the great mechanical hulls. Overhead, heavy suspended conduit lines—thick as tree trunks, made of vulcanized bitumen and braided wire—sag in permanent, motionless catenaries between rounded pylons.

Nothing moves quickly. There are no drafts. The air has zero humidity; it tastes faintly of dry carbon, baked brick, and cold zinc, though it is warm against the skin, like the interior of a furnace an hour after the fire has been pulled. 

Sound travels through the bedrock rather than the thin air. If you press a palm against the rounded lip of a conveyor channel, you can feel the microscopic, rhythmic ticking of internal escapements that have turned at the same rate for epochs. Far off, perhaps leagues away across the flat crest of the plateau, a single pneumatic piston might reset with a sound like a muffled bass drum struck underwater—*thum*—and then nothing more for days.

Life here is exceptionally sparse, dry, and fundamentally mineral. It does not drink, for there is no moisture to swallow; it does not breathe in rushes. Deep within the crawlspaces beneath the furnace terraces, blind lithic scavengers creep with the agonizing patience of clockwork. They have broad, flat carapaces made of fused slag and magnetite, moving on dozens of tiny, ceramic-tipped points that make a delicate, dry clicking—like beads dropped on slate—as they graze on the crusts of sublimated sulfur and dry machine-grease that glaze the foundations. They have no eyes; their heads are blunt, sensitive plates of thermal nerve-endings, mapping the warm hulls of the dead boilers.

The silence is the defining condition. It is not an expectant silence, nor the quiet of an aftermath. It is the permanent silence of a machine that has achieved perfect balance with its environment. There is no rust, because there is no water; there is no rot, because there are no microbes; there is no collapse, because every arch and buttress has already settled into the exact angle of ultimate rest. 

The city simply persists at the top of the world: vast, dark, warmly sleeping, its rounded iron chimneys pointing blindly into the empty night, functioning without an operator, without an output, and without an end.
