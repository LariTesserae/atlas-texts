There is no light here, but the air is thick with the friction of things. It is warm, humid as the interior of a working throat, and smelling of copper oxide, hot lubricating grease, and the rich, rotting mulch of sunless growth. 

This is the Upper Sump-Plateau—a continent-spanning shelf elevated thousands of meters into a ceiling of impenetrable thermal fog. Because no ray of sun has ever penetrated this altitude, the ecosystem does not borrow energy from light; it drinks the scalding waste of the Machine-Spines. 

Miles above the lowlands, the plateau is shaped by eons of torrential, hot condensation. The rock underfoot is old granite, ground down into soft, rounded saddles, hollowed by sinkholes, and deeply grooved by ancient drainage channels. But the stone is rarely bare. It is clad in thick, rubbery mats of blind vegetation: pale, tuberous mosses that swell with scalding sap, broad-leafed liverworts as tough as wet leather, and towering, fibrous tree-analogues whose trunks are wrapped tightly around sweating iron pipes.

The sound is immense and layered, a physical pressure against the chest. At the base of all hearing is the low, seismic thrum of the deep turbines—reciprocating engines the size of cathedrals, buried somewhere in the wet rock, pumping cooling fluid through the plateau’s veins. Over this drone rides the hiss: high-pressure steam venting through rusted relief valves miles apart, the steady, rhythmic *clack-whir* of counterweighted cable systems that still haul empty iron slag-tubs across the black abysses, and the omnipresent, deafening roar of hot waterfalls dropping through fissures into the dark beneath.

Water here is not a passive feature; it is an active, circulating circulatory system. It rains upward in some valleys where the high-pressure thermal updrafts catch the runoff from the pipe-forests. The atmosphere is permanently near 100% saturation. Droplets form directly on your skin, on the chitinous backs of wandering things, on the vibrating steel cables strung between cliffs. Every surface weeps. Water collects in vast, warm, black tarns where blind lungfish, blind river-crabs, and giant, hair-fringed amphipods mill in dense, tumbling shoals, feeding on the greasy bacterial scum that coats the surface.

The fauna here has no concept of the visual. Evolution has tuned them entirely to vibration, thermal drafts, and chemical scent. Large, six-limbed grazers, pale as tallow and heavy as cattle, wade through the steaming marshes, stripping the fibrous insulation off old steam mains with serrated beak-plates. Above them, navigating the tangle of dangling grounding-wires and rubber-tree tendrils, move long-bodied ambushers whose hides are ridged with acoustic pits. They communicate across the deafening roar of the engines with violent, percussive snaps of their tail-segments against hollow iron pylons—a metallic code that carries for leagues through the drenched dark.

Scattered throughout this vertical tangle are the relics of the high industrial age: half-swallowed outposts, rivet-studded observation domes now used as nesting dens by bat-analogues the size of condors, and immense, weeping cooling towers whose interiors have become vertical rainforests of hanging fungi and trailing, ropey vines. These structures are not dead; many are still energized. High-voltage lines crackle invisibly in the black mist, ionizing the water vapor with the smell of ozone and wet flint, occasionally discharging with a blinding, painless snap that leaves the air smelling of scorched moss.

It is a world of tremendous physical crowding. Space is contested by the relentless, blind push of root systems prying apart concrete revetments, by the heavy migration of scent-following herds tracing pipelines across narrow iron bridges, and by the incessant, mechanical pulse of a machine-landscape that has long since forgotten its operators, functioning now only as an artificial geothermal heart for a pitch-black, roaring jungle.
