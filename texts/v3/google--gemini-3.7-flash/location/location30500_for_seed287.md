The heat is the first thing that acquires body. It does not drift; it presses downward like a slab of warm lead, trapped thirty fathoms below the mantle of the upper earth. It smells of scorched tallow, wet rust, and the dense, bitter starch of bruised philodendrons.

This is the Lower Conduit, though what it once drained or driven has long ceased to matter. It is a vaulted hall roughly seventy paces wide, its ceiling lost in a perpetual haze of sulfurous vapor and low-hanging heat-shimmer. Here, the earth’s deep thermal veins meet an abandoned manifold of cyclopean ironworks: massive, grease-blackened flywheels twenty feet across, banks of cast-iron steam chests, and ribbed compression cylinders that stand like the columns of a sunken basilica. 

The air is loud with a heavy, monolithic cadence. Somewhere deep in the bedrock beneath the masonry, a vast hydraulic heart is still cycling. It operates on a massive, agonizingly slow rhythm—a low, subterranean *thoom-clack* that occurs perhaps once every four minutes, sending a deep shudder through the soles of one’s boots and rattling the beads of moisture on the undersides of the leaves. Between these strokes, there is the continuous, deafening hiss of high-pressure bleed-off valves, muffled by layers of wet fiber.

Water is not abundant, but it never entirely leaves. It exists almost solely as a cycle of sweat. The iron casing of a dead turbine will cool slightly on its leeward side, causing the heavy, hot air to condense against its rivets in fat, amber-tinted beads. These droplets trickle downward in precise, worn grooves, feeding the thick collars of vegetation that choke the bases of every machine. 

The plants here do not resemble surface flora, though their ancestry is recognizable. Deprived of sunlight, they have developed an oily, plum-colored and bruised-yellow foliage, thick as sole-leather and slick with an excreted wax that smells faintly of petroleum. Vast, paddle-shaped fronds crowd the narrow steel catwalks, their stems wrapping around copper pneumatic lines, drawing heat directly from the metal. Lichens as thick and fibrous as felt have calcified over the dial-faces of steam gauges, their hyphae prying behind the cracked glass to drink the mineral-rich rust within. Where a horizontal shaft spans the gap between two stone buttresses, it is entirely furred with hanging curtains of fibrous, stringy roots that drift gently in the draft of the radiator fins.

There are no insects in this vault. No beetles burrow through the rotting fibrous mats; no moths beat against the faint, dull amber glow that leaks from the inspection ports of the dormant boilers. The stillness of the life here is absolute, broken only when a frond shifts under the weight of its own collected condensate, tipping a thimbleful of hot, iron-tainted water into the machinery below with a sharp *tick*.

The erosion is mature, an ancient equilibrium. The rust does not flake anymore; it has become a hard, polished patina, dark as plum skin, sealed by the wax of the plants and the pervasive oil mist. Where stone meets iron, they have fused into a composite conglomerate, bridged by centuries of calcium weep and root-acid etching. The catwalks are sagged into permanent, comfortable troughs. 

Light is scarce and monochromatic. It bleeds upward from the floor grates in narrow, dusty bars of dull cadmium orange—the persistent, unquenchable warmth of banked geothermal coals far below. In this half-gloom, the machines look less like relics of industry and more like a strange, rigid variety of timber. Piston rods slide in and out of their packings with a sound like a wet sigh, moving only inches an hour, their polished steel gleaming wetly before being swallowed back into the dark cylinder heads.

Everything here belongs to the long duration. A single leaf takes three years to uncurl from the casing of an exhaust manifold, its tip slowly steering toward the faintest draft of warm grease. When the great piston deep below completes its compression stroke, the hanging root-systems sway in unison, once, and then settle back into the stagnant, sweltering dark.
