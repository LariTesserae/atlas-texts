High on the fractured shoulder of an unnamed, freshly uplifted massif, the basin sits like a hollowed tooth—bounded by razor-sheared slate and towering scree that has not yet had time to round its edges. The air at this altitude is cold, thin, and permanently saturated. A heavy, uniform mist hangs at shoulder height, muffling sightlines so that the entire space feels no larger than a ruined cathedral, walled in by the sheer, unweathered faces of young mountain rock.

Nothing here was manufactured, yet the basin functions as a colossal, open-air machine shop of raw geology. 

Through a tear in the physical laws that govern mineral formation, the stone has crystallized into immaculate, heavy-tolerance mechanical assemblies. Great crankshafts of solid titaniferous magnetite, six paces thick, emerge directly from the cliff base, seated in natural stone journals that weep a clear, lubricating lithomarge. Piston-shafts of unpolished basalt slide in and out of wet, moss-rimmed bores in the mountainside with a dull, subterranean *thump-hiss*, venting plumes of cold, condensing vapor into the lush greenery.

This is not a ruin; there are no rivets, no weld marks, no signs of toolpaths or human intent. The iron, bronze, and steel are native species of rock, grown with the seamless perfection of quartz crystals, yet organized into functional reciprocating engines and massive, slowly turning flywheels. 

The vegetation here is astonishingly vigorous, thriving on the mineral mist and the steady mechanical heat of the earth. Thick, spongy cushions of liverwort and clubmoss drape over rotating connecting rods, their fronds greased by clear rock-oil and dripping with mountain condensation. Long, fibrous ferns with serrated, blue-green fronds grow directly out of the gaps between meshing gear teeth, their stems supple enough to be drawn through the cogs without crushing, fed by the constant, cool water running down the face of the machinery. 

The floor of the basin is not dense with growth, but rather arranged in wide, spacious clearances of cracked black gravel and shallow, oil-filmed pools. Between these clearings, solitary mechanisms rise like ancient megaliths: an isolated drive-spindle thirty feet high, spinning in absolute silence on a bed of wet shale, crowned with a dense nest of broad-leafed alpine weeds; or a pair of opposed iron rocker-arms rhythmically nodding over a deep, perfectly rectangular sump of clear, icy water.

The sound is loud, pervasive, and utterly changeless: a heavy, rhythmic counterpoint of low-frequency thrumming, the steady suction of cold pistons, the clack of naturally sprung valve-stones, and the infinite, wet hiss of condensation sluicing down sheer rock. There are no wind gusts to break the cadence, no shifting weather. The light remains perpetually dim—a leaden, silver dusk that never brightens into noon nor dims into true night, sustained by the faint, static-blue ionization clinging to the tips of the higher ferns.

Life swarms throughout this mechanical biome. In the shallow, churning runnels between the iron outcrops, schools of pale, blind lungfish with armor-plated gills rest in the turbulent eddies created by churning cam-lobes. Flocks of soot-colored, needle-billed swifts roost by the hundreds in the warm vent-holes of the rock cylinders, entirely undisturbed by the four-stroke compression shuddering through the stone beneath them. Fat, translucent amphibians cling to the polished surfaces of reciprocating rods, their pale skin glistening with water and machine-seep, riding the cyclic motion back and forth through the cool mist with total indifference. 

Nothing in the basin accelerates, decays, or evolves. A fractured slab of slate, newly split from the rim above, remains suspended mid-topple against a spinning steel armature, held in an eternal balance of torque and gravity. The water weeps, the pistons stroke, the iron gears turn through their beds of dripping moss, locked in an unyielding, high-altitude equilibrium that has never known a builder, and will never run down.
