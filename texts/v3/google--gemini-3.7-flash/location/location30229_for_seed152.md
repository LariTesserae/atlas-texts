The floor of the chamber is set six fathoms below the surrounding plain, nestled into a deep rectangular notch cut directly into the living shelf of dark basalt. It is an urban space in its infancy—an under-croft or sunken plaza of a low-elevation settlement, laid out with clean, sharp-edged cyclopean masonry. Because it has only recently been hewn and fitted, the stone possesses zero softness; there are no rounded corners, no crumbled steps, no silted runnels. The chisel marks are fresh, pale grey crosshatches on charcoal rock, and the fractured faces of the pavers are crisp enough to cut bare skin. Yet, despite this architectural order, there is no machinery here, no metal fittings or mortar. It is pure, heavy dry-stone geometry, vast in concept but contained within a narrow, secluded pocket of the earth.

The air is warm and still, carrying the heavy, greenhouse density of a sheltered hollow. Light enters from high above, filtered through a lattice of broad-leaved fig trees and overhanging ferns that obscure the rim, rendering the lower level in a cool, uniform gloom. The atmosphere sits in a state of perpetual mild dampness. Intermittent beads of clear condensation form along the smooth basalt walls, gathering until they lose their grip and slide down the vertical stone faces in soundless, gleaming ribbons, soaking the fine, crushed-rock sand at the perimeter.

Though the built footprint suggests a dense township, the courtyard itself feels sparse and unhurried. Immense space is left between the architectural presences: a lone, monolithic lintel stands without a roof; a wide, shallow sunken bath awaits water that only arrives in seeps; broad stone alleys lead to open, empty alcoves. 

Life fills these voids with a quiet, vigorous momentum. Lush, broad-leaved ground flora—shade-tolerant arums, velvet-leafed nettles, and thick carpets of liverwort—has already begun to colonize the fresh gravel between the pavers. The growth does not choke the stonework; rather, it rises in deliberate, isolated clumps, feeding on the trapped humidity and the mineral-rich dust of the quarrying. 

Fauna thrives in the stillness. Small, fat-bodied geckos with skin the color of wet river slate dart between the stone joints, their movements virtually noiseless. Pale, heavy-winged moths flutter at the lower levels, resting on the underside of the leaves, while long-legged hunting spiders weave flat, almost invisible webs across the corners of the raw stone lintels. In the damp sand near the wall bases, small burrowing toads work their way into the grit, their respiration slow and rhythmic in the warm air.

The environment is in constant, low-amplitude motion. The biological cycle here is rapid: leaves unfurl, sweat out drops of moisture, drop seeds onto the virgin stone, and decay without rot into a thin, dark humus. The thermal currents change subtly as the day outside warms the upper rock, drawing a faint, barely perceptible draft up through the ferns, carrying away the scent of wet green cellulose and freshly split mineral. Sound is almost entirely absorbed—muffled by the soft leaves, the velvet moss, and the sheer mass of the unyielding, unweathered rock. Only the faint, soft *tick* of a falling water droplet and the dry whisper of an insect’s wing disturb the deep, sheltered quiet.
