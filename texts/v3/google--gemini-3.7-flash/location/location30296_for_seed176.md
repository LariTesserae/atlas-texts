The cleft sits seven thousand meters up, pinched between two newly thrust horns of black diorite, but it behaves like a greenhouse. It is narrow—barely forty paces from wall to wall—yet it extends vertically until the sky above is just a bright, clean blade of white-blue light, and the drop below vanishes into a rising floor of pressurized steam. 

The rock is young and brutal. The tectonic fractures that opened this fissure have not yet been smoothed by ice or centuries; their edges are as sharp as knapped flint, jutting out in sheer, razor-lipped shelves. Yet every square inch of this stone is weeping. Warm, mineral-heavy water seeps directly from the fresh faults, sheeting over the dark rock in trembling films and dripping from tier to tier with a dense, chaotic patter. Because of the geothermal venting surging up from the deep root of the mountain, the air inside this pocket is persistently, heavily warm—a thick, greenhouse air that smells of wet copper, boiled sap, and machine oil.

Clinging to these wet, vertical faces is an almost suffocating mass of green. Without room for horizontal spread, the vegetation stacks upward in dense, tangled tiers. Immense, ribbed liverworts the size of shields paste themselves flat against the rock, their translucent margins trembling under continuous falling water. Long, pale aerial roots drop twenty yards through the open air before anchoring into iron brackets or lower ledges, webbed over by thick blankets of golden-green moss that hold water like saturated sponges. Because the air is constantly humid and warm, the plants never cycle into dormancy; they simply swell, grow thick and fibrous, and drip.

Pinned directly to the eastern wall of the chasm is an industrial assembly: a monumental, slow-stroking hydraulic accumulator and its associated ironworks. It is not an entire city, but a single, massive piece of infrastructure bolted straight into the young stone with iron tie-rods thick as tree trunks. Riveted boiler-plate housings, stained with streaks of orange rust and lime-white mineral scale, nestle into natural notches in the cliff. Enormous brass-sleeved pistons—three feet in diameter—rise and plunge with an agonizing, deliberate slowness, taking two full minutes to complete a single stroke. 

The noise in the cleft is immense and relentless. The slow, deep thud of the low-pressure steam pumps resonates through the diorite itself, vibrating in the soles of the feet and rattling the water beads on the fern fronds. Layered over this sub-bass pulse is the hiss of steam releasing from relief valves, the hollow clatter of heavy steel linkage chains shifting against grease-blackened guides, and the deafening, ambient roar of falling water ricocheting between the sheer walls. 

High-altitude sunlight strikes down into the notch during the middle hours of the day, refracted into dazzling, saturated shafts by the mist. In these beams, the machine parts gleam wetly: polished bronze journals, dripping oil pans catching both runoff and lubricant, and cast-iron gantries draped in creeping, broad-leafed runners. 

Animal life is scarce, suppressed by the noise and the sheer verticality of the terrain, but specialized things endure. Translucent, blind tree frogs no larger than a fingernail cling to the wet gaskets of the steam pipes, thriving in the warm condensation, while tiny, dark-winged moths flutter in the dead zones of air behind the iron plating, feeding on the greasy algae that coats the cold-water conduits.

Everything here moves at the pace of pressure. The stone stays sharp, the pistons complete their patient, rhythmic cycles against the weight of the mountain, and the water falls endlessly from the high ledges, feeding an engine and a jungle locked together in a bright, loud, hanging pocket above the clouds.
