The basin sits at three thousand meters, cupped in the red knuckle of a broken plateau, but the air here does not bite; it stays thick, greasy, and body-warm, heated from beneath by miles of flooded flue-trenches and the vast, slow digestion of iron.

It is an industrial city built not for living bodies, but scaled to the anatomy of immense fluid circuits. Across miles of stepped travertine terraces, the architecture stands in wide, lonely increments: monoliths of cast slag, fluted cooling towers four hundred paces apart, and sprawling aqueduct viaducts that bridge the red gullies like fossilized spines. There is immense room between things. The scale is vast and bare. The city is laid out with the strict geometry of a circuit board, yet aged into soft-cornered maturity—the iron has bled deep umber stains into the white lime-crust of the stone, and every vertical surface has surrendered its sharp edges to centuries of mineral-laden condensation.

Water is everywhere, though it rarely falls as rain. It breathes upward from subterranean boiling vaults, meets the cooler highland draft, and drops immediately out of the sky as a heavy, lukewarm sweat. It weeps from the joints of bolted plates. It slides in thin, glassy sheets over the wide, flat roofs of processing halls, cascading off gutters into five-story catchments that shimmer with orange scum. 

And it is deafening. 

The sound does not come from wheels or sirens, but from the terrifying tonnage of water being moved under pressure through aged, hollow metal. Deep inside the earth and echoing through the canyoned streets, massive hydrostatic rams beat at a pulse of twenty strokes a minute—a subterranean *thung-clack* that strikes the chest cavity before it reaches the ear. Aboveground, open-channel sluices three lanes wide carry boiling mineral sludge along the terrace lips, producing a continuous, white-noise roar that shreds human speech within five paces. Every iron tower acts as an acoustic pipe, humming at a resonant frequency that turns the warm, wet air into a vibrating jelly. To stand in the wide stone plazas between the works is to be drowned in a relentless, mechanical surf.

Vegetation has little purchase in this saturated, vibrating chemistry. What lives here is opportunistic and pale: thick, gelatinous mats of sulfur-eating algae that line the drain-troughs in vivid chartreuse ribbons, and an iron-tolerant, wire-stemmed rush that roots exclusively in the rusted seams of dead machinery, shivering constantly with the vibration of the ground. 

Fauna is almost an error here. Occasionally, small, blind mud-skimmers—flat-bodied creatures with translucent skin and leathery fins—surface in the cooler settle-pools, feeding on the bacterial foam before diving back down into the miles of pipework that honeycomb the bedrock. There are no birds; the acoustic violence of the upper air ruptures their lungs.

The light is plain, steady, and filtered. The sun sits as an unblinking white disc through the thermal haze, casting soft, shadowless daylight across the landscape. Under this even illumination, the place reveals its peculiar stillness of intent. Though the noise is catastrophic and the machines are perpetually engaged, nothing happens quickly. The massive balance-beams of the pumps rise and fall with agonizing, deliberate grace, taking minutes to complete a single stroke. Slurry moves through the settling ponds at the speed of cooling wax. Silt builds, hardens into stone, is eaten by acid runoff, and reforms elsewhere in cycles that measure out across centuries.

Human presence has worn itself down to maintenance pathways: iron-rung ladders bolted into cliff-faces of rusted steel, narrow catwalks suspended over steaming siphon pits, and high, shuttered watch-cabins perched on the lip of the terraces. The structures are built to outlast dynasties of water. Thick cast-iron plates, three inches deep and bound by rivets the size of bread loaves, form the foundations of entire districts that sit empty, rinsed clean daily by the warm, deafening runoff, waiting for valves that turn once every fifty years.
