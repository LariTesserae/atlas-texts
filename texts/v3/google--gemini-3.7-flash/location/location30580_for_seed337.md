The bedrock here has no memory of wind. It has only just met the air.

This is the floor of the Great Sump, a wide, sunken tectonic trough that drops fifty paces below the level of the surrounding scrub plains. It is vast enough to hold its own horizon of sheer, pale limestone faces, but its floor is not flat; it is a dense, tiered labyrinth of active excavation and immediate habitation. The rock does not decay into scree or sand; every edge is razor-bright, sharp-cornered, freshly fractured by wooden wedges and bronze-tipped iron bars. There are no softened curves or water-smoothed gullies. The city does not sit atop the stone; it is subtracted directly from it, living quarters left behind as negative space while the quarry deepens around them.

Because the rock has only just been split from the earth’s mass, it sweats. Cool, subterranean moisture seeps through micro-fissures in the crystalline walls, beading along the chisel marks and running down into narrow, hand-cut runnels. The air carries that specific, mineral chill of a cellar opened at dawn, smelling faintly of cold slate, pulverized lime, and damp timber. 

Light floods the trough completely from above—a clear, even, pale daylight that bounces hard off the raw, unweathered stone, casting clean geometric shadows across the dense network of terraces. In this light, the density of the place reveals itself as almost claustrophobic despite the open sky. Thousands of dwellings are packed tightly against one another: square, cubical cells carved into the rising steps of the excavation, connected by steep notched logs, rope bridges, and narrow rock-spurs no wider than a man’s shoulders. Where stone was removed, timber bracing fills the gap; where stone was left, a family sleeps inside the hollow. There is no empty space between domesticity and industry. A hearth-pit sits three feet from a sheer drop where workers are driving split-logs into fresh seams to crack off the next tier of building material.

Vegetation has had no time to establish root systems. Here and there, in the older upper ledges where the stone has been exposed for a few seasons, thin scabs of liverwort and pale blue lichens cling to the damp corners of lintels. A few tough, leafless salt-bushes sprout from fissures where dust has settled, but for the most part, the world is entirely gray-white stone, unbleached hemp rope, and heavy split cedar.

Despite the intensity of human presence, the basin is remarkably quiet. The soundscape is muffled by the heavy mass of the stone and the constant, soft dripping of condensation. The clamor of iron against stone does not echo wildly; it is absorbed into the labyrinth of fractured planes. What carries instead is a low, rhythmic texture of labor: the heavy, dull *thunk* of wooden mallets driving dry pine wedges into wet clefts, the scrape of flat river stones used to smooth door jambs, the wet sliding sound of heavy blocks being dragged along cedar rollers smeared with tallow. Men and women speak in dropped registers, their voices swallowed by the deep rock. 

Movement, however, is constant and fluid. The dynamic of the sunken city is one of continuous rearrangement. A terrace that served as a communal drying yard in the morning might be quarried into two separate rooms by twilight. Slender counterweighted cranes made of stripped pine creak softly as they pivot, lowering blocks to the lower tiers or hauling up baskets of damp stone-flour. Ropes hum under tension. A thin mist rises from the deeper trenches during the middle hours of the day as the sun warms the wet floor, only to condense again in the cool shade beneath the hanging walkways.

Animal life is sparse and opportunistic. Small, ash-colored lizards dart along the fresh fractures to lap at the condensation beads before ducking into the split-lines. Occasionally, a hawk circles high above the rim of the trough, looking down into the grid of human activity as if inspecting an anthill, but it never descends. All the living energy of the place is focused inward and downward, pressing hard against the newly exposed crust of the world, building an empire out of the very acts of dismantling and remaining.
