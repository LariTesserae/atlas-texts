The terrace sits at seven thousand meters, wedged into a sharp notch between two obsidian teeth, yet the air here is a thick, sweltering bath. It smells of boiled iron, wet pumice, and crushed mint.

The rock is young—unweathered, glass-edged basalt fractured so recently in geologic time that the cleavage faces still gleam with mirror-smooth, iridescent sheens. There are no scree slopes or rounded pebbles; everything is either a razor-thin terrace, a clean vertical drop, or a knife-edge rib of black stone. Because of the altitude, the sky directly overhead is a deep, bruised indigo, carrying the stark daylight of the upper troposphere, but the heat radiating from the ground turns the atmosphere within this small basin into a dense, shimmering lens.

It is an enclosure no larger than the nave of a small cathedral, bounded on three sides by stepped cliffs and open on the fourth to a sheer drop into an ocean of white undercast.

Water does not fall here as rain; it is drawn out of the thin mountain air by three pale, slender pylons embedded in the rock. The pylons appear almost ceramic, though they possess no seams, joints, or grain, rising eight meters into the air like unsharpened needles. Around their bases, the ambient physics of state-change is slightly warped. Water molecules nucleate spontaneously along invisible, curving field lines in the air, gathering into fat, trembling globes of warm moisture that float for a moment before snapping downward to splatter against the stone. 

Because of this constant, silent harvest, the entire floor of the shelf is sheeted in a skin of water that never cools. It runs in swift, millimeter-thin laminas over the glassy basalt, falling from one stepped ledge to the next in silent, un-aerated ribbons. Where the water hits the hot rock, it hiss-weeps, sending up curling veils of steam that hang motionless in the windless notch.

Between these sheets of clear, hot run-off, the vegetation has established itself with strange, architectural precision. It is not an overgrown tangle, but an intensely vivid, deliberate scattering. Fern-like structures with thick, translucent stems the color of bruised plums grow directly from the hairline fractures in the stone. Their fronds are not fibrous but rubbery, coated in a hydrophobic oil that causes the condensing water to bead into perfect mercury-like pearls and roll off instantly. In the dry spaces between water-ribbons, broad, plate-shaped succulents lie flat against the dark rock, their upper surfaces patterned in geometric hexagonal pores that pulse very slowly—opening when the mist thickens, closing when the direct sun cuts through.

The silence here is heavy, damped by the ambient humidity and the low pressure of the altitude. There is only the soft *tock-tock* of condensed spheres collapsing onto the stone, the faint, continuous sigh of steam rising, and occasionally, a low resonant chord that seems to travel through the rock itself rather than the air—the acoustic byproduct of the pylons adjusting their draw against the tropospheric jet above.

Life of the moving kind is scarce. Every few minutes, a creature resembling a flattened, four-winged dragonfly with an exoskeleton of polished slate skims over the wet basalt. It does not land on the plants, but hovers an inch above the hot water, dipping a needle-like proboscis into the current to drink before darting upward, vanishing into the cold sky beyond the notch, only to return when its internal temperature plummets. 

In the center of the terrace lies the only other remnant of construction: a shallow basin carved directly into the obsidian, perfectly circular, filled with boiling water so clear the black geometric tooling on the basin floor is visible three feet down. The floor of the pool is inlaid with fine, hair-like tracings of a pale gold alloy that does not conduct electricity, but seems to anchor the local thermal anomaly, keeping the stone beneath your boots hot enough to soften pitch, while the mountain air three feet beyond the terrace edge is thirty degrees below freezing.

Here, the boundary between machine, weather, and geology has dissolved entirely. The heat keeps the rock from icing over; the cold air feeds the condensation; the tech feeds the water to the plants; and the plants, in turn, insulate the sharp seams of the young stone from cracking under the thermal stress. It is a sealed, suspended terrarium, balanced precariously on the shoulder of the sky, running through its quiet, humid cycles in total indifference to the frozen world below.
