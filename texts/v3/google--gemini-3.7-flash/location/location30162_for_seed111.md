The fissure sits nine thousand meters above the plains, pinched inside the needle-summit of a mountain that is still rising. The stone here has no memory of rain; it was thrust into the sky so recently in planetary terms that its edges remain as sharp as freshly knapped glass. Basalt sheets, black and glossy as cooled grease, intersect at violently acute angles, creating a narrow, vertical cathedral—a chimneylike vault no wider than forty paces, tapering upward into a suffocating apex where the thin, high-altitude wind cannot reach.

Down here, in the cold gut of the rift, there is no sun. What little daylight strikes the peak above is baffled away by a series of overlapping rock eaves, leaving the interior in a heavy, bruised near-blackness. Yet it is not dry. The air is cool, biting with the raw chill of the troposphere, but the interior surfaces are coated in a steady, thick condensation. Water arrives not from storms, but from the breath of what lives here.

The walls are not stone; they are meat and slate intertwined. Millions of blind, palm-sized climbers—creatures with the greasy down of seabirds and the articulated, triple-hinged ribs of soft-shelled crabs—carpet every square inch of the sheer obsidian. They are packed three and four deep, a shivering, interlocking pavement of warm bodies that clings to the rock with needle-tipped bone spurs. They do not migrate. They do not fly. They exist in an endless, slow-motion crawl across the vertical facets, feeding on the airborne frost-scum and the microscopic sulfur flakes pushed up through the mountain’s micro-fractures.

Because of them, the chasm is loud. It is not the sound of wind or water, but an acoustic mass: the wet, simultaneous clicking of a billion chitinous claws adjusting their grip, underlaid by a rhythmic, subsonic heave—the collective inhalation of a million primitive air-sacs. The noise is immense, flat, and claustrophobic, vibrating inside the teeth and the breastbone. When one tier of the colony shifts to avoid a drip of freezing condensation, the motion propagates across the wall in a slow, undulating wave, like grease setting in cold water.

Vegetation has no soil to grip, but it finds anchor in the molted husks and dung of the climbers. A pale, rubbery lichen, tough as boiled leather and colorless as lard, grows in stiff ruffs along the sharpest stone crests. It exudes a faint, vinegar-sharp acid that etches tiny, shallow footholds into the basalt, weeping a clear, sticky sap that never quite freezes.

Human presence here is ancient, minimal, and entirely desperate. High up on a narrow ledge—a razor-flake of rock barely broad enough to hold a kneeling man—are the calcified remnants of crude harvesting gear. Long, flexible poles fashioned from stitched rib-bones and dried sinew, tipped with simple slate hooks, lie half-buried beneath decades of guano and lichen. Someone once climbed to this breathless height, worked in the pitch-dark with blind tools to hook and drop the fat-rich creatures into sacks, and either died here or left their implements behind when the cold stiffened their hands. The leather lashings have dried to the hardness of iron, preserved by the bitter air.

Everything in the chimney moves to the cadence of the mountain's internal heat and the slow accumulation of mineral frost. A drop of condensation gathers on an obsidian keel sixty feet above, swells over the course of an hour, falls, strikes a clustered knot of downy bodies with a dull *thwip*, and is instantly absorbed into their greasy, water-repellent coats. The colony shivers, adjusts, clicks, and settles back into its dense, unyielding dark.
