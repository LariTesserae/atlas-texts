The floor of the world is terracotta, stretching past every horizon in an unbroken expanse of hand-smoothed, imperfectly leveled tiles. It is hot to the touch—not burning, but like a kiln floor hours after the coals have been raked out, radiating an inescapable, feverish thrum. There is no soil, no native stone, no bedrock of ancient mantle; beneath the tiles are simply more tiles, coursed in rough lime mortar that has not yet had time to fully cure. Fingerprints the size of small rowboats are set into the cooling clay, still wet with an unevaporated slip that gleams weakly in the dim, ash-tinted twilight.

A thick, boiling moisture hangs over the expanse. It does not fall as rain so much as it sweats directly out of the architecture. The air is heavy, brackish, and tasting faintly of iron, river silt, and woodsmoke. Water pools in the wide, shallow depressions where the tiles have bowed under their own immense, freshly laid weight. These are vast, tepid pans of water—knee-deep, stretching for leagues—where the surface skin constantly wrinkles and tears under convective currents rising from the sub-floor kilns.

Nothing grows here in any recognizable sense, though along the grout lines, white efflorescence blooms in thick, cauliflower-like crusts of salt and lye, growing inches an hour before dissolving into the passing currents of hot water. 

The architecture is embryonic, unweathered, and staggering in scale, yet entirely executed with the blunt vocabulary of primitive hand-craft. Miles apart across the wet flats stand titanic, unfinished structures: battered ziggurats of sun-dried mudbrick; colossal, hollow earthenware cylinders lashed together with hair-thick braids of damp sinew and unspun jute; and tilting ramps built from billions of rough-hewn, thumb-pressed bricks that taper off into empty air. There are no machines, no steel girders, no cut masonry. The entire continent has been shaped as if by an infinite workshop of potters and weavers working in absolute frenzy, yet there are no workers.

Instead, the land builds and unmakes itself through a slow, turbulent panic. A mile-long wall of green, unfired brick will bulge outward, sighing with a dull, wet suction, then fold forward into a shallow lake of red slip, splashing boiling mud across the flats. Where it collapses, the water immediately rushes in, hissing violently. Within minutes, the mud cools, thickens, and begins to re-knit itself into new stepped tiers, pinched and smoothed by unseen, phantom pressures that leave distinct palm-lines and gouged nail-marks across the rising facades.

The soundscape is strangely suppressed by the steam. It is quiet, but never silent: a continuous, low-frequency chorus of bubbling mud, the soft *clink-clink* of earthenware cooling and crazing under tension, and the distant, muffled *whump* of subterranean draft-flues venting gas through hollow ceramic chimneys. 

Spatial logic is unreliable across these distances. In places, the vast flat pans tilt without spilling their water, the surface tension holding miles of shallow red brine against a thirty-degree slope of herringbone paving. Elsewhere, the horizon seems to drop away abruptly into sunken basins where the brickwork is laid in impossible, self-swallowing spirals that funnel boiling steam down into dark, echoing voids. Light filters down from no visible sun, but rather from a high, stagnant ceiling of condensation that traps the amber glow of subterranean draft-trenches far below.

Everything feels made only minutes ago. The mortar is soft enough to yield to a heel; the clay still smells of riverbed and fire; the moisture pooling in the joints is fresh, clean, and uncolonized by moss or slime. It is a world entirely fabricated, vast beyond measure, yet possessing the raw, unstable, and clumsy infancy of a vessel just lifted from the wheel, still spinning, still wet, waiting to collapse under its own heat.
