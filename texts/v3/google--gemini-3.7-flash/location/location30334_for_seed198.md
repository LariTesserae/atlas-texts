The valley does not reach down to soil or river-silt; it bottoms out on iron. Across a span of miles, suspended between cold, mist-shrouded peaks, the entire gulf is occupied by a tiered, deeply layered labyrinth of industrial decking, waterworks, and structural steel. It is an elevated world built entirely upon itself, an immense terrace of trusses, riveted caissons, and fluted pipes that has long since weathered into a dark, oxide-black baseline.

The air is cool, thin, and permanently saturated. Low-slung mountain clouds drag heavily through the upper superstructures, condensing against overhead tramway cables and dripping in steady, rhythmic drops onto the levels below. Everywhere there is water: it runs in shallow sheets over corrugated zinc walkways, weeps from the seams of pressurized water-mains, and courses through endless networks of open cast-iron sluices. 

Despite the total absence of exposed bedrock or natural ground, the place is suffocatingly lush. Decades of dampness and coal-smoke soot have laid a rich, acidic substrate across the ironwork. Thick, spongy mats of pale mountain moss carpet the girders; dark strap-ferns erupt from the bolt-clusters; and heavy curtains of creeping ivy and cold-hardy saxifrage drape over steam manifolds and safety railings. The vegetation does not merely colonize the machinery—it is packed tightly around it, choking the narrow inspection catwalks, wedging into mechanical joints, and wrapping every dead pipe in a sodden, deep-green fleece.

The scale is vast, yet there is virtually no empty space. Looking across the expanse, one sees an unbroken, multi-tiered density: processing gantries bolted beneath railway spans, cooling towers nested inside structural arches, steam boilers wedged between vertical conveyor shafts. It is a regional system, stretching into the dim, gray distance where the silhouettes of massive suspension towers vanish into the rain.

Yet beneath this heavy, moss-dampened stillness is a state of relentless, kinetic turbulence. The entire infrastructure is alive with mechanical flux. Overhead, unmanned cable-cars rattle past through the vapor, tipping loads of wet aggregate down roaring chutes. Massive counterweights slide vertically on greased rails, shifting the balance of suspended aqueducts. Automated sluice gates slam open and shut in unpredictable rhythms, rerouting mountain runoff through churning turbine housings, while steam vents pop and shudder, releasing boiling white plumes that instantly shred into the cold fog. Walkways vibrate constantly from the churn of unseen pumps deep within the superstructure, and mechanical diverters on the rail lines clatter into new positions every few seconds.

Remarkably, all this frenetic motion is almost quiet. The sheer volume of wet plant matter acts as an acoustic sponge. The deafening iron-on-iron screeches, the hammering of pistons, and the crashing of water are swallowed by the thick blankets of moss and the dense, misty atmosphere. What reaches the ear is a muted, low-register murmur—the dull, muffled thud of distant machinery, the soft hiss of condensing steam dying against fern fronds, and the constant, whisper-quiet patter of water trickling down through hundreds of feet of constructed iron decks.

Animal life is sparse and cautious in this cold, churning machine-garden. There are no herds, no songbirds. Occasionally, a solitary highland raven glides through the lattice of rusted cross-beams, or small, dark water-beetles skitter across the dripping rubber gaskets of a vibrating pump. 

Light here is meager, filtered first by the high overcast sky and then broken apart by the dense crisscross of overhead bridges, pipes, and leafy cascades. It is a world of perpetual twilight and cold moisture, where monumental, endlessly shifting industry has settled into a permanent, overgrown equilibrium with the weather and the heights.
