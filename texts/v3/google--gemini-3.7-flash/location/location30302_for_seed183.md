At this altitude, the sky does not darken into blue; it bleaches out into a flat, searing white that burns against the retinas without warming the skin. 

The range is made entirely of dense, columnar calcite, grey slate, and sheared black glass, thrust upward into needle-peaks so steep and tightly packed they resemble a petrified storm. There is no soil, no lichen, no moss. The landscape is profoundly inorganic, yet it is drenched. The air, thin and bitingly cool, sits at a permanent, supernatural saturation point. Water does not merely rain down from clouds; it sweats out of the rock itself and condenses directly out of the empty air in heavy, shivering globules that cling to sheer vertical faces before sliding down in wide, silent sheets.

The scale is vast, but the space is packed tight with mass. Spire after spire rises thousands of feet from unseen rifts below, their flanks so close together that in many places one could reach across a chasm and touch the opposite wall. Yet the geometry of these chasms does not hold to ordinary perspective. As the spires rise, the gaps between them do not widen or narrow according to distance; space folds and pinches along clean, razor-sharp seams. Looking down into a fissure, the view shears sideways into an impossible repetition of the same wet wall, reflecting light with the brilliant, watery glare of a fresh fracture in flint.

Because of these spatial rifts, gravity here is neither uniform nor dependable. In the narrow alleys between the needles, droplets of water fall downward on the left wall while weeping upward toward the blinding sky on the right. Long, rope-like braids of water hang suspended in mid-air, spinning slowly like glass rods, vibrating with a low, hydraulic hum. 

The sound is immense and constant, a ringing, resonant roar that has nothing to do with wind. It is the acoustic friction of thousands of tons of pressurized condensation shearing against smooth mineral planes, mixed with the deep, percussive chime of the spires themselves as they settle and flex under tectonic and spatial stress. The sound carries the weight of an ocean, but there is no sea—only the perpetual cycle of moisture tearing itself out of the vacuum, running down the glassy stone, and evaporating back into the glare before it hits the floor of the rifts.

Scattered across this vertical labyrinth are the marks of an old, stubborn, and rudimentary habitation. They are not cities or temples, but survival stations built with the most elemental tools. 

Wedged into the dry recesses beneath overhangs—or lashed directly into spatial folds where gravity balances to a gentle zero—are small, solitary cradles made of dry-stacked slate and heavy, cold-hewn stones. Long-dead or long-departed hands used chipped flint adzes and counterweighted leverage to drive crude bronze wedges into the glass stone. From these wedges hang thick, braided tethers made of mineral fibers and cured gut, holding suspended platforms of split rock. 

These shelters are primitive, almost pre-architectural: simple stone circles without mortar, wind-baffles built from shingled shale, and shallow basins carved by hand to catch the pure, cold condensation before it slips over the abyss. At one ledge, a series of crude handholds has been hammered into the obsidian; beside them, a heavy pendulum of unworked basalt hangs from a braided line, not pointing down toward the earth, but tilted permanently at a forty-five-degree angle toward an invisible center of mass out in the empty glare.

There are no birds in the air, no worms in the rock, no spores drifting in the updrafts. The only movement belongs to the water and the light. The brilliance of the day refracts through the airborne moisture, casting blinding, prismatic caustic networks across the dark slate walls. These webs of light crawl and pulse across the rock faces with the deliberate, rhythmic pace of a lung breathing. 

When the light hits the weeping edges of the spires just right, the stone seems to dissolve into sheer, cascading reflection, making the entire mountain range look like a forest of rising, falling, and suspended rivers of liquid mercury, cold to the touch and roaring with the sound of a world being continually washed clean of everything except its own bare bones.
