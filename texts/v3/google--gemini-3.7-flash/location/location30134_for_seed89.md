At fourteen thousand feet, where the outside air is razor-thin and freezes before it can touch the lungs, there is a deep, bell-mouthed cleft in the mountain’s basalt crown. Outside, the crags are stark and dry, but within the lip of the fissure, the mountain breathes outward: a slow, unending exhalation of subterranean heat that turns the vertical world inside out.

The chamber is small—no wider than a parish hall, but soaring upward into a chimney of slick, soot-colored stone that tapers toward a distant slit of pale daylight. Down here, that light is nearly extinguished, diluted into a dense, purplish gloom. The air is startlingly warm, muggy with the collective respiration of millions of lungs and the slow, wet decay of organic matter. Water does not fall as rain; instead, the warm updraft collides with the freezing rim high above and rolls back down the walls as a persistent, lukewarm sweat. Droplets gather on every protrusion, trickling through centuries-deep layers of guano and polished stone, collecting in shallow, tepid basins along the floor before sinking back into the mountain's porous fractures.

The erosion here is ancient and settled. The rock has long since yielded its sharp edges to the endless passage of moisture and claw. Basalt ribs, smoothed until they resemble wet black leather, curve inward like the hull of an overturned ship. There are no sudden collapses, no jagged scree. Every contour has been worn down by thousands of years of rubbing, roosting, and the quiet chemical work of damp droppings.

What fills this pocket of warm darkness is an almost suffocating density of life. 

The walls do not look like stone; they look like a shifting, undulating pelt. Tens of thousands of high-altitude swiftlets and fat, thumb-sized bats roost shoulder to shoulder, their bodies packed so tightly that their heat creates its own micro-weather. Below them, occupying every horizontal ledge and hollowed-out niche, are colonies of blind, slate-grey cliff mice—heavy-bodied, slow-moving creatures adapted to the thick, oxygen-poor air trapped within the chimney. Beneath the carpet of warm fur and feathers, the floor is an ocean of black beetles, blind cave-crickets, and pale, ribbon-like flatworms that churn through the wet sediment in absolute silence. 

Yet the space is anything but quiet. The acoustics of the chimney act as an amplifier, trapping and multiplying the sounds of this massed metabolism. It is a continuous, deafening roar: the dry, rustling rattle of millions of wing-quills rubbing together, the wet click of claws against slime-coated basalt, the frantic high-frequency chittering of the nursery roosts, and the soft, rhythmic *slap-drip* of condensation falling into guano pools. It sounds like a river running over broad gravel, or a heavy squall trapped in an iron drum.

Vegetation has found no real foothold here, save for a slick, velvety skin of copper-colored cyanobacteria and microscopic fungi that coat the upper walls where the steam is thickest. They need no sun, subsisting entirely on the ammonia vapors and the thermal wash. 

Humanity has touched this place, but only as a thief that comes and goes on foot. There are no walls, no cleared paths, no monuments. High on an overhanging spur of basalt, wedged into a deep fracture, rests a cluster of five-foot poles fashioned from fire-hardened pine and notched with flint tools—left behind by hunters who scale the outer ice-faces to harvest the fat squabs and oil-rich mice from the lower ledges. A scrap of braided sinew hangs rotted and limp from one of the pegs; a single hearth-smudge from a long-dead torch of animal fat stains the ceiling directly below the opening. These are the only marks of an outside world: simple, desperate instruments of predation that the damp air is slowly turning to mush.

Time moves with a heavy, unhurried cadence in the chimney. The thermal breath never stops; the condensation never dries. Outside, seasons of bitter ice-storms and blistering ultraviolet sun sweep across the high peaks, but down inside the vent, the world remains dark, dripping, deafeningly alive, and wrapped in its own thick, feverish heat.
