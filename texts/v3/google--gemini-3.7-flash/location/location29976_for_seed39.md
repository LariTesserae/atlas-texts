The rock here has not had time to blunt. At this altitude—nearly eight thousand meters above the sea, where the air is pale, thin, and hard on the lungs—the geology is newborn in tectonic time. The ridges are sheer, violent fractures of dark granodiorite and basalt, their edges so keen they seem freshly cleaved from the crust. There are no softened talus skirts or rounded domes; the mountain faces are vertical sheets of raw, unweathered mineral, glittering with exposed quartz veins and greasy with constant moisture.

Despite the near-freezing cold, water is everywhere. A perpetual, low-slung ceiling of dense, slate-colored vapor presses down against the jagged crests, condensing continuously against the cold stone. The rock faces weep. Thousands of hairline runnels bleed down the fractured cliffs, coalescing into thin, braided sheets of black water that slide across wide, empty stepped ledges. Where the basalt steps flatten out into broad cirques, shallow, gravel-bottomed pools have collected, their surfaces filmed with cold tension, shivering under an intermittent, razor-thin wind.

There is almost no constructed presence on the ground—no roads, no concrete footings, no rusted cables anchored into the rock. The terrain remains entirely unbuilt, vast and vacant in its horizontal sweep. Yet the sky above the cloud cover belongs entirely to heavy industry. Miles above the peaks, beyond the grey soup of the condensation layer, high-altitude atmospheric extraction barges and heavy automated freighters make their steady, invisible transits. Their presence is felt not as visible geometry, but as a deep, sub-audible vibration in the bedrock—a low, rhythmic thrum of high-bypass turbofans and ionizing scrubbers that makes the water trembling in the rock pools form neat, concentric rings. Occasionally, the dim twilight under the clouds is stained with a greasy, industrial iridescence, or the faint, sharp smell of ozone and synthetic lubricants drifting down from the upper troposphere with the settling mist.

Between the barren, soaring scale of the cliffs and the empty shelves of stone, life thrives with quiet intensity. The vegetation is spare, kept low to the rock to escape the frost and the scouring air: dark crustose lichens that bite deep into the fresh mineral faces, dense mats of black liverwort hugging the wet crevices, and tiny, rubbery cushions of alpine saxifrage clinging to micro-fissures where the weeping water is steady. 

Because the landscape is vast and largely undisturbed from below, animals have colonized every tier of this embryonic height. Dense flocks of broad-winged, slate-grey petrels—migrants riding the strong thermal shears between the high ridges—roost in the vertical crevices by the thousands. Their soft, guttural chucking and the wet slap of their feathers echo faintly against the stone, muffled by the mist. Along the narrower ledges, heavy-bodied mountain ungulates with thick, oiled coats of pale wool move in sure-footed lines, grazing on the lichen slicks and the tough taproots exposed in the cracked shelves. In the cold pools, swarms of blind, pale crustaceans churn the fine mineral silt, feeding on organic fallout washing down from the moss-choked fissures above. 

The light never truly opens up. The sun is only ever a diffused, pale disk wandering behind miles of grey cloud, keeping the valley in a perpetual, cold crepuscular state. The world feels immense, sharp-edged, and strangely suspended: an untouched wilderness of raw mineral and abundant life, quietly vibrating to the mechanized pulse of the sky above it.
