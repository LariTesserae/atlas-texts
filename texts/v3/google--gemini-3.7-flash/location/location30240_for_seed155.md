The floor of this world is not stone, nor is it soil. It is a continent-spanning plateau of solid, ancient lignum—compressed heartwood kilometers deep, worn down by forgotten epochs of wind into vast, undulating flats as smooth as polished slate. There is no dirt, only grain. Where one might expect granite crags or scree slopes, there are only the worn-down, petrified stumps of primordial taproots, wide as townships, their growth rings acting as contour lines across a plateau that sits so high the atmosphere feels scrubbed, cold, and razor-thin.

It is bitterly cool, but not frozen. Intermittent bands of condensation roll across the expanse like breathing tides, leaving a slick, varnished sheen on the wooden plains before vanishing back into the pale, brilliant daylight.

Rising out of this sparse expanse are the cities. They are not crowded or clustered; they are laid out on a monumental, desolate grid, separated by miles of bare, polished cambium. These are industrial settlements of staggering scale, built not by stacking bricks, but through some ancient, violent synthesis of foundry-work and botanical force. Towering blast furnaces are formed from single, hollowed-out ironwood burls, their chimneys banded with red cast iron and sweating cold, oily resin. Heavy cable-cars and iron gantries stretch across the emptiness, their steel cables rooted deep into living vascular bundles that pulse under the tension.

The architecture belongs to a heavy, steam-and-piston era, yet it is utterly indivisible from the flora. Machine shops hundreds of feet high are walled with thick, fibrous bark that has been planed and greased, housing enormous, slow-turning flywheels forged from mineralized sap and cold-rolled steel. The technology operates on a logic of biological combustion: boilers feed on the slow, pressurized fermentation of internal xylem fluids, driving miles of reciprocating push-rods that clatter across the plains, linking distant mechanical districts to one another.

There is a profound, jarring loudness to the region. The silence of the high elevation is completely shattered by the acoustics of the ground itself. The hollow, vascular network beneath the wooden surface acts as an immense resonant chamber. Every stroke of an industrial stamp-mill, every venting of a copper pressure-valve miles away, vibrates through the floorboards of the world, amplifying into a deep, oceanic roar. Overhead, the wind catches the taut steel guy-wires and the petrified flutes of dead canopy-towers, producing a shrieking harmonic drone that never ceases.

Space here does not behave with strict fidelity. Because the vegetation *is* the tectonic substrate, the living architecture exerts a subtle, warping pull on perspective. A steam-crane that appears to be thirty miles distant on the flat horizon will suddenly cast its shadow over you; stepping across a major growth-seam in the plateau can cause the daylight to shift abruptly from crisp white noon to the stark, shadowless glare of an eclipse. The horizon curves upward at the edges, cradled by the long-dead swell of an ancient outer bark-wall that should be below the curvature of the earth, yet remains plainly visible, etched in sharp detail against the pale sky.

Animals are almost completely absent. Once in a long cycle, something small, pale, and chitinous might dart between the massive bronze teeth of an idling gear-train, or a single oil-slicked bird might be seen struggling through the crosswinds between two ironwood smokestacks, but otherwise the ecosystem is a closed loop of plant, steel, and steam. 

The machines do not seem to be operated by a visible populace. Rather, the city runs with the blind, rhythmic inevitability of an organ. Iron pistons plunge into cedar-lined cylinders; condensation drips in cold, rhythmic taps into deep, grease-filmed troughs; and the ancient, flattened plateau slowly flexes under the weight of its own mechanical canopy, holding its breath in the thin, cold light at the top of the world.
