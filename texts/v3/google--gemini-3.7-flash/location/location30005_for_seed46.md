Suspended twelve thousand feet up, inside a narrow, vertical fissure no wider than a chapel nave, the air does not circulate. It is baking, bone-dry, and intensely bright—illuminated by an overhead split in the cliff face where raw, unsoftened sunlight drops like an incandescent blade. 

This is an embryonic place, freshly torn from the crust. Its stone shows no roundness, no grain-softening from wind or rain; the walls are sheer planes of obsidian, fractured hematite, and dense, unpolished basalt, with edges so razor-new they seem capable of slicing shadows in half. There is no water here, not even in molecular trace within the rock. Instead, the chemistry of the fissure runs on dry thermal gradients and heavy, volatilized minerals that settle out of the heat as microscopic needles of zinc and sulfur.

Despite the drought, the floor and stepped ledges of the fissure are overgrown with a lush, silent, inorganic thicket. 

These are not plants of seed and sap, yet they possess the frantic, sprawling logic of jungle foliage. From the unweathered basalt sprout dense stands of what appear to be cast-iron ferns and coiled copper briars, their fronds pressed flat and rigid against the dry heat. They do not photosynthesize; they draw upon the immense thermoelectric potential between the blistering sun-struck upper rift and the cold tectonic iron deep beneath. Thick, jointed stalks of sintered silicate rise three spans high, terminating in wide, fluted leaves of razor-thin mica that tremble only when the earth itself shifts. They grow with strange, deliberate vigor, crowding the ledges in heavy clusters, but leaving vast, empty gulfs of arid space between one terrace and the next.

There is a severe, almost mechanical precision to how this life arranges itself. No human hand or factory ever laid a finger here, yet the landscape expresses the principles of scaled, heavy industry as if it were a fundamental property of matter.

Within the hollows of the basalt, naturally occurring crankshafts of dense pyroxene rotate against crystalline bushings, turning once every three hours in a slow, dry grind. They are driven by the expanding and contracting heat of the rock face, linked to interlocking systems of stone levers and self-tempering mineral rods that stretch like vines across the chasm. In the deeper pockets, embryonic pistons of solid beryl slide within unblemished cylindrical voids, drawing in the hot, static-choked air and compressing it into dry, rhythmic pulses. There is no grease, no steam, no fluid cooling; the surfaces glide on microscopic dustings of pure graphite, polished by their own nascent friction.

The sound of the place is sparse and restrained. Long minutes pass in dry, absolute silence, save for the faint hiss of thermal expansion. Then, with heavy patience, a basalt linkage somewhere fifty feet up will shift: a single, resonant *clack*, like an iron gate dropping into place, followed by the deep, subterranean groaning of a dry axle adjusting its pitch. The sound does not echo; the mica foliage drinks it instantly.

Reality here suffers from a quiet, localized fraying. 

In certain alcoves, the geometry of the mechanism refuses to resolve. A drive-rod made of vitreous quartz can be seen passing cleanly through a solid slab of slate without fracturing either, emerging on the other side to engage a rotating wheel of pure, unworked magnetite. In other corners, gravity lists sideways by twenty degrees, so that the dry, metallic vines hang toward the eastern wall rather than the fissure’s floor, their metallic tendrils fusing seamlessly into the stone as if the concept of growth and the concept of metal-casting were entirely interchangeable.

Nothing moves quickly. A new frond of stamped-tin silicate takes forty dry days to unroll, ratcheting outward tooth by tooth along a spiraled gear-stem. Below, in the basin of the pocket, shallow drifts of white talc gather around the base of the machine-shoots, totally untouched by wind, marked only by the angular geometric footprints where new structural crystals have recently dropped from the canopy above.

It is a miniature foundry without intent, a high-altitude womb of unweathered iron, stone, and silent, heavy greenery, baking quietly under the clear sky, steadily assembling itself into something vast, patient, and completely indifferent to flesh.
