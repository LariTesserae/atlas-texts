The horizon is a clean, ruled seam where the sky meets a floor of unglazed, kiln-baked terracotta and dressed pale limestone. There is no natural soil anywhere. Beneath the sky lies an intentional, continuous crust of laid brick, fitted flagstones, and poured lime slurry, stretching outward with the unbroken curvature of a continent. It is newly made: the mortar between the slabs is still sharp and unweathered, showing the drag of the wooden floats and the crisp, square edges of blocks that have not yet had their corners rounded by wind or sand. 

The scale of this paved expanse is vast enough to dictate its own meteorology. Across thousands of square leagues of low-lying, perfectly graded terrace, the midday sun strikes the unshaded stone, heating the ceramic floor until the air above it shimmers and fractures into layered mirages. The heat is dry and oppressive, hovering near thirty-eight or forty degrees Celsius for most of the daylight hours, baking the limestone until it radiates warmth deep into the late evening.

Yet this is not an arid wasteland in the traditional sense; it operates on a strict, rhythmic moisture cycle born of its own thermal inertia. 

During the rapid drop of temperature at night, the immense surface area of the pavers cools faster than the atmospheric column above it. Great blankets of heavy, warm maritime air drift across the lowlands from unseen distances, striking the cold ceramic expanse. By dawn, the entire constructed floor is slick with dense condensation. Water beads along the fresh lime-grout seams and gathers into shallow, millimeter-thin sheets across wide drainage planes. These gentle inclines—so subtle they appear flat to the eye—steer the dew into long, shallow collection channels cut directly into the paving stones.

For two or three hours each morning, the landscape is damp, luminous, and cool. Shallow reflecting pools stretch for miles, mirroring the pale dawn sky. A few hardy organisms have already found purchase in this embryonic world: tiny, pale crustose lichens have begun to bloom in the microscopic pores of the uncured mortar, and occasional tufts of wiry, salt-tolerant brassicas push through the hair-line relief joints every few hundred yards. These are sparse, opportunistic pioneers, scattered thin and far between across the endless brickwork.

As the sun climbs, the atmosphere changes rapidly. The heat flashes the standing sheets of morning moisture back into vapor, filling the first fifty feet of air with a dense, humid haze that smells of wet chalk, warm ceramic, and cured lime. By noon, this vapor lifts into towering, isolated cumulus clouds that drift high above the terrace. Sometimes these clouds build enough mass to drop sudden, brief sheets of rain across a twenty-mile band of paving, washing the dust toward the perimeter drains before vanishing under the relentless solar glare.

There is a striking absence of technology. There are no conduits, no metal fittings, no cabling, no engines, and no signs of mechanical fabrication. The entire world-floor is a triumph of pure masonry, ash-lime chemistry, and manual geometry. Every channel, retaining ridge, expansion seam, and reservoir cistern is constructed from fired earth and shaped stone, laid down with monumental patience and left vacant. 

The soundscape is one of immense, ringing stillness, punctuated only by the thermal breathing of the architecture. In the afternoon, as the terracotta absorbs the maximum heat load, one can hear the subtle, rhythmic *tink-tink* of millions of bricks expanding against their mortar beds, a dry, crystalline murmur that runs under the low rush of convection winds. When the localized thermal downdrafts sweep across the flats, they make a hollow, unhindered whistle, having no trees or natural hills to break against—only the flat, clean floor, slowly baking under a bright, indifferent sun.
