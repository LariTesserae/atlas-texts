At this altitude, the air should be thin and cold, but it is held warm in a vast pocket of stone, caught within miles of stepped megalithic terraces that climb endlessly through the dark. There is no natural ground here. Beneath every root system, every puddle, and every trailing vine lies dressed granite and fitted basalt. Decades or centuries of slow vegetative accumulation have created a thin skin of humus over the terraces, but the bones of this landscape are entirely engineered: monumental stairs, corbelled viaducts, endless tiers of retaining walls, and cisterns carved thirty feet deep into platforms that stretch beyond the range of sight.

It is nearly pitch-black. A dense ceiling of warm cloud settles into the high mountain pass, cutting off starlight and trapping the heat radiating from the colossal masonry below. 

What the darkness hides, the sound fills with almost physical weight. The acoustic space is vast and hard-edged—reverberating between sheer stone facades and vaulted arcades—yet it is completely drowned out by the volume of living things. The noise is constant, layered, and deafening. Millions of tree frogs occupy the damp seams between cyclopean blocks, their percussive clicking and trilling merging into a single, droning roar that vibrates in the chest. Above them, nesting within the deep friezes and drainage spouts of abandoned facades, colonies of thousands of night birds and swifts shift and shriek. The air itself hums with the friction of wings: clouds of heavy-bodied moths, beetles the size of palms scraping against wet stone, and rivers of microbats pouring out of the dry interior chambers of the lower terraces.

Water moves through the complex with quiet, patient order. It does not rage; it condenses. The high-altitude clouds drift through the warm forest canopy that has colonized the masonry, dropping moisture onto broad, leathery leaves and slick moss carpets. The droplets gather, slide down aerial roots, and find the ancient channels carved into the floors of the plazas. Long stone gutters, darkened by centuries of wet lichen, guide this intermittent trickle toward central reservoirs and cistern networks. In the blackness, you hear the endless, rhythmic *plink* and rush of water falling from carved gargoyles into deep, hidden pools below the plazas.

The vegetation is heavy, lush, and firmly anchored to the masonry. Fig roots have cast nets over vertical walls fifty feet high, their pale wood swollen and flattened against the granite, slowly working into the mortarless joints without toppling them. Everything has settled into an ancient, structural equilibrium. Where stones have shifted, they have done so slowly, finding a second rest in the grip of dense root systems and deep moss cushions. Epiphytes cling to carved lintels, bromeliads hold small pools of dark water three stories above the terrace floors, and thick mats of creeping ferns soften the edges of monumental stairways that lead nowhere in the dark.

Every surface is occupied. If you pass a hand along the face of a wall, you feel the cold, rough-chiseled stone give way instantly to the dry chitin of roosting katydids, the damp, smooth bellies of geckos hunting in the dark, or the furred mass of a sleeping spider colony. Snakes move slowly along the horizontal ledges between tiers, hunting by heat in the humid stillness, their scales rasping quietly over the grain of the granite. The food chain here is stacked tight and furious, sustained entirely by the sheer biomass that the warm, cloud-fed canopy produces on top of these endless stone platforms.

Farther out, where the terrace edges drop off into the drop of the mountain valley, the wind pushes upward, warm and wet, smelling of stone dust, crushed foliage, and animal musk. The architecture continues down into the abyss, tier upon tier, completely unlit, completely inhabited, roaring into the night.
