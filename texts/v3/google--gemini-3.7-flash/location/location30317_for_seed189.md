It exists beneath the bedrock, yet it is neither stone nor cave. It is an internal hollow—a single, dilated vacuole the size of a cathedral nave, suspended inside the taproot of a subterranean organism so vast that geological maps misidentify its vascular bundles as fault lines. 

Though deeply buried, the chamber is fiercely, relentlessly bright. 

The light does not fall from a sun; it is generated by the walls themselves, which are composed entirely of dense, translucent cambium that burns with a cold, pale-gold bioluminescence. Every square inch of the boundary is living plant tissue—taut, fibrous, wet with fine sheets of condensation that never pool into lakes, but cling to the bark-ribs like varnish. 

Yet for all this overwhelming mass of vegetable life defining the perimeter, the interior is shockingly empty. It is a near-vacuum of space. There are no soil beds, no thickets, no hanging tapestries of moss cluttering the center. You stand on an expanse of wet, freshly split heartwood, staring into hundreds of meters of clear, unobstructed air. The scale feels strangely intimate, like an attic or an egg, yet the distances deceive: a step forward seems to cover yards, while looking upward stretches the ceiling into a distant, soaring vault of green-gold membrane.

The noise is the first thing that destabilizes the mind. It is deafening. 

It is the sound of hydraulic tension operating at impossible pressures. Deep within the walls, million-gallon surges of sap race through microscopic capillary networks with the roar of low-altitude jet engines. Every few seconds, the sound is punctuated by the violent, percussive *thwack* of cavitation—bubbles forming and collapsing in the vascular tubes with the acoustic crack of artillery. Because the air in this pocket is warm and dense with vapor, the sound reverberates endlessly, rattling teeth and vibrating in the hollow of the chest.

Reality here is governed entirely by turgor pressure rather than standard physics. 

Where the sap pressure spikes, the geometry of the room distorts. A wall of pale cellular marrow fifty feet away will suddenly bow inward, not by bending, but by locally multiplying its own dimensions, bringing its fibrous surface within an inch of your face before snapping back into a distant horizon. Upward and downward are negotiable; the pull you feel is not planetary gravity, but the chemical draw of the root-flesh beneath your feet, which secretes a weak, electrostatic stickiness that keeps bodies anchored to the pale wood. 

There are faint, baffling traces of primitive presence here. Wedged into a jagged split in the floor—a young fissure, its edges still weeping sticky, amber resin—is a ladder made of fibrous bast, lashed together with braided root-hair. Its rungs are notched with crude scrapings from what looks like a sharpened river pebble or a flake of bone. Whoever made it did not build a shelter; there is nothing here to build with except the wood you walk on, which heals and swallows foreign objects within hours. The ladder is already being digested, half-submerged in a glassy skin of freshly grown cuticle.

Fauna is almost non-existent, but not entirely. High in the luminous emptiness, drifting through the loud, warm air currents like plankton in a sunlit sea, are solitary, translucent organisms. They resemble hollow glass chrysalises, six feet long, propelled purely by the expulsion of condensed vapor from their tails. They have no faces, no limbs; they graze on the electromagnetic hum of the light-emitting walls, brushing against the wood for a fraction of a second before recoiling back into the void.

The air smells sharply of crushed mint, ozone, and the sour, yeast-like tang of rapid mitosis. Everything here is in a state of violent, young expansion. The chamber was not hollowed out by rot or water; it was torn open by the plant’s own sudden, explosive growth cycle mere days or weeks ago. You can see the torn cellulose fibers along the ceiling still quivering, their cellular fluid leaking into the air as micro-droplets that create sudden, brief indoor rainbows across the blazing void.

To remain here is to exist inside the violent metabolism of a singular, non-sentient god. The walls hum, brighten to an eye-watering white, then soften to a bruised chartreuse as the organism breathes. You are standing inside a fresh wound that is also a womb, hollow and roaring, buried beneath the world, where the rules of space are rewritten by the sheer, ecstatic force of cellular division.
