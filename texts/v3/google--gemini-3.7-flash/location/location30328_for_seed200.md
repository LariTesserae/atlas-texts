The ground is new crust, glassy and sharp, unsoftened by the patient work of wind or centuries of rain. It is a vast, sunken basin where the floor of the world is still welling up from beneath, cooling into slick black basalt, jagged shelves of obsidian, and soft mounds of warm, steaming tuff. Yet there is no barrenness here; before the stone has even finished setting, the biomass has already swallowed it whole. 

It is a place of suffocating, unvarying heat—a permanent 110 degrees held fast under a roof of perpetual steam. The moisture does not fall in dramatic storms; it simply exists in the air at total saturation, weeping from the underside of leaves, condensing on slick rock faces, and rising again in thin, steady plumes from cracks in the ground. The water gathers in shallow, scalding pans and lukewarm, moss-rimmed depressions, never draining away, never evaporating completely, locked in an immovable, closed-circuit vapor.

The light that reaches the floor is dim and slate-green, filtered through layers of colossal, rubbery fronds and a suspended ceiling of low, sulfurous fog. There are no sunbeams, no sharp shadows, and no twilight to mark the passing of hours. The illumination is entirely diffuse, maintaining the same dull, emerald glow without end. 

Because the landscape never shifts—possessing no seasons, no tides, no diurnal rhythm beneath the shroud—life exists in a state of hyper-dense, steady-state equilibrium. The vegetation does not wither or shed; broad, vein-ribbed leaves the size of skiffs overlap in sheer, vertical walls of chlorophyll. Fleshy, primitive horsetails, liverworts the thickness of leather hides, and bloated, translucent fungi fight for every square inch of unweathered rock. Roots do not dig deep into the hard, embryonic stone; instead, they weave into a thick, springy mat that floats mere inches above the hot crust, trapping pockets of boiling mineral water beneath a living carpet.

And through every stratum of this suffocating green, fauna moves in overwhelming, staggering numbers. 

The sound is a relentless, physical pressure: an unbroken, multi-tonal drone produced by trillions of chitinous wings, the wet suction of countless soft-bodied crawlers shifting over leaves, and the subterranean hissing of gas escaping through mud. Pale, blind amphibians with translucent ribcages cluster in the warm shallows by the thousands, their throats pulsing in synchronized, rhythmic clicks. Armored millipedes, thick as tree limbs and segmented in oily iridescent black, wind through the root-tangle, their hundreds of legs tapping against the glass-sharp basalt in a continuous, dry rattle.

Above the floor, the mid-tier of vegetation is choked with life. Colonies of primitive, soft-shelled arthropods hang like heavy fruit from the underside of fronds, excreting sweet, hot nectar that draws clouds of pale, moth-like gliders. Predatory flatworms, long as ribbons and glowing with a faint, digestive bioluminescence, drape over the stems, never needing to hunt so much as wait for the sheer volume of passing bodies to brush against their sticky margins. Nothing here starves, and nothing overpopulates to collapse; the furnace simply burns at maximum capacity, continuously converting the earth’s internal heat and chemical soup into meat, shell, and spore.

The basin stretches for miles, an immense, low-lying expanse unbroken by hills or canyons. It is a regional cradle of embryonic geology held hostage by an ancient, runaway biology. Standing anywhere within it, the horizon is lost behind the dense, near-touching walls of wet leaves and the silver-grey curtain of steam. There are no paths, no clearances, and no echoes—only the heavy, hot air pressing against the skin, the sharp smell of fresh volcanic sulfur mingled with rotting sweetness, and the tireless, deafening thrum of a world that was born fully grown and refuses to change.
