The air arrives first as a physical weight: warm, velvet-thick, and smelling of cracked chitin, warm sap, and the metallic tang of fresh blood on hot stone. It is almost completely dark. Above, where a sky ought to be, an interlocking ceiling of black-leaved, leathery canopies and clinging vine-mats blocks whatever celestial light exists, leaving only the faintest deep-violet luminescence from bruised fungal blooms and the cold, intermittent sparks of chemical lanterns carried on the bellies of flying things.

This is the Floor of the Fracture—a vast, sunken trough stretching for leagues between raw, vertical scarps of freshly ruptured basalt. The rock beneath is young and violent. It has not known weather long enough to soften; the edges of the shale shelves are razor-sharp, the volcanic fissures are clean-lipped and jagged, and the talus slopes consist of needle-pointed slag that rings like broken glass underfoot. There are no rivers here, but the air itself sweats. Intermittent sheets of condensation run down the slick obsidian faces, collecting in shallow, tepid stone cups before being lapped dry or boiled away by the collective heat of the valley's inhabitants.

The silence here does not exist; it has likely never existed. The sound is a steady, concussive roar that vibrates through the soles of the feet and rattles the cartilage of the ear. It is the unceasing collision of millions of living bodies: the high-frequency screech of serrated leg-joints scraping against thorax plates, the wet thrumming of heavy wing-cases, the collective chittering of billions of mandible pairs chewing through fibrous root systems, and the low, resonant bellowing of blind, soft-skinned quadrupeds that barge blindly through the undergrowth like battering rams. 

Vegetation here does not grow toward light; it grows toward heat and friction. Black-stemmed ferns, tough as boiled leather and barbed like harpoons, burst out of every crack in the basalt, their fronds shredded and regrowing within the same cycle as herds trample them into pulp. Tangle-vines with pulpy, water-storing nodes snake across the floor, knitting together the sharp rocks with nets of pale, pulsing fiber. In the lower hollows, dense thickets of thorn-bamboo shoot upward in violent, creaking bursts, their joints swelling with trapped condensation that whistles under internal pressure.

Through this black, thorny jungle moves an ecology in an absolute frenzy of turnover. The biomass is so dense that creatures walk upon the backs of other creatures. Flattened, many-legged scavengers the size of shields scuttle over the shale in unending tides, their claws making a sound like hail on slate. Above them, suspended in the mid-canopy, long-limbed gibbon-like predators with pale, vestigial eye-sockets swing through the tangle, hunting not by sight but by the thermal wake left by the swarms below. They drop down like stones, strike with bone-spurred forearms, and are dragged back up before the ground-scuttlers can strip their limbs.

There are signs of an older, crude intention pressed into the chaos. In the alcoves of the sharp canyon walls, out of the direct flow of the herds, one finds places where the obsidian has been deliberately flaked into heavy, hand-held choppers, left lying beside heaps of marrow-cracked femurs. Here and there, rough barricades of interwoven thorn-wood and sinew are lashed across narrow defiles—not to hold territory, but to channel the stampedes into natural pit-traps where the stone floor has broken through into deeper, steam-venting caverns. These are not permanent dwellings; nothing here can remain stationary long enough to build a home. They are the quick, brutal fabrications of opportunistic hunters who strike into the dark, gorge on hot meat, and retreat before their barricades are pulverized by the sheer momentum of the next wave.

Everything in the Fracture is hurried, sharp, and brief. Life cycles are measured in rotations of the steam-vents. An insectoid creature pupates from the moist mulch of a fallen thorn-tree, inflates its wings in the dark, mates in a furious, mid-air grapple, drops its sticky clutch of eggs into a fissure of hot basalt, and is pulled apart by blind crawlers—all within the span of three hundred breaths. The ground is never cold; it is kept feverish by the volcanic bedrock below and the endless, churning friction of claws, hooves, and bellies above.

Looking out across the expanse from a high shelf of fractured stone, the darkness resolves not into emptiness, but into a shifting, undulating texture. The whole valley floor breathes and rolls like a black sea caught in an offshore gale. Pockets of bioluminescence flare up—a sudden smear of cold cyan where a colony of siphon-worms has been crushed under a bulk, or a trailing streak of yellow-green as a winged swarm erupts from a split boulder—only to be instantly swallowed back into the dark. The sound never drops, never pauses to inhale; it remains a solid, physical wall of noise, the voice of a landscape entirely consumed by the frantic, blind labor of staying alive for one more second.
