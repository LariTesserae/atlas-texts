The high shelves of the plateau stretch out under a bright, unsoftened sun, but everything upon them is glistening wet. At this altitude, the air is thin enough to carry sharp, clear daylight, yet warm and densely charged with moisture that rises from the lowlands in perpetual, silent drafts. As the vapor meets the broad, uplifted basalt tablelands, it condenses against every surface in a slow, unending sweat. Water does not fall as violent rain here; instead, it weeps steadily from stone faces, beads along millions of yards of exposed pipe, and sheets down the tiered flanks of the infrastructure into wide, shallow gutters.

There is not a single leaf, branch, or blade of grass across the entire continental rise. The world here has bypassed the green kingdom entirely, or outlived it. In place of roots and foliage, the bedrock and the architecture are stripped down to pure mineral and metal: dark, rain-smoothed basalt, slabs of dense slate, and enormous expanses of industrial iron and copper that have weathered into dull blacks, liver-browns, and chalky verdigris. 

Yet the absence of flora leaves no void in the living world. The plateau is choked with animal life—restless, dense, and layered so thickly over the built forms that the ironwork seems to have grown an outer hide of chitin, skin, and shell. 

They feed not on vegetation, but on the rich, sulfurous microbial slicks that bloom in the warm condensation, and upon one another. Billions of pale, heavy-shelled mollusks and jointed, flat-bodied scavengers encrust the vertical surfaces of the aqueducts, their rasping mouthparts scraping mineral salts directly from the weeping stone. Above them, nesting in every dry pocket beneath girder spans and corrugated roofs, are vast colonies of warm-blooded, leathery-winged gliders. They cling in interlocking clusters beneath eaves and crane booms, their bodies shifting in wave-like twitches that send a dry, rustling rattle echoing down the canyon-streets. 

The settlement itself is immense, built on the scale of a mountain range. It is not a city of domestic dwellings, but a vast, organized complex of heavy industry that has settled so deeply into its environment it functions like geology. Gigantic slow-stroke piston pumps, driven by internal steam and the gravity of collected runoff, beat with a low, heavy pulse that vibrates through the rock underfoot. Foundries and processing flues rise hundreds of feet into the warm air, exhaling clean, white steam that instantly dissolves into the high atmosphere. Overhead, power cables as thick as tree trunks hang in long catenaries between rusted towers, beaded with dew that catches the midday sun like strings of glass beads.

The design of the construction is old, settled, and rounded by time. The sharp angles of early ironwork have long since been softened by continuous water-flow and the abrasion of countless claws and shells scrambling over them. Canals paved with dark, river-smoothed cobbles divide the great mechanical districts, carrying warm, mineral-rich runoff between the machine halls.

The noise of the place is continuous, heavy, and rhythmic. It is a composite of the deep, subterranean thrum of engines, the roar of water tumbling over rounded spillways, and the unrelenting, high-frequency sound of the fauna: the clicking of millions of legs against sheet-metal gangways, the wet suction of clinging pods, and the sharp, territorial whistlings of the winged creatures wheeling in dense drafts above the cooling towers. 

The dynamics of this shelf are patient and self-sustaining. There are no sudden seasons, no abrupt storms. The vast machinery turns slowly, smoothly lubricated by water and fat, processing mineral slurry and condensing high-altitude fog across centuries. The animals reproduce and die across the rivets and stone gutters, their calcified remains dissolving slowly in the acidic runoff to be laid down again as new mineral crusts upon the steel. Everything is bound in a vast, warm, metallic equilibrium, baking under the bright sky and dripping continuously into the dark stone below.
