The glare arrives first, stripped of heat by five thousand meters of altitude. It is an unsoftened, diamond-cut light that strikes the high cirque and shatters against thousands of wet, curved planes. Everything here is polished to a lens. 

The basin is small—barely four hundred paces from one sheer wall of dark, iron-veined diorite to the other—perched like a chipped bowl in the mountain’s throat. Despite the absolute, marrow-splitting cold, it is not dry. It weeps continuously from every seam. Heavy, supercooled brine, thick with dissolved salts and vitreous minerals, wells up through the cracked bedrock and cascades over the terraces in fast, silent laminations. It does not freeze into solid sheets; instead, it exists in a state of violent, half-gelid flux, crusting into razor-thin rimes of vitreous ice for mere seconds before being dissolved and swept away by the next pulse of freezing water.

Crowded into this freezing basin is a dense, vertical hive of human-scale habitation, ancient beyond reckoning. The architecture is primitive, built of dry-stacked basalt river-stones, mastodon-thick ribs of long-extinct upland beasts, and heavy, wet sod. Thousands of tiny, domed cells and low-slung galleries are pressed together like barnacles on a tidal rock, stacked twelve tiers deep against the cliff faces. Millennia of weeping brine and scurrying feet have eroded the masonry until the city has lost all sharp angles; doorways are rounded like swallowing mouths, stairs have smoothed into undulating ramps of dark stone, and the entire settlement looks less built than melted into existence, settled in an equilibrium of perpetual wash and wear.

Across these saturated terraces, the movement is frantic, almost terrifying in its speed, yet carried out in near-total silence. 

Because the air is thin and the stone is perpetually coated in thick, viscous film, sound has no resonance here. The city runs on a frantic, anthill kineticism: hundreds of small, furred and hide-wrapped figures scurry across the slick ramps, lashing woven-sinew nets, passing heavy baskets of crushed salt, and resetting fish-bone weirs. They build and rebuild with desperate manual speed, their wooden mallets and antler picks striking without ringing, absorbed by the drenched stone and the muffled, dense atmosphere. Scaffolding made of lashed driftwood and whale-spar goes up in an hour, is submerged by a sudden surge of brine, rots or loosens, and is torn down and reassembled elsewhere before the sun can shift.

Mingling directly with the builders are the basin's true multitude: an overwhelming, hyperactive biomass of strange alpine fauna. White, ribbon-like amphibians, long as a man’s arm and blind with pearlescent skin, churn through the shallow street-gutters by the tens of thousands, their slick bodies braiding and unbraiding in the rushing brine. Flocks of downy, four-winged diver-birds with heavy needle-beaks plunge from the upper crags into the narrow architectural channels, tumbling through the foam to snatch the ribbons, their wet feathers scattering blinding sprays of droplets that catch the high-altitude sun like clouds of broken glass. Along the masonry walls, colonies of hard-shelled, spider-legged grazers scuttle sideways with frantic, twitching urgency, scraping the sparse, oily black lichen from the stone before the next wave of brine drowns them.

There is virtually no green here. The only vegetation consists of those iron-black crusts and a tough, translucent liverwort that grows only in the joints of the bone-scaffolds, feeding on grease and salt. 

The brightness of the place is exhausting. The sun, unimpeded by cloud or smog, turns the entire basin into a blinding white-and-silver furnace that gives off no warmth at all. To look down is to look into a mirror of rushing, foaming water; to look up is to be blinded by the frost-slick diorite cliffs towering into an ink-blue sky. 

And through it all, nothing speaks. There are no shouts, no bells, no mechanical roars. The only sounds are the soft, wet *thwack* of bare skin on brine-slick stone, the hiss of liquid dragging over worn rock, the rapid clack of thousands of chitinous legs scrambling over bone, and the sharp, shallow gasps of lungs working frantically in the freezing, light-drenched thinness of the air. It is a city suspended in a permanent, hyperactive trance—eroding and renewing itself every hour, pinned between the ice and the sun.
