The expanse is entirely without light, yet it is neither cavern nor void. It is an ocean-wide basin of pure, pressurized mass, perfectly warm, perpetually soaked, and held in an absolute, invariant equilibrium.

The floor does not consist of stone or sediment. The substrate is pure vegetal material—a dense, continental weave of rigid, lignified beams and iron-hard rhizomes that interlock across thousands of leagues. Because it is young, these growths bear none of the soft, rounded decay of an aged swamp; their profiles are crisp, cleanly cleaved, and knife-sharp. Stems thick as aqueducts rise with machine-straight verticality, their edges octagonal and precise, unweathered by wind or time. 

There is no sky to speak of, only an ascending lattice of colossal, ribbed canopy-plates that stretch outward until their sheer mass creates its own ceiling miles above. Between these vertical members, the air is thick with warm, condensing vapor. Moisture hangs at the threshold of rain without ever breaking into a downpour; instead, warm distilled water weeps uninterrupted down the polished flutes of the vegetation, forming silent, unbroken sheets of runoff that slide over slick bark and drain into low-lying arterial troughs.

Although no hand ever built a workshop or forged a bolt here, the entire landscape operates with the terrifying, heavy-gauge logic of a continental manufacturing floor. The plants do not merely grow; they function. Deep within the structural root-beams, vascular bundles under immense hydrostatic pressure act as hydraulic rams, silent pistons of solid fiber that maintain the tension of the vast floor. Saps rich in galvanic salts and heavy metallic esters circulate through miles of sealed, braided tubing, generating a low, ambient current that warms the dark to a steady, unchanging heat.

Every element is locked in place. There are no seasons, no diurnal rhythm, no fluctuations in pressure or chemistry. The distillation of water from the upper ribs to the lower troughs is mathematically continuous, balanced precisely against the capillary return of fluid upward through the central trunk-shafts. The system does not expand, nor does it die back. It exists in a perpetual, closed-cycle hum of maximum efficiency.

Sound is almost entirely absent. The water moves in laminar sheets, clinging to fibrous guide-strings rather than splashing. The massive internal shifts of sap and the slow stroking of plant-fiber pistons are lubricated by dense, slippery lipids, dampening all vibration into an infrasonic thrum so low it registers only as a weight in the chest. There are no wings, no burrows, no spores drifting on drafts, no scavengers. 

In the pitch darkness, if one were to move across the low-slung terraces, the terrain would present itself through touch as a vast, warm labyrinth of razor-edged ribs, cold-sweating pipes of living timber, and slick, perfectly parallel troughs of moving water. Everything is wet, hard, and perfectly still, suspended in an unlit, industrial silence that has never been broken, operating a world that produces only itself.
