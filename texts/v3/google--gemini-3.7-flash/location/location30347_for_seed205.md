The floor is not stone or dirt, but a petrified, fibrous weave sixty miles thick—a continental shelf of fused, fossilized heartwood elevated into the hot, thin air of a mountainless plateau. 

Here, at an altitude where the sky turns the deep, dry cobalt of a kiln-struck glaze, the heat does not rise from the soil; it radiates from the plant matter itself. The great wood breathes. It is a world of vascular architecture, where titanic, hollow trunks the diameter of city-states arch over open, empty plains of pale lichen-crust. Because the scale is so vast, these arches do not feel like a forest ceiling; they feel like geological strata, massive ribbed bridges spanning distances so great they carry their own micro-climates, casting shadows that take three hours to walk across.

Despite the total dominance of plant life, the land is not choked; it is cavernous and sparse. The vegetation grows on a structural, monumental scale, leaving immense miles of sunbaked, empty marrow-floor between each soaring buttress. It is like standing in the bleached ribcage of an ancient wooden leviathan.

Water does not gather in rivers. At this elevation, the air is too hot, the evaporation too swift. Instead, moisture exists as a continuous internal pulse: deep within the structural walls of the wood, sap runs under tremendous hydraulic pressure. At dawn and dusk, when the pressure shifts, the bark fissures weep. Thick, resinous condensation oozes down the grain in vertical channels, pooling in smooth, knee-like hollows and petrified knotholes before evaporating back into a faint, sweet haze. These warm, shallow basins—some no wider than a palm, others the size of courtyards—are the only drinkable water, lukewarm and tasting of tannin and copper.

The silence one might expect from such vast, empty distances is entirely consumed by the fauna. The noise is constant, physical, and near-deafening. 

Trillions of dry-lunged, chitinous organisms live within the hollow architecture. Millions of pale, jointed *drum-beetles*, the size of shields, cling to the sun-facing burls and beat their abdomens against the hollow wood in synchronized territorial waves. The sound does not dissipate in the open air; the wooden floor beneath one's feet functions as a sounding board, vibrating with a deep, percussive thrum that rattles the teeth in the jaw. Above them, drifting through the high arches where the heat is thickest, are swarms of translucent, membrane-winged siphon-flies. Their flight produces a high, shearing whine like steam escaping under high pressure, rising and falling with the wind.

The ecology is entirely biological, without mineral soil. The "sand" that gathers in the hollows is fine, dry sawdust ground down by the ceaseless mandibles of wood-boring ungulates—tall, blind creatures on four stilts of dense bone, whose heads are broad, rasping chisels. They move sparsely across the immense floor, miles apart from one another, grazing the bark down to the pale living cambium beneath. When a stilt-grazer dies, it does not rot; it is stripped by the swarms within an hour and its bones are rapidly overgrown by parasitic climbing cords of scarlet moss that dry into stiff, fibrous cords within days.

Human presence here is minimal, fragile, and clinging.

Occasional camps exist inside the sheltered hollows of cracked structural burls, marked by faint smoke from dried-sap fires. The people of this high wood possess no metal; they have no stone to mine. Their tools are formed from the serrated jawbones of the burrowing beetles, honed against petrified bark, and bound to handles of boiled vine using pitch. They build their shelters not by cutting wood—the ancient heartwood is as hard as basalt—but by weaving the thin, whip-like green tendrils that descend from the high arches into basket-like pods suspended just above the vibrating ground to escape the bone-shaking frequency of the drum-beetles.

To travel here is to navigate a world that feels both skeletal and violently alive. The light is bright and stark, casting hard-edged shadows through the colossal latticework overhead, while the air smells persistently of sun-cured hay, hot pine oil, and the dry, powdery musk of millions of singing carapaces. You walk for days across a flat expanse of amber-colored grain, under arches that look like petrified rainbows, enveloped in a roar that never pauses, waiting for the dusk-sweat of the wood to fill the drinking bowls.
