At this altitude, the sun has the sharp, unattenuated glare of a dry mountain plateau, but the ground itself refuses to dry. 

The entire landscape—spanning across the breadth of a wide upland valley and climbing into the flanking passes—is built entirely of hand-fired earthenware, mortared slate, and unglazed ceramic tile. There is no raw dirt, no exposed mountain bedrock, and no vegetative green. Every square foot of terrain is an overlapping assembly of shallow basins, low fluted retaining ridges, interlocking conduits, and dense fields of vertical clay shingles wedged together with lime. It is packed edge-to-edge without a single gap of untouched earth, yet the construction shows the irregular, repetitive pressure of primitive human hands: finger-grooves in the clay lip of every water-pan, chisel marks on the edges of cleaved dark slate, and uneven firings that shift the ground's color from chalky ochre to kiln-black and raw terracotta red.

Because the construction is recent and the elevation is high, the edges remain razor-sharp. Frost has not yet spalled the clay, and no lichens have managed to seed on the vitrified skins of the tiles.

Water is everywhere, born of a heavy, perpetual condensation that bleeds out of the cold upper atmosphere. The air is cool, thin, and bracingly bright. Under the fierce midday light, millions of wet ceramic surfaces catch the sun, turning the valley into a vast, fractured mirror that stings the eyes. Moisture does not fall in violent storms; it gathers directly onto the cool baked surfaces in fat, clear beads, running down the deliberate incline of the architecture.

The water moves slowly. The slope of the entire valley floor has been calibrated by its builders to deny speed. It creeps across thousands of nested terraced trays, spilling over flat thresholds in silent, millimeter-thick sheets. It fills narrow, hand-chiseled sluices, lingering in shallow square pans where fine mineral silts settle out in thin, pale rings. 

The density of this infrastructure is total. There are no roads or open courtyards that are not also catchment surfaces. Steps are grooved to act as drains; the broad walkways between terrace walls are simply wider water-steps where the film of water is kept shallow enough to wade. Low walls, built of millions of overlapping terra-cotta scabs mortared with ground river-chalk, divide the region into a relentless, cellular lattice. 

Sound here is hushed, dampened by the sheer breadth of the expanse and the lack of turbulent motion. There is no animal cry, no insect hum, no rustle of dry leaves. What remains is a soft, continuous, textural acoustic: the faint *tink* of cold water dripping into a full pan; the microscopic sigh of porous, unglazed pottery drinking moisture up through capillary action; the slow, sporadic crack of a lime seam curing under the high sun.

At the edges of the valley, where the stepped ceramics meet the distant ridgelines, the work simply ceases in clean, unfinished terraces of dry-laid red bricks waiting for mortar. But within the inhabited system, the water continues its patient, engineered transit, sliding from one bright earthenware plane to the next, clear and sterile, washing over an empty, brilliant continent of fired clay.
