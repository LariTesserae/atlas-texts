It is a vertical pocket, four meters wide and sixty meters tall, trapped between the outer insulated hull and the primary draft-core of an elevated thermal exchange tower. 

Everything here is newly assembled. The structural steel retains its crisp, unrounded edges; the zinc galvanizing on the diamond-tread catwalks is bright and spangled, unbroken by rust, though slick with a continuous skin of cold moisture. The mill scale on the massive H-beams has not yet begun to flake, and the bead-welds are clean, blue-tempered ripples that meet at precise, unweathered right angles. There is no stone here, no soil, no bedrock. The entire environment is suspended two thousand meters above the valley floor on concrete pylons, but within this narrow interstitial void, the outside world does not exist.

The air is an icy, hyper-saturated fog. Water does not merely fall; it is manufactured by the surface differentials. The towering core wall to the left is an unbroken face of corrugated alloy, chilled by forced air within, while the outer skin to the right is cool from the mountain altitude. Between them, the humidity condenses instantly, running down the vertical flutes in smooth, glassy sheets that break into heavy, tearing drops at every structural seam. The atmosphere stays poised at the exact threshold between dense mist and driving rain. 

The sound is absolute and concussive. It fills the chest cavity before it reaches the ears: the catastrophic, unrelenting roar of three-megawatt induction turbines spinning several decks above, combined with the percussive drumming of tons of condensing water crashing onto lower drainage baffles. It is a sound with weight—an acoustic pressure so dense that individual clanks of pneumatic relays and the hiss of high-pressure bleed lines are swallowed whole, detectable only as sharp vibrations traveling upward through the soles of heavy rubber boots. The catwalk grating vibrates at a frequency that turns standing water into a pattern of rapidly shifting standing waves.

Light is sparse and amber. Sealed, explosion-proof bulkhead fixtures are bolted to the structural columns every fifteen meters, their thick prismatic glass filmed over with condensation. The light barely travels; it creates localized cones of orange-gold that illuminate the dense spray before dying in the cavernous gray-black shadows of the vertical drop. Looking up, the shaft disappears into a churn of pale vapor and the dark geometry of overhead cross-bracing. Looking down through the open steel grating, the drop vanishes into a roaring, black well where drainage funnels gulp thousands of gallons per minute into the return pumps.

Between the mechanical presences—the vertical run of insulated copper conduits, the high-pressure glycol lines, the bolted maintenance platforms—there is mostly raw, empty volume. It is a sparse, utilitarian chasm. 

Life has had only weeks to find this place, and it has found almost nothing to hold. There are no birds, no spiders, no flying insects drawn to the lamps; the acoustic violence and relentless downdrafts make flight impossible. The only biology is a microscopic film: a slick, translucent smear of cold-tolerant iron-oxidizing bacteria and pioneering green algae taking hold along the drip-edges of the lowest steel lip, where the amber light hits the continuous trickle of condensate. 

The environment is in perpetual, violent motion. Every few minutes, a pneumatic purge cycle triggers somewhere in the stack: a sharp drop in ambient pressure snaps through the void, the mist twists into violent vortexes, and the sheet-flow on the walls shudders and tears away in mid-air, atomizing into a freezing cloud before settling back into its steady, downward race.
