It is an interior world, dropped miles beneath the stone mantle of the planet, yet it is neither dark nor claustrophobic. It is hollow on a continental scale—a sub-lithic vault so wide that its horizon curves away into pale, freezing distance, empty of everything except light, cold air, and an immense, violent architecture of living wood.

The light here is surgical and total. It does not come from a sun or a single ceiling, but from the plants themselves and the sheer, mineral skin of the cavern miles overhead. The upper rock is encrusted with thick, cold-burning phosphors that cast a stark, shadowless noon across the void. Down in the open air, this glare catches the glassy rime of ice that coats every surface, refracting into a pale, blinding wash of silver and jade.

Despite the fact that living vegetable matter forms almost the entirety of the terrain, the space feels astonishingly empty. There is no soil, no rolling earth, no stone underfoot. The world is built out of massive, solitary structural struts—pale, iron-hard trunks and hollow cellulose pylons, some the diameter of mountain ridges, that bridge the gulf between unseen floor and distant ceiling. They span leagues of empty air at sharp, violent angles, crisscrossing like the framing of a demolished world. Between these great structural beams lies sheer, freezing space: voids five miles deep and fifty miles across, filled only with draft winds and falling crystals of frozen sap.

The climate is bitingly cold, hovering just above the point where moisture locks entirely into stone. It is not dry, but damp in sudden, localized pulses. Heavy condensation gathers along the undersides of the great timber arches, forming thick, gelatinous beads of sweet, freezing dew that eventually lose their purchase and drop through the gulfs like silent glass shot. Low, fast-moving sheets of ground-fog occasionally tear through the lower spans, wetting the bark until it turns black and slick, before evaporating into the dry, crisp chill of the higher voids.

What makes the place terrible is its speed. This is not a slow, patient forest. The mega-flora exists in a state of hyper-pressurized, explosive youth. Driven by volcanic heat beneath the floor and the fierce light above, the colossal white stalks shoot upward with audible violence. A spartan, fibrous spar thick enough to carry a fortress can lance through three miles of empty air in the span of a single week, its bark cracking like cannon shot as it hardens in the frost. 

Because the growth is so rapid, the engineering is unstable. Enormous cantilevers of cellular wood overreach their own strength, buckling under their own icy weight. Long, terrifying moments of quiet—where nothing is heard but the whistling of downdrafts and the microscopic tick of frost—are suddenly shattered by the thunderclap of a structural collapse. A pylon snaps leagues away, and the sound rolls through the vast, clear emptiness like an artillery barrage, followed by the slow, sweeping arc of a billion tons of white timber plunging into the deep mist below. The landscape is perpetually destroying and reconstructing its own scaffolding.

Animal life is sparse, dwarfed and scattered by the scale. What lives here moves on long, spindly claws or wide, membranous gliding sails, clinging to the lee sides of the great columns where the wind cannot pry them loose. There are solitary, pale things that graze on the frost-lichens, moving with slow, methodical caution, their hearts beating slow against the cold. They are silent; sound here travels too far, and the air belongs to the creaking wood.

Human presence is faint, precarious, and fiercely primitive. There are no towns, only tethered camps lashed to the knuckles where three or four giant struts intersect. Survival is an art of hand-tools and salvage. The people here work with bone gouges, stone adzes, and long ropes braided from fibrous green taproots. They drive fire-hardened hardwood pegs into the fresh bark to secure their hanging platforms, wrapping themselves in the cured, leathery skins of deep-vent worms. 

They do not build walls; the wind would tear them away. Instead, they lash down low, woven baskets of wicker and tendon that swing beneath the beams, out of the glare and away from the shattering falls of overstressed wood above. They harvest the condensation beads in bladders of skin, chip the sugary amber that bleeds from fresh frost-cracks, and watch the slow, flexing geometry of the great arches, learning to read the pitch of the wood’s internal groaning so they know which half of the continent is about to snap, and which is about to grow.
