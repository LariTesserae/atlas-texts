High in the cold shoulders of a mountain range, where the air should be thin and freezing, there is no air at all—only the deep, pristine bowl of an alpine sink, drowned to its brim in clear, jade-toned meltwater. 

The entire basin is barely four hundred paces across, a contained amphitheater framed by submerged, moss-slicked terraces of shale. Yet within this bounded bowl exists a vertical city, woven by hand from the flesh of giant, fibrous water-reeds (*calamus-palustris*) and the tough, buoyant heartwood of sunken mountain pines.

The water is cool—the precise temperature of spring thaw that never quite warms, held in a quiet thermal equilibrium by subterranean glacial seepages. Because of the altitude and the clarity of the snowmelt, sunlight penetrates fully from above, casting long, straight shafts of amber-green light through the water column. The beams slice down into the central void, illuminating a town built not on the floor, but suspended in the middle of the fluid volume.

### The Architecture of the Drift

The construction is distinctly urban in its ambition and layering, yet it relies entirely on the craft of knotting, wedge-work, and counterweight. 

Great cylindrical communal towers—three and four stories high, fashioned from concentric hoops of green alder wrapped in dense, waterproof wicker—hang suspended in mid-water. They neither sink nor float to the surface; their buoyancy is meticulously tuned. Immense blocks of river-granite, bound in harness-ropes of braided nettle-fiber, drag down from their bases, while air-filled bladder-gourds and dried root-floats pull toward the light. 

Between these floating towers, the water is remarkably open. There is no crowding of clutter; the city breathes through wide, empty avenues of blue-green water. A swimmer crossing from the Council Rotunda to the terraced domestic clusters travels through fifty yards of clear, undisturbed void, guided only by thick, moss-bearded transit cables strung between the anchors. 

Every surface is living plant. Where the timber ends, the vegetation takes over: thick mats of submerged water-moss create soft, carpeted interior floors; curtains of feathery bladderwort act as dividing walls inside the woven spheres, catching drift-food while muffling movement; and massive kelp-like liverworts, some as broad as canoes, are trained along the guy-ropes to serve as sound-baffles and shade. 

### The Soundscape of the Basin

For all the slowness of the water's drift, the place is deafeningly loud.

Water carries vibration with ruthless fidelity, and this mountain bowl acts as a resonant bell. The low, structural creak of thousands of lashed timbers constantly flexing against the deep thermal currents produces a continuous, groaning chord like a wooden ship in a gale. Above this drone sits the rhythmic *clonk-clack* of counterweights—smooth granite boulders gently bumping one another in their rope cradles forty feet below the lowest hab-sphere.

The woven reed walls themselves are hollow and perforated; as water breathes through them, they whistle and sigh in low, fluting tones. Added to this is the perpetual chatter of life: the high-frequency snapping of tiny freshwater caridean shrimp scouring the thatch, the resonant drumming of bull-lungfish signaling from their hollows in the shale floor, and the sharp, percussive thumps of human divers striking bone mallets against signaling gourds to mark the change of watch.

### Life in the Suspended Tier

The ecology here is intensely integrated with the built settlement. 

Schools of pale, blunt-headed char dart through the wide transit lanes, grazing on the algae that coats the guy-lines. Enormous cold-water crayfish—some the size of a man’s forearm, their shells stained indigo from the shale minerals—inhabit the foundations, domesticized to clear detritus and trim away rotten fiber from the structural knots.

In the upper layers, closer to the shimmering ceiling of the surface, flat garden-rafts made of layered peat and floating sedge produce water-cress, edible tubers, and the heavy, sweet-scented white lilies whose fibers are spun into dive-harnesses. The people move without hurry, kicking with broad paddle-fins made of stitched beaver-tail hide, their movements slow and calibrated to preserve breath and avoid disturbing the silt-traps below.

Tools are simple, heavy, and purposeful: scrapers made from split river stones, awls ground from elk horn, and curved blades of fire-hardened slate used to shave the reed ends flush. There is no fire here; meals are cured in salt-vats anchored near the frigid bottom springs, or dried on the few narrow gravel ledges that break the surface along the caldera’s sheer rock rim.

Over decades, the settlement does not decay so much as it petrifies. Silt and lime precipitate out of the mountain water, gradually encrusting the wicker walls in a hard, pale rind of tufa rock. Old towers lose their buoyancy as the wood calcifies, descending inch by patient inch over generations toward the floor, where they become silent, stone-laced foundations for the next layer of woven homes tethered above them.
