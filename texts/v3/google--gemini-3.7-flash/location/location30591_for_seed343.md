The drop does not end; it merely dissolves into a blue, sun-baked haze several miles below. 

Here, at an altitude where the air should be thin and freezing, an unaccountable warmth rises from the abyss—great, lazy thermal plumes that carry the smell of sweet rot, sun-cured sap, and the chalky tang of dry guano. What rock exists in this landscape has reached the very end of its geological life. There are no jagged crags, no sharp fractures or fresh scree. The earth has been whittled by uncounted millions of years of wind into smooth, isolated needles—monolithic cylinders and rounded stone bones that stand miles apart from one another, rising straight out of the lower mists like the pylons of a sunken continent.

Yet very little bare stone can be seen. The rock has been claimed entirely by a single, ancient lineage of vascular titan-flora: the *strangle-cedars* and aerial marrow-trees. Their roots do not simply cling to the stone; they have enclosed it, replaced it, and then stepped out into the void. Miles-long cables of fibrous, iron-hard wood—some as thick as galleon hulls, others braided into loose, whipping suspension bridges—span the terrifying chasms between the stone needles. These root-bridges do not sag; they are tensed, living architecture, swollen with pressurized sap and covered in an unbroken pelt of silver-green club moss and condensation-drinking succulents. 

Between these massive, living spans lies vast, empty volume. To step off a root is to fall through two leagues of uninterrupted golden light. The world is mostly hollow sky, punctuated by these colossal, horizontal veins of green wood.

And filling that hollow sky is an acoustic pressure that never relents. The sound is deafening, a physical weight against the chest. It is not the roar of an engine or the crash of an ocean, but the combined, amplified vibration of billions of living things. Millions of kite-wasps, their iridescent chitin catching the high sun, carve through the thermals in sweeping sheets that drone like distant pipe organs. Flocks of leather-winged, sickle-beaked cliff-swifts—creatures whose lineages have never touched the unseen ground—nest by the hundreds of thousands within the fibrous bellies of the suspended root-trees. When a thermal shifts, entire leagues of the sky boil over in a sudden, screaming vortex of hunting, mating, and dying. Down below, drift-polyps the size of haystacks ride the warm updrafts, clicking their hollow shell-plates together in rhythmic, percussive rattles to orient themselves against the vertical winds.

Moisture is intermittent and comes not from rainstorms, which break far below the root-lines, but from the daily rising of the valley breath. Every morning, warm fog crawls up the stone needles. The moss-pelt drinks it instantly, swelling from gray to deep viridian, weeping heavy droplets into the leaf-traps and hollow burls that pepper the titan-vines. By midday, under the steady, warm light, the air is dry and clear again, though the heart of every root remains soaked and cool.

Humanity, or what has taken its place, exists here as an afterthought stitched into the bark. 

Their settlements are not towns, but scattered, solitary blisters made of woven bark, reed-pith, and dried tendon, lashed directly to the undersides of the great bridges to stay sheltered from the predatory swarms above. A single dwelling might cling to a vine fifty feet thick, suspended thousands of feet above the blue floor, tethered with braided vine-ropes and counterweighted with smooth river stones dragged up centuries ago. 

Their craft is entirely manual, quiet, and sharp. They use no metal; iron would only rust in the morning mists or weigh down the bridges. Instead, they work with the black, petrified thorn-wood of the marrow-trees, chipping it into razors, and carve the light, hollow bones of the thermal-gliders into fishhooks, needles, and pulleys. Long, swaying walkways of simple basket-weave connect the living chambers to communal drying racks, where sheets of insect-meat and drift-polyp flesh are cured in the endless wind. 

A hunter sits on the edge of a vine-span, feet dangling into three miles of emptiness. Around him, the air thrums with such intensity that the moss beneath his palms shivers in time with the sound. He holds a long, fire-hardened lance tipped with the serrated horn of an aerial beetle, watching a drifting cloud of green-winged flippers rise from the heat shimmer below. He does not look for the earth. No one here believes the earth has a bottom; they know only the stone bones, the endless wood that binds them, and the warm, shrieking wind that keeps them all aloft.
