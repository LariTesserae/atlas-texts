The plateau sits at an altitude where the sky loses its blue and settles into a thin, bruise-colored slate. It stretches so far in every direction that the curve of the planet is not an abstract concept but a visible slouch at the edge of vision, where low banks of gray stratus graze the ground like dragged wool.

It is bitterly cold, but not entirely frozen. Moisture does not fall in sheets; it condenses. It gathers on the underside of stones, beads along the edges of ancient, polished shale, and hangs in an ambient, freezing mist that clings to whatever stands upright. The air has the taste of wet iron and crushed quartz.

There are no roads here, no shelters, no towers, and no signs of human occupation. Yet the landscape is intensely, relentlessly industrial. 

Rising out of the wide expanses of gravel are the shapes: mammoth, solitary machine-masses, hundreds of feet long and utterly devoid of crew or cabin. They are not ruins; they are operational, running on closed, self-sustaining cycles that have outlasted whatever purpose engineered them. Massive drive-chains, links thick as draft horses, turn with the slowness of continental drift. Blind, cast-iron boilers, their rivets smoothed by centuries of wind-scour, radiate a dull, greasy warmth into the frost. Miles apart from one another, automated extraction heads and steam-cleaving sifting barges drag themselves across the flats on broad caterpillar tracks, moving perhaps three yards in a day. 

Because of the thinness of the air and the damp, porous nature of the ground, the sound does not travel. It is swallowed entirely. A drive-piston the size of a grain silo descends into a grease-well with no more noise than the soft, damp *shuff* of a boot in wet sand. The vastness absorbs everything. If a plate of five-inch steel groans under torque, the sound dies within twenty paces, leaving only the profound, ringing silence of the high plains.

Around these slow iron leviathans, life is dense to the point of suffocating.

Millions of creatures occupy the gravel plains, moving in massive, silent sheets across the flats. They are low-slung, heavy-bodied things with pelts like wet felt and wide, splayed pads that leave faint, quickly-melting depressions in the rime. They have no vocal cords, or no use for them; they communicate through the tremor of the earth beneath their pads and the slow turning of their blunt, eyeless heads. They drift in the wake of the crawling engines, drawn to the radiant heat of the engine housings and the water that boils off the cooling vents.

Where an iron track presses into the earth, it leaves a trench where the frost melts into lukewarm, oily brine. Within minutes of an engine passing, thousands of small, pale crustacean-like organisms emerge from the deep shale to feed on the mineral sludge, only to be scooped up by the sweeping snouts of the herds. Flocks of fat, heavy-winged birds—feathered in grease-slick charcoal down—roost by the hundreds on the turning drive-shafts and warm manifold pipes, riding the machinery in absolute stillness, rising only in silent, synchronized clouds when an iron valve vents a plume of pale, sulfurous vapor.

Vegetation has no height here. The wind, which moves constantly without howling, shears anything that rises more than an inch above the grit. Instead, there is a dense, rubbery lichen that binds the gravel together in vast, spongy mats. It is the color of oxidized copper and lead, drawing its moisture from the grease-mist and the perpetual damp that seeps from the engine tracks. It coats the machines themselves, creeping up the rusted hulls and wrapping around non-moving hydraulic arms until the metal and the tundra share the same mottled texture of age and erosion.

Everything here has settled into an equilibrium so old that the distinction between the natural and the mechanized has worn away. The rocks are rounded by eons of wind; the machines are rounded by the same wind. The animals treat the giant iron mechanisms not as foreign intrusions, but as geological fixtures—migrating thermal vents that carry them across the high, flat world through the dim, eternal twilight. 

Time passes in long, slow exhalations. A boiler cools slightly; the herd presses three inches closer to its flank; the mist thickens, beads into cold drops upon millions of silent, breathing backs, and rolls back into the stone.
