At this altitude, the air is too thin to hold a secret, and the light is aggressive—a naked, white-gold blaze falling straight down through an atmosphere with almost no dust to soften it. The glare strikes a narrow, scooped amphitheater of rock perched against the sheer eastern shoulder of a nameless spire, nine thousand meters above the unseen plains.

It is a small space, no larger than the footprint of an ancient village square, but packed so tightly with vertical stone that moving through it requires turning the shoulders sideways. 

Here, the rock is not a uniform mass, but a dense labyrinth of dark, iron-rich slate needles, clustered together like organ pipes or a petrified reed bed. They rise eight to twelve feet high, crowded against one another with only inches to spare. Ages of steady, scouring wind and freeze-thaw cycles have smoothed their flanks, giving them the matte, polished texture of oiled horn. There is no soil here, and nothing that could be called vegetation—not a single moss, not a spore of lichen. The slate exists entirely in its own mineral terms, black and deep plum-grey, cut by razor-thin seams of translucent white quartz that catch the sun like mirrors.

Despite the dry altitude, the cirque is perpetually damp. Moist air rises violently from lower thermal layers, condensing instantly as it hits the frozen stone teeth of the shelf. Droplets form on the tips of the spires, swelling under the furious sun, then sliding down the cold mineral flanks. As they travel into the shaded crevices between the tightly packed pillars, the temperature plunges, and the water hardens into thin, glass-like beads of rime. 

The sound of the place is small, precise, and constant: the *clink* of ice cracking as thermal expansion wrenches it from the quartz, the faint hiss of meltwater flash-sublimating where sunlight strikes a wet face, and the dry, flute-like sigh of the wind being sheared into a hundred thin ribbons by the stone forest.

Tucked into this mineral crowd are signs of sparse, deliberate human handiwork, ancient and rudimentary. 

Near the center of the shelf, where the pillars part slightly around a natural depression, several of the slate needles have been modified. Grooves have been hand-pecked into the stone with harder river cobbles brought up from unimaginable depths below. Around these notches, stiff ropes made of twisted sinew or hide—greased against the dry air and blackened with age—lash the stones together to form rigid, triangular braces. Low walls of dry-stacked slate chips span the gaps between several pillars, forming knee-high windbreaks that trap falling condensation into shallow stone basins.

These basins, carved into the solid rock floor with stone chisels, are rimmed with pale mineral crusts where water has pooled and evaporated thousands of times. Small, primitive tools lie cached in the dark recesses between the pillars: gouges of chipped flint, a smooth rubbing-stone stained with powdered red hematite, and bundles of split bone pins bound with dry gut. There are no fires here—there is nothing to burn—and no signs of long-term shelter, only the patient, repetitive labor of someone collecting pure condensed water and marking the slow turning of the sun.

The dynamics of the shelf are cyclical and swift. When a shadow falls across a sector of the pillars, the dampness locks into frost within seconds; when the direct sun returns, the rock steams, releasing faint, drifting veils of vapor that sparkle in the blinding glare before dissolving into the blue void above the mountain’s lip. Everything is hard, clear, and stripped down to cold rock, piercing light, and the quiet, ceaseless labor of condensation.
