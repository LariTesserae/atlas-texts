The darkness here is thick, hot, and relentlessly wet. It is not the cold emptiness of a cave, but a sweltering, blind expanse held at the bottom of some vast, forgotten depression. The heat does not radiate from a visible sun or vent; it saturates the air like a fever, hanging in dense, suspended vapors that constantly liquefy against whatever they touch. Everything weeps. 

Across this low, drowned basin lies a city of impossible, archaic scale. There are no machines, no wiring, no glass or forged metals. The architecture is composed entirely of porous, black-grey tufa, hand-hewn river basalt, and vast courses of lime-mortared cobble that have stood so long their sharpest cornices have melted into smooth, organic mounds. Broad, paved causeways—twenty strides across—cut through the dark, disappearing into the distance before dipping beneath shallow, boiling sheets of standing water.

The density of the place is strange. The buildings are monumental, structured with the deliberate urban logic of plazas, stepped terraces, colonnades, and deep drainage channels, yet they are separated by immense, empty gulfs of warm silt and pooling brine. You can walk along an elevated promenade flanked by towering, windowless tenements, only for the street to terminate abruptly over an expanse of vacant, steaming mud that stretches for hundreds of yards before the next cluster of megaliths rises into the pitch.

Because there is almost no light, the city is mapped through temperature, weight, and the quiet behavior of its moisture. If your eyes adjust to the absolute minimum—a dull, non-luminous gray where the dense mist catches the faint, ambient heat-glow of the earth—the structural logic of the place begins to betray itself.

Gravity does not always agree with the masonry. In the grand plazas, where thousands of flagstones lie settled and warped by ancient subsiding earth, the water does not flow downward. Scalding sheets of condensation gather on the stone, slide horizontally across perfectly flat plazas, and then ascend the fluted sides of stone obelisks in smooth, silent sheets, peeling off their summits as heavy, upward-falling rain. 

Elsewhere, the city exists in a state of quiet, recursive erosion. Deep below the street level, vaulted siphons and sewer tunnels—carved from solid shale with primitive chisel-strokes still visible beneath centuries of mineral skin—carry boiling torrents that make no sound at all. You can step into a subterranean archway and feel the scalding humidity billow against your skin, seeing the sheen of wet stone curving above you, yet the water rushing past your ankles has the acoustic presence of smoke. 

There are no animals. Not a single insect skitters across the hot, slick pavers; no blind fish inhabit the pools that collect in the sunken courtyards. The only life is a thin, leathery skin of liver-colored lichen and iron-tolerant biofilm that coats the foundations where the stone meets the mud. It grows in wide, sparse rings, slowly digesting the lime mortar, releasing a faint, metallic musk that mixes with the heavy smell of boiled river silt.

The environment is in perpetual, silent motion. A thermal cycle operates in the dark: every few hours, the ambient pressure shifts, and the air becomes so saturated that the darkness itself seems to condense, dropping several degrees before surging back into oppressive, suffocating heat. When this happens, the architecture bleeds. Millions of gallons of hot water bead instantly upon every surface, running in uniform, rhythmic rivulets down miles of carved facades, pooling in the street gutters, and draining into basins that empty into the sky.

Distances here are unreliable. A flight of megalithic steps might look thirty paces long, but descending it takes minutes as the steps subtly expand beneath your weight, their basalt surfaces permanently warm and slick as wet tallow. A colonnade of square-cut pillars will seem to recede into an open square, but walking between two of them places you immediately on the upper terrace of a structure half a mile away, the air growing suddenly wetter, the smell of damp lime sharper, while behind you, the road you just walked has folded into a sheer, dripping wall of rock.

The city does not feel ruined so much as finished and left running, a giant, non-mechanical hydraulic organ operating in total blackness, endlessly washing its own ancient stones with boiling water that cannot decide which way to fall.
