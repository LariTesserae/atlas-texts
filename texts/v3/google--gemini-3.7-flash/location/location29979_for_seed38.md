There is no stone beneath the feet here, or if there is, it lies hundreds of fathoms down, crushed under the compacted weight of ten million years of unbroken shedding. The ground is a vast, spongy continental mantle—a peat-continent raised high into the warm upper air, where mountains have long since been softened and smoothed into gentle, undulating swells by centuries of biological accretion. 

Because of the height, the horizon feels curiously flat and near, dissolving into an all-enveloping, lukewarm milkiness. The atmosphere is a closed loop of breath and condensation. Massive, low-hanging banks of warm mist drift sluggishly across the rises, snagging on the tips of great vegetal domes before breaking apart into perpetual, fine-grained drizzle. It is not cold rain, but a steady, tepid bath that keeps every surface slick, glossy, and weeping. 

Water does not carve jagged canyons here; the landscape is too soft and ancient for sharp edges. Instead, runnels of condensation gather in vast, gentle hollows, forming wide, shallow pools that sit suspended in the crowns of gargantuan, flattened root-shelves. These perched wetlands—phytotelmata the size of small seas—are rimmed with thick, rubbery cushions of pale yellow liverwort and black, fibrous ferns whose fronds do not rustle, but hang heavy with grease and water. 

The light never sharpens. It filters down through the upper mist-strata as a dim, uniform jade glow that slowly shifts to warm amber toward the middle of the long days. There are no distinct shadows, only subtle variations in density: the pale silhouette of a distant root-arch receding into the vapor, or the dark, bruised purples of an under-canopy cavern where the light gives out entirely.

Vegetation here does not merely cover the landscape; it *is* the landscape, operating as architecture, bedrock, and soil all at once. Vast, slow-growing vascular organisms, wider than cities and barely taller than a modest hill, form the rolling topography. Their outer bark is as thick as sea-ice, scarred by deep, weeping fissures from which sap the color of tea wells up and cools into amber ridges. Above these ancient bases rises an interlocking web of fleshy, tubular stalks, bulbous water-storing reservoirs, and dense carpets of bioluminescent lichen that smolder with a faint, dying ember-light when the twilight deepens. 

Dotted across these endless swells are rare, inexplicable intrusions of hard matter: smoothed monoliths and low, circular stone foundations, completely without mortar or sharp corners. They have been worn down by thousands of rainy centuries until they resemble smoothed river pebbles, engulfed on all sides by the creeping wood. Roots the thickness of galleon hulls have split some of these stone rings apart, lifting their segments high into the humid air and cradling them like fossilized teeth. No tools remain around them, no metal, no inscriptions—only the stubborn, mute presence of rock that was once shaped by intent, now half-digested by the lichen and the moss.

The ecosystem moves with immense, unhurried patience. The fauna are soft-bodied, heavy, and largely silent. Great blind gastropods with shells made of lacquered wood-fiber graze upon the slime-films of the lower trunks, taking weeks to cross a single root-span. In the shallow, sky-high water pans, pale siphon-feeders with translucent skin gently pulse, filtering the dead spores that settle continuously from the air. Sometimes, a low vibration runs through the fibrous floor—a resonant, sub-audible shudder caused by the slow collapse of a cavernous peat-pocket leagues away, or the shifting of water deep within the root-labyrinth. 

Sound travels poorly in this place; the endless padding of moss and the thick, humid air absorb every sharp impact. What remains is a quiet so profound it has texture: the rhythmic, heavy *plop* of condensed water falling from giant bulb-leaves into black pools; the soft, tearing sound of a sprout pushing through rotten pulp; the distant, sighing exhale of a spore-vent opening to the damp wind. 

Everything here has settled into its final, most durable form. There is no urgency in the growth, no competition for light where all light is equalized by mist, and no rush to decay where death is instantly woven back into the living floor. The high plateau breathes slowly, vast and saturated, holding its moisture against the sky in a warm, eternal twilight.
