At this altitude, the air is cold and heavy with the weight of unfallen rivers. It does not rain in drops; rather, the atmosphere is a perpetual, chilled mist that condenses upon every available micron of surface, running in continuous, glass-clear sheets over an unbroken, continental expanse of living fiber.

This is not a forest growing upon a world; the vegetation *is* the world’s upper crust, an embryonic megastructure suspended high above an unseen lithosphere. It is a nascent biome in the first furious hours or years of its self-assembly—unweathered, uncarved by time, showing no signs of decay, rot, or particulate erosion. Every surface is pristine, raw, and fiercely new. There are no rounded pebbles, no silted riverbeds, no soil in the traditional sense. Instead, there is only the wet, pressurized green-and-slate mass of hyper-dense vegetative flesh, endlessly extruding itself into the cold mist.

The scale is planetary, yet the density allows no horizon. To look out is to look into an impenetrable lattice of colossal, braided vascular bundles—each the width of a highway, climbing and intertwining into towering, vaulted ribs that hold up their own micro-climates. These are not trees with bark and leaf as known to ancient taxonomy. They are synthetic botanical engines, guided by molecular directives that operate well beyond classical mechanics. Deep inside their pale, translucent skins, microscopic lattices of crystalline carbon and light-harvesting polymers pulse rhythmically, directing hydraulic pressures capable of lifting oceans into the sky.

The sound is immense and obliterating. It is a continuous, deafening roar born not of wind or thunder, but of sheer metabolism. Trillions of metric tons of water are pumped upward per second through rigid, capillary manifolds. The noise is a combination of hydraulic shrieking, the deep bass hum of cavitation within miles-thick root-pylons, and the violent, rhythmic snapping of new cellular walls locking into place at the speed of a galloping horse. It fills the chest, vibrating through the wet fibers underfoot so intensely that the water running across the substrate dances in standing wave patterns.

Light struggles to pierce the canopy above, which sits layered twenty miles deep in some vectors. What little illumination exists is a dim, bruised twilight, filtered through leagues of wet, chlorophyll-dense foliage and pale, membrane-like leaves the size of sails. This weak ambient daylight is supplemented from within the flora itself: rhythmic, slow-traveling waves of cold, bioluminescent chemiluminescence—cerulean, mercury-white, and deep moss-green—that surge through the translucent walls of the conduits, signaling the delivery of mineral slurries to the upper growth-fronts.

Water is everywhere, near-freezing and extraordinarily pure. It weeps from the joints where giant structural fronds fuse into load-bearing arches. It cascades in smooth, unbroken sheets down vertical flutes of freshly grown xylem, pooling in vast, suspended reservoirs that span miles before spilling over the sheer, un-eroded edges into the dimness below. The spray creates an inescapable, swirling vapor that clings to everything, maintaining a slick, friction-free skin across the entire landscape.

There are no animals here. No insects burrow through the wet pith, no birds nest in the structural vaults, no fish swim in the torrential flumes. The ecosystem is completely devoid of fauna, yet it lacks no complexity. The flora possesses its own active, self-regulating agency. Specialized tendrils—thick as cables and tipped with sensory nodules—whip slowly through the mist, sampling the chemical makeup of the ambient air and adjusting the growth vector of neighboring arches in real-time. Valves the size of houses contract and dilate with wet, muscular slaps to divert surging torrents of chilled fluid away from overloaded stems.

The air smells sharply of crushed mint, ozonated water, raw cellulose, and the clean, metallic tang of unspent synthetic catalysts. Because nothing has had time to die, there is no scent of humus or earth. The landscape is entirely alive, entirely functional, and accelerating.

Every minute, the geography subtly alters. A new buttress, glistening with condensation, forces its way upward through a tangle of feeder-vines with a sound like tearing sheet metal. The floor beneath—a woven raft of pressurized, hollow reeds—creaks and shifts under the shifting distribution of millions of gallons of water. It is an architecture without an architect, a wilderness that is entirely technological, an ocean of cold, wet, roaring green constructing itself in the dim upper reaches of the world.
