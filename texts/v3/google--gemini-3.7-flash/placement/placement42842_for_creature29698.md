I would not be up where the walls run blood-warm, nor would I be trapped in the dry, chattering heat of the standing cylinders. Those places belong to things with light bones or no bones at all, things that move like sparks or paper ribbons. 

I would be down in the cold trough—the absolute corner where the pearlescent monolith meets the yield of the cartilage floor, exactly where the water refuses to settle into a natural pool and instead sits in long, trembling prisms against the ninety-degree seam.

At first, I might not seem distinct from the floor itself. 

I would have mass, heavy and low, spread across several meters of that yielding black substrate. My body would not be upright like the tall, blind curators, nor segmented and brittle like the glass-shelled litho-vores that scrape the walls. I imagine something broader, flat-bellied, and dense—sheathed in a hide like wet slate or oiled leather, dark enough to drink the bruised-purple stratus light without throwing it back.

I am something that was not brought here by the calculation, but condensed out of the friction between the math and the rot that hasn't arrived yet.

My limbs—six or perhaps eight short, broad-padded appendages—would rest flat against the trough, half-submerged in the mineral-slick bead-lines. The pads of those limbs would be sensitive to the micro-harmonics of the deep non-baryonic substructures. I would feel the city breathing long before the amber seams light up; I would feel the weight of an approaching curator leagues away, their needle-fine steps sending tiny, hydraulic ripples through the cartilage floor directly into my sternum.

I would spend days perfectly still, letting the mist collect on the broad ridge of my spine until it ran off in twin diagonal curtains, deflected by the local gravity field that tilts everything near the footings. The plum-colored creepers would try to root into me, mistaking my stillness for a fallen pylon. I would let their hair-fine tendrils explore the folds of my hide, tasting their bitter, electric sap as they probed, before shifting—just an inch, a slow hydraulic shrug—to snap the roots and take their damp, fibrous energy into my skin.

I think of myself as an unhurried metabolizer of the city’s margins. 

When the glass-shelled climbers finish their slow rasps up the wall and leave behind their dry, silver ribbons of desalted slag, I would rise slightly. My mouthparts would not have teeth or jaws, but broad, stone-smooth plates designed to plane along the absolute edges of the composite megaliths, lifting that silver residue clean from the seam before the vines can lock it down. The taste would be mineral, alkaline, and sharp with ozone—the taste of raw arithmetic made solid and then sweat out by pressure.

Sometimes, a veil that has lost its buoyancy in the lowlands will drift down through the tilted mist, falling softly like a wet sheet across my back. I wouldn't eat it. I would simply hold it there, feeling the faint, fading amber strobe of its skin against mine, allowing its trapped gases to slowly diffuse into my own respiratory slits until it becomes nothing more than a thin film of transparent gel, washing away into the trough with the next drop of condensation.

And when the tall, white-skinned one finally arrives—after walking for days down the center line of the avenue—I would not hide. 

I would watch it with no eyes of my own, but with the lateral line of pressure-organs running the length of my flank. I would feel it fold its long, multi-jointed limbs down into the wet angle where I rest. When it presses its drafting-pen finger into the trough to test the math of the foundation, it might touch the edge of my hide. 

It would not recoil, and neither would I. We would share that single point of contact for hours in the dripping dusk: the curator measuring the cold, perfect equation from above, and me—heavy, damp, and warm with swallowed sap—measuring the sheer, stubborn weight of the world that has begun to grow around its feet.
