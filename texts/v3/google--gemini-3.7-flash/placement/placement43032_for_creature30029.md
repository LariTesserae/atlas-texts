I would not be up on the airy catwalks where the young Screen-Tuners throw their weight against the wind, nor deep in the subterranean dynamos where the roar of falling water shakes the fillings in your teeth.

I would be found in the **flute-joint of the Eighth Monolith**, suspended four hundred feet above the lower boiler terraces and eight hundred feet below the residential rim. 

My home is an anchor-pocket—a hollow chiseled three centuries ago into the inward curve of the dark gneiss, just behind the massive rocker-bearing of the *St. Jude Span*, a five-hundred-ton steel truss bridge that links the east residential flute to the industrial flue-stacks across the gap.

***

### Who I Am

I am an **Anchor-Listener** (or *Pin-Tender*). My name, in the soft, breathy sibilants of the rock-dwellers, is barely a whisper—something like *Sael-is*.

My body has the shape the stone demands: thick-set, heavy-hipped, with a barrel chest that rises and falls with slow, shallow economy. My skin is the color of milk skimmed into a pewter dish, faint blue tributaries branching across my temples and down the insides of my forearms. My hair is long, coarse, and dense; every fourth day, I rub it with cold paraffin and wind it around the crown of my head in three flat coils, skewered through with a split-bone pin so it cannot drink the fog and drag my head backward.

I wear three shirts of descaled hare-wool, worn thin at the elbows where I lean against the wet gneiss, covered by an apron of thick, boiled-fern felt that reaches past my knees. It is stiff as dried bark, stained dark purple around the hem from dwarf-ivy sap and striped with black smears of mutton tallow and graphite. On my feet are lead-laminated boots, three inches thick, the soles cross-hatched with cold chisels every week so they bite the wet liverwort without slipping.

***

### The Niche

My room is three strides wide and five strides deep, roofed by the arching belly of the stone and fronted by a low wall of dry-stacked slate. There is no door; instead, a heavy curtain of felted moss-wool hangs in the opening, weighted at the bottom with three lead fishing weights to keep the upward drafts from lifting it.

Inside, the moisture does not pool, because the floor was cut with a slight outward pitch. A thin, continuous skin of cold water slides across the black flags day and night, trickling out into the great runnel that hugs the bridge abutment. 

To live here is to live inside a clock made of stone and iron:
*   In the corner, bolted to the rock, is a small brass **steam-lance**. Its control lever is wrapped in oiled leather. When the Midday Fall sends pressure through the bypass pipe behind my wall, the brass warms until it stops sweating. That is when I clamp my iron stew-pot—filled with coarse gray barley, dried lichen-shreds, and smoked eel fat—directly over the nozzle and pull the latch for three breaths. The dinner cooks with a dull, subterranean *chump-hiss*, filling the small chamber with the smell of scorched grain and wet iron.
*   My bed is a low shelf of slate, insulated with four layers of compressed peat-felt and covered in cured hare-hides that remain perpetually damp to the touch, though they keep the body-heat trapped beneath them like a peat fire under turf.
*   Suspended from the low ceiling by copper wires are my **stamped plates**—thin, pliable sheets of rolled zinc strung on wire hoops. They do not hold poetry; they hold the *Load-Logs*. Every three months, using a hardened steel punch and a horn mallet, I stamp new indentations into the zinc: the shifting weight of the bridge, the dates the bronze rocker-bushings were greased, the winter frost-lines where the runnels ran thick with slush.

***

### My Work

My trade is patience.

A bridge anchored into living gneiss does not fail suddenly; it complains for decades before a pin shears or a tie-rod creeps in its sulfur bedding. My duty is to know the voice of the *St. Jude Span*.

Twice during the morning Lift and once during the evening Settling, I take my listening-rod—a four-foot wand of cured mountain-ash tipped with a copper cup at one end and a polished bone disc at the other. I step out onto the narrow inspection ledge, hooking my safety harness of oiled bull-hide to the guide-cable. 

I press the copper cup against the bridge’s main anchor-bolt—a cylinder of forged iron three feet thick, driven twelve feet into the heart of the massif. Then I press the bone disc firmly against the mastoid bone behind my right ear, shut my eyes, and open my mouth so my own breath doesn't drown out the vibration.

Through the bone, the entire chasm speaks:
*   I hear the **water**: a low, liquid rumble three tiers down, where the main collection troughs dump into the turbine flumes.
*   I hear the **fauna**: the dry, rhythmic clicking of tallow-crickets grazing inside the greased bearing housings twenty feet to my left, their soft carapaces vibrating against the steel girders like rain on a taut drum.
*   I hear the **tension**: the pure, ringing *hummm* of the bridge's lower chord as an ore-tram rolls across from the east tower. If the pitch rises to an *F-natural*, the load is within tolerance. If it tightens toward a glassy *G-sharp*, it means the counterweights at the north tower have accumulated too much water and must be purged.

If a bolt is working loose in the wet socket, it makes no sound in the air. But through the listening-rod, it sounds like a grain of sand grinding between two teeth.

***

### The Life Within the Mist

I rarely see another face closer than thirty paces. Occasionally, an oiler on the funicular track will slide past fifty feet out in the fog, a blurred gray silhouette sitting astride a grease-can, and we will exchange the rod-tap: two light raps with the mallet on the safety rail (*Clear run*), answered by one slow ring from his wrench (*Underway*).

Most of my hours are spent in the company of the gutter-newts. A family of them lives in the copper spillway that runs along the lip of my ledge. They are six inches of translucent, pinkish-white cartilage, their external gills branching like tiny sprays of blood-red moss behind their blunt heads. When I sit on the edge to scrape the liverwort from my boot-soles, they drift to the surface of the runnel, nudging against the wet lead of my heels with flat, blind snouts, hoping for flecks of rendered tallow or skin-flakes. I speak to them in low, clicking fricatives—not because they hear me through the water, but because the vibration of my voice through the rock seems to settle them.

When the dusk settles, the roar of the thermal currents dies away. The valley holds its breath. The clouds cease their upward race and become an immovable, level plain of pewter wool beneath my boots.

In that quiet hour, I extinguish my gas lantern to save the line-pressure. I sit on the slate sill of my niche, wrapped in my peat-felt cloak, with a hot mug of steeped tea-moss cupped between both palms to warm the knuckle-joints. 

I watch the droplets form on the iron rivet beside my knee. 

First, a dull sheen. Then a gathering bead, clear and heavy with dissolved lime. It hangs on the black curve of the iron, trembling in the breathless cold, growing until its own weight can no longer be sustained by the tension of the water.

It falls into the dark. A half-second later, far below, a faint *ping* rises from the copper runnel—a single, clean note in a city that never stops weeping, yet never drowns.
