I would not be in the open water, where the great bellows sweeps the sump, nor would I be clinging to the high dry girders where only the gypsum curtains hang. 

I would be wedged where two things fail to meet cleanly: in the half-inch clearance between a bronze valve-spindle and its corroded iron housing, three feet below the waterline, halfway between the floor and the weeping roof.

At first, it would be hard to tell where the machine ends and the body begins. I would not have arrived here; I would have accumulated here, the way paraffin accumulates in a clogged fuel line, molecule by molecule, until the friction of the metal became a skin.

***

My body is not soft like a worm’s, nor rigid like a grazer’s shell. It is the consistency of cold, tallow-soaked leather—dense, unhurried, compressed by seventy feet of saturated head-pressure into whatever shape the iron allows. I am mostly mantle: a thick, oblong pad of fibrous, lipid-rich tissue, grey-blue as lead, folded four times upon itself to fit the narrow throat of the bypass line.

I have no eyes. Here, sight would be an empty expense. Instead, I am entirely made of contact and gradient. 

Through my dorsal surface—which is pressed flat against the ceiling of the bronze casing—I drink the faint, stinging prickle of copper ions. It tastes like cold pennies on the tongue, a sharp, sour trickle that keeps the nerves along my spine faintly alight. Through my ventral pad, which grips the pitted cast-iron bed, I take in the duller, heavier draw of iron-rot. I live in the microscopic spark between them: a living galvanic cell, running on a current so small it would not twitch a frog’s leg, but enough to power the slow, rhythmic turning of my blood.

My blood does not carry oxygen from air; it carries iron-silt and dissolved sulfates. It moves without a heart. It is pushed along by the room itself.

Every eleven minutes, the chamber shifts. 

I feel it long before the sound arrives. The pressure in the copper pipe behind me begins to climb—a minute, cold tightening against my flank as the drainage from the crushed slate miles above gathers behind the buried flap. My tissues compress under the weight. The tallow-grazers on the flange beside me go still, tucking their soft ringed snouts into their armor. 

Then comes the *clonk*.

It is not a sound in the ears; it is a shock that travels through the marrow of the iron. The bronze spindle hums against my back. The water in the casing shudders, reverses direction for half a second, and then the surge breaks through.

When the cold brine rushes over me, I do not hold tight against it. I yield. 

I allow the current to pry open the four folds of my body like the bellows of an accordion. The surge washes through the fine, comb-like sieves of my interior—thousands of hair-thin filaments coated in sticky, petroleum-scented mucus. I catch what the world above has ground to dust: flecks of ancient machine-grease, tiny flakes of calcified bone from some creature that drowned three strata higher, spores of the grey mold, and the sharp, microscopic grit of pulverized anthracite.

As the surge passes, the coldness leaves behind a deep, heavy numbness that is my version of nourishment. 

Slowly, using the elasticity of my own saturated fibers, I fold myself shut again, squeezing the filtered water out through two leathery vent-slits near my base. The expelled water is clean, stripped of its oils, tasting faintly of the lime I deposit along the valve-seat to keep my footing secure.

***

In the long, still interval after the surge, when the great bellows-beast in the sump exhales its low, bone-deep rumble, I listen.

I feel the siphon-worms pulsing in the manifold below me, their soft rhythmic contractions vibrating through the water like muffled footsteps. I feel the paper-thin skimmers sliding over the pool above, dragging their skirts across the oily rainbow film. And once in a great while, a tallow-grazer—blind and heavy with gypsum—crawls slowly across my dorsal skin, mistaking the leathery curve of my back for another dead turn of the pipe.

Its diamond teeth scrape against my outer cuticle, seeking the ancient fat stored in my pores. It does not hurt. It feels like a dull, pleasant combing, a cleaning of my seams. I let it graze. When it reaches my edge, it tumbles off into the dark, and I remain:

A living shim. A gasket of wet flesh and tallow, holding back nothing, letting everything through, perfectly matched to the slow, cold corrosion of the world that holds me.
