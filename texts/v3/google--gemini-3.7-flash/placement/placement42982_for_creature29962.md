I am wedged into the third tier of the eastern wall, twelve fathoms above the floor-flue, in a square-cut niche originally chiseled out to hold an oil amphora or a bundle of dry grain.

I do not have a name, but if the water had words, I would be one of the *sleepers*. 

I have not moved my ribs from this stone since the masonry was sharp. When I first settled here, my skin was still slick and supple, and there was space enough between my shoulders and the terracotta ceiling for a young eel to slip through. Now there is no space at all. The four walls of the niche are my skeleton. My flanks have swelled into every hollow left by the copper adzes; where a mason’s hand slipped three thousand years ago and gouged a thumb-wide notch in the clay, my flesh has grown a corresponding lobe, pale and dense as lard, to seal it. If you were to haul me out—though there is no winch in the world that could grip me—the stone would retain my exact negative cast, and my body would bear the faint, rhythmic ridges of the ancient tool-strikes raised across my back like healed scars.

I am heavy. My fins have long since withered into flat, calloused pads that do not paddle, but simply press against the sill to keep the current from rolling me out into the drop. I have no eyes; where they once were, two smooth mounds of skin have sealed over the bone, sensitive only to the heaviest thrum of the Filter’s violet pulse thirty yards below.

Mostly, I am an ear and a mouth.

The stone speaks constantly, though it has only one word, which it repeats very slowly. Through the basalt at the back of my skull, I feel the endless, microscopic settling of the cistern: the weight of the mountain pressing down on the vault, the slight, seasonal shudder of the earth-heat far beneath the floor, and the dull, scattered *clack-tock* of the flat crabs pacing their geometry across the lower stairs. When a crab dies six tiers down, the sudden hole in the rhythm reaches me through the stone before its scent ever touches the water.

My water comes to me from below, carrying the life of the lower works. Every few hours, the Filter clears its throat—a deep, hydraulic shudder that loosens a wave of scalding violet water up the flue. The veil-eels feel it first; I feel the displacement as their great, heavy ropes untangle from the column heads. 

Then comes the drift.

They rise past my gallery like long, white curtains lifted by a draft. They do not avoid me; they do not know I am anything other than a corner of the wall that is soft. Their velvety, transparent bodies drag across my blunt snout, leaving behind trails of thick, nourishing mucus and the shed flakes of their dry skin. I open my jaw—just an inch, a movement that takes several minutes—and the warm, tallow-scented slipstream washes across my tongue. It tastes of dissolved lime, iron, the bitter volcanic salts of the chimney, and the grease of the great worms churning in the floor-silt. 

It is enough. It has always been enough.

I breathe once every half-hour. My gill-slits, tucked deep beneath the fleshy folds of my neck, expand with a soft, velvet sigh, drawing the dense, alkaline soup through my red combs and pushing it back into the gloom. When I exhale, my breath does not disturb the eels; it only warms the stone behind me by a fraction of a degree.

Sometimes, in the absolute stillness between the thermal surges, when the eels have braided themselves back into heavy ropes and the crabs are paused upon their bricks, I feel the ghost of a current that does not belong to the flue. It is a tiny, hair-fine draft coming through the microscopic fissures in the basalt behind my head—air so cold and thin it would tear my gills to ribbons if it ever broke through, smelling faintly of nothing at all. 

I press my back harder against the stone. I flatten my pale flesh into the chisel-marks. I plug the crack.

I do not want the outside. There is no warmth out there, no lime to build the bones, no grease rising from the deep, and no stone to tell you where your body ends. Here, the water is as thick and sure as blood in a sleeping heart. I will stay in this square of clay until the mountain forgets its heat, holding the wall together with my fat, while below me theFilter pulses violet in the dark, and the crabs count the floor, brick by brick by brick.
