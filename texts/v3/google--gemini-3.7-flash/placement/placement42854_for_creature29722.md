The first thing that exists is not sight, and it is certainly not thought. It is the shear.

It enters through the sternum, through the small pads of cartilage between the double-hinged ribs, running straight up into the dense, oil-sheathed braincase. The needle does not merely hum; it twists. Two kilometers of solid, wind-scoured granite are torqueing against the jetstream, and that torsion has a physical taste—iron, dry cold, and a high, glassy vibration that makes the marrow in my forelimbs itch.

I would not be in the air. The sky belongs to the bone-splinters, the locked ratchets of the sickle-wings who have forgotten the meaning of weight. Nor would I be flat upon the noon-face, broad and brainless as a roofing shingle, grazing moss while the ultraviolet light fries the oil in my scales.

I am in the seam. The knuckle where the windward curve of the flute turns sharply into the absolute, blade-cut violet of the leeward shadow. 

Here, the temperature difference across my own hide is forty degrees. My left flank—covered in thick, hexagonal granules of slate-gray—is warm enough to sweat a thin film of mineral grease. My right flank, tucked into the shadow-crack, is crusted with microscopic frost that has wicked up from the cloud-deck kilometers below and frozen the instant it met the unshielded cold. 

I am small, low, and heavy. My center of gravity is not in my chest or my head, but low in the pelvis, anchored by the three dense hematite river-pebbles resting in my belly-folds. 

They are old stones. Their surfaces have been rubbed slick by the bellies of four generations before me. They do not belong to this spire; they are river-melt stones from the floor of the world, carried up when the towers were young, or passed along the leeward ribs from clutch to clutch across ten thousand seasons. When I tuck my snout down against them, I can feel their specific densities. The smallest one is quartz-veined and vibrates with a dry, buzzing treble—good for wedging into the shallow breathing-pores of the silt-tongues. The middle one is pure ironstone, dark as a bruise, cold as space; its mass matches the fundamental harmonic of this specific needle’s second rib. 

If I set the ironstone on the ledge, it does not roll. The rock here has no level surfaces, only equilibrium angles worn smooth by the gale. 

My upper eyes—the amber lenses—are shut against the noon-flare. Through their thick mineral caps, the world is only a dull, molten gold. But my lower eyes are wide, swimming in their dark oil, watching the micro-movements within the shadow-cleft. 

Three inches from my snout, a silt-tongue is weeping.

It is an old mollusk, its vulcanized mantle swollen with the mineral water it has drawn up through the vertical veins of calcite. It smells of wet chalk and cold iron. I do not touch it yet. I wait for the needle to speak.

A mile away, across the blue gulf, a sternum-reader strikes the rock. *Tock. Tock. Tock.* A three-beat percussion that travels through the granite beneath my claws like an electric pulse. An updraft is shearing along the eastern face. The laminar wind is tightening its grip, pressing three kilograms harder per square centimeter against the windward bores.

Inside the rock, the tone shifts. The deafening wall of drone drops half a step into a thick, gut-churning rumble.

*Now.*

I do not use my claws to pry; claws break against vulcanized slate. I take the middle pebble from my pouch with the prehensile pads of my inner forefoot. I slide the iron edge into the microscopic weep-line just behind the mollusk’s acid-foot. Then, lowering the flat, heavy crown of my skull, I tap.

Not hard. I do not strike to crush. I strike to match the spire’s own torsion.

*Clink.*

The ironstone rings. The rock beneath it groans in sympathy. The mollusk’s hydraulic vacuum—which can withstand the lateral pull of a hurricane—shivers at the exact frequency of the granite’s shear. The seal fails. The dark, rubbery mass drops softly into the pouch of my belly, its cool, desalinated moisture soaking immediately into my lower scales.

I do not eat it alone. 

Down in the chimney, three meters below the shadow-shelf, the clutch is waiting. I can feel their collective weight through the stone: five bodies stacked like roofing tiles, interlocked by their double joints, sharing the residual heat of the noon sun stored in their granules. Their bellies are empty, their pouches holding only the smooth stones that keep them grounded against the vacuum-pull of the vents.

I turn, flattening my body until I am scarcely thicker than my own skull, and slide backward into the narrow, dark throat of the fissure.

The wind roars over the lip above us—a solid, unyielding ceiling of sound and speed that would strip the skin from my ribs if I raised my snout two inches too high. But here, in the cold, vibrating dark, the roar becomes a cradle. 

We press our chests together. The ironstone pebble passes from my claw to my sibling’s claw, still carrying the faint vibration of the mollusk's release. We stack our weight against the wall of the world, feeling the great needle sway its millimeter of ancient tolerance against the sky, waiting for the bronze light of dusk to tell us the stone is ready to sleep.
