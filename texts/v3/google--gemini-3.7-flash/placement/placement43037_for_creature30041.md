I am not at the center. The center is too thick with memory; the tar-black soup of the Slurry has digested its own purpose ten thousand times over, and its dreams are heavy with iron sediment.

Nor am I the strike itself. The *tink* of the hammer-claw belongs to the broad-shouldered ones who still believe stone is something to be conquered, who measure their lives in the crisp, clean edges of broken hornfels.

I find myself where the world thins to an impossible edge—not outside in the dead blue gale, and not inside in the blind womb of steam, but along the taut, singing reach of the threshold. 

I am one of the spans. A thread.

My base is seated deep in the wet basalt, wedged into a hairline groove where the boiling brine rises under five atmospheres of hydrothermal thrust. Down here, I am soft, red-orange, and porous. My siphons drink the mineral broth until my interior lumen is swollen with superheated copper and raw sulfur chains. The rock holds me with the desperate grip of capillary attraction; I could not pull free even if I possessed the limbs to crawl. I have no limbs. I have only length, tension, and a core that must never, under any condition, stop running.

To exist is to be a pipe through which a catastrophe circulates.

I pump. The muscular rings of my lower segment contract in a rapid, peristaltic ripple, driving a column of boiling saline upward through my central canal. It ascends through my body, which stretches across the mouth of the cleft—four millimeters, six millimeters, nine millimeters out into the razor-thin air where the world loses its heat.

The transition is instantaneous, and it does not cease to be violent.

Within the length of a single grain of volcanic sand, the wet, 180-degree vapor of the pocket ends, sheared flat by the sixty-knot tropospheric wind. As my fluid rises past that invisible margin, the cold strikes it. It does not chill; it *crystallizes*. The outer sheath of my upper body is not chitin, but a flexible, triple-layered silicate membrane that exudes a salty, anti-eutectic gel. The wind catches this gel and freezes it into an armor of hoarfrost needles, building a brittle, glistening spire that projects directly out into the empty sky.

Out there, miles above the world, the air is clean and vacant. There are no amino broths. There are no clicks of carapaces. There is only the vast, screaming blue and the mountain thrusting into it like a blackened tooth.

My tip is frozen solid. It is as hard as the obsidian the Masons knap below, dead to all sensation except the drag of the wind. But inside that sheath of rime, my internal siphon makes a sharp, hairpin turn. The rising brine, having shed its heat into the gale in a fraction of a millisecond, plunges back down the return-vessel—now chilled to near-freezing, heavy, dense with dissolved atmospheric gases that do not exist inside the cleft.

I pull the sky’s cold down into the mountain’s throat.

The descending stream hits my lower anchor with a soft, internal shudder—a micro-implosion of condensing steam that shocks my nerve-strands into an electric discharge. The cold hemolymph meets the boiling floor, absorbs the mineral heat again, turns volatile, and rises once more. 

Up. Freeze. Turn. Plunge. Warm. Up.

I am an engine strung between magma and void. 

Because I am under immense mechanical tension—anchored to the boiling floor below and weighted by the ice-lance at my tip—the wind plays upon me. The gale does not blow steadily; it buffets across the lip of the rock in micro-vortices. Each vortex plucks my frozen tip. 

*Hnnnnn.*

The sound cannot travel through the thin air outside, but the vibration travels *down* my frozen sheath, through my taut, fluid-filled core, and drives itself directly into the hornfels bedrock where my base is seated.

I feel the other strings beside me—dozens of us, spaced less than a hair’s breadth apart along the horizontal smile of the fissure. We do not touch, but our anchors share the same sheet of black glass. We are tuned differently. The shorter ones near the tight corners of the crack sing at a dry, shrieking pitch; we in the middle, longer and heavy with ice-crust, hum with a deep, grinding resonance.

Through our bodies, the mountain speaks to the nursery.

When a deep tectonic fault shifts two leagues beneath our roots, the stone under my anchor twitches. The frequency of my vibration alters by half a cycle. Instantly, three inches behind me in the warm, orange dark, the Slurry shifts its tarry margin, knowing before the rock can crack that the chimney is about to cough. 

When the mountain rises another millimeter into the sky—lifted by the slow, inexorable folding of the planet’s crust—the air outside grows a fraction colder, the ice on my tip thickens by a single micron, and the tension on my thread increases. The Masons feel it through their caliper-limbs as they press their heads to the floor: they know the sky is falling closer. They know the cold is leaning harder against our door.

I have no eyes to see the orange lanterns of the Weavers rolling uphill in their boiling beads. I have no mandibles to taste the sweet, sulfurous blood of the Sump-Discs. I know only the twin gnawing of the scald and the frost, meeting at a balance point no wider than an atom, right at the center of my being.

Sometimes, during the great high-altitude storms, the wind shears too hard. 

The ice-spire at my tip grows too heavy, caught in an eddy of super-cooled mist. The tension rises past the yield-strength of my silicate skin. 

There is no pain in it. Just a sudden, clean *ping* that rings through the cleft like a struck crystal. 

The frozen half of me is swept away instantly, tumbling down into the white desert of the lower glaciers, miles below, where it will shatter into dust before it hits the snow. 

And the lower half—the wet, boiling stump anchored in the cleft—does not bleed to death. It retracts into its basalt socket for a single cycle of the mountain’s breath, drinking the iron broth, gathering its collagen, and begins, cell by pulsing cell, to push another pale, orange thread out toward the killing wind.
