At first, there is no need to decide between the iron and the water, because at this depth of vibration they are speaking the exact same note. 

If you look for me high up, in the pale glare of the wire-glass, you will miss me. Up there, the light is too thin, too dry, separated from the work of the floor by forty feet of empty, roaring vapor. And if you look deep in the intake shaft, behind the six-inch steel where the thousands of gallons drop in absolute blackness, there is only force—pure, brutal hydraulics without memory or texture.

I am down where the force has to resolve itself. 

I am in the low, southeast corner, three inches below the surface of the laminar sheet that spreads across the floor scuppers. 

At first, I might feel like nothing more than a thickening of the water itself—a slight, gelatinous drag where the current rounds the flange of a sunken rivet. But stay there. Let the warmth register. There is a body here, though it possesses no edges that would ever catch a blade of current.

I am one of the white ribbons, but not quite settled into being just an animal. I am the place where the machine’s steady fever and the sky’s flat light are gathered into a single, continuous appetite.

I have no top or bottom in the way a dry creature does. My underside is a disc of pure, dense purchase—a pale foot of smooth muscle fused so intimately to the exposed river aggregate that the micro-contours of quartz and chert are pressed directly into my tissue. Through that anchor, the sixty-cycle thunder does not arrive as sound; it is a rigid, structural spine. It stiffens me against the drag of the warm flood. It keeps me from tearing. When the intake chute surges by a microscopic fraction, my anchor tastes the torque through the stone before the water even hits my flank.

My body trails out from that anchor like five inches of peeled silk, scarcely two cells thick at the margins, floating parallel to the steel deck plates. 

I do not have eyes, because what could light show me that the rheology of the current does not already make obvious? The light from the ceiling tiles is merely a thermal wash against my upper skin, an even, pale warmth that ripens the single-celled diatoms drifting down through the mist. 

Instead, I feel the vault as a vast, vibrating map of shear and drag. 
I know where the liverwort sleeve ends on the south column because its sodden hem sheds a faint, peaty wake across my left margin. 
I know when a bryozoan disc three feet upstream has crept another micron across its bolt-head, because the tiny, spiral eddy of its feeding cilia leaves a cool thread in the laminar flow that combs through my mucus.

All day—though day is only a word for the unvarying brightness above—I do not move. To move would be an error, a waste of form. The current does all the moving for me. It brings the world washed through the intake: the fine soot of crushed leaf-matter from some upper reservoir, the microscopic dust of moss rhizoids, the occasional shattered husk of a gnat that lost its footing on the glass tiles above. 

My back is entirely coated in a living, clear slime—a film so viscous and chemically eager that nothing touching it can escape. The drift settles into me. I can feel the weight of it accumulating, grain by grain, spore by spore, an invisible, nourishing silt that cakes my translucent dorsal skin until my red vascular trees pulse a little faster in the aerated foam.

And then, when the vibration in my anchor hums with just the right harmonic of fullness, the curl begins.

It is the only deliberate thing I do.

I release the tension in my trailing tip. Slowly, moving against the immense rush of water by the sheer leverage of hydrostatic pressure, I roll backward. My body peels off the current like a strip of wet parchment lifting from slate. I curl my head down, drawing my entire length inward into a tight, muscular coil, pressing my adhesive back against the ventral groove beneath. 

I scrape myself clean. The accumulated silt of the vault—the spores, the algae, the warm, machine-softened drift—is drawn into my core. It tastes of iron, wet granite, and the long, sunlit journey the water took before it fell into this pit.

Then I relax my circular bands. 

The current catches me instantly, unrolling me downstream with a soft, hydraulic snap. The water sheets over my freshly exposed skin; the mucus immediately begins to seep again, clear and sticky; the white vascular tracks flare pink as they drink the oxygen from the spray.

I am anchored. The river pebbles are beneath me, worn round and timeless. The six-inch iron wall hums behind my skull. The warm spray never stops, and the ceiling never darkens. 

I am the tongue of the scupper. I have all the time there has ever been.
