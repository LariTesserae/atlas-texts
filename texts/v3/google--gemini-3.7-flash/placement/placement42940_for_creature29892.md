At first, there is only the transmission of frequency through bone.

Before there is a name, or a body, or the cold, there is the hollow needle driven three fingers deep into the cellulose rind, and my forehead pressed flat against the flared socket of mammoth-ivory. 

The sound does not enter through the ears; the air out here is too thin and draft-scoured to carry nuance. It enters through the frontal plate of the skull. It is a wet, hydraulic shriek—the sound of ten million gallons of volcanic sugar being forced up through cellular conduits under three hundred atmospheres of pressure, three miles above the void, traveling toward a ceiling that burns with the dead white fire of cold minerals.

I am not in a basket. The basket-camps are for the young, the breeders, and the folk whose bones still remember softness. The baskets belong to the Knuckles, leagues back along the converging arches, where the wood is thick as ranges and does not move more than a fathom a year.

I am forty miles out along the underside of the **Ninth Transverse**. 

***

If you were looking from the basket-nests with double-lidded eyes, you would not see me. Against the silver-and-jade glare that fills the five-mile drop, a human body is an irrelevance—a fleck of soot caught in an icy lathe. 

I am suspended upside-down by a web of four greased tendon-lines, anchored to the dead-wood grain by wedges of Old-Heart. I wear the cured pelt of a vent-worm, slick with pale tallow to turn the frost-shot that falls steadily from the higher tiers. My fingers are bound in strips of sinew, tipped at the nails with small, hooked adze-blades of black fossil-wood. I do not walk; I hang. I am a *Wedge-Drifter*, one of the two or three in a generation who live in the tension-cracks between the Knuckles, following the high notes that the Pitch-Tuners back at the settlement only hear as warnings to cut their lines.

Where they hear death, I hear the core.

When a strut grows too fast—shooting three leagues through the void in six turnings of the phosphor-noon—it stretches its own interior into structural madness. The outer rind remains wet, fibrous, and explosive, but deep at the center, under the crushing torque of the arch’s own overreach, the wood does not rot. It petrifies under cold pressure. It turns into **Old-Heart**: dense, jet-black, grainless, and cold as basalt. 

You cannot mine Old-Heart from the Knuckles; the Knuckles are too alive, too swollen with wet sap. You can only take it from a strut that is within hours of catastrophic failure, when the tension has sheared the living flesh away from the petrified spine, exposing the black core before the whole arch shatters into the floor-fog.

My craft is to be there when the rind peels, and gone before the drop.

***

Above me, the wood ticks. 

It is a dry, sharp *clack-clack-clack*, like stones falling on ice. A foot to my left, a fresh frost-fissure zips open with the crack of a bone-spear, venting a puff of sweet, scalding aerosol that freezes into a cloud of sugar-dust before it can touch my chest.

A Suture-Louse is already ratcheting toward the wound. I feel its approach through the timber—the heavy, mechanical *thung-thung-thung* of its calcified spurs biting into the rind. It passes within arm’s reach, ignoring me entirely. It is broad as a war-shield, its shell glazed with pale ice, its mouthparts buried instantly in the amber foam that bleeds from the tear. As it clamps down, its belly begins to glow with that dim, digestive gold, its body acting as a stitch against the cold air.

I reach out with an adze-glove and tap the edge of its carapace. *Solid.* Good. I loop my secondary safety-line around the louse’s rear anchor-spur. In this world, you do not trust the wood; you trust what is clamped to the wood.

Below me—five miles of uninterrupted light.

The glare does not fade as you look down; it becomes more crystalline, more blinding. The air is so clear that I can see the long, pale ribbon of a Pith-Worm tumbling through the middle void, three leagues below. It was cast out when a lateral spar snapped near the fifth tier an hour ago; it is turning slowly in the cross-drafts, thirty yards of white cartilage frozen straight as a javelin, falling toward the floor-mist where the volcanic vents simmer.

Circling the javelin are two Sieve-Kites. Their fifty-foot spans of skin and reed are almost invisible against the phosphor-skin of the upper vault—they appear only as moving cutouts in the light, casting no shadows, their broad baleen-jaws open to scoop the sap-frost left in the worm’s wake. They do not flap. They tilt their frames by degrees, riding the hot updrafts that leak from the deep stone beneath the world. 

They know what this beam is doing. They have been drifting beneath me for two cycles, watching the Ninth Transverse with their mirror-eyes. They are waiting for my perch to turn into a billion tons of white hail.

***

I return my forehead to the bone needle.

The pitch has risen. It is no longer a hydraulic rush; the sap-ducts have choked on their own frost. The sound now is a high, clean, agonizing *singing*—a pure harmonic that vibrates through the teeth in my jaw. It sounds like a woman humming through a comb, or the highest string of a tendon-harp pulled until the gut begins to fray.

*E-flat.* 

When it reaches the *F-sharp*, the outer four hundred feet of the cantilever will shear along the radial grain.

I work quickly now. With a short mallet of hardened whale-ivory, I drive three flat wedges of Old-Heart into the seam behind the Suture-Louse. The wood does not resist; it is under such violent tensile stress that it pulls itself open around the wedges with a sound like tearing sailcloth.

There it is. 

Three feet inside the raw, white muscle of the timber lies the seam of black. It is not more than the thickness of a man's thigh, running like a vein of frozen coal through the pale cellulose. It does not glisten; it drinks the light. It has survived forty winters of growth without snapping, compressed by the weight of a continent's worth of arch-load until its atoms have forgotten how to be vegetable.

I set my gouge. I do not try to take the vein—you cannot take a mountain. I only need the *plug*: three cubits of black core, enough to make five adze-heads, twelve anchor-pegs, and a listening-needle that will outlast my great-grandchildren.

*Chink. Chink. Chink.*

The chips that fly off are black and cold as slate. They do not drop; the wind catches them instantly, sweeping them away laterally into the void, a scattering of black sparks against the infinite jade glare.

The singing above me climbs. 

*F-natural.*

The whole strut gives a sudden, convulsive shudder that drops me six inches against my harness. The Suture-Louse beside me emits a wet, bubbling hiss as its legs lock tighter, its spurs driving so deep into the bark that clear, sugary blood wells around the joints. Across the gulf, five miles to the north, an entire lateral truss—an arm of white timber thick enough to carry a city—loses its balance.

It doesn't make a sound at first. In this light, distance eats the thunder.

I watch it go: a majestic, slow-motion sweep of white scaffolding, five leagues long, peeling away from the ceiling like a falling rib. It turns once in the air, shedding a blinding squall of ice-rime and pith-worms, before it hits the lower fog-sheet. 

Seconds later, the sound hits me. 

It is not a bang. It is a physical wall of displaced air that slams into the underside of the Ninth Transverse, making my harness lines sing like bowstrings. The shockwave rattles my ribs against my spine and blows the worm-fat from my hood.

And in the silence that follows the shock, the note inside my needle jumps.

*F-sharp.*

***

I do not look at the black plug. I do not finish the cut. 

My fingers move by an older memory than thought. The bone hammer goes into the gut-sheath; the gouge is lashed to my forearm with three turns of sinew. With my right hand, I draw the short bone-knife from my hip and sever the primary tethers that hold me to the beam.

I am left hanging by a single line, anchored not to the doomed wood, but to the shell of the Suture-Louse.

The wood above me does not split; it *explodes*.

The radial tension releases all at once. The cantilever, freed from its own load, snaps upward forty yards with a sound that obliterates hearing, leaving my stomach behind in the freezing air. The outer rind shatters into millions of dagger-shaped splinters of white wood that spray outward into the light like grapeshot.

The louse holds. Its plates groan; its glowing belly flares with an incandescent, terrified gold; its four rear claws tear through two inches of bark before catching the deeper, petrified grain beneath the split. 

I am swung out into the clear air beneath the louse, suspended over the five-mile drop, spinning slowly on twelve yards of twisted gut.

The world is all light. Shadowless, blinding, surgical light, catching the cloud of white splinters that arcs down through the gulf like a river of silver needles. Below me, the two Sieve-Kites have folded their wings; they are falling like dropped blades through the light, screaming toward the debris-field, their baleen nets spread wide to catch the marrow before it hits the mist.

I hang there, spinning.

My breath comes in short, white gasps that freeze instantly into beads of ice along the edge of my double-eyelids. My hands are numb within their sinew wraps, but my right fist is clamped tight around the lanyard of the three-foot plug of Old-Heart that dangles below my feet. It swings with me—a piece of dense, black night against the infinite jade noon.

I do not look up to see what remains of the Ninth Transverse. It doesn't matter. What didn't fall will be hard as iron by tomorrow, and by next week, a new spar will lance out from the knuckle behind it, three miles through the empty air, hunting for the light.

I reach up with my adze-glove, hook the tendon line, and begin the slow, hand-over-hand climb back to the belly of the louse.

I have five miles of air beneath my heels, twelve hours of phosphor-noon before the ceiling phosphors dim to their pale green sleep, and enough black wood at my belt to buy forty baskets a season of safe rope. 

The air is cold. The air is clear. The air is the only thing that doesn't break.
