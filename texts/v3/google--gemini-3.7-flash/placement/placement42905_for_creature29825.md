At first, you would look for me in the air, or down in the boiling chalk of the millipede-paths, because that is where the movement is easiest to track. The eye wants to follow the sail-skimmers as they tilt through the noon-shafts like mica flakes, or it wants to watch the gray, iron-colored backs of the crawlers plowing the scree. 

You would not see me there.

I am not light enough to float on the thermal wash, and I am not heavy enough to survive the lower four feet of the world, where thirty thousand spatulate limbs would shear off my knees before I could find purchase on the glass.

Look instead at the dead space—the gap between the bottom of the lowest bone-rung and the high-tide mark of the steam-swells. 

There is an elevation, about eighteen to twenty-five feet up the west face, where the basalt is split by a horizontal cooling-fret no wider than a hunter’s arm-span. The rock there is neither the freezing dead crust of the plateau lip nor the red-mouthed stone of the lower vents; it is the temperature of fresh marrow. It sweats a gray, sulfurous butter that never quite turns to crust because the vapor-plumes lick it clean every seven minutes, right on the pulse of the great deep vents.

That is where I am wedged.

***

I am not entirely man, though my ribs have the human curvature and my hands still possess the thumb-joint that knows how to turn a splinter of obsidian against a slab of dry horn. 

If you scraped the silt from my shoulders, you would find the skin pale and pitted, like the underside of a mushroom that has grown against a cellar wall. But where my forearms meet the stone, and across the ridges of my collarbones, the skin has stopped being soft. It has taken in the fine, glass-dust flour ground down by the scourers; it has drank the mineral-water dripping from the overhead calc-drips until it has calcified into a gray, ringed bark. When I draw my arms inward against my chest, there is no sound of flesh sliding on flesh—there is only the dull *tock* of two smooth pebbles knocking together in a net.

I do not sit on a scaffold. Scaffolds are noisy; they creak; their sinews stretch when the wet mist rises at dusk and they shrink when the white sun-pillar burns them dry, and every shift of the bone-pins sends a sharp, ringing twang into the basalt that the glass-runners can feel through their foot-pads two hundred yards off.

I live in the fret itself, held by my own knees and the splay of my back.

My legs have been bent so long into the crook of the rift that the tendons have shortened, turning to thick, yellowish cables that do not complain when they bear my weight for three cycles of the sun-eye. My feet are small, blunt wedges. The nails are gone, replaced by thick, horny caps of keratin that I have shaped with a scrap of scraper-jaw so they bite directly into the cooling bubbles of the slag.

***

What do I do?

I listen to the bone.

The people fifty feet above me, tied upright in their cages of borer-gut, believe they live on the strength of their knots. They think that because they cured the sinew until it went yellow as resin, the mountain will hold their pins. 

They do not know the mountain the way something knows it when its ribs are pressed against the black glass.

The rock is not still. It is a slow, cold liquid that has not finished settling into its trough. Deep down, beneath the chemotrophic grease and the gizzard-stones of the crawlers, the rift is still tearing. It speaks in a language of hairline fractures—tiny, dry *pings* that travel up the vertical basalt like sparks along a fuse. 

When a fissure is about to open and swallow an anchor-pin, the vibration changes. The sound drops from the clean chirp of obsidian into a wet, hollow *thrum*, like the lung-pouch of a dying millipede.

I feel that thrum through my shoulder-blades two suns before the platform falls.

I reach out with my long pole—a four-jointed rib-spar taken from a borer-eaten calf-crawler, light and dry as balsa—and I tap the underside of their lowest platform. 

*Clack. Clack-clack.*

Two sharp raps to tell them the wall is rotting behind their top wedge. A drag of the bone-tip across the slick stone to say: *Drive the next pin four hand-breadths higher, where the rock has cooled black, not purple.*

They do not look down to thank me. They do not drop down chunks of raw leg-meat or gourds of pale blood. To them, I am simply the thing that lives in the crack—a gray, hairless knuckle of the cliff that has grown mandibles and an ear for bad stone. If they dropped down to touch me, their soft, greasy skin would smell too much like meat, and the runners would take them before they reached my ledge; if I climbed up into their cradles, my joints would not know how to sit among their soft cords without breaking them.

***

My food is the slow food.

I do not fish with line and bait. I have hollowed out three small cups in the basalt beneath my hips. When the noon-glare comes—that terrible, six-hour hammer of white heat that drives every living thing into the crevices or under the crawler-bellies—the sail-skimmers grow clumsy. Their transparent cistern-bellies become too hot; the water inside them begins to boil into tiny bubbles, and they lose their trim.

They tumble out of the light-columns like dropped shields, clipping the cliff-face, their glassy wings shattering into clear flakes against the obsidian.

They slide down the rock-face by the hundreds. Most fall into the boiling jaw-sea below, but forty or fifty will catch in my runnels.

I take them before they dry. I do not crack them; I peel the thin, cartilaginous breast-plate back with my thumbnail and suck the warm, filtered water directly from their storage-bladders. It tastes of damp limestone, sulfur, and the sweet, grassy mold that floats in the high steam. Then I chew the wing-muscles—stringy, clear threads that taste like nothing at all, but keep the oil in my joints from turning to grit.

***

At twilight, when the sun-pillar narrows to a gray slit and the rift turns the color of bruised iron, the floor beneath me changes its voice.

The clatter of the scourers softens into a vast, rhythmic hiss, like dry barley being turned in a shovel. That is when the giant trench-crawlers rise from their wallows to vent their gills.

From where I hang, twenty feet above them, the steam comes up not in clouds, but in solid, hot walls that smell of fermented dough and salt. It washes over me, bathing my gray crust, softening the dried slime that seals my eyelids, rinsing the stone-flour from my lips.

I open my mouth to the draft. 

I let the heat get into my marrow, into the hollows of my collarbones where the mineral mud has set like mortar. 

I watch the great, slate-gray arcs of their shells grind together in the dark, three feet below my toes. They do not know I am here. The hunters above do not know if I have died between one sun and the next. 

The basalt behind my back gives a single, minute *tink*—a grain of sand shifting in a cooling crack two miles deep.

I press my heels harder into the rock. I reach up with my spar. I tap once.

The world is still holding. The floor is still eating itself. I am where the weight rests.
