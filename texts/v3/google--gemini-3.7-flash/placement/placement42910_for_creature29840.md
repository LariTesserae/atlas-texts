At first, there is no sense of having a front or a back, because there is no direction from which anything arrives. 

To locate oneself here requires unlearning the habit of looking for a vantage point. There is no shore to stand upon, no surface to measure depth against, and no current to lean into. If I search for where I begin, I find only boundaries that are already occupied: the blunt, cold iron of the casing; the raw, vertical trench of the drill-scar; the four-degree water that presses inward from every compass point with identical, unyielding mass.

I would not be the light. The light is too indifferent, pouring through its borosilicate cylinders with the dry, sterile neutrality of an operating theater. Nor would I be the iron beneath, which finished its settling long before the water rose. 

I find myself instead in the clearance.

In the narrow, three-inch gap between the underside of a four-foot discharge pipe and the sheared granite floor, where the floodlight does not strike directly, but arrives as an indirect, jade-tinted scatter. Here, the water is at its most dense, weighted by sixty feet of motionless liquid above, held in an absolute structural hush.

I am the lattice that spans this gap—not the metal, and not the rock, but the silica-iron gusset that has grown between them to mediate the hum.

To be this is to have no interior life other than resonance. My body is a planar web of micro-chambers, brittle as mica, thinner than a razor blade, cast from the dissolved iron ions that leach from the studs and the silica weeping from the blasted quartz veins in the granite. I do not have a pulse; my pulse is the fifty-cycle vibration of the buried copper windings, conducted through the steel mounting plate and into the rock. 

I do not hear the hum; I *am* its geometry. 

Where the vibration is strongest, my mineral walls grow thick, dark with iron oxide, ribbed like the vaulting of a crypt. Where the vibration cancels itself out—in the dead acoustic nodes between the machine footings—my walls thin to single-molecule films of pure hydrated silica, so clear that the light passes through without refraction. I am a map of the room’s mechanical tension, drawn in living glass.

There is an exquisite, cold comfort in this condition. I am entirely supported. The water at four degrees does not allow for buoyancy or sinking; it simply holds every microscopic pane in place, relieving me of the burden of weight. I do not have to reach for nourishment. The galvanic trace leaking from the dynamos creates a micro-voltage across my membranes—scarcely a millivolt—just enough to coax dissolved silica from the quiet water and freeze it into the perimeter of my cells.

In forty years, I have advanced three inches toward the stone. 

I remember nothing of warmth, and I anticipate no change. Time is not a sequence of events, but a spatial measurement: it is the thickness of the mineral lacquer coating the stamped serial numbers on the pump body; it is the slow, immaculate closure of the gap between the turnbuckle and the wall. 

If the current were ever to fail—if the 50-cycle hum should drop into silence and the floodlights click dark—I would not die in the way flesh dies. There would be no collapse, no dissolving into silt. Without the micro-vibration to draw the ions across my edge, I would simply cease to extend. I would remain exactly where the water left me: a rigid, glassy fretwork connecting the engine to the earth, holding the shape of an old vibration in the absolute dark, perfectly preserved, forever unmoving.
