I would not start by looking down from above, because here there is no *above* that doesn't immediately reflect itself right back into your eyes. 

To be here is to lose the habit of the upright spine. At first, you might look for a perch—a branch, a ridge, some vantage from which to survey the miles. But there are no perches, only the knuckles of stone that rise two inches above the skin of the water, and the sun is so uniform that elevation gives you no advantage at all.

So you lower yourself. You let the warmth take the tension out of your joints until you are resting flush against the pink feldspar.

***

At first, I might think I am the water itself—the slow, creeping sheet that doesn't know how to run, only how to spill from one wide saucer of gneiss into the next. But water has no memory of the stone before the wearing down; it just comes and goes as vapor and mist. 

I need something that remembers the weight.

I find myself settled in the shallow crook where a dry lichen-crusted hummock slopes down into an inch of sun-baked drift. My body is wide, rounded, and astonishingly thin—a heavy disk of leathery bone and dark horn, wider than a shield, with edges that taper to a soft, flexible flange that seals against the rock.

I am one of the old plates. Not quite the largest of the sun-plates, but old enough that the algae growing across my back has formed its own micro-topography: miniature forests of olive-green cyanobacteria and rust-colored filaments that wave gently when the surface film ripples. 

My eyes are set high on my flat skull, right at the waterline. Through the top half of the lens, I see the endless, pale expanse of midday sky and the shimmering spires of midges drifting like smoke over the warm pans. Through the bottom half, I see the microscopic world of the granite floor: the pink feldspar grains, the translucent flat-leeches gliding like glass ribbons over the silt, and the shadow of my own wide rim.

I do not hear the chorus through the air. In the air, the roar of the pearl-throated frogs is a deafening, metallic hiss, too vast to mean anything. 

I hear it through my belly.

My lower shell—smooth, pale, and worn slick by centuries of sliding across wet quartz—is pressed tight against the bedrock. The stone is not silent stone; it is an immense, rigid membrane. Every time a million tiny frogs inflate their pearlescent sacs and drive their calls downward into the floor, the vibration shoots through the granite directly into my plastron. It thrums in the marrow of my wide, flattened ribs. It is a steady, rhythmic boiling in the bones—a heartbeat that doesn't belong to me, but to forty square miles of continuous rock.

A clack-bill wades past. Its broad, pebbled foot comes down three inches from my snout. The water displacement lifts my marginal scutes just a fraction of a millimeter before the suction re-engages. The bird doesn't see me; to the bird, I am simply a darker slab of gneiss where the water is slightly shallower. It pauses, its honey-colored bill poised, waiting for a shield-tick to loosen its grip. The bird's patience is sharp and hungry; mine is merely ambient. I close my lower eyelids—thick, pale, mineral membranes—and wait for the hollow *thwack* of its strike to ring through the stone.

When it moves on, I move. 

It takes me half an afternoon to cross the span of a single granite apron. My limbs are short, broad paddles tipped with blunt, horn-capped claws that find purchase in the microscopic fractures of the rock. I do not lift myself to walk; I slide. My lower shell acts as a squeegee, scraping away the fine pink silt and the diatom film, leaving a gleaming, wet trail of bare stone behind me that the frogs will claim for their egg-sheets by dusk.

Ahead of me, a stunted scrub tree grows from a hairline joint. A few of its thick, coin-shaped leaves have dropped into the shallows, yellowing and softened by the warm, stagnant water. I extend my neck—thick-skinned, wrinkled like ancient slate—and crop the leaf with a slow, heavy scissor of my jaw. It tastes of bitter tannin, iron, and the sweet, dense sun that has baked into it for months.

As the sun begins to lean toward the horizon, the direct glare finally breaks. The pink of the granite deepens, matching the long, rose-colored reflections that stretch across miles of unruffled water. The heat in the air thins slightly, but the stone beneath me remains intensely, fiercely hot, radiating the stored fire of twelve hours of direct light.

I do not have a home to return to, because there is no *elsewhere*. There are no caves, no high nests, no deep pools to sink into. 

I pull my blunt snout partly beneath the rim of my carapace, let the warm inch of water lap over the bridge of my nose, and let out a long, slow hiss of spent air that stirs the sedge tufts beside me. The mist is already beginning to gather, rising from the floor to meet the falling light. 

Under my ribs, the frogs are beginning their night-roar. The stone rings and rings, and I hold it down, keeping it from floating away into the sky.
