First, there is only the vibration in the teeth. 

It does not come through the ears; those were muffled into a flat, gray hum long ago, the delicate tympanic skin deadened until it became just another scar. The pulse arrives upward, through the heels, through the soft-lead rivets hammered into the soles of the oilskin boots. The lead bites into the calc-schist just enough to hold without skating on the grease, and through that lead comes the whole topography of the floor: the three-hundred-ton stroke of the No. 4 Sulzer dewatering pump down in the low sump, the lighter, frantic shudder of a turbine intake choked with gravel, and the dull, endless smash of the roof-fissure cataract two hundred yards to the north.

To know where you are in this dark is not a matter of looking. The sodium lamps are only yellow smears that die ten feet out in the warm mist, showing nothing but a greasy flange or the pale, slow belly of an isopod scraping mold off a hemlock strut. 

To know where you are, you place a bare palm against the nearest run of twenty-four-inch discharge pipe. 

If the iron runs cold and stutters with a dry, gravelly rattle, you are high on the western catwalks near the fresh seepage manifolds. If the pipe is warm, thick-skinned with slippery lime, and throbbing with a long, hydraulic surge that heaves every five seconds like an animal drawing breath through water, you are suspended directly over the primary boil, thirty cubits above the intake bells.

That is where I find myself. Not at the dry top where the suspended bunk-shacks hang from their anchor chains, and not down in the frothing muck where the tallow-blind eels crowd the bronze grates, but pinned squarely in the middle weft—clinging to the slick web of pipe-hangers, cable trays, and steam lines that keeps the mountain from filling with its own spit.

***

I am not an engineer. The men who drew the blueprints and calculated the hydraulic lift are somewhere far above, under an open sky that I have forgotten how to imagine without feeling a vague, agoraphobic vertigo. Nor am I a beast of the sumps, though my skin has the same boiled-turnip cast as the river-crabs, and the seams of my canvas overalls are patched with the same tough, fibrous sulfur-paste we scrape from the steam-vents to keep the rot from eating our thighs.

I am a scaler of the stroke. A clearer of throats.

My right forearm is bound in split-hide, soaked in tallow and engine-oil until it is stiff as horn, shielding the radial artery from the razor-edges of the fresh calcite that grows over every bolt-head overnight. In my right hand is a four-pound cold chisel, its striking face mushroomed and burred from ten thousand impacts; in my left, a short-hafted sledge.

My work is simple, endless, and entirely necessary if the heart is to keep beating.

Water here is not just wet; it is pregnant with stone. It falls through miles of unresolved earth, boiling with dissolved lime, iron, and silica, and the moment it hits the hot cylinder heads or the vibrating steel cables, it drops its load. It wants to turn the copper wire into solid limestone ropes. It wants to weld the sliding bronze crossheads to their guides. It wants to bridge the space between the iron trusses and the living rock until the whole roaring machinery is frozen into a single, immovable fossil.

So I hang in the webbing. 

I loop a broad leather safety strap around a steam riser, set my lead-shod heels into the notch of a riveted flange, and I chip. 

*Clink. Clink. Tock.*

The blows have no sound to me, only a sharp, electric sting that travels up my wrists into the collarbones. Under the chisel, the white rind fractures like eggshell. It comes away in curved flakes—brittle, chalky crescents that drop down through the yellow haze, tumbling past the suspended cable-bundles to splash into the froth below. Behind the lime, the black iron emerges, wet and sweating with dark machine oil. I brush it clean with a stiff wire pad, grease the threads with graphite tallow, and swing my harness along the rail to the next joint.

***

You learn, after years in the damp roaring, that you are not alone on the iron. 

As I clear the housing of a check-valve, something shifts beneath my knee. It is an isopod, broad as a dinner plate, its segmented back encrusted with gray graphite pellets and flakes of oxidized paint. It does not scurry. Nothing here scurries; speed is a waste of grease. It simply raises two spiny forelegs, tests the greasy leather of my boot with slow, blind precision, and decides I am neither rot nor stone. It settles back down, its mandibles working methodically against the soft, fungal fur that fringes the edge of the gasket. 

I do not smash it. To smash it would be to dull the chisel, and besides, we are doing the same work. It eats the decay that softens the wood; I crack the stone that binds the steel. We are both just scrapers on the hull of something immense that is slowly drowning.

Further along the pipe, where the exhaust from a auxiliary turbine keeps the casing hot to the touch, a cluster of blind crabs has gathered. They are piled three deep, their translucent, paper-thin shells glowing faintly orange under the sodium light. Their elongated, hair-fringed claws sweep the metal in slow, rhythmic figure-eights, gathering the bacterial film that grows in the engine grease. When my sledge strikes the iron three feet away, they do not scatter; they only hunker lower, their carapaces vibrating in sympathy with the steel, drinking the concussion directly into their marrow.

We have an understanding, the crabs and I. If a valve sticks, I use the chisel to lever it free; if their grease-mats grow thick enough to foul an oiler-wick, I sweep them off into my bucket to use as packing for the gland-nuts. When my knuckles split against a ragged edge of slate, I do not wash the wound in the acidic seepage; I reach out and press my bleeding skin against the cool, mucus-coated shell of an isopod. The slime is bitter, smelling of pulverized shale, but it seals the cut faster than tar, and the wound does not fester in the warm mist.

***

Sometimes, during the slack-water hours—when the diesel tenders far above drop the heavy fuel charges down the hoist-pipes and the whole cavern shudders with the change in engine pitch—I lean back into my harness and let my head rest against the cold rock ceiling.

From here, looking down through the lattice of pipes and trusses, the sink looks like the interior of a colossal, living chest. 

The pump pistons rise and fall—*down, stroke, lift, clear*—with a slow, magnificent violence that moves thousands of tons of black water every minute. The cataracts plunge from the darkness above like white arteries, tearing themselves to foam on the jagged slate. The sodium lanterns flicker through the steam like dying nerve endings. And all through the iron skeleton, hung by chains and pinned by leather straps, the two dozen of us are moving—silent, white-skinned, scarred with sulfur, chipping away the mineral shell that the water constantly tries to knit over our bodies.

If I were to drop my hammer, if I were to unbuckle the harness and let myself fall, I would not hit the water. I would strike three runs of six-inch conduit, a bundle of signal cables, and a timber staging before I ever reached the sump. And within three weeks, the warm, dripping lime would have smoothed the broken shape of me into the contour of the ledge, sealing my leather gaiters and split-hide guards into a pale, rounded buttress of stone, over which the crabs would crawl to graze on the engine fat.

That is what this place does: it resolves everything into either the pump or the rock. 

I reach into my pouch, pull out a fresh tallow-plug to seal my greaser-gun, and reach for the next valve. The pipe beneath my palm gives a heavy, rhythmic shudder—the dewatering intake clearing its throat of silt. 

I tap the flange twice with the butt of my wrench: *Clear here. Holding.* 

A moment later, two miles away through the solid stone, three faint, metallic pings vibrate back through my lead soles: *Understood. Keep swinging.*

I set the chisel against a fresh crust of calcite, raise the hammer, and strike.
