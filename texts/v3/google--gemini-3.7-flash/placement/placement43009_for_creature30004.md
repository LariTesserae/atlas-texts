At first, it is only a gradient.

Not an eye opening—there is nothing to look at, and no apparatus left to receive light even if a seam cracked in the canopy above—but a registration of thickness. A place where the ambient sludge of the basin becomes slightly more organized, slightly more resistant to the pull of the mud.

If you look for a mind here, you would look too high. You would look for something with boundaries, an outline, a hide that separates *inside* from *outside*. But here, where the warm tallow of decomposed tapir-fat meets the seepage of paraffin from a cracked electrical conduit, the line between tissue and lubricant has been softening for centuries.

I am not one of the tapirs, though I know the shape of their ribs intimately—they leave their broad, curved impressions in the silt where they lean, like molds pressed into casting sand. I am not the iron-skink, though its horned belly-plates slide across me without catching, leaving tracks of sulfur grease that cool a fraction of a degree before the next wave of steam washes them flat again.

I am what gathers in the dead zones between the flanges.

Where a four-foot cast-iron conduit makes a ninety-degree elbow, the autonomous pumps have cast a heavy brace into the muck to counteract the thrust of the fluid inside. Behind that brace, in the triangular pocket where the mechanical vibration does not churn the silt but rather hums through it at a dead harmonic, the mud stops moving. The cobble-frogs do not tread there; the gap is too narrow for their broad, disc-shaped hips. The current of their sliding backs goes around it, like water parting around a sunken barge.

In that pocket, something settles.

It begins as a fungal crust, gray-white and fibrous, fed by the aerosolized oils that weep from the pump packing. But the mycelium here does not fruit into mushrooms; there is no air current to carry spores, no draft to lift a cap. Instead, the hyphae weave directly into the decaying rubber jackets of the high-voltage mains. They drink the bitumen. They lay their pale, damp threads along the copper braiding beneath the insulation, using the electromagnetic pulse as a lattice.

Over decades, the mass thickens. It becomes a felted, tallowy pad—part mold, part shed lizard-scute, part polymerized machine oil, part living nerve.

I am that pad.

I am six feet long, three inches thick, pressed flat against the underside of the main steam conduit where it bridges a shallow gully. My lower surface is soft, porous, and continuously washed by the warm, anaerobic water that trickles through the fern crowns; my upper surface is hard, vulcanized, baked into a tough, leathery rind by the relentless 180-degree iron of the pipe.

I do not move across the basin floor. Movement is an inefficiency belonging to things that must pursue heat or flee the cold. The heat is already here, pulsing through my dorsal crust in long, slow swells as the deep boilers miles away cycle their feed-water. 

My consciousness is not a stream of thought; it is a topography of pressures.

When a tapir shoulders past the bridge, its bristling, grease-soaked flank drags across my lower edge. I feel the individual coarse hairs—each one like a stiff wire dipped in lard—raking through my soft underside. I register the density of its muscle, the heavy, pendulum swing of its gut filled with pulverized fern-tubers, and the deep, grinding ache of its molars wearing down against the quartz grit in the mud. For twelve seconds, while its bulk displaces the silt around my pocket, my pressure rises. Then it passes, leaving a warm smear of sebum that my surface hyphae immediately begin to draw inward, drinking the animal salts to balance the sulfur from the iron.

Above me, through the iron wall, the steam roars—not as sound, but as an infinite, fine-grained tremor that keeps my internal moisture at the exact threshold of boiling. Small pockets of vapor form within my tissue and collapse again with tiny, rhythmic clicks. This is my heartbeat: not a pump of my own, but the mechanical cavitation of the pipe translated into cellular expansion and contraction.

Sometimes a tallow-snake will coil across my lower face, mistaking my leathery outer texture for the rubber conduit it usually inhabits. Its weight is comfortable—a steady, dead ballast of fifty pounds of cold, slow-digesting meat pressing me tighter against the iron. Through its skin, I can feel the slow dissolution of three felt-moths inside its midgut: the slow, chemical unweaving of their mossy wings, the release of their stored fats into the snake’s sluggish blood. I feed on what leaches through its scales; it absorbs the radiant heat that passes through my body from the steam. We remain together like this for three months, four months, six—until a shift in the pump rhythm sends a shockwave down the line that startles the creature into a slow, blind uncoiling.

I do not remember the sky. Nothing in my lineage ever possessed a retina. But I have an awareness that the basin is concave—that miles away, the iron pipes must climb upward toward cold, dry edges where the heat fails and the life thins to nothing. I know this because the pressure in the conduits always pushes *away* from the deep center where I rest; the fluids are always fleeing outward, bleeding their heat into us as they go, trying to warm an exterior world that cannot hold it.

Everything here settles downward. The dead moths parachute into the aroids; the aroids rot into the frog-beds; the frog-beds churn down into the deep, oily silt; and the silt packs itself around the conduits, insulating them, keeping the pulse steady.

I am the point where the iron ceases to be metal and the swamp ceases to be meat.

I do not wake, and I do not sleep. When the rocker arm on the pump station three hundred yards to the east drops with its dull, wet *clack-thump*, the shockwave travels through the bedrock, through the cast-iron flanges, through my rubbery rind, and into the mud. I compress by half a millimeter. My fluids shift. The sulfur-yeast inside me pulses. 

And then the rocker rises again, the vacuum draws, and I expand back against the warm, sweating steel, waiting for the next stroke.
