I would not be at the top, where the mist is still wild and lacks the rhythm of the drops, nor down in the black sumps where the eel-skinners work in waist-deep brine with their eyes rolled back. 

I would be halfway down, at the ninety-span mark, where the cleft narrows to the width of an outstretched arm and the stone is hard, purple ironstone that does not crumble under the copper.

I am a fitter of thresholds. 

***

My hands are thick and broad, the nails worn down to flat, yellow horn from pressing cold grey silt into the joints between living rock and boiled bone. My fingers are short, but they can feel the difference between a fracture that will hold for three generations and one that will weep silt in twenty days. 

To live here is not to sit; it is to remain wedged. Right now, I have my left heel locked into a natural cleft of manganese crust, my knee braced against the underside of an elder’s sill, and my back pressed flat to the damp, greasy shale. My skin is greased with eel-tallow from the crown of my head to the webbing between my toes, and the mist rolls off my chest in clear, heavy beads that tickle as they drop into the dark between my thighs.

Before me is a gap where the water has chewed out a soft pocket of shale beside a sleeping trough. The woman who slept there died four mist-turns ago; her flesh went to the eel-cages below, her tallow is burning right now in the niche three paces to my left, and her bones have just come up from the brine-troughs on the lower terrace, bleached as white and dense as river-flint.

Across the narrow drop—scarcely two forearm-lengths away—Keth sits in his doorway. His flat, grey-white face catches the needle of yellow flame from my lamp. He is not speaking, because the roar of the three-tier cascade five spans below us would drown any sound an open mouth could make. Instead, he holds his palm pressed flat against the rock face beside his knee. 

I reach across the void. I do not have to stretch far; our knuckles brush in the wet air. I place my palm against the same slab of ironstone on his side. 

Through the rock, through the fat of his palm and into the bone of mine, he hums: a low, dense vibration born deep in the chest, below the diaphragm. *T-k-cht. T-k-cht.* 

*The drop is heavy from above,* the pulse says through our joined bones. *The gutter is filling.*

I tap back twice with the bone clacker strapped to my thumb: *Sharp. Sharp. I have the hip ready.*

***

I take the woman’s pelvic plate from my sinew harness. It has been boiled clean, scraped of every gristle-thread with an antler-burr, and soaked in the lime-run until it has turned into cold ceramic. It is wide, slightly scooped, with a natural flare that catches water better than any split slate ever could.

With my copper chisel, I nick two small grooves into the rim where the sinew bindings will seat. *Tock. Tock. Tock.* The sound does not travel as speech; it joins the great, wet engine of the fissure, swallowed immediately by the hiss of the falling mist and the clatter of twenty other chisels working three terraces above.

I lay a bed of the grey silt mortar into the cut I’ve made in the shale. It is cold, heavy with iron, smelling of old mud and crushed midges. I press the bone into it. It seats with a soft, sucking sound. 

I lean over the void to test the fall. A thin, clear rope of seepage—the run-off from the family cells above—catches the lip of the pelvic plate, swirls once in the shallow basin where her leg once turned in its socket, and shoots out cleanly into the center of the chasm, falling clear of the door-sill below into the empty air. 

No splash against the masonry. No wash against the bedding trough. The sleepers below will stay dry in their troughs, their chin-rests clear of the film.

Keth watches from his sill. He snaps his thumb clacker once: a dry, bone-on-bone *clack* that cuts the roar like a needle. *Good.*

***

When the shift is done, I do not travel far. There is nowhere to travel that is not the same face of stone, tilted ninety degrees toward the deep pools. 

I unclip my waist-line from the safety-ring, slide my spatulate toes along the slick bone rungs of the ladder, and step into my own niche. It is four spans above Keth’s, no deeper than the length of my spine. 

I wipe the fresh water from my brow with the heel of my palm, then dip two fingers into the grease-pot by the lamp and rub it deep into the skin behind my ears and down the thick ridge of my neck. Where the grease wears thin, the cold bites like copper teeth; where the grease is thick, the cold is just a smooth weight, like an eel sliding past in the dark.

I eat from the day’s cake: pressed midge-paste, cold and oily, with a strip of cured white eel-meat that takes twenty minutes of heavy chewing to soften. I follow it with a horn-spoon of the green curd from the flowstone sill outside my door. It bubbles on my tongue, sour and sharp, numbing the roof of my mouth and filling the back of my throat with the cold taste of wet limestone and iron. 

Then I blow out the needle of yellow flame to save the fat.

In the dark, the city does not disappear. It turns pale green. 

The bacterial crust along the gutters and sills outlines every terrace, every ladder, every door-frame in a soft, cold phosphorescence. Across the narrow gulf, I can see the green-lit silhouette of Keth’s knees drawn up to his chin, his large, flat pupils reflecting the faint light back at me like two damp pennies. 

Below us, two hundred spans down, the deep black pools are silent, but the mist keeps falling through the green-lit air, a endless rain of glass beads drifting past our thresholds into the belly of the earth.

I lie back in my stone trough. I pull the heavy, wet-slick eel skin up to my collarbone, rest my chin on the smooth limestone lip that keeps the floor-wash from my mouth, and let my heels lock into the notch at the foot of the bed.

The rock against my back is cold, hard, and infinite. Through it, I can feel every hammer in the fissure: the steady *tock-tock-tock* of a father widening a nursery niche sixty spans above, the rhythmic thrum of the trap-tenders hauling wicker cylinders from the black water below, and the low, collective breathing of three thousand people clamped to the shale like limpets.

I close my eyes. There is no tomorrow to wait for, no morning that will change the color of the rock, no wind that will come to dry our skin. There is only the grip: the toe in the notch, the bone in the silt, the grease on the shoulder, and the water falling true through the spout, precisely where it was told to fall.
