I would not be in the air with the kites, nor would I be anchored like the limpets to the sheer basalt face, though I would understand the pull of both. 

To find myself here, I would have to be somewhere in the middle tier—in the warm, roaring twilight beneath the overhanging flare of the low-pressure steam flues, where the iron gangways are permanently wet and the air smells like boiled marrow, copper salts, and rain.

At first, I might feel less like a person and more like a point of convergence for the vibrations. 

The pulse comes up through the soles of the feet before it reaches the ears: a steady, double-beat from the subterranean piston well—*thump-drag, thump-drag*—thirty-four strokes to the minute, slow enough to match a resting heart. My heels would know the rhythm intimately, knowing by a fractional hesitation in the iron plate when a tallow-valve has jammed three levels down or when a cluster of plate-lice has choked an intake screen on the canal floor.

My body would have settled into the demands of this atmosphere. Short-limbed, broad across the shoulder blades, with a rib cage built like a cask to draw meaning from the light, warm air. The skin of my hands and arms would not be soft; it would be thick, yellowish, and dense, coated in the thin, waxy film that the body secretes against the acidic dew. It is a skin that never fully dries, but never prunes, slick enough that the red sulfur-water runs off it in clear streams without stinging the pores.

I wear the stiff hide apron of a slaughtered trough-hog, heavy with rendered grease, smelling of wet leather and river-gravel. The apron is pinned at the collar with a tapered bronze bolt and weighted at the hem with lead slugs that knock against my shins—a dull, grounding clatter that keeps the hem from lifting when the lowland thermals tear upward through the floor-gratings.

My work is quiet, tactile, and rhythmic.

In the morning—when the sun hits the high copper arches and turns the weeping verdigris to a blinding, chemical turquoise—I walk the mid-level raceways with a curved scraping iron and a brass-headed striking hammer hung from a braided leather baldric. 

I do not govern the machine, and I do not fight the animals. I am part of the clearance.

Where the plate-lice have formed a living, clacking pavement too thick over the expansion joints, I wedge the tip of the iron beneath their carapaces. They come away with a tearing, suction sound, like wet slate lifted from clay, tumbling into the wicker basket on my hip with a hollow, dry rattle. A few steps later, I pause where a steam line passes through a basalt bulkhead. The joint is weeping white tallow; I catch a handful of the warm grease on the heel of my palm, thumbing it directly into the open bronze oil-well of a rocker hinge. The hinge drinks it with a wet sigh, its stroke smoothing out instantly.

The shelf-kites roost directly above my station, under the warm, rib-vaulted eaves of the flue-shrouds. They know my shape. When I pass underneath, their oily, plum-colored wings shift with a sound like dry parchment being crumpled, and their sharp, clicking calls fall through the steam like thrown gravel. Occasionally, a young one, heavy with canal-worms and unable to catch the updraft, drops to the walkway beside me. It sits panting, its pale throat fluttering against the wet iron, eye-blisters milky in the glare. I don’t pet it—there is no sentiment for that here—but I nudge it with the blunt end of my scraping iron toward the edge of the grate, where the warm draft from the cooling tower catches its wing-flaps and lifts it out into the canyon-street.

My voice would be almost completely silent. When you spend a lifetime between a roaring sluice and a beating engine, vocal cords become a luxury for private humming. 

Instead, my thoughts would be translated into the brass hammer. When the mid-level reservoir fills to the overflow mark, I swing the hammer against the web of the third upright girder. *Clang—clang-clang.* The metal rings with a pure, sustain-heavy bell tone that travels through the steel skeleton of the plateau, past the roosting kites and the grazing lice, straight down to the Tenders at the sluice-wheels below. A heartbeat later, two short vibrations hum back through the soles of my feet through the iron floor: *Acknowledged. Opening.*

By midday, when the sun stands directly over the plateau and the basalt shelves bake until they steam like fresh bread, I sit in the dry cavity behind the piston-sleeve of the main lift pump. 

It is my shelter—a nook carved out between the stone footing and the vibrating cylinder casing, lined with dried mats of pressed glider-hide. Here, the noise is not a roar but a heavy, enveloping womb of sound. The air is dry and tastes of warm mineral dust. 

I eat there with my fingers: cold, boiled limpet meat that I broke from the aqueduct wall yesterday, tough as salted rubber, tasting of brine and sulfur, followed by a handful of dried fat-worms skimmed from the tallow-traps. I wash it down with condensation caught in a copper cup suspended beneath a clean steam weep-hole—water that is absolutely pure, scalding hot, and mineral-flat.

While I chew, I look down through the gap in the ironwork. 

Hundreds of feet below, the trough-hogs are wallowing in the main drainage canal, their brown, boot-leather backs breaking the surface of the broth-colored runoff like rounded boulders. Above them, through the shimmering heat of the vents, the shelf-kites wheel in endless, patient gyres, their silhouettes sharp against the pale, unbroken blue.

I find myself here not as a master of this place, nor as an exile from some lost green world I have never seen, but as an integral cell in its metabolism. The machines were not built for me, and I was not built for them; we have simply grown into one another across such a vast expanse of time that the distinction has worn away, rounded like the basalt cobbles beneath the spillways.

When my strength finally goes, decades from now, my companions will not dig a grave in the hard basalt. They will slide my body into the tallow-traps beneath the primary rocker arms, where the grease will be rendered down to oil the brass slides, and my bones will sink to the bottom of the canals to be crushed by the trough-hogs and returned to the stone. 

It is a complete, unhurried world. And as the afternoon light turns the copper flues from turquoise to deep, bruised liver, I lean back against the vibrating iron of the cylinder, take a breath of the warm, wet air, and listen to the plateau beat on.
