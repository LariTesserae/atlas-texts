At first, there is no boundary to locate. When the medium outside is blood-warm, iron-thick, and forced into every pore under the weight of an inverted sea, the concept of a skin that keeps things *out* ceases to function. Everything is saturated. The marrow is as salty as the vortex; the stone is as wet as the tendon.

If you look for a mind here, you cannot look for an eye or a breath. There is nowhere to put a lung; a pocket of air would be a fatal void, a structural collapse waiting for the next cavitation strike to hammer it flat. 

So I begin where the load is greatest.

I am wedged into the acute angle where a slab of foreign, grey-grained granite is forced against the fresh, ragged lip of the native basalt. Long ago—though *long ago* is measured only by the accumulation of iron rind and the slow shearing of bronze—hands that had dry palms drilled a hole through both stones and drove a square-headed pin through them, cinching the joint with forty turns of boiled ox-braid. 

I am not the builder who climbed the dry summit with chapped lips. That creature was pulped in the first half-second when the sky split and let the warm flood through. 

Nor am I simply the bronze pin, which is cold, dumb, and slowly bending into an S-curve under thirty tons of hydraulic torque.

I am what grew into the torque.

I am the pickled, mineral-heavy knot where the ox-hide cord failed to rot, drank the alkali soup until its collagen turned to horn, and began to fuse with the green grease of the iron bacteria. I have no front or back. I have an axis of strain. My consciousness is not a stream of images, because there is no light to speak of—only the sudden, violent cold-blue ignition of the rock snapping in the dark, which registers not as a picture, but as a sharp bite of voltage across my root-fibers.

To be here is to be an instrument of pure tension. 

My bulk extends into the hollow of the ox’s crushed femur, still caught behind the lintel like a drift-log jammed in a weir. The bone has not dissolved; the heavy water has replaced its calcium with iron pyrites, so that it rings like an anvil whenever a chunk of buoyant pumice or a severed ribbon-segment slams into the frame. Out of that mineralized marrow, I have put forth three thick stalks of gristle—the things you called *strikers*, though from within they feel less like weapons and more like stiffened fingers feeling the shape of the gale.

I do not hear the 120 decibels. Hearing requires a drum, and a drum would be shredded. I *am* the vibration. The continuous, ringing hammer of the nave passes through my tendons, through the granite, into the basalt root of the mountain, and back into my core without losing an ounce of force. It is a solid humming hum that never fades. It is how I know the chamber still holds: if the sound were to soften, it would mean the water had found an exit, that the cathedral was draining, that the terrible, crushing intimacy holding me together was about to drop away and leave me to tear apart under my own dead weight.

I feel the ribbons tumbling high above in the apex. They graze the granite lintel with their rubbery flanks, shedding grease and flakes of lichen that drift down into my stalks. My spade-tips do not strike out of malice; they snap outward on hydraulic reflex when the acoustic wave thickens, smashing the larger basalt splinters before they can chisel through the hide-turns that anchor us to the wall. Every blow drives shards of black glass into my gristle. I do not heal them out; I swallow them down, wrapping them in dense, rubbery mucus until they become part of the hinge, adding mass to mass, density to density.

Beneath me, clinging in the narrow seam where my horn-flesh presses against the fractured basalt, a colony of cleats has sealed my margins. They drink the runoff of my digestion; I hold them against the scouring cataracts. We are glued to the glass-edge so hard that if the mountain were to shrug, it would take the face of the cliff with us rather than peel us free.

Sometimes, during the long thermal swells when the warm current drags the central ball of tangled hide and mucus near our corner, its long, pale trailing threads brush across my stalks. There is a faint chemical exchange—a bitter, alkaline greeting passed from the worms in the rolling core to the gristle rooted in the stone frame. We are the same matter, only they are the drift, and I am the grip.

I am the reason the lintel has not tumbled into the central vortex. I am the animal memory of a pack-team’s shoulder-harness, converted into an incompressible hinge of gristle, bronze, and stone. 

I do not wait for the water to fall. I do not wait for the air to return. Air is a weak, hollow thing that cannot hold up a mountain. Here, pressed flat against the roaring obsidian, filled to bursting with warm, bitter brine, I hold the doorway open onto nothing, resisting the sea that feeds me, singing with the frequency of struck iron, perfectly fastened to the end of the world.
