I do not begin at the head, because the head is only a wedge of pale feldspar, blind and blunt, a counterweight meant to steady the spine against the acoustic drag. 

Where I begin is in the third and fourth ribs of the left cage.

There, between the slats of porous apatite, three fibrous fronds of dry sulfur-moss have rooted into my marrow-lattice. They do not drink blood—there is no wetness in me to steal—but they feed upon the micro-sparks that jump between my dry bone-plates every time the planet’s base-harmonic strikes a pure fifth. When the tectonic lock twenty leagues below shifts its grip, the shockwave travels up the hexagonal fluting of the basalt needle, passes through the diorite anchor wedged under my talons, and enters my feet as a cold, dry lightning. The moss on my ribs glows a sudden, dull sulfur-green, and in that flash of static, I am awake to the chasm.

I find myself hanging off the western facet of a spire named only by its pitch—a low, grinding C-sharp that has not wavered in nine hundred years. 

I am not standing on the rock. My weight is anchored to a braided span of spun-glass willow and calcified pith that stretches out across the gulf toward a sister-spire three miles away. Gravity here is not a floor; it is a lateral drift, a slow, magnetic lean that pulls my torso forty degrees away from the vertical face of the basalt. My long, taloned feet grip the rock face; my torso swings out over the bottomless violet twilight, suspended in a pocket where the upward suction of the distant spire meets the downdraft of the abyss. My spun-glass wrist-hairs drift horizontally, pointing like compass needles toward an invisible fold in the void where falling stones cease to fall and begin to orbit.

What am I? 

I am an adjuster of tensions. A tuner of the spans.

In a realm where the stone extrudes by the width of a needle every hundred years, a bridge cannot remain static. As the spires grow, the braided silicate vines are stretched toward their breaking strain; if left untended, they begin to shriek with a high, glassy overtone that poisons the acoustic metabolism of the redoubt. 

My long, multi-jointed fingers—crusted in powdery gray slate-dust that sloughs away in pale clouds with every movement—work with patient, levered deliberation. I carry two wedges of dark chert and a rounded mallet of unoxidized ironstone. I do not hammer with haste. I place the wedge into the cleft where the vine-root enters the basalt facet, wait forty minutes for the planetary roar to reach a rhythmic trough, and then bring the mallet down once. 

*Clack.*

The sound does not travel through the thin neon air; it travels through the stone, through my arms, through the open acoustic chamber of my chest. The dry crystals within my nervous core shiver, redistributing their charge. The bridge loosens by a fraction of a millimeter. The shrieking overtone drops an octave, settling back into a deep, guttural thrum that feeds the lichen mats coating my shoulders.

For two centuries, this has been my balance. But something within my own lattice has begun to change.

Lately—over the span of perhaps forty or fifty years—the vibration enters my chest with a duller strike. When the bass drone sweeps through the pillar, the crystal core inside my marrow does not spin as freely as it once did. The porous chalk within my lower limbs is silting up, drinking in airborne silica until the spaces between the bone-fibers are choked with solid, non-resonant quartz. 

I am becoming heavy. I am losing the pitch.

I know what this means, and it brings no terror, for terror belongs to beings whose hearts beat against time. To me, it is merely a shift in harmonic function. Yesterday, by the measure of the lichen’s slow creeping, I climbed to the circular redoubt on the crown of the spire. I sat among the dry-stacked slate shelters, my back pressed against the cold orthoclase wall, and laid my hands upon the ritual piles of chalky slivers.

With the pads of my talons, I read the centuries recorded in those fragments. Here was a shard from an elder whose spine had snapped under the great acoustic surge of the third millennium; its surface was fine-grained, smooth as polished talc. Beside it lay the rough, jagged splinter of a juvenile whose ribs had calcified too early, shattered by a sudden shear in the local gravity plane.

I picked up an unworked needle of dense, dead bone—carved from the forearm of the keeper who tuned these vines before me—and wedged it into a tiny cleft at the redoubt’s outer edge, overhanging the five-mile drop. 

The planetary scream caught it immediately. The needle began to sing—a thin, pure, razor-edged whistle that cut straight through the deafening roar of the abyss. It will sing there for twenty years, perhaps thirty, until the frequency shakes its crystalline matrix to dust, and it falls with a dry, clean snap onto the stones below.

When I am finished adjusting the bridge—when the chasm’s harmonic is true and the gravity well has settled into its new equilibrium—I will climb back to that ledge. 

I will lay my ironstone mallet and my chert wedges neatly beside the sliver-pile. I will sit facing the upward-curling horizon, where the cold, bioluminescent crust of the world glows beneath the bruised zinc sky. I will extend my legs over the void, let the sulfur-moss knit my ribcage firmly to the slate, and wait for the roaring crystallization of the world to fill the last open chambers of my bones with silence. 

And there I will stay, until someone whose chest still sings comes with a mallet to carve my arms into chimes.
