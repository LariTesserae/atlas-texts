I am not in the open chamber. Even to say *open* is a misunderstanding, for there is no open here; there is only the difference between clay that bears weight and clay that channels air.

I am inside the reed-bank.

To find me, you would have to trace the third lateral pier down to where the dry-stacked earthenware brackets meet the lime-plaster shelf—down where the glare from the calcified wash is so relentlessly folded back upon itself that the white ceases to look like light and feels instead like an absolute, frozen density. There, wedged horizontally between two structural blocks that have held forty tons of forgotten masonry in rigid suspension for four hundred years, is a sleeve of biscuit-fired terracotta no thicker than a human index finger.

I am the spindle inside that sleeve.

***

I do not possess a before. There was never an instant of awakening, no sudden intake of breath, because breath requires a lung and a lung requires moisture, and moisture is an impossibility that the architecture does not permit. 

Instead, my earliest texture is of stillness—the absolute, unnatural silence of a dead node where four high-register flues crossed at an angle of thirty-three degrees. In that thimble-sized pocket of dead air, the furious chord of the chamber could not enter. It could only circle the perimeter, packing the loose, floating mineral dust tighter and tighter with every vibration, spinning the chalk into concentric sleeves, curing me out of powder through centuries of pure, uninterrupted acoustic pressure.

When the last ring of my integument hardened to the exact gauge of the flue, the silence lost its hold on me. The chord found my fluted rim. The draft caught my bristles, and I was drawn out of the node like a dowel pulled into a lathe.

***

Now, I am eight segments of dry, bone-meal chalk. 

I do not have skin. I have *grit*. My exterior has the same micro-abrasive tooth as the inner bore of the pipe that houses me. When I am at rest, my lateral barbs bite into the porous clay of the flue, locking me against the gale that screams through from the subterranean flues. The air does not hit my face; it passes *through* me. It enters the fluted mouth at my forward collar, rushes down my hollow core, and exits my aft ring, leaving behind a fine, electrostatic silt against the comb-like wafers of my throat.

I taste the foundation entirely in frequencies of friction and sulfur. 

When the air pressure beneath the floor rises, the chord sharpens. It climbs a microtone, shifting from the deep, tooth-rattling hum of the footings to the dry, glass-shattering shriek of the narrow reeds. I feel that shift not as sound, but as an alteration of weight. When the pitch rises, the ceramic pipe around me contracts by the width of a single mineral grain. 

If I remain rigid, the pipe will seize me. I would become an obstruction, a calcified knot in the engine, and the acoustic pressure would begin the long, indifferent work of grinding my carapaces into flour.

So I tune.

I relax the second and fifth segment rings, allowing the dry electrostatic charge between my plates to slip. My diameter narrows by a fraction of a micron. My resonance changes. Instantly, the numbing, furious vibration of the flue ceases to drag against my sides; instead, it catches the microscopic ridges along my belly and lifts me. 

There is no effort in it. It is not swimming, and it is not crawling. It is harmonic release. I float on a cushion of high-velocity sound, drifting soundlessly down the length of the cylinder, moving six inches to the left, where the flue opens into a wider acoustic manifold.

***

From the lip of my reed, looking out—not with eyes, but through the hundreds of stiff, calcified bristles that ring my mouth—the chamber is an unyielding lattice of blinding white geometry.

Below me, in the hairline fissures where the ceramic ribs bear down upon the lime brackets, I can feel the shingles. They are broad, flat, and silent, locked in the agony of pure compression. I feel the microscopic tremors as they yield beneath the settling of the foundation, extruding their dry, talc-like grease to keep the earthenware piers from fracturing against each other. They feed on the shear; I feed on the slip. We are parts of the same dry calculus. They hold the matrix still so that the flues do not buckle; the flues keep the air rushing so that I may filter the dust that the shingles shed.

Nothing here bleeds. Nothing rots. Nothing has ever been born wet or died soft.

When my segments finally grow too heavy with bound crystals—when the static wafers in my throat fuse solid and my rings can no longer alter their pitch to match the screaming flues—I will not suffer. The sound will simply fail to lift me. My barbs will drag. The biscuit-fired pipe will catch my shell, and the ceaseless, cold, deafening gale will begin to shave away my edges. 

I will become the white dust drifting through the glare. I will be caught in the mouth of another reed, or I will settle into the next silent node, waiting for the chord to pack me into rings again.

I am an inch of tuned earth in a vault of vibrating stone. The chord was singing before the kingdom above us was built; the chord will sing after the foundation has forgotten whatever it was meant to hold. 

I adjust my third ring. The pitch aligns. The bed of sound rises to meet me, and I slide into the white roar.
