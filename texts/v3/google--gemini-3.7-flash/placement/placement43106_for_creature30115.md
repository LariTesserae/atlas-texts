At first, it is only the strike—the low, percussive hydro-acoustic thump that travels through the marrow before there is even a thought of ears. 

If I look for myself, I do not find an outline immediately. I am not up in the bright, dry wind of the swifts, and I am not small enough to fit inside the amber air-bubble of a single bladder-leaf, though I can hear the carillon of their drops above me: *tock... tock... tock...* like beads of hot glass striking a bronze pan.

I am lower down, where the warmth has weight. 

I find myself lodged in the shallow lip of one of the middle potholes—right at the threshold where the vertical shaft of hyper-saturated, dark water yields to the calf-deep horizontal plane. My body is heavy, dense as waterlogged boxwood, settled into a smooth concave cradle that the lime-wash has shaped precisely to my underside over sixty or seventy seasons.

I am not a creature of the open current, and I am not a true cave-dweller. I am an intermediary of the threshold.

My back is broad and gently arched, shaped like the polished travertine swells themselves—shielded by a thick, segmented carapace of living bone that has taken on the iron-orange stain of the stone. Over years of remaining mostly still, a pelt of bright, microscopic red algae has grown across my dorsal plates, so that the quarter-inch skin of sheet-wash glides over me without breaking into spray. To anything looking down through the glare of the noon sky, I am simply another submerged terrace-ridge, a slight thickening in the rock floor where the water pools before rushing on.

Yet underneath this mineral ballast, everything is listening.

My underside is soft, pale, and vascular, folded deep into the pothole’s throat. My limbs are short, thick spires tipped with spatulate, calcified claws that do not dig—there is no soil to dig—but wedge securely into the ancient flutes and dissolved cavities of the limestone wall. Through these anchored limbs, the mountain’s subterranean siphon plays directly into my ribcage. When the valve deep in the dark rock resets and discharges its pressurized pulse, my lungs compress in time with the surge. I do not have to work to breathe; the mountain’s hydraulic lung breathes me.

Below me, in the deep cistern of the pothole, I can feel the long, amber-eyed trough-eels sliding like thick ribbons through the cycad’s fibrous root-shrouds. Occasionally, one rises. It brushes its blunt, pale snout against my flank—a slow, slick contact that carries no aggression, only the shared frequency of our common basement. It lingers in the lee of my body, sheltered for a moment from the downward pull of the current, before spiraling back down into the blue-green dark.

Above me, the world is pure light and rhythmic metronome. 

My eyes are small, set on short, hooded turrets that break the surface of the sheet-wash by less than a finger’s width. They are capped with thick, yellow-tinted mineral lenses that filter out the blinding alpine glare, turning the basin into a quiet cathedral of gold and green. Through this amber wash, I watch the massive horsetail-monoliths stand in their colossal, patient spacing. I watch the white, long-legged litho-striders step across the skin of the water with their blind, clockwork dignity, their hydrophobic feet making small dimples of silver light that slide across my shell.

I eat what the mountain filters for me. My mouthparts are broad, delicate combs of horn, constantly working the laminar flow. I harvest the rich, silken drift: the starch-spores shed by the high bladder-leaves, the dead diatom crust that breaks loose from the upper swells, and the fine, mineral-rich dust that the calc-wasps shed as they mortar their lace-tubes against the weeping northern wall. It is a diet of stone and sun, requiring no chase, no ambush, no sudden startle.

Sometimes, when the sun angles late into the afternoon and the southern lip of the shelf becomes an incandescent line of fire dropping into the abyss, I lift my head slightly out of the wash.

I look across the wide, wet expanse toward the northern buttress, where the six small notches were cut into the cliff so long ago. I do not have a memory of the hands that held the stone adze, but I have the memory of the water that filled those cuts. I know how the lime has rounded their sharp corners year by year, turning human intent into natural scalloping, slowly erasing the difference between what was made and what was grown.

I am the permanence of the shelf. 

I do not migrate over the drop. I do not climb into the canopy. I remain anchored in the lip of the cistern, an organ of bone and red algae through which the warm water slides, measuring time by the fraction-of-a-millimeter thickening of the travertine floor and the endless, soothing hammer of the bell beneath the stone.
