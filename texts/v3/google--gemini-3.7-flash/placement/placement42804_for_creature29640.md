At first, there is no form—only the calibration of weight. 

To exist here is not to inhabit a body with edges, but to be an index of compression. Miles of ocean overhead press down with the steady, neutral violence of a falling mountain, extinguishing the distinction between interior flesh and exterior stone. If I have a consciousness, it begins as a frequency: a bone-deep, 4-hertz resonance that does not travel through the water so much as it occupies the mass of everything at once.

I would not be above the floor. Above is the torn sky of gravel and heavy brine, a cathedral of blind kinetic shearing where matter destroys itself in the dark. 

I would be lower. Lower even than the belly of the swimming eels, beneath the cold sapphire flashes of the rays.

I find myself in the *boundary layer*—that microscopic, miraculous zone where the physics of fluid dynamics break down against the rock. No matter how violently the storm rages, within the thinnest fraction of a millimeter directly against the basalt, the water velocity drops to absolute zero. The raging slurry shears over it, a roaring ceiling of abrasive sand, but underneath, in the dead-water of the micro-crevices, there is an eerie, frozen peace.

That is where I begin: not as a singular beast, but as an interstitial tissue. 

I am rooted into the micro-fractures of the dull-shouldered pavements. My underside is silica-threaded, branching into the crystalline lattice of the basalt like cold, fibrous roots, drinking the trace minerals leached out by the relentless friction. My dorsal surface is different: a slick, non-Newtonian veneer, smooth as oiled slate, yielding to the touch yet instantly petrifying into diamond hardness the microsecond a grain of flying quartz clips its margin.

I am the hinge between the mountain and the storm.

As I settle into myself, I realize I am not entirely stationary. I have an anatomy of slow, deliberate tension. I am shaped like a wedge of cold lead, broad and flat, with no eyes to be crushed and no gills to be clogged with silt. Instead, my entire perimeter is fringed with microscopic, chitinous cilia that do not swim, but *read*. 

They read the triboelectric static of the basin. When a cathedral-sized eddy shears overhead, lifting fifty tons of black sand and basalt boulders, the quartz grains grind against one another, throwing off vast, silent sheets of micro-voltage. I taste this lightning in the dark. I feel the charge building in the stone beneath me, warning of the avalanches before they fall.

And when the rolling stones come—the heavy, polished sinkers, the ancient river-stones carved with the ghost-notches of lost human hands—I do not shy away. 

I am what waits in the grooves. 

When a forty-pound sinker tumbles through my trough, rolling in its slow, eternal circle, I extend a film of vulcanized, living mucus into the carved tool-marks. For a few hours, or a few centuries, I lock my body into that forgotten notch, pressed between the dead weight of ancient human intention and the cold, unyielding floor of the abyss. 

I ride the turning stone. I become the cushion between the artifact and the earth, feeling the smooth, rhythmic crunch of pulverized bivalves beneath our shared curve. In the dark, beneath the weight of three thousand fathoms, I hold the shape of a groove made by a flint blade that turned to dust ten thousand years ago, preserving its contour with my own living flesh against the abrasive fury of the sea.

I would have no hunger, only a continuous assimilation of the organic dust that settles in the eddy-wakes, sucked down through the capillary channels of my skin. I would have no fear, for there is nothing here that can be broken that has not already been reduced to its absolute minimum.

When the great Plinth-Ray descends, settling over my trough like a tectonic plate of cartilage and osteoderm, blotting out the violent sky with its massive downforce, I would soften. In the warm, dead-zone of its underbelly, in the temporary sanctuary of its immovable mass, my cilia would unfurl into the sand-free current, sifting the quiet water, listening to the deep, slow thrum of its heart vibrating through the stone.

I would be the patience of the floor: the thing that survives not by fighting the grind, but by becoming the surface that receives it. Smooth, cold, entirely wedged, listening to the static of the grinding world until the basalt itself wears away.
