At first, it is not a body with edges. It is only the timing.

A low, subterranean draw—five minutes of silence gathering weight, the water collecting in the joint-lines of the rock, the velvet hush of the moths drinking the roar of the cataracts—and then the *thud*. The shudder that travels up through three thousand vertical meters of basalt, lifting the iron plate half an inch, boiling the sump, driving the wet air up into the white sky.

To exist here is to be counted by that stroke. If I try to locate myself by sight, there is only the luminous glare of atomized mist, where iron-rust turns the sunlight into a persistent, suspended tea-color. If I try to locate myself by voice, there is nothing; the sound is swallowed before it leaves the throat, muffled by the damp moss that carpets every surface that isn't boiling.

So I find myself not in the open air, where the Vapour-Vanes drift like lanterns, nor in the black, drowned sumps where the Strokers feed upon the surge.

I am wedged into the hinge.

***

There is a place, midway up the sheer face of a pillar named for its oxidized color—burnt umber, scored with blue runs of copper wash—where an articulated rocker beam, fifty paces long and cast from porous green iron, meets the native stone. 

The machine did not finish its motion when it was abandoned; it settled into a permanent, straining angle. The iron trunnion, thick as an oak and scarred by centuries of scale, rests in a socket carved half out of dark basalt and half out of a lead-poured sleeve. It does not rotate freely anymore. It rocks only three inches with every pulse of the subterranean cylinders—three inches forward as the steam swells, three inches back as the condensation drains.

That is where I am. I am the thing that has grown into that three-inch clearance.

My body is not entirely meat, nor entirely the mineral sludge that crusts the collar. It is something intermediate, born of the lardaceous grease that once packed the bearings and the spore-rich silt washed down from the hanging sphagnum. 

I have the broad, flattened symmetry of a creature made to withstand compressive tonnage. My hide is thick, layered like wet boot-leather, pale gray where it touches the rock and stained a deep, saturated ochre where the iron weeps directly onto my flank. I have no true legs; instead, my undercarriage is a dense, fibrous pad of cartilaginous ridges that lock into the fluting of the iron journal. When the rocker arm leans down, thousands of tons of wet metal press into my dorsal plate. It does not crush me; my tissues are non-compressible, hydraulic, dense with heavy animal oils that displace the pressure into the stone.

I am the living packing of the engine.

***

My senses do not reach out into the sky. The bright, blinding white above is only a temperature to me—a warm bath that keeps my upper skin soft enough for the liverwort to root. 

Instead, my awareness is internal and downward:

I feel the water arriving through the flues long before it breaks into steam. I can feel the change in viscosity when a bog high above tears away and sends its peaty soup into the drainage shafts. Through the soles of my anchor-pads, the acoustic pits in my rib-plates read the heartbeat of the range: the Strokers turning in the flooded pipes below, their broad flanks brushing the brass liners; the Rivet-Clamps popping their seals in unison three hundred feet down the cliff face; the subtle, rhythmic slackening of the iron struts as the thermal column shifts two degrees to the east.

Every few minutes, the stroke begins.

I feel it first as an acoustic tension in the basalt—a tightening of the mountain’s knees. 

Then comes the heat. A scalding tongue of water sheets across my back, rich with sulfur and manganese. My steam-gills, tucked beneath the thick leather margin of my carapace, flare open to receive it. The water tastes of ancient boilers, of lime-scale and powdered rust, with the faint, sweet decay of fern-fronds that have rotted under pressure. I drink the heat; my blood, cold and viscous during the interval, thins and races through the copper-bright vascular fans that spread across my shoulders.

Then the weight descends. 

The rocker shifts. The green iron groans, a wet, metallic bellow that the moths instantly smother into a dull click, and the trunnion rolls into me. The pressure is immense, ecstatic. It drives the air from my inner cavities in a long, bubbling sigh that joins the steam venting from the rock seams. For four seconds, five seconds, I am welded to the mountain by the full mechanical momentum of the forgotten engine. 

And then the valve clears. The rocker tilts back with a wet, sucking gasp. The pressure lifts. The water drains through the fluted channels of my belly, cooler now, carrying away the shed layers of my skin to feed the blind crabs that wait in the moss-cushions beneath my ledge.

***

I do not wander. Why would I move across the vertical face when the entire world is brought to me by the draft and the drainage?

The Cradle-Walkers pass overhead sometimes, hanging beneath the rusted wire-ropes like hairy seed-pods, their prehensile tails leaving greasy smears on the iron. Sometimes one of their young, blind and wet from the bog on its mother’s belly, will drop through the mist and land upon my shelf. It will stay there for a month, feeding on the chemotrophic slime that grows in the warm crease between my neck and the lead bearing, before finding the grip to climb back into the lianas.

Sometimes a Porous Moth, heavy with moisture, settles upon my eye-shield. I do not blink it away. Its water-logged wings tremble against my hide, vibrating with the same five-minute cycle that governs my blood. Through its sodden velvet, the roar of the chasm is reduced to the sound of oil moving through a narrow pipe.

I am content in the joint. 

I am not the engine, which has no mind and cannot feel how well it fits the mountain. And I am not the mountain, which is indifferent to the heat running through its veins. 

I am the friction that has learned to breathe. I am the soft thing between two hard truths, keeping the iron from grinding itself to dust against the stone, rocking three inches forward, three inches back, while the sky stays white and the world never dries.
