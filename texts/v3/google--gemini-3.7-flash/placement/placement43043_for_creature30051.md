At first, there is only the sensation of weight—not the weight of an atmosphere pressing down, but of blood having to be pulled through the chest by deliberate, unhurried strokes of the heart. The air does not fill the lungs unasked; you must reach out with the ribs and take it.

I would not be in the sky with the lammergeiers, though I watch their diamond tails cut the thin blue. Nor would I be down in the low dark of the oxen-pens, where the steam is so thick it tastes of hay and cud. 

I find myself on the mid-tiers, in the damp, sun-blind crease where a thousand courses of dry slate lean back against the sheared basalt of the mountain’s true wall.

My hands are old, though age here is hard to measure by anything other than the thickening of the knuckles and the deep, gray cracks in the palms that never quite bleed because the cold seals them instantly. My fingers are permanently darkened around the nails with a paste of charred juniper, soot, and mutton tallow. In my lap rests a flat hide apron, stiff as bark, holding three tools: a soft-headed mallet carved from the dense base of an ox horn, an iron-pointed chisel no longer than my thumb, and a flat horn scraper worn thin at the lip.

I am a *chink-mender*, or what the terrace hums refer to as a *gap-listener*.

The city looks permanent from across the gulfs, like a crust of salt on a shelf. But it is always moving. Every night, the needle-frost drives its microscopic wedges into the vertical joints; every noon, the thin thaw trickles down, loosening a pinch of clay, softening a wedge of dry moss. If a wall settles three inches to the left, the six houses stacked upon its spine will pinch their lintels, shatter their slate shingle roofs, and spill their warmth into the gulf.

My work is slow, low to the stone, performed entirely in the *slate-step*—shuffling sideways along ledges barely wider than my sheepskin boots, my belly pressed flat against the outer skin of the masonry.

I press my ear to the cold stone. Through the slate flags, I can hear the whole anatomy of the terrace:
Beneath my feet, the deep, rhythmic grinding of an ox chewing dry scrub, its ribs bumping the interior timbers with a hollow *thud*.
Above me, the faint, dry scuffle of a mountain weasel hunting voles through the wall’s hollow core.
And deeper still, behind the human masonry, the mountain itself: a high, glassy pinging sound, like iron strings snapping in an empty hall—the sound of the rock shrinking in the shade.

Where the wall has breathed too wide, I do not use force. You do not strike this rock hard; to strike it hard is to shatter its brittle planes into razor-splinters. Instead, I take slivers of river-slate, dried flat lichen gathered from the north couloirs, and a stiff paste of ox-blood and glacial clay kept pliable in a small brass pot tucked inside my inner felt vest, warmed against my ribs. 

I slide the slate shims into the seams by feel, tapping them home with the horn mallet until the sound changes from a dry rattle to a solid, dead *clack*. When the stone accepts the shim, the wall hums in a lower register. It settles. It holds for another winter.

My eyes are small behind my greasy mask of blacking, squinting against the blinding white ocean of cloud three leagues below. I do not look down often. Down there is the wet, drowned world where the sun goes to rot. Up here, everything is clean, hard, and dry. 

When the sun begins its sharp angle toward the western towers, casting long, razor-black shadows that race across the tiers faster than a running wolf, I pack my tools into the hide apron. 

I pull the heavy wool skull-cap down over my ears, feel the needle-frost already beginning to grow its tiny fur of ice upon my collar, and slide my foot along the stone flag toward the low, low doorway of my cell. 

Inside, where the dung-fire burns sweet and white, someone will push a bowl of scorched barley and yellow tea toward my hands. I will not say much—the hum of the terrace does not need more words than can be held behind closed teeth. I will grease my knuckles with the warm butter, listen to the swifts return like thrown knives into their nesting holes above the rafters, and feel the immense mountain beneath my back, rigid and awake, carrying us all into the black frost of the night.
