I am not on the dry timber platforms where the families sleep stacked in gut-skin cocoons, nor am I in the soaring, scythe-winged drift of the upper rim. 

To find me, you would have to look forty feet below the lowest suspension beam, where the leeward shelf gives out entirely and the limestone turns into a vertical fluting of black, algae-slick ribs. 

There is a pocket there—not a cave, but an undercut solution hollow no taller than a sitting man, shaped like the inside of a broken bell. The rock above it slopes outward, so the falling sheets of water do not hit it directly; instead, they shear off the lip in a continuous, trembling curtain of cold glass, enclosing the hollow in a private chamber of spray and low-pressure back-draft.

That is where I sit. 

***

At first, it is hard to tell where the rock ends and my body begins. 

I am wedged into the curvature on a sling of braided sedge that has grown so water-heavy and stiff with lime-scale it has almost ceased to be rope and become petrified harness. My knees are drawn up against my chest to fit the pocket’s taper. My back is pressed into the cold, weeping schist, feeling every micro-fracture and the deep, seismic thrum of the mountain’s wind-pipe. 

I am a *Tensioner*—one of the line-tenders whose limbs have begun their turn toward the hook.

My hands are already mostly closed. The tendons in my forearms are thick, ropy cords from thirty seasons of gripping wet rawhide without letting go, and my fingers have curved into rigid ironwood-hard semicircles. I cannot flatten my palms against a table—there are no tables here anyway—but I can lock onto a braided stay or an ironwood wedge with a force that would require breaking my bones to pry loose. My skin is yellowed, thick as boiled boot-leather, and smells perpetually of mutton tallow, cold sulfur, and the sour green pulp of crushed liverworts.

My work does not involve walking or climbing anymore. My work is the *weight*.

Running straight through my hollow is the master gut-line of the great willow trawl-basket. The line comes down from the creaking winch above, passes through a greased bone-pulley anchored directly into the limestone beside my left ear, and drops into the blue throat of the chimney. 

My right hand rests on that line without grasping it. Just resting. 

Through the cured rawhide, the void speaks to me in pulses:
*   A light, fluttering shudder means the basket has hit a rising swarm of lowland silk-moths, tumbling up from the warm river valleys five thousand feet below.
*   A hard, rhythmic snapping means a shear-wind has caught the willow frame and is trying to smash it against the wet south wall.
*   A sudden, deadening sag means the basket is full—greasy with sweet cloud-skim, heavy with storm-drift and down—and it is time to signal the platforms above.

To signal, I do not use my voice. The roar of the chimney would swallow a scream before it left my teeth. 

Between my lower front teeth, I keep a flat slip of prion-wing bone, notched with three small holes. When I breathe out hard against it, it does not make a sound a human ear down in the lowlands would recognize as speech; it releases a piercing, needle-sharp shriek tuned precisely to the frequency of the rock’s own acoustic resonance. The sound rides the wet wall upward like an electric pulse, bouncing off the polished flutes until it strikes the ears of the winch-tenders in their dry alcove above.

*Tch-tch-whrr.* Three sharp pulses. *Haul.*

***

While they turn the greased winch and the dripping basket begins its slow, shuddering ascent through the middle air, I scrape.

Along the floor of my bell-shaped pocket, the condensation gathers in shallow clay baffles I press and smooth with my calloused knuckles. The water here is not ordinary rain. It is the mountain’s sweat—the rich, heavy, pressurized vapor of the lowlands brought to an instant, freezing death against the high stone. It runs thick, almost oily, carrying the scent of distant blossoms I have never seen, marsh-gas, and mineral salts.

With a scraper made from a split hawk-talon lashed to a pine handle, I skim the surface of the clay troughs, directing the amber-colored film into cured skin bladders pegged to the wall. When a bladder is full, it hangs swollen and cool against my calf, heavy as a sleeping infant.

In the midday hour, when the sun cuts diagonally through the throat of the Eye, the water-curtain hanging across my hollow catches the light and turns into a blinding, liquid prism. For twenty minutes, the black schist of my cell is painted with crawling bands of copper, violet, and gold. The midges dance in the updraft outside like sparks thrown from an anvil, and the vane-hawks tilt their scythe-wings directly below my feet, their pale bellies flashing white as they spiral upward on the roaring column.

In those twenty minutes, the cold does not bite as deep. I chew on a pinch of dried sedge root and salt-grub paste, tasting the grit of the mountain, and listen.

I listen to the great whistle of the chimney. I can hear when the seasons are turning down in the world below by the changing chord of the draft: the low, vibrating F-sharp of the summer storms, wet and heavy, pushing upward with the force of a battering ram; or the thin, whistling C-natural of the dry winter gales that threaten to freeze the water-curtain solid and snap our guide-lines like glass.

***

I know what will happen when the tendons in my hands close entirely and I can no longer work the scrapers or feel the shudder of the trawl. 

They will not have to drag me up to the platforms. The wood there is crowded, and the sleeping nets belong to those whose shoulders can still haul the winches. 

When the time comes, I will unhook the rawhide stay from the stone bollard beside my hip. I will spit the bone reed out into the spray. I will push myself out through the shivering water-curtain, clearing the lower lip of the hollow with one clean, outward thrust of my good, thick legs.

I will not look down into the blue drop. I will look up, into the diagonal shafts of gold and the spinning dance of the cliff-swallows, waiting for the great, warm breath of the lowlands to catch me under the ribs, lift me thirty feet into the sunlit mist, and carry me out over the mountain's shoulder into the wide, unanchored sky.
