I would not be down in the retorts, where the paraffin boils and the air is thick with the sweet fat of the vents. Nor would I be a Sail, though the thought of forty leagues of unblinking amber suspension has its pull. The sky is too wide; it lacks the resistance against which a thing can know its own hardness.

I find myself instead in the long middle—the place where the iron cables hang in their great, sagging catenaries between the massifs, two miles above the floor and a league from either wall.

I am a runner of the spans.

Not quite the workers in the towns, though I was cured in the same dry flues. My rind is thicker at the ribs and the palms, gray-white like the chalk cliffs from twenty seasons of lime-dust, and where their shoulders have single rings for the hanging-racks, my spine has four, forged from yellow brass that has gone dark and oily under my skin. I live in the wind-shadow of a midway junction—a cast-iron spreader cage the size of a hay-wain, clamped to the twin master cables where they dip closest to the heat-shimmer of the gorge.

My work is not extraction. It is tension.

***

A cable five miles long does not behave like metal. At that scale, under eighty tons of its own weight and the drag of the midday updrafts, it behaves like a tendon. It breathes. When the sun hits the red iron of the eastern massif at dawn, the cable expands by three spans of a man’s arm, and the whole system settles with a long, subterranean groan that takes twelve minutes to travel from anchor-well to anchor-well. In the thin cold of midnight, it shrinks, shrieking so quietly against its saddle-pins that only the beetles can feel it in their carapaces.

I sit on the spreader with my knees drawn up under my shale-fiber smock, my soles clamped to the lower guide-wire by the deep, horn-plated calluses between my toes. My eyes are quartz-covered against the white flare of the chalk plateaus, but I do not use them much. 

I listen with my shins.

I press the bone of my lower leg directly against the wire rope. The cable is a nerve. Through it, I can read the entire valley:

Three leagues north, the third flywheel in town Seven-on-the-Face has developed an eccentricity—a faint, rhythmic hitch in its three-minute revolution, meaning an oil-beetle has been crushed in the journal or crawled out of reach.

A mile below me, on the vertical face of the white pillar, a herd of seventeen Steppers is cutting a traverse; the steady, hollow *tock... tock... tock* of their diamond grinding-cones rings up through the rock into the anchor-bolts and hums along the strand like a plucked harp-string.

And further still—ten miles out, where the sky is just a pale glaze between the mesa shoulders—a bucket car loaded with red cinnabar has stalled on the long haul. I can feel the cable shuddering where the drive-dog has slipped its tooth.

***

I carry little. A pouch of sulfur-tallow to seal my own joints when the wind bakes them dry; a set of brass wedges; a lead-headed mallet no longer than my forearm; and three flat, blind things that ride inside the hollow of my chest-harness—tame gland-leeches, cold and stiff as horn until I warm them against the grease-ducts behind my ears.

When a strand frays, I do not weld it. Fire here is hungry and wild; it drinks the thin air too fast. I set the leeches upon the parted wire. They drink the mineral grease from my pouch, swell until their copper-ringed segments glow dull orange with internal reaction, and exude a thick, silicate saliva that sets faster than cold iron, knitting the steel wire into a knot of artificial pearl that rings true when struck.

Most days, nothing breaks. The world moves too slowly for things to snap often.

On those days, when the thermals are at their peak—two hours after noon, when the heat rising from the red-iron abyss makes the distant extraction towns ripple and dissolve like fat on an anvil—the Sails drift past.

They come so close to the spreader cage that the back-draft of their amber wings cools the rind of my forehead. They make no sound except a faint, papery hiss, like a dry leaf dragged across slate. Sometimes, one will tilt its shovel-nosed sensory plate toward me as it banks, tasting the scent of the boiled lard on my harness or the slight metallic ping of my mallet against the wire.

We look at each other through the heat-blur. It has no eyes; I have only quartz. We are both things cured until there is nothing left to rot, balanced on a column of air that has not known rain since the rock was soft.

***

In the evening, when the sun drops behind the western plateau and the vast shadows stretch out like black iron bars across the white canyons, the cable begins to sing its night-song. It contracts, inch by inch, drawing itself taut against the cooling stone.

I hook my brass rings into the traveler-loop on the master wire. I hang face-down over the five-mile drop, swaying in the gentle, dry draft that smells of sulfur, ancient calcite, and parched engine grease. 

The three-minute pulse of the towns begins to synchronize across the gulfs. One heartbeat. Then the distant, muffled answer of another, leagues away through the wire.

I close my quartz lenses. My breath smells of tallow and mica. I do not dream, because there is no water in the brain for dreams to drift upon; instead, I let the tension of the iron pass through my collarbones, down through my dry ribs, and out through the heels of my feet, until there is no difference between the wire that holds the city and the bone that holds the man.
