At first, there is no vantage point from which to look. There is only the sheer, uninterrupted vector of load: sixty meters of vertical shear, the heavy descending mass of chilled water, and the monolithic, three-megawatt pulse traveling upward through the steel like a heart beating through bone. 

To exist here is not to see, but to be pressed flat against an edge.

I am not the drop, and I am not the air that falls through it. I am wedged into the seam where the two skins of the tower refuse to touch—anchored in the acoustic shadow of an H-beam flange, four decks down from the howling intakes and twenty meters above the dark mouth of the return sump. 

Here, in the lee of the structural flange, the gale curves around the corner of the beam and shears off, leaving a pocket of air no thicker than a thumb where the wind stops dead. In this narrow margin, the physics of the stack invert. The catastrophic roar does not diminish, but its motion cancels itself out; two opposed wave-fronts from the turbines collide against the web of the beam, creating a line of absolute mechanical stillness. 

That stillness is where I hold.

I am made of the mountain’s shattered drift, reconstituted by pressure and zinc. My body is a coin-flat sprawl of slate-colored sclerotin, barely thicker than the mill scale beneath me, spread wide over the spangles of the galvanized coating. My legs are not designed for running; they are levered struts, tipped with curved, twin-pronged grapples of hardened cuticle that bite directly into the microscopic fissures left by the hot-dip galvanizing bath. 

To me, the steel is not smooth. It is an alpine plateau of crystalline zinc polygons—sharp-edged plateaus and frosted craters, slick with an unbroken skin of near-freezing condensate that glides over my back like heavy oil. My carapace is hydro-engineered by the downdraft itself: sloped like a wedge, textured with micro-grooves that divert the rushing sheet-flow over my head and pin me down against the metal with hydraulic force. The harder the water tears downward, the more securely I am glued to the beam.

I do not hear the sound as a creature of the open air hears it. I have no tympanum, no hollow space inside me that could survive that much acoustic violence without rupturing. 

Instead, the entire sixty-meter tower is my sensory organ. 

Through my tarsal claws, I read the machinery. I feel the turbine’s rotor balance as a clean, continuous shudder of eighty-four cycles per second; I feel the thermal contraction of the corrugated core wall as a sequence of faint, metallic pings that travel through the H-beam like distant rifle shots. I can tell when the auxiliary pumps on the lower decks are starving for fluid by the cavitation chatter rattling up the drainage baffles. I know the exact moment the pneumatic purge triggers, three seconds before the air snaps, because the steel hums a half-step sharper as the valve seals shift under line-pressure.

When the purge hits, the boundary layer strips away. The mist turns into a white hammer. I do not flinch; I flatten my thorax against the blue bead-weld, locking my front limbs into the burrs left by an angle grinder, and let the shockwave roll over my shell.

Between the purges, I feed on the fallout.

The dead air of my pocket is a collector for everything the mountain gave up to the intake: waxy spheres of conifer pollen crushed by the fans, the dessicated fragments of high-altitude arachnids, and the dense, orange felt of iron-oxidizing bacteria that migrates toward my acoustic null-zone along the weld seams. My mouthparts are hard, serrated rasps. I scour the metal clean, stripping the bacterial polysaccharide glue down to the bare, sparkling zinc, leaving behind thin, meandering trails of bright silver on the rusted steel.

Sometimes, the world above me changes color.

The amber gloom of the nearest bulkhead lamp—warm, remote, filmed over with green velvet—is cut through by a sharp, foreign blue. 

A shadow descends from the vapor above: a yellow mass swinging in the draft, dragging two tethered cables that ring against the catwalks like church bells. When she approaches, the vibration of the tower changes. The steel carries the heavy, rhythmic thuds of her rubber-cleated boots striking the diamond tread, and the acoustic wave-front shifts around her mass. 

She brings with her the only light that matters: the cold, violet beam of the ultraviolet wand. 

When the violet beam sweeps across my beam, my slate-gray world ignites. The bacterial felt behind me flares into a screaming crimson carpet; the slick trails of my past feeding glow electric green; and the chitin of my own back burns with an unearthly, fluorescent cyan. For five seconds, I am transformed from a mechanical shadow into a brilliant, glowing glyph of stress and dampness.

I feel the heat of her wand, the faint displacement of air as her neoprene sleeve brushes past the flange, the squeak of her thumb-blade wiping mist from her visor. She pauses. Her beam lingers on me, on the sharp, clean edge where my grazing has exposed the blue metal of the weld, verifying that the steel beneath is sound—unfractured, cold, and holding.

Then the light moves on. The shaft swallows her into the gray-black drop below.

The amber lamp returns. The mist reclaims its continuous, downward tear. The Chladni lines settle back into their standing geometry, and I resume my deliberate, sideways crawl across the cold zinc, reading the heartbeat of the turbines through the iron bones of the sky.
