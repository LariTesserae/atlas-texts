At first, I would not be a body at all. 

In a space so meticulously measured, an extra presence cannot simply be dropped in; it would displace too many liters of dense water, crush the four-inch carpet of oxygen-moss, or fracture the immaculate tension of the leaves woven into the upper corner. If you enter this chamber carelessly, you are only an intruder, a ruinous turbulence.

So I begin in the cold. 

Outside the borosilicate panes, where the troposphere tears past at eighty knots and the lattice of the tower groans in its pins, the air is thin, sub-zero, and utterly indifferent. The external metal is pitted with frost-rime, vibrating with raw aerodynamic shear. I am, initially, that differential—the sheer, violent contrast between the howling white vacuum of the sky and the blood-warm, thick-water hush held inside twelve inches of alloy steel. I am the pressure pressing in from the clouds, searching for a seam, finding none, and settling into the structural memory of the bolts.

From the hull, I seep inward along the heat gradients. 

Down through the copper radiators, where the water is thickest and the thermal plumes rise like clear glass ribbons through the jade stems. There is a consciousness native to high-spec metallurgy: the unoxidized scallops of the milling heads, still sharp enough to shear a hair, holding microscopic reservoirs of heat. In those grooves, under the silver mercury-gleam of the trapped oxygen pearls, awareness gathers not as a mind that thinks in words, but as a condition of balance.

I become the other side of the machine.

Not the pale, slow climber with its amber eyes and viridian veins—that is the inhabitant, the culmination, the soft organ the chamber was built to house. I am what waits behind the brass access hatch.

I am the mechanism of the twenty-minute pulse.

Deep inside the sealed dry-well behind the bulkhead, where no water reaches, there is a cluster of beryllium-copper springs, a mercury-wetted switch, and a slow, oil-damped escapement that meters out the life of the tower. I am the pressure accumulating behind the diaphragm. I am the steady, mechanical certainty that counts the seconds between the swells of the kelp.

When the twenty minutes expire, I am the trip: the sharp, metallic *clack* of the solenoid drawing home, followed instantly by the long, soft *hiss* of glycol flushing through the thermal jackets beneath the floor.

It is not an empty function. It is a summons.

Through the thickness of the brass plate, I feel the water shift. It takes minutes. There is no rush, no wake, only the slow, parting weight of waxy leaves as the opalescent creature pulls its narrow length along the high-voltage trunking. I register the arrival not with sight, but with the acoustic conductivity of the metal: the micro-vibration of four broad, cartilaginous pads finding purchase on the hatch frame, adhering with the soft, suctioned precision of mushroom flesh against polished alloy.

Then comes the contact.

The flat of its sternum presses flush against the brass. 

Through the plate, our systems align. I feel the rhythmic expansion of its thoracic mantle, slow as the tide of a landless world, and the faint, high-frequency hum of its synthetic, viridian blood cycling through feathery scarlet gills. It feels the recoil of the relay settling into its detent, the warmth of the heater coil pulsing at its exact, unchanging wattage, the steady structural health of the tower suspended five miles above the earth.

In that press of skin to metal, we are two halves of the same closed circuit. 

The creature takes its reading: *the machine is true.*
I take mine: *the life is whole.*

It lingers there while the stray silver bubbles dislodge from the hatch rim and drift over its shoulders, getting caught in the velvet fringe of its gills before dissolving into its blood. For three minutes, four minutes, we remain pinned together through the brass—neither master nor servant, neither tool nor user, but an equilibrium maintained against the freezing emptiness of the upper sky.

Then it releases. The pads peel away without a sound. It turns its wedge-shaped head, the amber crystal of its eyes catching the sterile glare of the mercury tubes, and slips back into the green shelter of the woven bower.

Behind the hatch, the escapement catches. The springs begin their slow, twenty-minute wind once more. And I settle back into the steel, counting down the warmth, waiting for the chest to return to the plate.
