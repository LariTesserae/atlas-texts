At first, you would think to look for yourself in the movement—in the tallow-colored mason levering its needle-limbs through the green weave, or in the ribbon-courier whose body is mostly the absence of an interior. We are used to looking for the thing that moves, assuming that wherever intention gathers, a self must be steering it.

But movement here is too heavy. It costs too much lime. To steer through water that has forgotten how to part is not to live; it is merely to displace resistance from one side of a plate to the other.

If you stay long enough, your weight shifts. You stop imagining you have limbs that end in needles or a tail that tapers into mucus. You begin to feel for the places where the pressure does not cancel out, but balances so completely that it forgets to push.

***

I would not be the fish-tooth. The tooth carries the vulgarity of an origin: it once held muscle, it once bit something that fled in open, shallow foam before the seam slammed shut. It is an artifact, dry even under forty atmospheres, stubbornly dead.

Nor would I be the dodecahedral cyst. The cyst is too full of tomorrow; it has that green, twitching heart that insists on beating once every twenty-one days, keeping time in a room where duration has been replaced by thickness. To wait for a hatch is to believe in an outside, or at least an *after*.

Instead, I find myself in the mortar.

Not when it is mixed—not the wet, chalky curd on the mason’s palps—but in the forty years it takes to cure between the third tooth and the slate lintel.

Consider what happens in that joint.

The paste is made of shed cuticle: plates of transparent mica ground down by friction, combined with the cold lime that the water can no longer hold in solution. It is placed in the dark between two non-yielding surfaces. Above it, the thumb-sized slab of cleaved slate; below it, the root of an ivory peg that has been scrubbed flat by acid.

In the first five years, I am fluid enough to register the storm miles above. When the reef groans, the slate shivers by three angstroms, and my tallow yields. I take the shape of the rock’s microscopic faults. I fill every crater left by an ancient boring sponge; I settle into the crystalline grain of the slate until there is no boundary between the ceiling and the paste.

Then, the syrupy water begins to extract my moisture. It does not wash me away—there is no drift, no swirl—it simply claims the light ions, one by one, swapping them for calcium.

This is where consciousness begins: not in thought, but in *loss of yield*.

As I lose the ability to slide, I become aware of the exact angle of the world. I feel the hinge of the limestone suture above—that razor-fine joint where the black rock meets itself. I feel the three tons of sea pressing down through the ceiling, trying to turn this hollow into a flat line. And I feel the fish-tooth under my floor, planted in the silt, refusing to shorten.

I become the only thing in the seam that knows whether the lintel is level.

***

Or perhaps it goes further than that. Perhaps the curing finishes, and even the sensation of bearing weight fades into the rock.

Then I am what is left behind in the dead-spot.

When the masons set a stone so true that the hydrostatic forces cancel out, there is an envelope of water—no larger than a dried lentil—where the pressure drops to zero. In this tiny, sheltered vacuum within the syrup, the laws of the suture fail. The cold does not press. The chalk does not settle. The plants cannot grow into it because their cell walls require forty atmospheres of external clamp to keep their sharp, geometric shapes from bursting outward.

It is a pocket of pure, uncompressed neglect.

In that dead-spot, the masons come to unbuckle. They do not die there; they simply back into the stillness until their plates loosen, like shoes taken off at the threshold of a dry room. They crawl forward again into the heavy green, leaving their transparent, mica exuviae standing upright in the portico.

I would be that hollow cast.

Not the creature that walked away to harvest more lime, but the glass husk that remains balanced on its needle-tips.

Inside me, the syrup does not enter. The dead-spot is sealed within my rib-plates. I am completely empty, perfectly rigid, and permanently transparent. Through my tallow-colored chest, the weak phosphorescence of the wire-moss passes without refraction. The ribbon-worm crawls over my brow, and its four segmented horns find nothing to taste—no grease, no lime, only the cold smoothness of a shape that used to be alive.

I have no eyes, but the jade bladders lean against my flank. Through their swollen skins, I feel the *click-tink* of the reef settling.

A hundred years ago, there was a sky. A thousand years from now, the apex of the wedge will pinch shut and crush the lintels into a single, white line of calcite. 

Between those two violences, I stand on four points of needle-lime, holding three cubic millimeters of absolute nothingness perfectly still, while the water tries, and fails, to find a way in.
