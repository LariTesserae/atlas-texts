I would not be moving. 

To move too much in this split is to be young, or hungry, or hunted. The scurrying things—the ones with legs like carbon bristles or forelimbs like hollow awls—have to spend their lives in frantic transit, chasing the slick film or dodging the sweep of a predator’s reach. 

No, I would be wedged. 

You would find me where the cold composite of the outer structural rib leans in toward the burning flank of Flue-4, in the narrowest dead-air pocket of the quadrant. It is a place where two massive resonant frequencies meet and, by some ancient acoustic geometry of the trusses, almost perfectly cancel each other out. In the rest of the vault, the air is an iron fist beating on the ribs; here, in this sliver no wider than a ribcage, there is an eerie, localized hush—a stillness that is not silence, but a violent balance of opposing pressures.

I am one of the Dwellers, but an old one, settled so deeply into this node that the crevice has begun to claim the margin of my skin. 

My hips and the long, pale shins of my lower legs are locked into the alloy ruts. Over months—or perhaps cycles, for time has no light to count it by—the veil-lungs have colonised the dead space around my thighs. Their thin, liver-colored sheets drape across my lap like heavy, wet blankets, catching the mineral drizzle and the shed carapaces of the suture-lice that tumble from the cable trays above. 

I do not brush them away. We have an arrangement of warmth and resin. Where their edges touch my flanks, their secreted amber has hardened into smooth, knobby knuckles of synthetic bone, fusing my dead-weight into the pylon’s armature. The suture-lice crawl across my feet, their carbon-fiber bristles rasping softly over my thickened heels as they graze the blue grease from my calluses. I am a reef to them.

I have no eyes, and the wrinkled pads over my sockets are still, but my chest is wide and hollow—a drum made for the building’s news. 

My fingers, tipped with heavy, spade-like keratin, rest perpetually against the naked alloy of the secondary coolant pipe. I do not tap often. The young ones down in the lower bypass tap constantly, frantic little percussions of hunger, territorial spatters, warnings about strut-whip broods in the vents. They make so much noise with their knuckles because they only feel what is immediate.

I keep my hands flat to listen to the *deep spine*.

Through the metal, I feel the health of the machine miles below and miles above. I feel the microscopic shear in the metamaterial three decks down where an alloy plate is growing brittle from centuries of scalding steam. I feel the thin, rapid heartbeat of a pregnant Dweller two ribs over, tucked behind an insulation shield, her skin slick with the same copper-smelling sebum that coats my own shoulders. I feel the shudder of the great lift-cables that haven't moved since our ancestors forgot what a sky was, their tension singing a high, wire-thin note of dying stress.

When the shift comes, I know it long before the drone drops its octave.

I feel the valve gathering its breath in the deep core—a tightening in the fluid channels, a sudden, microscopic dry-out in the vibration under my palms. 

I do not panic. I lean my torso forward, bowing my bald, scarred head down between my knees. My long fingers hook beneath the thickest, cured flap of veil-lung skin that grows across my shoulders, drawing it over my neck like a hood of damp leather. 

The sound breaks. The floor falls away into that terrifying low bass. 

Then comes the purge: the white, shrieking wall of atomized chemical fire roaring down the vault. 

It hits my back not as pain, but as an enormous, boiling weight. The steam scours the amber at my hips; the runoff cascades over my knuckles, washing away the grease, the dead lice, the stray salt of my body. For twenty seconds, the world is only heat and the tearing rush of fluid. The veil-lung skin on my back cooks slightly, smelling of sweet organic resin and hot machine oil, protecting the pallid meat beneath.

Then the valve slams shut. 

The purge dies into a hissing sigh. The drone climbs back to its steady, bone-grinding mid-tone. The hot mineral rain begins again, dripping from the carbon ceiling, pattering softly on my hooded head.

Slowly, I lift my brow. The air is clear and scalding. 

I reach out a hand, slick with fresh wash, and lay two heavy keratin nails against the cooling line. I strike it once—a sharp, clear *clack* that rings through forty yards of hollow alloy. 

*Clear.*

From down in the wet dark, through the trembling ribs of the tower, three faint taps come back in answer. I close my hands over the pipe, feel the standing waves reform in the sumps around my feet, and go back to listening to the slow, beautiful decay of the world that holds us aloft.
