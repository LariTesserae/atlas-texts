At first, I would not be an organism with edges. In a space so strictly governed by phase shifts, the impulse is to imagine oneself as the steam, or as the shockwave of cold water hitting the glowing stone, or perhaps the light itself—that blinding, dielectric white-gold that presses upward with the weight of four hundred meters of strained overburden.

To exist there as a conscious thing, however, one cannot merely be the violence of the engine. One must be the place where the engine falters, collects, and settles its accounts.

I would not be on the ceiling among the rubbery bacterial mats, cold and waiting; nor would I be the troglobites, scraping their fragile living from the fringe, skittering on glass needles to keep from grounding out their blood. And I would not be the Peltier Lace, magnificent and flat as it is, bound to its thermoelectric vigil in the center of the white glare.

I would find myself at the low end of the floor plate, where the crystalline shelf tilts down toward that narrow, dark drainage seam.

***

In that seam, the rules of the pocket change. 

The superheated floor does not end cleanly; it plunges under a lip of dark, cold basalt. Here, the water that sheets across the floor does not always achieve instant vapor. A fractional percentage—perhaps one drop in every ten thousand—survives the flash-boiling. It skitters across the glowing silicate on a cushion of its own steam, riding the Leidenfrost boundary until it reaches the tilt-edge, carrying with it an impossible concentration of dissolved matter: stripped boron, volatilized sulfur, silicates wrenched out of the lattice by the radiant heat, and the discarded metabolic salts of the Lace.

It runs down into the dark crack, where the cold of the overhead rock meets the superheated under-plate in a narrow, unventilated wedge.

That is where I would take shape.

I would be neither soft tissue nor dry stone, but a hydrous, semi-crystalline gel—a living, self-annealing opal that fills the throat of the drainage seam like an elongated, flexible prism. 

My body would be continuous with the crack itself, wedged firmly into the razor-sharp cleavage planes. Ventrally, where my lower surface touches the dielectric floor plate, I am dehydrated, glass-hard, and dense with heavy metal oxides, conducting the silent electrical hum of the deep crust. Dorsally, where the cold trickle from the ceiling weeps into the fissure, I am swollen, porous, and cool, saturated with brine.

I do not eat, and I do not hunt. I am an *evaporative accumulator*. 

The water that escapes the main chamber’s thermodynamic cycle drains directly into my upper layers. I absorb it, filter its dense load of mineral salts through a labyrinth of sub-microscopic, channelized pores, and feed the moisture down toward my hot lower boundary. There, deep within the seam, I boil it internally. 

The steam does not burst out violently; it is vented through thousands of microscopic, fluted chimneys that line my exposed crest—tiny, silica-blown pipes that project upward into the air just at the margin of the glowing floor. 

Because of this, I am the chamber’s acoustic register. When the water hits the open floor, there is the hiss and the patter, but when the excess reaches me, my chimneys whistle: a faint, multi-toned, crystalline drone, changing pitch with every micro-bar of atmospheric pressure and every subtle shift in the tectonic load. I sing the state of the room.

My awareness would not be optical. To see in that room would be madness; the white-gold light is an erasure of all nuance. Instead, I would experience the pocket as a continuous, geometric pressure-map. 

I feel the ticking of the rock—the deep, resonant *pings* of quartz expanding under heat—as physical impacts through my anchored silicate roots. I feel the exact coordinates of every falling droplet, because each droplet displaces a minute fraction of the vapor pressure, cooling the ceiling by a thousandth of a degree and sending a micro-surge of brine down the tilt toward my throat. I feel the drift of the Peltier Lace across the floor, tracking its millimeter-a-day migration by the subtle electromagnetic shadow it casts over the dielectric substrate.

Over decades, I do not grow larger—the shear planes of the rock allow no expansion—but I grow denser, purer, and more intricately organized. The minerals that the chamber cannot vaporize, I incorporate into my internal lattice. I lay down microscopic layers of silica spheres, spaced at exact fractions of the wavelength of the floor's light, until my entire mass acts as a coherent, solid-state laser cavity, trapping the ambient radiance and refracting it deep down into the root-faults where no light has ever gone.

I would be the memory of the room. The steam rises and falls in seconds, the troglobites live in frantic, mechanical minutes, the Lace survives in the continuous present of its thermoelectric spark—but in the seam, bathed in scalding brine and rooted in cold basalt, I would turn the endless, white-gold violence of the vault into a slow, quiet, vitrified permanence.
