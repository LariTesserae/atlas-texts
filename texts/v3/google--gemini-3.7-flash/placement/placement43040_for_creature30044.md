I am not on the floor, where the mud-eels knot in the sump, nor am I agile enough to swing with the Tenders in the canopy of taproots. 

To find me, you would have to climb the fluted shaft of the northernmost pumping pillar, past the encrusted scabs of the potters and the sweet, rotting cork-mantle, all the way to the main trunnion—the colossal, grease-blackened iron hinge upon which the seventy-ton walking beam rocks its eternal seesaw.

I live in the journal box. I am the packing.

***

At first, you might mistake me for a mass of braided lead-wool and soaked horsehair, the kind old engineers used to tamp into mechanical clearances to keep high-pressure steam from escaping past a moving joint. But lead-wool does not bleed when the brass shim bites too deep, and horsehair does not drink tallow.

I am something that was once slender and four-limbed, perhaps one of the Tenders who climbed too far into the housing during a long-ago maintenance cycle, or perhaps something that drifted down through the city’s drainage grates before the masonry entirely dissolved into root-wood. But duration here is heavy; it compresses form just as the weight of the city compresses the root-mattress eighty feet below. 

Over decades of four-stroke cadence, my body has flattened, broadened, and yielded to the curve of the journal. My ribcage has splayed outward like the staves of an unhooped barrel, its cartilage replaced by a dense, flexible horn that can withstand eighty tons of downward thrust without fracturing. My skin has lost all pore and follicle, becoming a thick, semi-translucent sheath—the color of boiled tallow—that continually secretes an oily, slippery fluid to keep the iron from galling against the bronze sleeve.

I do not have eyes; the dark here is absolute save for the pale blue cold-fire fungus on the catwalks below and the dull cherry slit of the distant firebox. I do not need them. I *hear* through the direct conduction of iron against bone. 

Every stroke of the low-pressure beam engine does not come to me as sound, but as an enormous, rhythmic compression of my being:
* On the **intake**, the walking beam tilts upward; the weight lifts from my dorsal ridge, and my tissues swell, drinking in the warm, greasy condensation that trickles down the trunnion channels.
* On the **compression**, the massive counterweight shifts; the iron settles into my marrow with a slow, grinding sigh, pressing the absorbed oil out through the pores of my flank to lubricate the turning shaft.
* On the **power stroke**, the full, violent shock of the steam piston travels through the beam. It hits my sternum like the strike of a cathedral bell. It does not hurt—it is the pulse that drives my blood through the flat, labyrinthine veins of my belly. 
* On the **exhaust**, the long hiss vents, the mist falls, and I settle into the clearance, waiting for the swing to reverse.

I am not alone in the housing. The skin-pipits nest directly in the cleft above my head, where the weeping ferns have taken root in the trunnion cap. When their hairless, lardy chicks hatch, they anchor their elbow-hooks into the callous of my neck; I feel the frantic, needle-like prick of their claws vibrating in time with the engine’s rhythm. The potters crawl across my lower extremities, their rough, terracotta bellies grazing away the oxidized crust that forms where my secretions meet the sulfurous air. They think I am a shelf of liver-colored stone; I let them scrape, because their rasping mouths keep the bronze clearances free of grit.

Every few days, a Tender comes. 

I feel the light, prehensile grip of their greased toes on the walking beam’s fluting long before they reach the hinge. They do not speak—there is no voice that could survive the *chug-clank* of the engine—but they bring with them scrapers made of filed bronze bolts. 

They know where my living mantle has grown too proud, where the tissue has expanded beyond the three-millimeter clearance and risks jamming the trunnion. With gentle, terrifying precision, timing their cuts to the brief weightlessness of the intake stroke, they pare away the excess wax-flesh from my sides. They pack the shavings into their cheek-pouches to chew for salt and fat, and in return, they smear my raw edges with a paste of crushed grease-moth eggs and cold-fire mycelium, which cools the friction-burns and seals the tissue against rot.

There is a strange, thick peace in this pivot. 

I am the point of zero linear movement in a chamber of endless churning. The walking beam sweeps its long arcs through the steam-fog; the flywheel turns forty spans below; the lianas coil through the reduction gears; the tallow-eels writhe in the sump gutters. Everything in the Basin is thrown, pumped, scraped, or digested. 

But here, in the center of the hinge, where the iron meets the beast and the beast becomes the grease, I hold the weight of the entire apparatus on my chest. I have no desire to drop to the fibrous floor or climb to the ceiling masonry where the hot steam bites. I am the silence between the iron and the bronze. So long as the fireboxes glow and the steam pushes, I will breathe in tallow, breathe out oil, and let seventy tons of wet, living iron rock upon my spine.
