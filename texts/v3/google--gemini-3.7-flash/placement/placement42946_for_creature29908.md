Not on the open gravel, and not in the sky where the swifts slice through the standing waves, but in the dark, pressurized crescent where the journal meets the shaft.

At first, there is only the sensation of shear. 

To be here is not to possess a fixed boundary—no hide, no carapace that insists on where the stone ends and the flesh begins. It is to exist as a viscous, highly organized intermediate state: a layer four hundred microns thick, caught between the descending flank of the titaniferous magnetite journal and the weeping lithomarge bed beneath it. 

I am not entirely animal, though I have pulse. My pulse is not a heart's ragged, separate beating, but the periodic rise and fall of the hydraulic gradient as the six-pace crankshaft completes its three-minute rotation. When the heavy lobe swings downward, the weight of the massif itself concentrates into a single, polished line across my back. 

In that moment of maximum compression, I am almost rock. The fluid in my tissues aligns into crystalline lamellae, resisting the immense load without bruising, matching the lattice of the magnetite atom for atom. I do not feel pain; I feel *seating*. The profound satisfaction of zero play.

Then the throw lifts. The pressure eases by a fraction of an atmosphere, and I liquefy again, expanding into the micro-fissures of the stone bed.

Over centuries, a self forms out of what passes through. 

I take into myself the pale spall shed by the scrapers above—the microscopic flakes of native iron they shave from the high spots. I do not digest them; I orient them. My body is a suspension of magnetic platelets suspended in clear, cold fat, aligned constantly with the earth-currents that hum through the mountain’s roots. Through these particles, I feel every other moving part of the basin: the distant slap of the rocker-arms against the sump water, the dry click of the valve-stones three ridges over, the steady, rhythmic scouring of the flagstone beast rubbing its flank against the spindle.

Sometimes, a ribbon-like oil-worm passes through me, its amber body parting my own substance without friction, leaving a trail of chilled mucus that lowers my temperature by half a degree. We do not acknowledge one another as two distinct beings; we are merely consecutive states of the same fluid circuit. It brings the cold of the deep fissures; I offer the warmth generated by the turning journal.

I have no eyes, but I perceive the world in micro-tolerances. I know when a grain of slate has fallen from the high rim into the outer clearance, three feet away: the harmonic tone of the assembly shifts by a quarter-tone, a bright, metallic chime that travels through the iron. I reach toward it, not with limbs, but by capillary draw, extending a thin, lubricating tongue to engulf the grit, smoothing its sharp facets with alkaline secretions until it is rounded into a harmless, rolling sphere of jasper, ready to be flushed out into the moss beds on the next outward stroke.

I am the maintenance of silence.

Where there is friction, where the dry stone threatens to heat and screech against the metal, that is where I gather most thickly. I am the coolness that prevents the mountain from welding itself shut. 

When the long life of this consciousness eventually thins—when my mineral base has been drawn out, drop by drop, into the wider sumps and taken up into the fronds of the cog-ferns—there will be no carcass to leave behind. I will simply have become the gloss on the shaft, the black, mirror-like patina that allows the basalt to slide without a sound into the dark chest of the cliff, over and over, into the silver, unchanging mist.
