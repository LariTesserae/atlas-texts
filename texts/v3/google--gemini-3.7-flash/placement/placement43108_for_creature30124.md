At first, there is only the sensation of differential torque. 

Not thought, and certainly not breath, but a sharp, clean axis of strain running through a series of interlocking horn-plates. One side is anchored into the cold slate scarp where the rock does not give; the other is pinned into the moving mineral lace of a creeping tenement facade that drifts three inches every dusk toward the center of the basin.

I am not in the open plazas, where the violet light makes hard shadows on the talc floor, nor am I buried in the dead-weight silence of the deep hexagonal drifts below. 

I find myself in the *seam*.

I am suspended in the vertical fissures—the cold, hair-thin clefts where the self-extruding city tries to pull away from the bedrock that cradles it. 

My body is not jointed like the weft-walkers with their reed legs and sweeping spinnerets, nor is it stacked in heavy lanterns like the bellows-forms. It is long, flat, and ribbon-like: a double spine of dry graphite wire woven through hundreds of paper-thin mica shingles that overlap like the tiles on a dropped roof. I have no front or rear, only an upper tension and a lower tension, held taut across a thirty-foot chasm by anchor-spurs that have sintered directly into the chalky masonry on one side and the slate scarp on the other.

I am a hinge. A living strain-gauge.

Where the city moves, it creates shearing forces. As a facade slides on its dry talc beds, it tilts imperceptibly outward, threatening to sheer its own spires off before the bone-mineral has fully set. I exist to feel that tilt. 

The air in the cleft is utterly stagnant, stripped of every wandering grain by the bellows far below. When the wall shifts, the mica plates along my spine slide over one another with a microscopic, dry scrape that yields no sound—sound is devoured instantly by the porous walls—but produces a continuous, shivering cascade of piezoelectric charge. 

I feed upon that static hum. It charges the hair-springs coiled inside my narrow silicate nodes, keeping the plates flexed against the draw of the abyss.

Through the plates, I feel the city’s silent, feverish metabolism. 
I feel the rhythmic thump-less pulsing of the bellows three flues down, drawing the ozone-sharp atmosphere across their talc vanes. 
I feel the light, skittering footsteps of a weft-walker six stories above, its velvet pads discharging minute sparks into the lintel as it runs to seal an over-stressed arch. 
And far below, in the compressed talc floor where light never penetrates, I feel the grinding rotation of the sieve-drifts, sorting the fallen snow of calcified lace into the dense hexagonal foundations that make my wall creep an inch to the left.

I do not look at the city. My vision is merely an array of graphite quills along the rim of my upper shingles, attuned to the violet phosphorescence reflected down the cleft. When a new tier of arches extrudes overhead, the violet dimness flickers, cut into geometric slats by the newly spun bridges. That is how I know the sky has closed again.

Sometimes, the tension becomes too great. A facade accelerates, slipping on a fresh vein of salt-crust, and the chasm widens faster than my ribbons can stretch.

When this happens, my anchor-spurs do not pull free. They cannot. They are fused with the lime. Instead, the central graphite hair-springs within my midsection are pulled to their absolute elastic limit. As they reach the point of fracture, they discharge their stored potential in a single, silent arc of static that leaps thirty feet across the void to the slate wall.

The spark is violet and completely mute. But in the dry vacuum of humidity, that discharge carries an immense electrostatic pull. It snaps the drifting facade back half a millimeter—just enough, just long enough, for a weft-walker to glide across the gap, vomit a plume of micronized lime into the stress-fracture, and lock the wall into the bedrock with a fresh, needle-sharp buttress.

My life is this holding. This quiet measurement of shear and fracture.

I know the arc of my existence will not end in violence. It will end in balance. 
Eventually, the tenement to which I am pinned will cease to creep; a bellows will fail, or the sieve-drifts below will lay their hexagonal pilings in a new geometry, and the facade will lock permanently into place against the slate. 

When the sliding stops, the mica plates will no longer scrape. The static charge that keeps my hair-springs supple will slowly bleed away into the chalk masonry. I will stiffen. The dry air will pull the last trace of flex from the graphite. 

Then, as the extruded towers above spin their gossamer walkways across the cleft, the falling snow of mineral dust will settle upon my shingles, filling the gaps between the horn-plates, fusing the overlapping tiles into a single, continuous bracket of solid, chalky stone.

I will not fall into the talc basin. 
I will become the lintel that supports the next tier.
