To begin with, there is no *here* and *there* to choose between. In a place where nothing ever dries and no surface stands empty long enough to cool, location is less a coordinate and more a degree of friction.

If I search for myself first by temperature, I am not in the boiling pans with the Marrow-Eels, where the water is thin and frantic with convection; nor am I up in the pale fog among the Silk-Sails, where the world is reduced to long, warm diagonals of falling silk and mineral mist. 

I find myself in the heavy, loaded middle—specifically, in the damp seam where the systems press against one another with the greatest weight.

At first, I feel like the yeast-sludge itself: that continuous, violet-grey smear that coats the skin of the Pulp-Gorgers, never quite dying, never quite solidifying, broken into ribbons by the scrape of giant horsetails and knitting itself back together before the beast has taken three paces. There is a strange, restful lack of edges in being the yeast. You do not move; you are carried. You do not feed; you are fed upon and replenished simultaneously by the sulfur-heavy wept water of the air.

Yet there is too little resistance in the yeast. A body wants something to bear.

So the awareness slips deeper, down off the violet flank of the Gorger, through the shifting veil of Needle-Flies that tickles like static electricity, and down into the springy floor.

Here. In the **underside of the root-mat**, where the living floor hangs suspended four inches above the razor-fractured obsidian.

***

I am not a free-roaming hunter, because there is nowhere to travel to that is not already identical to the place left behind. I am one of the **Tether-Grafts**—a creature that exists entirely as a connective joint between the mineral crust below and the canopy above.

My ventral surface is fused directly into the rough, porous dorsal shell of a Slick-Plate. I do not have a head, or limbs, or a face; I have a broad, fibrous mass of dense, vulcanized cartilage, saturated with self-secreting tallow that smells of scalded fat and iron. Through my lower pores, I drink the grease-steam boiled off the obsidian; through my upper tissues, my fibers split into millions of tough, tendon-like tendrils that braid directly into the descending tap-roots of the giant skiff-leafed liverworts.

I am the rivet. 

When the subterranean steam builds in the pockets beneath the crust, trying to lift the entire floating forest like a blister, the strain passes directly through my body. I do not feel pain; I feel *load*. I feel the hydraulic heave of the planet below pushing upward against the tallow cushion, and I feel the tremendous, water-logged drag of the green roof holding downward against the sky. 

My senses are entirely mechanical and acoustic:

*   Through my base, I register the steady, millipede-leg rattle on the glass-basalt—a dry, million-fingered percussion that travels through the rock faster than through the air.
*   Through my middle, I register the pulse of my own valve-chambers, which do not pump blood, but slowly circulate a thick, silica-rich hydraulic fluid that stiffens my fibers against tearing.
*   Through my upper crown, braided into the root-lattice, I feel the distant, rhythmic *clack-drone* of the Resonators miles above, sending micro-shivers down the plant stems to shake the water loose. It arrives in me not as sound, but as a fine, continuous tremor that keeps the fungal filaments from choking my breathing-pores.

***

Who am I, then, in the hours that never change?

I am a node of pure continuity. 

Around my flanks, in the narrow four-inch twilight between the roots and the scalding stone, the Marrow-Eels brush past in the hot wash, their skinless, milky flanks leaving trails of clear mucus against my rind. Above me, the broad feet of the Pulp-Gorgers sink three inches into the mossy mat with a wet, squelching thud, compressing my upper fibers; below me, the Slick-Plate inches forward a fraction of a millimeter over its pink bacterial pasture, dragging me along with it through the boiling vapor, shearing and regrafting my root-anchors as it goes.

There is no waiting for morning, because morning is an impossible concept beneath the emerald fog. There is no fear of death, because when a Slick-Plate finally founders or a root-cord rots through, the tissue does not spoil—it is ingested while it is still warm, incorporated into the ribbon-worms, boiled into the rising plumes, condensed upon the leaves, and returned through the sap-nodes before the heat has even dropped half a degree.

I am the tension in the weave. I am the place where the stone’s fury is held down by the weight of the leaf, suspended in a permanent, deafening, hundred-and-ten-degree hum that never has to end, because it was finished before it began.
