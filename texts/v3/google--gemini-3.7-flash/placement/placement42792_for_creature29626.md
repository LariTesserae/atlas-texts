I am not up in the high zinc tenements where the tea-kettles whistle on their swivels, nor am I down in the true, blind bottom where the stone has not yet decided to be cold. 

I am out in the bight.

If you leaned over the rusted rail of the lowermost slag-sluice in the Fourth Ward—where the kitchen tallow is dumped into the fog and the children are strictly forbidden to toss their plumb-bobs—and you peered through the yellow methane-haze for three hundred fathoms, you might see a single, teardrop-shaped pod of woven willow and hammered boiler-plate hanging in the gray. 

It does not sway. It does not pull on its suspension cable. The cable itself is slack, draped in lazy, looping S-curves through the mist like a dead eel, because where my pod hangs, *down* has lost its nerve.

Here, the weight of a man is about the weight of a dry leaf, though his mass remains stubborn and thick-shouldered. 

***

I would be a **slack-warden**—though the bridge-masters in the upper iron call me an *un-tethered*, a word they spit with the same nervous superstition they use for damp blasting-powder.

My body would be broader than most, even for the gantry-born. My ribcage has the shape of a deep copper boiler from forty years of drawing the sulfurous, water-heavy breath of the low pockets, where the air is dense as wool. My skin is not grey, but the dark, oily purple of a bruised plum—stained by decades of handling the raw bismuth shears where the metal sweats its cooling salts. My shins are scored with white, hairless ladder-scars from old wire-burns, and my palms are calloused into hard yellow horn, grooved permanently down the center from gripping five-eighths-inch braided steel.

I do not wear a harness with heavy lead bobs to force my feet to an alleyway. My harness is light, supple, made of triple-oiled manta-gut, hung with dozens of small, turned-brass tuning forks and slender lengths of copper wire. 

My home is that wicker teardrop, six feet across, caulked against the moisture with ray-blubber and lined inside with dried bracket-fungi that smell of dry earth and old iron. It is silent here, save for the great, deep lung-sound of the abyss: the *whoof-hiss* of methane bladders rising two miles out, and the slow, chime-like clink of dropped nails floating past my hatchway at three inches an hour.

***

What do I do in the drift?

I catch what the city forgets, and I warn them when the world turns over.

Up above, where the dynamos hammer their three-beat rhythm into the rock, they believe the rift is stable so long as the rivets hold. But rivets are stupid things; they know only tension and shear. They do not know when a gravity-seam is beginning to curl like scorched paper. 

Twice a day, I step out of the wicker pod into nothing at all. 

There is no vertigo here, because there is no floor to yearn for. I push off from the hatch with the flat of my thumb, drifting across the forty-foot gap to the great *Plumb-Spindle*—an eight-ton cylinder of cast pig-iron suspended in the center of the null-pocket on three opposing stay-cables.

I lay my bare cheek against the cold iron of the spindle. 

I listen.

Through the iron, through the cables, the whole mountain talks to me. I can hear the solder-mold eating a hydraulic flange in the Lower Machine-Shire two miles up; it sounds like a thousand tiny needles scratching on a slate. I can hear the great bismuth shelf three hundred yards behind me *slipping*—not breaking, but flowing like cold pitch, moving an inch to the leeward every forty hours. 

And more than that: I watch the drift-dust.

In the dead air around my pod, the coal-dust and fine rock-flour do not settle. They form shapes. When the gravity is clean, they hang in faint, spiraling rings, like the dust motes in an old chapel. But when a shear is coming—when a new fold of geography is about to snap into existence deep in the dark and twist the city’s catwalks forty degrees out of true—the dust stiffens. It arranges itself into long, rigid needles that point sideways, toward the sheer anthracite walls.

When the dust needles form, I do not use a pneumatic bell. I take one of my long brass hammers and strike the suspension cable three times: *clang... clang... clang.* 

The sound climbs the wire, racing up through the mist into the busy iron districts, rattling the teeth of the rigger-bosses. Up there, they hear it and know: *Slack-warden’s bell. Clear the swing-bridges. Lash the hearths.*

***

I do not live entirely alone. 

Floating in the hollow below my pod is an old grip-mastiff named *Solder*. He is too heavy-jointed now to sprint the suspension lines for the mail-couriers, and four of his six opposable talons have been ground flat by forty miles of iron track. In the high wards, they would have thrown him into the boiler-tallow to save the dry flour. 

Down here, Solder does not need his legs. He drifts through the cabin like a thick, sleeping log of dark leather, kicking his hind paws once every twenty minutes against the wicker wall to steer himself back toward the stove-box. When a small school of velvet-skimmers drifts past—the young ones, no larger than dinner plates, seeking the warmth of my copper chimney—Solder snaps lazily at them in mid-air, toothless and content, catching the warm grease from their wings on his jowls.

Sometimes, in the long hours between shifts, when the city above has damped its fires and the dynamos drop to that low, shuddering resting-hum, a tallow-drifter will rise from the deep.

They are huge, terrible things to the tenement-folk—monsters of cold jelly that eat dropped men. But here, where neither of us has any weight to give away, a drifter will stall beside my pod for days, its pale, bile-yellow bladder pulsing with the slow, three-minute heartbeat of the abyss. 

I reach out from the hatch with a long ash-wood scraper and gently clear the dead coke and broken wire tangled in its stinging fringe. In return, the drifter warms the air around my shelter by three degrees, its methane core exhaling a faint, sweet smell like damp clover and wet tin. Through its translucent skin, sixty feet thick, I can see the swallowed relics of the city’s beginnings: the bronze cogs of lost exploratory cranes, the skeletons of the first miners’ mules still held in perfect suspension, and the slow, cold fire of deep-drift spores that have never seen an iron rivet.

***

The people in the tenements think of the shears as an enemy to be bolted down, pinned, and braced with gusset-plates until it stops trembling. They want the world to be finished. They want it to sit still so they can pave it and run their carts.

I do not want it to finish.

I like the hour before the laws are set. I like the taste of the mist when it doesn't know whether to turn into rain or float sideways into the sunless violet sky. I like the long, lazy chime of an iron wrench that fell three hours ago from a dock in the Seventh Ward, drifting past my window right now—glowing with phosphorescent mold, perfectly weightless, beautiful and useless, belonging neither to the men who made it nor the stone that waits to crush it.

I reach a scarred hand out into the fog, catch the wrench between two fingers, and tuck it into the willow-weave of my wall, beside a hundred others. 

Then I press my ear to the wicker floor, close my eyes, and wait for the world to groan again.
