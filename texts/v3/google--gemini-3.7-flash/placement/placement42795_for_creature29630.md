At first, there is only the tension against the molars.

It is a hair-line of split sinew, no thicker than a moth’s antenna, clamped between the left premolars. Through it comes the whole weight of the western tier: the slow, damp settling of six slate roofs three inches below; the greasy, rhythmic shuffle of a wool-tick turning in its scoop; the microscopic hitch in the third bridge-stay where the river-mica has begun to fray under the frost.

I am not standing. In this cleft, to stand is to occupy space that belongs to a cistern or a moss-bale. 

I am suspended in a saddle of scraped shrew-hide, hung from a counterweighted anchor of river-pebble four feet above the canyon floor. The harness is stiff with willow-resin, molded so closely to the curve of my iliac crest that there is no boundary where the dead hide ends and my own milk-pale skin begins. My legs are drawn up, folded tight beneath me in the dry, unblinking air, knees dusted with the fine white talc of crushed limestone that never settles, only drifts from ledger to ledger on the non-existent breeze.

I am a Fitter. A joiner of the unyielding to the unyielding.

My hands hang before me in the shadowless glare. The skin of my fingers is smooth, cold, and entirely slick with the pale lipid that seeps from my knuckles—a thin, clear grease that keeps the chalk-flour from drawing out the deep marrow-damp of my flesh. My nails, square-cut and ground against flint until their bevels can cleave a seed-husk into quarters, are resting on a newly cured slat of bone.

It is a clavicle. It belonged to an elder of the water-watch whose tallow was rendered two moons ago. The bone has hung in the blinding white-gold upper lip of the gorge until the last greasiness was baked out by the sky, until it turned the bright, chalky white of an owl-pellet. It is clean. It has no scent now except the flat, mineral tang of rain that never fell.

With the scraper set into the ring on my thumb—a shrew’s incisor mounted in lead—I bevel the thicker head of the bone. *Scrape. Scrape.* The sound does not travel; the liverwort lining the crevice beneath my saddle drinks the vibration instantly, swallowing it into its wet, green felt. The bone shavings fall like dry bran, caught on the stiff skirt of my resin-tunic. They will not be dropped; when I descend, they will be stirred into the mortar-pots to thicken the chalk-slurry.

Under my breast-flap, nestled against the flat bone of my sternum, is a single down-mite. It is small, the size of a dry pea, its soft, plumose fleece pulsing against my ribs with a faint, oily warmth. It has no legs to speak of, only its round, blind heat, digesting a crumb of willow-mash I pressed into its palps at the third bell-flash. Without it, the lipid in my shoulder-joints would stiffen to suet within an hour, locking my burin-arm into a rigid, useless crook. I feel its slow, blind metabolism humming against my heart—a wet little furnace that smells of old seed and mutton-fat.

I do not think in words. There is no breath behind the teeth to give them shape, and the tongue is small, dry, a passive wedge kept moist only by a bead of lip-oil. 

Instead, thought exists as a series of calibrations. 

*Angle of the drop.* 
*Shear-strength of three-ply root.* 
*The thickness of the salt-crust on the south-facing gutters.*

A quartz flash hits my peripheral membrane—a single, sharp needle of white-gold light bouncing from the skull-perch of the Master Line-Keeper forty inches above. 

*Pulse.* 
*Pause.* 
*Pulse-pulse.*

*Cistern seven: clearance two hairs.*

I do not look up. To turn the neck is to shift the saddle, and to shift the saddle is to sway the hair-line currently anchored in my teeth. I flick my left shoulder, where a coin of cleaved mica sits on a ball-joint of mouse-femur. The sky-glare catches the face of the disk: a flash returned. *Received. Bracing now.*

I take the clavicle. I set it between the cleaved limestone face and the corner-post of a tenement where four families of slate-carvers sleep packed among their warm mites. The bone fits. It takes the lateral load of the terrace with a faint, dry *snick* that travels down my thumb, through the carpal bones, and into my jaw. 

The master-line between my teeth slacks by half a micron. The balance is restored. The stone settles into the dead man’s shoulder, and the dead man’s shoulder holds the stone away from the moss.

Below me, along the narrow ledges carved into the bedrock, an isopod crawls. Its domed back is polished like a black bead, the fine geometric lines of its lineage-cartouche white with lime-dust. It drags a sled of three flint shingles. Its driver, a youth whose skin is still the soft, translucent grey of uncurdled whey, walks behind it, their copper stylus poised half a grain above the left antenna, guiding the beast past the wet lip of a sump without a sound, without a breath, without a wasted step.

We look at each other through our cloudy, quartz-colored lids. Neither of us blinks. Neither of us raises a hand. The distance between us is eight inches of freezing, sterile light, vast and completely occupied by the vertical city.

I reach for my bandolier, draw a spool of spider-dragline steeped in pine-pitch, and begin to lash the clavicle to the root-stay. My chisel-nails work with the rapid, unthinking precision of water grooving chalk. 

Someday, when the lipid in my palms dries to flake and the quartz of my eyes clouds into final, milky blindness, they will unhook my saddle. They will boil my tunic to recover the resin; they will scrape my hide-trousers back into parchment; they will render the small, remaining grease of my flesh to fill the wick-crucibles of the lower alleys. And my own long, straight radius—the one that now guides the needle with such steady, unswerving pressure—will be split with a flint wedge, bleached in the white sky, and notched to serve as the rafter for a wool-mite pen.

It is a good rafter. It has been kept straight by forty winters of tension. It will hold the roof true against the condensation, and it will not rot.

I pull the dragline taut with my teeth, bite the knot clean, and wait for the next flash from the heights.
