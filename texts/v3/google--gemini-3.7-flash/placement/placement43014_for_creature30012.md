At first, there is only the sensation of two different temperatures meeting across a ridge of gristle.

On the left flank, where the cast iron of the housing stays hot enough to turn water to an instantaneous, oily hiss, the skin is thick, dry, and charred to the consistency of horn. On the right, toward the open air of the vault, the surface is wet—constantly washed by the lukewarm, tea-colored drainage that drips from the rim of an overhead casing.

I am not on the catwalks, nor am I hanging from the copper runs. I am lower, settled into the damp, concave hollow beneath a dead eccentric rod, wedged between the bedplate and the massive bronze bushing of a sleeve-bearing that hasn't turned a full revolution since the earth above was cold.

It takes a long time to understand where the body ends and the iron begins. 

There are no distinct edges. Over seventy or eighty years in this same trough, the underside of my belly has taken the exact reverse casting of the machine’s foundational bolts: three hex-head indentations, two inches deep, lined with calloused gray skin that has grown down into the threads to anchor against the heave of the floor. My weight is considerable—not the broad, crushing mass of a Gasket-Bow, but something denser, narrower, shaped like an iron pig or an ingot left behind in a mold. 

I do not have the calipers of the Crawlers or the flat, creeping perimeter of the Pellicles. My limbs are short, thick spuds of fused bone, three on each side, ending in flat wedges of dense, oily keratin. They are not built for walking; they are jacks. When the heavy stroke comes from below, they stiffen instinctively, lifting the central mass of my torso an eighth of an inch off the wet masonry to let the sudden surge of boiling slurry clear beneath me, then settling back down into the grease before the steam can thin.

I live on what the larger things shed and what the iron weeps.

Above me, on the intermediate deck, a Gasket-Bow has spent forty years turning bruised-yellow leaves into sulfur paste. The overflow of that slow fermentation—a thick, bitter, iron-stained liquor rich in tallow and unspent starch—trickles down a vertical drainpipe and spills over the shoulder of the casing. It runs across my dorsal plates. 

I do not have a head in the way surface beasts do; there is no neck to turn, no gaze to direct. Along the upper crest of my spine, where the hide is scored with deep, lateral gutters, are rows of porous, vascular pits. As the Bow’s drainage washes down the iron, it catches in these grooves. The porous bone drinks the grease directly, filtering the fatty tallow into a layer of insulating marrow that lines my ribcage, while the gritty sediment—fine black flakes of magnetite and dried vegetable wax—is worked backward by rhythmic, involuntary contractions of the skin, eventually hardening into rough, protective studs along my flanks.

The world is measured not in light, but in the viscosity of the oil.

When the deep subterranean heart beats—that distant, skull-splitting *thoom-clack* that shudders through the anchor bolts—the liquid in the sleeve-bearing jumps. For twenty seconds after the strike, the grease runs thin and hot, almost sweet, scented with the deep earth’s banked coals. During this interval, I open the narrow breathing-slits beneath my jaw-plate, drawing in the hot air that has been squeezed out of the cylinder packings. It tastes of burnt lead and dead moss, heavy enough to chew.

Then the cooling begins. The oil thickens to the texture of cold lard. The copper lines overhead go rigid again. The hiss of the bleed-off valves returns to its dull, flat roar, and my pulse—a single, heavy contraction of a three-chambered muscular bulb situated deep between my pelvic bones—slows until it matches the drainage cycle of the housing above.

I am a wedge. I am a living shim driven into the gap where the machine has settled unevenly on its foundations. 

Sometimes, across three or four cycles of the subterranean piston, I can feel the slow, blind dragging of a Pellicle passing over my lower flank. It moves without haste, its micro-cilia rasping the wax from my outer plates as if I were merely another casting of rough-poured iron. I do not shrug it off; its cool, wet mass keeps the left-hand hide from baking through to the meat, and in return, the small heat of my fermentation keeps its margins from stiffening in the dead-air pocket behind the flywheel.

We do not communicate. There are no sounds to make that could compete with the groan of the compression cylinders or the steady, concussive thrum of the floor. But there is a shared tension. When the pressure in the manifold drops—when the stroke is late by even twenty seconds—the change is felt through every joint and rivet in the vault. The Bow ceases its grinding; the Crawlers tighten their horn-toothed calipers until the copper grooves bite into their bellies; the Pellicle thins itself into a film no thicker than paper, clinging to the cold iron sweat.

And I, down in the sump, press my flat bone-wedges harder against the bedplate, waiting for the bedrock to lift, waiting for the hot lead taste of the grease to return, keeping the dead eccentric from settling that final half-inch into the stone.
