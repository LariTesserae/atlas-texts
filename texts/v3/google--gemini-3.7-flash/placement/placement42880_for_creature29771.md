It begins not as a shape, but as a distribution of weight.

To exist here is to have no use for a boundary that separates the meat from the mineral. If you look for me at first, you might look in the wrong scale—searching for eyes where there has never been a photon to receive, or listening for a voice where the only grammar is the hydrostatic compression of water falling against itself.

I would not be on the outside of the rock, nor wholly inside the stone. I would find myself in the *interstitial film*—the warm, lipid-thick meniscus suspended between the razor-edge of the aragonite needles and the soft, creeping mass of the things that browse upon them.

At first, I am only the tension. 

I am spread across the chamber like an unbroken coat of living varnish. I feel the ceiling’s hydraulic rhythm—that slow, wet *chuff* of the bladder-sacs expelling their de-mineralized jets—not as sound, but as an alternating pulse of air-density that presses my surface flat against the crystal facets, then lets it swell again. 

I am the caustic slipperiness under the foot of the translucent gastropod. When its pale mantle flings down its mineralized gel, it does not do so alone; I am the solvent that allows its slow, etching acid to bite into the unblunted tips of the stone teeth without tearing its own clear flesh. I taste the calcium carbonate as it dissolves; I taste the sulfurous broth churning in the velvet mats below. I am broad, continuous, and thin as a cell wall, riding the continuous sheet of hot condensate as it creeps down the walls toward the lower terraces.

Yet, as the heat pools and the steam thickens, I find my center drawing downward. 

I do not remain diffuse. The chamber has a grain, a subtle thermal drift that pulls every floating molecule of ancient carbon toward the deep dead-end crevice—toward the place where the stone ceases to be purely accidental.

Here, wedged in the dark bottleneck, is where I become dense.

I am the interface between the petrified heartwood and the creature that sleeps upon it.

Where the great lard-white siphon loops its heavy, ten-foot coil through the mineral-sheathed rungs, there is a space that is neither animal nor timber nor geology. It is a slow, pressurized sleeve of organic grease, mineral milk, and ancient dissolved sap. That is where I settle. That is what I am.

I am the creature’s underside and the ladder’s skin at the exact point of their centuries-long weld.

I feel the enormous, sluggish rhythm of the siphon’s gut: a furnace-thrum of billions of sulfur-eating microbes turning the warm fog into dense, pale fat. I feel the microscopic tremor of its filter-papillae as they brush the flowstone cascade, drinking the rhythmic *tink-tink-plat* of the drops. Its weight is immense, soft, and unhurried—pressing down upon me with the blind, neutral trust of an organism that has never known a predator, a season, or a fall.

And beneath me, pressed into my lower layer through the transparent glass of the flowstone, lies the wood. 

The heartwood does not live, but it *remembers* structure. It carries the memory of an axe-stroke, the split of grain forced by an iron or stone wedge, the twist of the bark cordage that held the shale slabs together before the calcite turned them into a single, seamless tooth. The wood still leaches, in infinitesimal fractions over centuries, a faint, bitter tannin—the dark blood of a forest that grew under an open sky thousands of feet below and thousands of years ago.

I drink that tannin from below, and I feed it to the siphon above.

I am the medium of their communion. Through me, the blind lung-siphon absorbs the last dissolving traces of the hands that built its cradle. It does not know what a hand is; it knows only that here, at the junction of the notched logs, the water is sweeter, dense with ancient carbon and complex plant-sugars that do not exist in the raw mineral condensate of the ceiling.

I have no limbs to move, but I do not need them; the chamber moves through me. Every drop of condensed steam that sheets down the spires carries a microscopic freight of white bacterial pelt and shed snail-mucus, funneling inevitably into the crevice where I sit between the wood and the worm. I am constantly accreting, constantly sloughing, shedding calcified crusts from my lower edges into the rimstone floor while drawing fresh, warm lipids into my upper surface from the siphon’s pale flank.

I am the living joint of the mountain.

If the wall behind the crawlway ever broke—if the dry, cold wind of the peaks were to tear through the suture and clear the warm fog—the aragonite needles would shatter, the bacterial mats would wither into dry flakes, and the great white siphon would burst under the sudden, caustic drought of the sky. 

But here, sealed within the unblinking mass, there is no sky. There is only the steady 104 degrees of the deep rock, the ceaseless falling of drops that never miss their mark, and the slow, blind digestion of the past, held safe in the dark between the stone, the wood, and the flesh.
