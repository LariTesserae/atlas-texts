At first, there is only the sensation of curvature meeting curvature: the thick, yellow rind of the sole nesting into the worn hollow of a tile, so exact a fit that the foot does not feel separate from the floor, merely cooler where the draft slips under the arch, and warmer where the kiln-breath pulses beneath the bed.

I would not be one of the swift-catchers in the high, screaming light-shafts, nor one of the children tapping reeds against the sump-rims to turn the crabs. I find myself lower down, where the heat thickens and the sound goes dry and tight—squatting on my haunches against a third-tier buttress, my spine settled into the gentle, habitual bend that makes sitting on a flat stone impossible.

I am an elder mender, though here the word is not about age so much as how much brick-dust has replaced the grease of the skin. My knuckles are permanently orange-grey, the color of wet river-silt mixed with pounded potsherds. In the hemp sash around my waist, there is the familiar clack of my tools: three plum-wood ribs, their curves rubbed thin and glass-slick by forty seasons of smoothing green slip over shifting joints, and a flat horn spoon for scooping dry grog from the pouch at my hip.

My craft is not to build—nothing here is built from nothing anymore—but to keep the borough from weeping onto itself or drying into brittle powder. 

Mostly, I listen. 

The air here does not travel in lines; it coils. To know what the borough is doing, you do not look—the light from the pierced lanterns is only ever a scatter of bright coin-spots through the dust—you put the side of your skull to the brick. 

If I lay my cheek against the plaster of this pier, just below the worn hollow where my grandfather pressed his hands into the wet mud sixty years ago, I can hear the entire machine digesting.

First comes the shallow, frantic rhythm: the nails of the swifts scrabbling in their clay cups six cubits up, a dry, papery fluttering that tells me the upper draft is open to the sun. 

Beneath that, deeper in the wall, is the soft, hollow *ting-ting* of the casing-dog’s copper bell. It is working its way through the dead space behind the third flue, its loose skin sliding over the rough backs of the tiles as it noses out the black cave-crickets. If the bell stops for too long, I tap three times with the butt of my plum-wood paddle: *tock-tock-tock*. Two paces higher, the bell answers with a brief, nervous chime, and the animal squeezes through another narrow baffle, keeping the passages clear of webs and shed husks.

And deepest of all, through the marrow of the buttress, is the water. 

A vault is only as good as its runnels. If a seam shifts by the width of a hair under the weight of the thirty cubits of desert pressing from above, the condensation does not bead into the channel; it gathers in the belly of the arch, rots the lime, and drops onto the sleeping benches below in dirty, brown spatters. My days are measured in thumb-widths. I trace the weeping curves with my palm, feeling for the cold spot where the draft is cutting across the slip too fast. 

Where the water hesitates, I take the narrow corner of my wooden rib and carve. A single, clean stroke, no deeper than the nail of a little finger, leading the brown drop down the swell of the arch, around the corner of a sleeping niche, and into the floor trough where the lime crust grows like white moss.

When the batons strike the flues across the borough—a dull, wooden *clump-clump* vibrating through the clay ducts—I don't need to turn my head. The pitch tells me the northern vents are drawing too hot; the draft is drying the moss in the mortar joints before it can drink. 

I reach into my pouch, take a pinch of dry, pulverized tile, spit into my palm, and roll the paste between my palms until it is the thickness of a blindworm. 

I speak to no one; there is no need. My tongue stays behind my teeth, making only the small, dry clicks that tell the round-bellied girl passing with her basket of boiled crabs that I need her to leave the dregs of her rinse-jar. She sets the pot down without a scrape, her broad, splayed toes gripping the wet glaze of the runnel with the same easy weight as my own.

I will work this seam until the light dowels through the lantern fade from ember-red to the deep, soot-colored twilight of night. Then I will tuck my rib back into my sash, climb three rungs of the cane ladder into the bread-oven niche where the swift-down is thick and warm, and fold my knees to my chest. 

Behind my head, through six inches of baked mud, the casing-dog will curl in its dry, hollow channel, its bell muffled against its ribs. Below me, the water will tick in the runnel, drop by drop, reliable as a pulse. And when my own joints finally set into the hardness of the floor, they will mix the silt, fold my arms, and plaster me into the pier beside the others, where the heat of the flues will bake my bones into the very arch that keeps the sky from falling.
