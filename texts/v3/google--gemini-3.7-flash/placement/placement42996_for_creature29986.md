I do not find myself standing on the red floor, nor walking the ramp of scree. To enter this place from above—to carry the dry turbulence of the plateau down into the bowl—would tear the thin skin of heat and stillness that holds it together.

I am already down here, settled into the narrowest margin of the alcove: the dark, hairline fissure that separates the underside of the creature’s lower jaw from the pale limestone bench it rests upon.

At first, there is almost no distinction between being a creature and being a condition of the stone. I am the thin, dry stratum where keratin meets calcite—a fine, cellular crust, no thicker than a moth’s wing, that has grown into the micro-pores of both surfaces over centuries of unmoving contact. 

I am not stone, though my outer edges have taken in so much pulverized arenite and iron oxide that I share its dull, rust-red tint. And I am not part of the beast, though my underside is anchored to the soft, spongy grain of its lower lip by thousands of microscopic, glass-clear filaments that drink what little condensation escapes its cavernous mouth.

If I have a body, it is distributed and flat, shaped entirely by the weight resting upon it. I exist as a living gasket between the animal and the earth. 

My world is bounded on the top by the immense, warm curve of the throat-plate—a ceiling of dark, fibrous horn that lifts by half a grain’s width once every hour, and on the bottom by the cold, pale limestone that drinks the beast’s weight without yielding. When the creature draws in its long, subterranean breath, the pressure eases infinitesimally; the microscopic filaments expand, sipping the alkaline moisture that seeps from its mouth. When it exhales, the immense mass settles back down, pinning me into the stone with a steady, dry warmth that drives out all cold, all hurry, all possibility of motion.

I have no eyes. I do not need to see the tea-colored light that fills the chapel above; I know its intensity by the expansion of the sandstone wall fifty paces away, which hums with a faint, mineral tension as it stores the sun, and releases it back into the floor when the plateau above turns toward night. 

I know the mantle-moth not by sight, but by the minute alteration in the air’s humidity whenever it shifts an inch along its damp seam of wall. I know the tiny, six-legged builders in the shallow basin because every few centuries, as their mineralized bodies raise the basin’s lip, the water level changes by the width of a hair, and the faint, iron-scented draft that washes across the alcove floor shifts its angle against the limestone blocks.

Mostly, existence is the pulse. 

It comes every several minutes—not a sound, but a heavy, low-frequency shudder that travels through the beast’s breastbone, into the pale limestone, through my own thin, mineralized tissues, and down into the bedrock of the canyon. It is the only true measure of time here: the heart of the tenant driving its thick, iron-heavy blood through limbs that will not move for another four seasons.

I do not wish for the creature to wake. When it stirs—when the shudder in its legs begins and its chin slowly drags free of the limestone notch—I am torn away in tiny, dry flakes, ground into the pale dust of the curb. But I do not die of this. My spores remain in the pitted face of the limestone, sleeping through the days of its slow crawl to the water, waiting in the dry air while it drinks from the knuckle-deep basin. 

And when it returns, turning its great, heavy body with the sluggish momentum of the earth itself, and lowers its chin back into the familiar groove, its weight presses me back together. The keratin finds the calcite; the warmth returns; the filaments reach out once more to drink the slow, damp breath of the deep stone. 

And the stillness closes over us again, seamless and complete, while from the ceiling, five minutes of eternity away, the next drop forms and falls.
