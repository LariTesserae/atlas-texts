At first, there is only the sensation of pressure against the sternum. 

Not sound in the way ears receive it—long ago, the tympanum thickened, scarred over by the endless, seventy-knot scouring of warm vapor, until hearing became something felt through the jaw, the collarbone, the balls of the heels. The world does not speak; it hammers. It is an octave so low it rearranges the fluids in the belly, rising out of the root of the pillar like the groan of an iron mountain being slowly twisted by giants.

To find me, you must look where the stone ceases to be an obstacle and becomes an interior. 

I would not be out in the pure blue drift with the *Cete-veils*, though there is a terrible, heart-stopping beauty to their boneless yaw across the thermals. To be a Drifter is to be eternal ocean-drift, never knowing the resistance that gives a thing its shape. Nor would I be deep in the drowned silt of the dead-nodes where the families huddle in acoustic silence, tapping their quiet, frightened codes into each other's wrists while the grease rots on their skin.

I am wedged precisely at the lip: the place where the *Node* gives way to the throat of a whistling bore. 

Here, the air is a dense, living animal, warm as blood, smelling of fermented spore-dust, crushed lichen, and the sharp, wet iron of the stone. My feet are bare, the soles broad and ridged like bark, wedged into a parabolic groove worn smooth by ten thousand years of condensation and the passage of creatures before me. My skin is the color of dried brick, sealed under layers of red ochre and the yellow, rancid tallow of a Drifter’s trailing skirt. The gale does not chill; it coats everything in a swift, slick sweat that runs down my ribs in warm threads before the updraft whips it off into the sunlit void.

My second eyelids—the pale, milky curtains—are half-drawn against the tearing mist. Through them, the world is a churning kaleidoscope of blinding emerald and blue-grey vapor. Clouds do not drift here; they detonate upward, shredded against the parabolic prows of the *Coriath*, throwing sudden, flashing shafts of sunlight across the void that vanish before the pulse can settle.

What am I? 

I am a **Flute-Tender**, a reader of the stone’s tension. 

In my right hand, lashed to my wrist by three cords of braided vine-gut, is a hammer of dense, black river-gneiss, smoothed into an egg that fits the hollow of my palm. I do not use it to break the rock. I tap. 

*Clack. Clack-clack.*

I lean my forehead against the damp rock wall and wait for the iron-vein to answer. 

Through the bridge of my nose, through the teeth in my skull, the spire tells its daily story. A crack opening four hundred cubits down on the windward shear, where the weight of a giant bromeliad bladder is prying a slab loose; the low, rhythmic buffet of a leviathan grazing the acoustic cushion three miles out, its vast cartilage ribs displacing thousands of tons of rushing air; the high, frantic *ping-ping-ping* of a hunter’s hammer two spires over, signaling that a drift-line has snagged and meat is coming up the drop.

I live in the between-spaces. My back is anchored by two drop-cables, their bone-hooks wedged deep into the fossilized root-tunnels of ancient ferns. If both hooks fail, there is no panic—only the long, weightless arc out into the sunlit abyss, the sudden silence as the rock drops away, and the warm, welcoming updraft that holds a body like a lover for three beats of the heart before letting it slide down into the floorless blue.

Beside me, in the sheltered scoop of the flute’s leeward lip, a colony of Harp-Gnats has strung their web of resin lines across a narrow cleft. They hum at a sharp, piercing D-sharp, shivering so fast they look like a smear of amber light. Every few moments, a speck of scud-plankton or a tiny, wind-tattered beetle strikes the strings with a faint *thrum*, and the blind gnats dart across the singing web to harvest the paste. 

Sometimes, I reach out with a grease-coated thumb and take a share of the mash, pressing the sweet, oily protein against the roof of my mouth. It tastes of cloud-water, fern pollen, and sunlight.

I do not wish for the ground. The stories the old ones tap into the rock—of a time before the world was fluted, when earth was flat and water sat still in broad, stagnant sheets upon the mud—sound like a suffocating nightmare. To be trapped on a floor, where the air does not carry you, where every step is heavy and the sky is an empty ceiling rather than an ocean you can lean your whole weight against? That is the place where breath goes to die.

Here, matter is light, precious, and singing. The stone is alive with sound; the air is thick with food; the drop below is not an end, but the great, warm lung that exhales us into being. 

A shadow sweeps across the cavern mouth—huge, silent, blotting out the bright tropospheric glare. I feel the barometric drop in the pit of my stomach before I see the sweep of cedar-grained skin: a *Cete-veil*, forty meters of undulating silk and lipid-bone, banking hard off the acoustic wall of my spire. It passes so close I can smell the clean, sweet slime of its hull; I can see the plate-sized Kite-fleas scuttling across its ventral ridges.

I do not reach for a harpoon. I simply press my palms flat against the vibrating gneiss, close my milky lids, and let the giant wave of displaced air hit me, laughing silently into the roar as the wind pins me safe and hard against the bones of the world.
