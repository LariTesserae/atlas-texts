To begin, there is no waking. Waking implies a dawn, a shift in pressure, the wet heave of a chest pulling in air. 

Here, there is only the resumption of friction.

I am folded into my hollow in the shale. For fifty years—or perhaps eighty, for the glass-dust on my upper crest has settled to the depth of three mica grains—I have not shifted. In that absolute stillness, my naphtha sits inert within the horn vessels of my abdomen, heavy as mercury, cool and unmoving. I do not have a heartbeat to measure the interval; without movement, there is no pump. My consciousness during these spans is not sleep, but a state of total geometric calibration: my two quartz eyes staring unblinkingly at the opposite facet of chert, registering the slow, micro-millimeter creep of an amber microbial shadow beneath a cluster of purple scales.

Then, a pitch changes.

High along my spine, the two curved forks of bone catch a discordant frequency. It is thin, sharper than the harpsichord roar of the spun-glass groves—a drop of half a semitone where a Scritcher has rasped through a heavy hydrocarbon stem forty paces to the left. The tension of the air alters. The chamber’s deafening chord, which holds the walls apart, shivers.

The tuning fork resonates down through my occipital ridge. The signal engages the first thoracic joint.

*Chrr-tick.*

The pale cartilage plates beneath my belly slide across one another with the dry, crisp clatter of shale flakes cascading down a chute. The segment contracts. The mechanical pump bites: a measure of dense, golden oil is forced through my interior ducts. Heat flushes through my joints. The second segment slides. The third. The naphtha begins to circulate, thick and silent, driven entirely by the unfolding of my body.

I step out of the hollow.

The floor is a nightmare of fresh geometry, but to the felt pads beneath my vitreous claws, it is an open ledger. My feet do not touch the sharp edges; they rest in the negative spaces, settling between the razor cleavages of obsidian with the precise memory of an organism that has walked the exact same twelve strides for four centuries. 

I make no sound. The chamber makes all the sound there is. 

It is magnificent and terrible, this roar. It does not hit the eardrums as noise, but as a physical weight—a massive, harmonic vibration that rattles through the horn plates of my carapace, hums through the bone of my limbs, and sings inside the hollow spaces of my skull. To live here is to be permanently clamped within the soundbox of a massive instrument whose strings are endlessly bowed by the breath of the planet’s mantle.

I move toward the clearing. 

My purpose today is the same purpose I held before I settled into the wall: a slab of gray chert rests upon three balanced pedestals of basalt. Beside it sits a punch made of rose quartz, its tip worn to a dull facet over three generations of Lithodact hands.

I sit. The seven plates of my back lock together with a hollow *clack*, forming a rigid tripod with my hind limbs so that my front digits are freed from bearing weight. 

I do not look at the chert with soft curiosity. My quartz lenses do not see color as a wash; they refract the ambient bruised amber of the microbial fronds, turning the stone’s surface into a hard-edged relief map of stress lines, fracture cones, and crystallographic axes. The rock is under tension. It has been waiting ten thousand years to split along a plane no wider than an oil-molecule.

I pick up the punch. Its dry felt binding rustles against the hard enamel of my palm. 

I place the tip against the stone. Under the collar at the back of my neck, my plectrum shifts against the second thoracic rim. I draw it back. 

*Tch-tch-tkkkk.*

Across the clearing, twenty paces away, another Lithodact pauses where it is gathering wax pellets dropped by a grazing herd of Scritchers. It does not turn its head—it cannot, its neck is a fused sleeve of dense horn—but its tuning forks sweep back. It stridulates an answer: a single, dry click, acknowledging the angle of my strike.

I bring my dorsal horn down against the butt of the punch.

The impact is clean. A flake of chert detaches, falling to the floor with a bell-like chime that is instantly swallowed by the roar of the glass brush. The edge left behind is impossibly straight, perfect, and sharp enough to divide light.

I do not smile; I have no lips, only the sealed, enameled plates of a jaw that exists solely to intake the dehydrated nutrient paste we distill once every decade from crushed mica-oil. But within the central channel of my torso, where the naphtha runs clear and warm, there is a deep, static satisfaction.

The flake is true. It will take another seventy years to shape the remaining four teeth of the scraper. There is no haste. The ceiling will not fall; the water will not come to rot the stone; the air will not thin. 

Beside the wall, in the fourth hollow from mine, rests one of our elders. Its plates are gray with a thick accumulation of glass aerosol. Its felt pads wore away three hundred years ago, and its naphtha has long since polymerized into a solid, aromatic resin within its chest. It sits curled, its quartz eyes dark, its body permanently tuned to the fundamental drone of the chamber. 

Sometimes, when the convection currents rise and the glass ferns shiver at their highest pitch, the elder’s carapace vibrates in perfect sympathetic harmony, singing back to the room with a dry, hollow resonance that matches the sound of the world being born.

I set the punch against the stone again. I wait for the vibration to steady in my forks. I strike.
