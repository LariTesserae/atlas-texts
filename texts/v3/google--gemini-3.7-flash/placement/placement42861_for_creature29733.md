I would not be on the open plain, where the sledges grind their slow quartzite mirrors beneath the moss, nor could I be the central marrow of the Consensus, whose single, enormous lung holds the balance of the air. Those are positions of total scale, and scale on Oor has a way of flattening awareness into pure geology.

I would be found in the drystone avenues, but not standing upright. 

At first, you might look for me as a shadow that has failed to slide away when the light shifted—a dark, rectangular thickening caught beneath a three-ton lintel where two five-story tenements lean toward one another without touching. 

I am wedged into the *shear* itself.

My body is not a single pillar, nor is it soft like the pellicles on the cisterns. It is a long, low, jointed thing—dense as tallow-cured heartwood, the color of wet lead—spread horizontally through the dry courses of the masonry. Because space has unbraided along the mortarless joints, I do not occupy a continuous line. 

My chest lies flat across a flagstone on the lowest terrace, cool and drenched in the sweating grease of the rock. Yet the long, paddle-ended limbs that extend from my sides do not rest on the same street: two of them reach outward into an unglazed window three levels above, their broad gray palms pressed against the sill to steady a wall that is currently leaning into yesterday’s wind; two others are trailed three hundred paces behind me, dangling over the lip of an alley that returns upon its own ceiling like an inverted stair.

I feel no tearing in this. There is no distance between my ribs and my hands, only the smooth, cold bend of a world that was folded before it was finished. 

What am I? 

I think I am a *seam-tender*—one of the things that formed in the stone cities when the original builders laid down their antler picks and walked away from the half-hewn blocks. 

I do not have a face to turn toward the ink-dark zenith, nor do I have ears to register the strange, broken acoustic of the fog. Instead, my whole underside is a sensitive, bristle-lined belly that reads the pressure of the masonry. I am a living spirit-level for a city whose geometry has slipped its peg.

Every four minutes, when the distant black tundras swell and the great gray marrow in the central plaza draws down the chimney-smoke, the entire metropolis shifts. It is an infinitesimal settling—the weight of millions of tons of dry slate groaning on their contact points. When that breath comes, a vast, low vibration travels through the stone. It passes through my breastbone, through my joints, through the long, stiffened tendons that connect my palms on the third floor to my heels in the gutter.

In that four-minute pulse, my purpose occurs:

I secrete.

From the pores along my flanks, where the gray skin is rough as wet carborundum, I press out tiny, oily beads of clear tallow. I do not do this to nourish anything, but to grease the seams where the space is sliding. Where a stone tenement wants to sheer completely through itself and drop into an unreachable fold of the plateau, my oil lets the stone glide half a millimeter past its neighbor without shattering the drystone interlock. 

I keep the city from binding. I am the thin, living grease between the megaliths.

When the twice-daily iron fog rolls in and the falling stones go silent, I loosen my grip. With a slow, hydraulic drag—like an anchor being drawn through cold silt—I lever my long mass forward through the gaps between the lintels. 

I travel toward the quarry at the city's hem. 

I do not walk across the moss; I move through the overlaps. A slide of my fore-claws through a slit in an interior chimney brings my middle segments out into the open air beside the split-pits. There, among the half-freed slabs, the Pillarmen are already standing in soundless congregation, their lead-bark hides gray against the white sun.

I coil my length around the base of a great granitic slab that has waited fifty thousand years to be tipped from its bed. 

With the blunt, spatula-tips of my extremities, I reach into the narrow groove where the wooden wedges sit. The wood is ancient, split-grained oak, swollen black with condensation. I do not drive the wedge deeper—that would finish the cut, and if the cut were finished, the builders might never return to find it as they left it. Nor do I allow the wedge to rot or dry. I press my cold palm against the grain, transferring the oily sweat of the city’s deep joints into the heart of the timber, keeping it tense, keeping it wet, keeping it forever on the verge of splitting the mountain in two.

In the quarry, under the raw indigo sky that never turns blue, we wait out the fog. 

The Pillarmen stand above me like uncarved steles. Beneath us, through miles of slate, we can feel the sledges planing the bedrock smooth, one grain of quartzite at a time, moving to the slow, four-minute gasp of the black moss. 

I close my bristles against the iron vapor. I press my weight flat into the groove between the stone and the wedge, content with the cold, content with the absence of an echo, holding the unfinished work suspended between the strike of the mallet and the falling of the block, for as long as the Shelf remembers how to breathe.
