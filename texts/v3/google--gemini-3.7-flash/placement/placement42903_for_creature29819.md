I would not be at the crown, where the wax-beetles work under the open scour of the sky, nor down in the sulfur pools where the water finally gives up its work and falls away. 

I would be in the throat of it—somewhere in the lower third of the cleft, where the hydraulic pressure is at its most dangerous and the living wood is forced into direct, stubborn contact with the sheared slate.

I find myself wedged into a compression pocket where three great boles have been grafted across a fault in the rock. The stone behind me is wet, sharp-edged, and dead-cold; it smells of pulverized pyrite and old snow. The wood in front of me is three feet thick, rounded, smooth as worn river-horn, and perpetually warm with the slow, thick friction of its own inner fluids. I sit with my spine curved against the slate and the soles of my prehensile feet braced hard against the cambium, suspended thirty paces above the drainage runnels.

I am one of the listeners, but not the oldest—not yet one of the great Tension-Tasters whose bones have begun to calcify into the stillness of the perches. I am younger, still flexible enough to wedge my shoulders into the fissures where a runner-stem is slipping its bindings, yet heavy enough in the chest that the thin air feels like home.

My skin is the dull, tallowy white of a peeled switch. Over my forearms and the curve of my ribs runs that fine, silvery pelt—short as velvet, so dense that the perpetual mountain fog gathers upon it in discrete, icy pearls without ever soaking through to the blood beneath. When I lift an arm, the beads run down the channels of my tendons and drop off my elbows in cold, clean pellets.

I would have very little to carry:
A horn gourd at my hip, its interior greasy with the sour, pine-bitter paste of crushed liverwort and auxin-rich gall-sap; a bundle of tough, cured sinew rolled around a split bone peg; and an awl ground from the femur of an old water-runner, worn smooth where my thumb rests.

Mostly, I am still.

In this cleft, you do not move until the wood invites you to. To walk hastily across a suspended tension-root when the upland seeps have surged is to miss the warning frequency—the faint, dry *tink* of over-stretched cellulose that precedes a rupture. And so I spend long spans of time doing what looks like nothing: resting my forehead, my sternum, and my flattened palms directly against the wet, liverwort-clad bark.

Through the bone of my forehead, the city is not quiet. It is an engine of immense, muffled torque. 

I can feel the vast siphon-pull three levels above me, where the water is being drawn into the residential nodes; it registers as a rhythmic, dragging pulse, like a heavy wet rope being hauled across gravel. Lower down, right beneath the balls of my feet, I can feel the quick, shiver-like flutter of the flume-eels shifting against the interior walls of a major main. They are grouping together, shingling their pale bodies over a scour-mark where the water hammer has begun to fray the inner lining. When they align, the vibration smooths out from a harsh rattle to a greasy, continuous hum. 

I exhale. The breath leaves my mouth in a sudden white plume, catches in the sub-canopy twilight, and settles into the moss beside my knee.

A split has opened near my right shoulder. It is small—a hairline rupture in the bark where a structural arch has twisted half an inch under the weight of an unseasonal rime-crust on the upper canopy. The gap weeps a thin, sticky resin that smells of menthol and iron.

I do not hurry. I unhook the gourd with one hand, use my teeth to pull the wooden stopper, and scoop a dollop of the grey-green graft-paste with my middle finger. I work it deep into the split, pressing hard until the warmth of my hand softens the wax and drives it into the torn grain. Then I take a fresh, flexible whip-shoot—no thicker than my little finger, trained down from an upper lateral branch—and bind it tight across the wound with three turns of wet sinew, knotted off with a sharp downward tug of my toes.

In four months, the shoot will fuse with the parent bole. In two years, the split will be gone, replaced by a raised, braided knot of grain twice as thick as the wood around it.

When the work is done, I drink. I do not carry water; to carry water here would be like carrying stones to a quarry. Instead, I find a weeping nodule—a small, swollen eye in the bark where the phloem runs shallow—and pierce the seal with the tip of my awl. A single, heavy bead of sap wells up, cloudy and pale as tallow. 

I take it onto my tongue. It is intensely cold at first, numbing the roof of my mouth with the sharp, medicinal burn of wintergreen and raw wood-sugar, but as it goes down it flares into a steady, localized heat behind my breastbone. My heart slows by two beats a minute. The cold in my fingers recedes.

Below me, in the deep root-beds anchored into the rubble, I know whose ribs are currently feeding the arch I sit upon. It was an elder named Sorn, who taught me how to read the tension in a runner-shoot by tapping it with the back of a fingernail. He went into the taproot node two seasons ago, sewn in with peat and wet moss. Now, when I lay my ear against this junction, the wood does not hum with Sorn’s voice—it hums with the stiffness he added to the truss, holding the rock walls apart so the rest of us have room to breathe.

I pull my knees up to my chin, tuck my arms inside the beaten-felt vest cured in spruce-tar, and lean back into the hollow between the bark and the shale. The mist creeps in thicker now, turning the bruised green twilight into slate-grey wool. 

Above, the high wind hits the ravine, a dull, freight-train roar that never reaches down here. The living city shifts half an inch beneath me, groaning once through all its millions of joints, settles, and holds. I close my eyes, feel the pulse of the water mains against the back of my skull, and listen for the next change in the grain.
