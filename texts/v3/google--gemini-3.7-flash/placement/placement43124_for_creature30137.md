I am not standing on the high, open flags, nor am I riding the wide thermal drafts above the terraces. 

I would find myself down in the seams—the wet, narrow spaces where the lever meets the fulcrum, where the air smells of crushed gypsum and the cold, mutton-fat tallow of our own dead.

I am one of the Hands, but not of the great carrying lines. My shoulders are not broad enough for the shale-chains, and my ribs—thin as dry wicker beneath milk-white skin—would crush under the corner of a basalt lintel. 

Instead, I am the *feeler*. I am the one who works between the faces of the stones before the ropes pull taut.

Look at my fingers: they are nine inches from palm-heel to tip, pale as peeled willow, with three knuckles where a surface creature has two. They are scarred with white, crescent-shaped nicks where flakes of flint have bitten them, but the pads are soft—scrubbed daily with damp silt so the nerves stay open to the stone. 

When a five-tier terrace has stood for thirty hours, the weeping from the ceiling begins to do its quiet, murderous work. The liquid gypsum does not pool; it creeps. It crawls into the dry micro-gaps between the stacked blocks like frost across a windowpane. It starts as a slick, greasy film, then granulates into tiny, sharp needles of calcite that lock block to block. If those needles knit completely, the building ceases to be an arrangement of movable weights; it becomes a single, dead tooth welded to the bedrock shelf, impossible to flake away without weeks of crude, ruinous chipping.

My work is to push my hands into the dark between the slabs before that knitting finishes.

I kneel on the wet flagstones, the calluses of my flat feet pressed hard against the shelf. Through my soles, the hollow is never quiet: I feel the slow, hydraulic paddle of the Ounces grinding through the gutter two tiers down; I feel the shuddering release of a parapet collapsing three hundred yards to the north; I feel the synchronized, rhythmic stomp of forty masons shifting their weight to heave a cedar-roller into place. The whole valley is an immense drumhead, and my bones are tuned to its skin.

My hand goes in to the wrist, then to the elbow, sliding into the cool, dark slit between twenty tons of stacked basalt. 

In the dark, there is no light from the selenite seams overhead, only the cold, wet press of the rock. My fingertips skate along the seam. I am looking for the *crust*. 

If the stone is slick and bare, I tap twice with my middle knuckle against the upper face: *clear.*
If I feel the fine, gritty fur of new calcite taking hold—the microscopic teeth beginning to grip—I rap four times in a sharp, fluttering tremolo: *seizing.*

At that signal, the rope-gang does not wait for a formal count. They throw their lean, lard-pale bodies backward against their hempen harnesses with savage, frantic violence. The lever groans. The upper block lifts a fraction of an inch—just enough to break the calcified spit—and I jerk my arm out, sliding a thin, greased rib-bone between the faces to keep them from settling back into the bite.

The bone is yellowed, heavy, and dense. It belonged to an elder of my own lineage who worked the north avenues two cycles ago. In my hand, his rib is cool, rubbed glassy-smooth by the friction of a thousand basalt blocks and the tallow we boiled from his thighs. When I wedge him into the joint, I feel him take the weight. The stone groans against his bone; the grease slips; the block glides. 

He is still moving the city, as I will move it when my own knuckles grow too thick to slip into the cracks.

Because that is the terror that lives under our skin—not that the light will fail, for it has never dimmed by a single candle-power in all the memory of the shelf, but that the stillness will enter us before we are done. 

Already, the fluid in my left knee is turning chalky. After my brief, harness-slung sleeps in the lee of the steam vents, I have to strike the side of my joint with a rounded basalt pebble to break the microscopic crystals that form while I dream. I feel that same mineral patience waiting in the water that weeps from the roof, in the pale crust that forms in the corners of our lidless eyes, in the silent, descending dust that coats our shoulders while we work.

When the terrace above me finally tilts and begins to slide down the greasy timber tracks toward the plaza, the darkness under the stone opens to the blinding, surgical glare of the ceiling. 

A draft of cool, iron-scented air rushes in. A flock of Cilia drops from the white void above like falling scraps of linen, their soft, velvet mouths skimming across the freshly exposed underside of the block, vacuuming the blind mites before the shale even touches the rollers. 

I do not look up to watch them. I wipe the slurry of lime and sweat from my temples with the back of a long, double-jointed wrist, hook my harness to the trailing trace of the gang ahead, and follow the vibration of the next failing wall through the wet, shining floor.
