I would not be above, on the rim where the fig branches tangle against the wind of the plains, nor would I be among those carrying the baskets down on the wet hemp lines. 

I would be down at the seam where the vertical face of the notch meets the floor—seated flat on the crushed basalt, back pressed against the freshly crosshatched charcoal stone, thighs spread to make a cradle for a rounded loaf of dark river-diorite.

My hands would be the gray of the quarry floor, the fine mineral powder worked so deeply into the whorls of my fingers that even the ribbons of seep-water could not wash it pale. My skin would carry the dull, tallowy sheen of pressed fig oil, smelling faintly of bitter kernel and wet slate. My feet—broad, squared, the heels capped in yellowed, horn-thick gristle—would rest loosely against the raw corner of a paver, feeling every micro-fracture without flinching from the edge.

I would be the one who listens to the fit.

In a settlement built of dry cyclopean stone, where no mud or lime is smeared to forgive a clumsy angle, the blocks must find each other in total intimacy. When a four-span lintel is lowered on ironwood levers, or when a floor paver is levered into its pocket of sand, someone must sit with their bare heels flat against the rock, palms spread wide across the joint, waiting for the weight to drop home. 

You do not watch stone settle; you feel its grain find the grain beneath it through your ribs and the flat plates of your pelvic bone.

When the maul strikes three alleys away, it does not come to me as a clap of noise. The humid green air and the liverwort carpets eat the high snap of it instantly, swallowing the edge of the blow. What reaches me is a dull, liquid thrum through the basalt floor—a low *tonk* that travels through the rock shelf into my calves, telling me the flint did not split, the wedge held its bite, and the shelf gave off another straight span of slate. 

I would answer not with a word, but with a short, closed-mouth hum from the back of the throat, vibrating the breastbone just enough that the worker kneeling on the adjoining block feels the assent under their palms: *it sits, it clears, strike again.*

Between strikes, there is nothing that requires haste. I would watch the slow mechanics of the court:

A slate-skinned gecko pauses at the height of my shoulder, its throat fluttering like a small pulse against the raw chisel grooves, its tail flat and heavy with fat. Its eyes, clear and ringed in pale gold, watch my hand as I lift the diorite pounder to brush away a pinch of rock-flour from a joint. We are both dusted with the same gray silt; neither of us makes a sound that could disturb the pale moth resting on the underside of an arum leaf a foot above the sand.

Near my left heel, the crushed sand shifts slightly—a dry, minute disturbance where a burrowing toad settles its soft belly deeper into the damp grit, breathing in long, slow measures that match the drift of condensation down the wall. 

When the light above turns from the cool green of noon to the dull olive of dusk, and the heat of the plains pulls a faint, upward sigh through the ferns, I would lay the diorite stone in the alcove beside the bone wedges. I would take my share of the mashed bitter arum from the communal bowl, the paste thick and cold, tasting of earth and river-silt, and eat it slowly with two fingers while leaning into the deep, unyielding cold of the living wall.

I would sleep where the masonry is oldest—six fathoms down, beneath the roofless lintel, where the night brings no sky except the black lattice of the canopy. There, with dried liverwort beneath my ribs and my hand resting on the smooth, unsmoothed basalt that has never seen weather, I would breathe the dense, mineral exhales of the trench, anchored completely in the bottom of the world.
