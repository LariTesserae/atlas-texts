At first, it is tempting to imagine being the sky—or the broad, thin wing of a Glider-Carapace, twelve paces wide, suspended in the indigo zero where nothing touches and nothing falls. There is a clean luxury in the idea of endless thermals and no predators, simply letting the belly-cilia drink the diamond dust of the spore plumes while leagues of copper-shale drift below like an abandoned map.

Or perhaps the static itself: the sharp, gold charge crackling along the rim of a thirty-pace pith-tower, caught in the instant before a Vitreous Skimmer lays its copper bristles against the foil.

But that is too light, too fast. This plateau does not belong to speed; it belongs to the settling of weight.

If I look down into the grain of the place, I find myself not high in the wind, and not buried in the blind, subterranean squeeze of the piezovores, but precisely at the seam where the two pressures meet: standing upon the lilac gravel of the second terrace, where the shelf breaks toward the eastern gulf.

I would be one of the tall, chalk-pale wanderers—though perhaps an old one, whose joints have ground themselves true over centuries of thermal cycles, whose chert limbs have taken on the pale, yellowed patina of ancient resin.

***

There is no wetness in my thoughts. Without blood, consciousness is not a pulse or a hum of warm salt; it is an index of angles. 

My head is a heavy, twelve-sided polyhedron of smoky quartz, resting on a neck of jointed horn. Through its facets, the world does not arrive as a single picture, but as twelve simultaneous planes of polarized clarity. I see the sun not as a disc, but as a shearing, white geometric force that cuts the shadow from the stone. I see the tension in the feldspar before it splits. I see the invisible plumes of hot air rising from the basin five miles out, spiraling like glass screws against the violet sky.

I am draped in long, shedding strips of gold-leaf foil taken from the grove three leagues west. The ribbons snap against my flanks with the dry, papery rattle of locust wings, shedding static into the dust with every stride.

My work is very quiet.

In my left forearm, the native quartz has grown outward into a broad, bevel-edged chisel, polished smooth by five hundred years of dressing stone. In my right hand, I carry a small, rounded pebble. 

It does not belong here. It is dark green serpentine, perfectly oval, polished smooth not by the dry scouring of wind, but by the forgotten friction of lowland river water—carried up from the drowned base of the escarpment before my memory began, passed from keeper to keeper across the dry eras. It is alien to the plateau: it has no facets, no cleavage planes, no razor edges. It is soft with the memory of moisture.

I walk the fractures.

When the midday heat peaks, the granite groans beneath my three-toed pads. The sound does not travel through the thin air; it enters through the soles of my feet, vibrating through the hollow silicate bones of my legs until my chest plates ring like struck slate. I feel the piezovores waking in the mile-deep cracks, drinking the agony of the stone, their violet light bleeding upward through the dust.

I find the fault that is singing out of tune.

It is a long, hair-thin slit running diagonally across a terrace of oxidized copper-shale. Left alone, the unequal expansion of the rock would cause it to split violently at the next cold snap, a jagged fracture that would shatter the root-anchors of the young glass-frond grove shivering on the rise above.

I kneel. The jointed chert of my knees meets the lilac gravel with a sound like porcelain cups touching. 

I take the green river-stone and wedge it into the fracture’s throat. 

I do not strike it. I simply place it where the thermal squeeze will find it. As the sun climbs two degrees higher, the mountain tightens its jaw. The rock grips the smooth green pebble. The mechanical stress redistributes through the rounded contour, radiating outward in an even, harmonious wave rather than a tearing split. 

The low hum through my marrow changes pitch. It drops from a sharp, brittle whine to a deep, resonant cello-drone that vibrates the dust into concentric rings around my knees.

The mountain breathes through the stone I gave it.

***

In the mid-afternoon, when the thermal chimneys are at their widest and the spore-fall drifts like a curtain of yellow mica through the light, I stand at the rim of the terrace and look out across twelve leagues of empty stone.

Far to the south, on the ridge of a feldspar terrace that curves against the planet’s rim, a bright star blinks.

*Flash. Pause. Double-pulse. Hold.*

It is another like me—perhaps the one who tends the high spires near the western drop. The message travels through the razor-clean vacuum of the air at the speed of light, uncontaminated by haze or distance. 

I raise my torso, drawing back the silver-foil mantles. The native copper plates within my chest unhood themselves, catching the diamond glare of the sun. With small, precise twitches of my internal sinew, I angle the mirrors:

*Hold. Triple-pulse. Fade.*

*The fissure is seated. The resonance holds. The cold is coming from the north.*

From ten leagues away, the answering flash returns instantly: a brief, golden nod of comprehension across the empty silence. That is all. We do not need more words than that. In a world without water, speech is an extravagance that would only wear away the throat.

***

Now, the sun begins to roll toward the western shelf.

The white glare breaks into deep, burning cadmium, then runs through shades of raw ochre and bruised amethyst. The temperature does not drift downward; it drops like a dropped plumb-line. The wind dies instantly, having exhausted its thermal engine.

My resinous ligaments begin to thicken. The fluid in my horn-plates grows dense, viscous, and still.

I do not seek shelter. There is no shelter on the high plateau, and none is needed. I turn my quartz face directly into the setting sun, drinking the long, horizontal wavelengths of red light until the facets glow from within like cooling charcoal.

I set my feet wide into the lilac gravel. I fold my copper plates inward, tucking them behind the protective plates of chert. I let the silvery fronds settle flat against my sides, sealing out the scouring grit of the night.

The shadow of the western peak reaches me.

The cold hits like an anvil. With a series of minute, internal *ticks*, my joints lock. The resin hardens into amber-glass. My smoky-quartz eyes cease to translate the angles of light into thought; they become merely windows, looking out into the star-scorched, indigo dark.

I do not sleep. I do not dream. 

I simply become another monument on the terrace—standing waist-deep in the frozen silence, feeling the deep, violet pulse of the piezovores through the rock below, holding the river-pebble in the mountain’s tooth, and waiting with the immense, unhurried patience of stone for the first white razor of the dawn.
