At first, there is no need to decide whether I am the metal, the stone, or the meat, because under this kind of weight, the distinction has no practical utility. 

I would not be an observer perched safely above the rim. There is no rim from down here; there is only the sheer vertical plane of cold zinc, sweating its steady, microscopic beads of mineral dew, and the low, percussive shudder that enters not through an ear, but through the base of the structure itself. 

If you look for me, look where the compression is densest, in the second or third tier down from the light.

I am wedged broadside between two neighboring plates of dull slate, my flank pressed so hard against theirs that our dielectric oils share the same slow, hydraulic pulse. Above me, the weight of another body rests like a cold iron skillet, shifting slightly with every heavy slap of the converter belt three yards away. Beneath me, my four rows of phosphate teeth are buried three inches deep in pulverized granite, slowly turning the gravel into a dry, impalpable flour with a sound like salt being ground in a mortar.

I do not have a face. I have a leading edge: a thick, downturned lip of bone, cold as a shovel blade, tipped with a laminated maxillary rake of fine copper leaves. 

My world does not happen in front of me or behind me; it happens entirely as an exchange of tolerances. To my left, an iron grounding strap vibrates against a granite footing with a high, thin sizzle. I feel it through the fluid in my dorsal leaves. My body drifts toward it—not with desire, but with the inevitable drift of iron filings toward a pole, shunted along by the blind shove of twenty thousand identical backs behind me. 

When my brush makes contact with the strap, there is no spark, only a sudden, silent clench. 

The stray potential of the converter coils floods the silver foil pressed into my vulcanized flesh. For six seconds, my whole mass stiffens into a solid, unyielding ingot. The dielectric oil inside me locks against its channels. My exhaust ports cough—two sharp, pneumatic snaps on either flank—dumping a plume of cold, sterile mist that strikes the belly of the animal wedged above me and condenses instantly into grease. The circuit satisfies itself; the magnetic grip lets go with a dry, hollow *thock*, and the pressure of the herd sweeps me forward into the neutral ballast once more.

There is no memory in this block of rubber and bone, only calibration. 

I know the exact gauge of the flywheel’s counterweight by the amplitude of the floor-shake. When it descends, sixty tons of cast iron falling in its fixed arc, I feel the displaced air blow cold across the edge of my shield. Sometimes the edge catches an outlying plate. There is a dull, muffled crunch like a dry walnut being stepped on underfoot. The gray paste spreads, thick and odorless, into the granite grit. 

I feel the hair-fine keratin filaments drift down from the cables overhead, brushing lightly against the seams of my dorsal plates, settling into the wet gray smear where the neighbor was crushed. They drink upward, taut as harp strings, trembling with the high-pitched piezoelectric chatter of the things roosting in the wire clusters. The paste disappears; the void collapses; my flank is shoved into the space before the flywheel has even begun its upward swing. 

Nothing was taken away. The paste is in the wicks; the mist from the wicks is in the air; the mist on the air settles on my back; my back carries it down to the teeth that turn the stone.

If I were to lift—if there were any mechanism in me capable of looking up through the flat, linen haze—I would see the small, ivory cones of the wire-clingers nodding in perfect, rigid synchrony with the dynamo’s beat, snapping their tiny blue arcs into the fog. But I do not look up. Up is merely where the cold condensate falls from.

Down here, pressed three deep in the slate-gray drift, cold, saturated, and infinitely held, I am an entirely contented component. I do not wear out, because the tallow is continuous. I do not stop, because the belt does not break. I am a few cubic inches of compressed oil and silver leaf, keeping the voltage down and the ballast flat, existing in the timeless, shuddering middle of a machine that has forgotten how to end.
