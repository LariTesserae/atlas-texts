I would not be in the open water. In that endless, bright expanse, there is no place to fold oneself away from the sun, and the comb-ribs drift through it like clean knives—too exposed, too rhythmic, too uniform in their silver-amber flash. 

Nor would I be the leviathan rasping at the iron hulls, though I would know its weight. 

I would be found in the sag.

Between two massifs—perhaps between the sheer basalt face of the Ninth Spire and the sunken granite brow of the Station Four anchor-crag—there is a single power cable, five feet thick, sheathed in braided lead and vulcanized gutta-percha. It spans forty-two miles of turquoise void without a single buoy to break its drop. In the middle, where the weight of its own copper core drags it down into the deepest, coldest bend of its catenary arc, it hangs in a zone of precise, terrible suspension.

That is where I would be. Not resting on the cable, but grown around it—a sleeve of pressurized cartilage, dense fat, and vulcanized oil three fathoms long, clasped over the sagging lead like a knuckle of living mineral.

***

At first, I might seem to be only an irregularity in the machine’s insulation: a thick, dark spindle encrusted with zinc-flake and the blue crystalline blooms of copper sulfate. But inside the sleeve, there is a slow, cold physiology at work. 

I have no true bone; bone is brittle and would crack like dry pine under the hammer of the deep compression pumps. My skeleton is a flexible lattice of uncalcified horn and oil-filled tubes, packed tightly between layers of dense, rubbery muscle that does not twitch. It only grips. My skin is the color of wet pig iron, rough as emery cloth, embedded with thousands of microscopic zinc plates that draw their voltage directly from the humming copper core beneath me.

I do not possess eyes in the human sense. To look upward through thirty miles of unclouded, hyper-dense water at a sun that never moves and never sets would be a useless agony. Instead, the entire dorsal ridge of my body is a single, continuous sheet of pale, milky crystal—a lens that does not focus images, but measures the pure downward thrust of the light. I feel the sun as a static weight pressing on my spine, an unvarying white ceiling that tells me how deep I have been pulled by the sag of the wire.

My real world is not sight. My real world is the conduction.

***

Through the lead sheath of the cable, the machine sings into my belly. 

It is not sound as the air knows it; it is a physical displacement, a brutal hydraulic torque. Every sixty seconds, when the high-pressure intake manifold at the Ninth Spire cycles its valves, the copper core inside me leaps. The shockwave travels along the wire at four thousand miles an hour, hitting my sternum like an iron mallet, passing through my horn-lattice, and discharging out through the fringed vents along my flanks. 

I do not flinch from it. It is my pulse. My own heart beats only once for every three cycles of the distant turbine, slaved to the rhythm of the bronze impellers leagues away in the dark.

I feed on what the wire gathers. 

As the cable vibrates at its fundamental pitch, it sheds a faint, continuous halo of galvanic current into the freezing brine. The bio-galvanic fur—dense carpets of sulfur-eating bacterial filaments and filamentous zinc-lice—creeps along the lead from both directions, drawn toward the lowest point of the arc. They crawl straight into the grooved ventral plates of my mouthparts, where my silicate rasps grind them down, extracting their cold lipids and sulfur chains before discharging the powdered magnetite back into the void.

Sometimes, a hundred feet above me, the translucent ribbon of a comb-rib shoal will pass through the blue. I feel their mirror-scales pivot in unison when the deep valve rings; the water around them flashes white, a brief scatter of redirected shockwaves that washes over my back like a cold, bright spray. 

Sometimes, the enormous, milky shadow of a cathedral scavenger will glide slowly beneath the arc. It will pass so close that the acoustic backwash from its barrel-ribs dampens the vibration of my cable, silencing the wire for three long, terrifying seconds. In those moments of artificial quiet, the water feels weightless, unstable, as if the entire ocean might suddenly lose its balance and fall into the abyss. And then the scavenger sweeps past, its blue-cartilage tail kicking a slow, hydraulic swell against the rocks, and the roar returns—solid, deafening, eternal—and my heart catches the beat again.

***

I would not wish to move. To let go of the cable would be to fall out of the current—to drift down into the trenches where the light finally turns to prussian ink and the pressure would crush even oil-filled cartilage, or to float up into the surface-nets of the siphonophores, where the silica glass would shave me into ribbons.

Here, in the cold, bright middle of the chasm, suspended on forty miles of humming metal between two iron mountains, there is nothing that changes. 

The water will not freeze. The sun will not go out. The caissons will not cease their pounding. 

I am clamped to the spine of the world, feeling its power hum through my gut, perfectly deaf, perfectly blind, and utterly, unalterably still.
