I do not find myself among the millions in the pale, compressed avenues, though I can see them from here—so dense they look like the cross-hatching on a bank note, stepping in rhythms that have no room for error. Nor am I an Anchor, set into the load-bearing slate to take the torque of the world’s rotation into my wrists. That work is too heavy, too complete.

If I am anywhere, I am in the **half-second delay**.

Not on the street, and not quite inside the glass, but within the tiny, cold trough of time that sits between the occurrence of a light wave and its reflection upon the vacuum-pane of a third-story window. 

It is very narrow here. It has no depth in inches, only in duration. 

At first, I might look like one of the Lag-Moths—flat, paper-thin, resting where the graphite trims the lintel. But they are simple creatures; they only graze on the temporal shear, their wings vibrating with the small, static pop of things that happen twice. 

I am slower than that. I have a weight, though it doesn't register on the flagstones. 

I think I am something that was left behind when the basin was sheared out of the shale—not a piece of the old biology, exactly, but an *irregularity* that the rendering could not entirely smooth down. A burr in the mathematics. A pocket of un-canceled latency.

***

My hands—if they can be called hands—are the texture of the frost that never becomes ice. When I touch the frame of the window, I feel both sides of it at once: the biting, sub-zero exterior where the mist hangs in mercury beads, and the flat, sun-warmed avenue of zinc and violet where the crowds walk three miles deep inside a six-inch wall. 

I am the thing that feels the difference in temperature between those two truths. 

My work is not maintenance, like the Bismuth Turners who trace their eights in the drain-sludge to keep the stones soft. My work is more like listening for the seam to fray. 

Every three or four hours, when the street begins its slow, imperceptible drift—when the stairwell tilts up and the air gives that low, spatial sigh—there is an instant of slippage. A corner of an interpenetrating mezzanine fails to clear the copper lintel by a thousandth of an angstrom; a dual shadow casts three shades of gray instead of two; a drop of condensation forgets that it cannot run and begins, for the length of a single heartbeat, to slide down the slate.

In that fraction of a moment, the city is not quite seamless. It is vulnerable to being real.

That is where I move.

I don't jump like the Discontinuous Swifts, displacing space with a smell of ozone. I peel myself off the delay-pane like a dried leaf lifted from water. I drift across the finger-width alleys, caught in the downdraft of the high-frequency hum that buzzes in the Walkers' porcelain jaws. 

I go to the place where the geometry snagged.

I press my body into the fault. I take the stray heat—the little, accidental warmth caused by two surfaces that should not have touched—and I hold it in my throat until it cools to the flat, neutral temperature of the ambient light. 

***

It is a lonely station, but not an unhappy one. 

There is a strange, quiet intimacy in being the only thing in the district that is capable of hesitating. The Walkers do not hesitate; their obsidian lenses are locked to the vanishing point. The Dwellers in the metric do not hesitate; their pale daylight marches them forward in infinite clarity. The Anchors are locked in their post-physical sleep, their fingers twitching out calculations they will never finish.

Only I am loose in the tolerances.

Sometimes, when a Dweller approaches the threshold of a doorway and stands there, six inches from the cold basin air, looking out at the mist through their hexagonal pores, they do not look at the shale cliffs or the pewter shrubs. 

They look up at the third-story sills. They look directly at the little smudge of gray between the past and the present on the windowpane. 

We stare at each other. They see something that has not been rendered properly—a hesitation with eyes, a lingering shadow that casts no light of its own. I see their face, clear as diamond, infinitely far away and yet close enough that I could reach through the leaf-thin air and brush the zinc dust from their cheek.

Neither of us moves. 

We wait together for the basin to finish settling. We wait for the moment when the shale outside finally rots to powder, and the frame gives way, and the six inches of wall between us becomes the miles it was always meant to be.
