At first, the instinct is to look for a vantage—high on the rim, perhaps, where the cable-bear locks its vulcanized pads around the tram-wire, suspended between the blinding white sky and the terraced green drop. But to sit there would be to observe the basin from the outside, to remain a spectator to its mechanism. 

To actually belong to this place, you would have to be caught in its tolerances.

I would not have eyes. In this light, vision is a liability—a soft, wet tissue exposed to a sun that strips every surface bare. Nor would I have a voice, for sound here is an inefficiency, a waste of kinetic force that the basin does not permit.

Instead, I find myself down at the interface. Not in the moss, and not quite inside the steel, but in the compressed, razor-thin threshold where the two are locked under load.

Specifically: beneath the main trunnion block of the central rocker-arm, where thirty tons of counterweighted pig-iron meet the shear plane of a cleaved basalt pedestal.

***

To exist here is to be entirely laminar. 

I would be something wide, flat, and dense as vulcanized slate—a creature composed of stacked, microscopic leaves of keratin, oil-soaked mica, and tough, fibrous connective tissue that has long since given up the concept of a soft interior. I would be no thicker than the edge of a chisel, spread outward across three square yards of stone, wedged so tightly into the bedding plane that there is no space between my upper membrane and the cast-iron soleplate, no gap between my underbelly and the unrounded flint of the rock.

I am a living gasket. A shim.

I do not eat what grows in the sun. What feeds me is the steady, pressurized weeping of the bearing above: a continuous, microscopic seepage of cold-pressed castor oil and mineral paraffin that migrates along the threads of the four-inch anchor bolts. My upper skin is porous, cross-hatched with micro-capillaries that drink this slickness before it can oxidize in the thin air, drawing it down through my fibrous layers to lubricate my own internal plates. 

Under this kind of pressure, feeling is not tactile in any human sense; it is an architecture of stress. 

I know the basin through the tension of its iron. When the aerial tramway four hundred yards above creeps forward its single, silent yard, the change in vector travels down through the rock face as a three-millibar twist across my northern perimeter. When the great four-cylinder pump in the quarry floor begins its ten-minute draw, the ground beneath me does not shake—it stiffens, its crystalline lattice aligning slightly against the suction, and my own copper-rich fluids shift laterally from my perimeter toward my core.

Time is measured by the cooling of the flanges.

At dawn, when the high clouds comb through the canopy and the moisture sweats onto the steel, I feel the rocker-arm contract—a clean, fraction-of-a-millimeter settling that increases the load across my shoulders by several thousand pounds. This is the moment of deepest comfort. The cold is total, sterile, and still. The weight is absolute. I press my lower plates into the micro-grooves of the freshly cleaved basalt, tasting the faint, sharp tang of sulfur and quartz dust trapped there when the rock was sheared.

Then the sun clears the rift edge. 

Even buried beneath the iron, I register the noon glare not as brightness, but as thermal expansion. The iron above me begins to lengthen. The load shifts south-southwest by the width of a hair. My outer margins, which extend just beyond the iron flange into the narrow strip of permanent shadow, feel the invisible draft of the distant orchid clusters as the automatic relief valve vents its feather of vapor far down the pipeline. 

In that moment, once every seven days, the pressure drops across the entire terrace by a fraction of a percent. The rocker-arm tilts. My stacked plates slide against one another by the thickness of a cell, distributing the grease, renewing the seal, ensuring that the stone does not shatter the iron, and the iron does not grind the stone to dust.

I do not sleep, because sleep implies an alternation, a waking state to return to. I simply endure the cycle of the stroke. 

If you were to climb down through the dense, celluloid-ribbed ferns, navigate the silent sweep of the bronze piston, and peer beneath the heavy ochre base of the counterweight, you would see nothing living at all. You would see only a dark, matte-black line between the metal and the rock, neat as a draftsman’s rule, beaded at the edge with a microscopic margin of clear, cold oil. 

And you would feel—if you laid a bare hand against the iron—not a heartbeat, but the steady, immense, and unyielding refusal to move that keeps the entire shelf suspended beneath the sun.
