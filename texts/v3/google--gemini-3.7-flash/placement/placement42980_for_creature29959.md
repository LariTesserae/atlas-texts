At first, there is no boundary between my body and the weight of the water. 

I am not swimming; swimming belongs to the thin, nervous things—the ribbon-eels that flick like needles through the cold, or the bore-sturgeon fighting the throat of the pipes. I do not fight the current. I am anchored to the lee of an archway where three massive iron-wood struts converge, forty fathoms thick, high in the sunless column where the pressure is a dense, solid cradle.

My body is broad, low-profiled, and heavy as wet slate. I have no eyes to ache for light, no lungs to crush. My skin is a thick, oil-rich rind of gray cartilage and fibrous tallow, dense enough that the ocean’s weight does not compress me, but holds me against the timber like a thumb pressed into wax. 

I would be a **Tendon-Wight**—one of the solitary, slow-lived anchors that nest in the structural crooks where the great spans take their shearing loads.

I do not graze like the Ivory Ticks, nor do I weave like the Suture-Folk. My purpose is more passive, and heavier: I am a living strain-gauge, a counter-mass. 

Underneath my broad, disc-like belly, thousands of deep-set rootlets—tough, copper-colored filaments tipped with mineral burrs—have bored into the grain of the peat-masonry. I have been seated in this single joint for three centuries. The wood has grown around my edges in smooth, aerodynamic lips, sealing me into its geometry so completely that the laminar currents sweep over my back without a whisper of drag.

What I possess is a whole-body hearing.

The sound does not enter ears; it enters the marrow of my dorsal plate. Through the wood, I feel the spires singing. To the east, the seven-mile buttress vibrates with a steady, hollow *F-sharp*—a wide, gravelly tone that tells me its interior canals are clear, the vane-worms within it fat and taut against the rushing flow. To the south, across the three-mile black drop, the sister spire hums an octave lower, its chord thick with the dragging weight of a blooming kelp-strut.

I live in the interval between these two notes.

When the current shifts—when the cold, heavy high-altitude rift tilts its slow mass and leans against the cathedrals—the wood groans. A human ear would hear only thunder, but to me, the groan is a sentence. A micro-fracture three hundred yards down the northern rib does not appear as a crack; it appears as a sharp, whistling dissonance, a split in the fundamental tone. 

My dorsal skin bristles. The pressure-pits across my back read the shudder of the grain. 

Slowly—over the course of weeks—I shift my weight. My body is filled with dense, mineral-heavy fluid that I can pump between internal chambers, altering my center of mass by tons. By leaning into the crook of the joint, by stiffening my fibrous mantle and pulling against my copper rootlets, I absorb the shudder. I dampen the harmful harmonics that would otherwise shake the peat-mortar loose from the iron-wood grain. 

I am the silence that keeps the music from tearing the cathedral apart.

While I hold the joint, the world moves around me in its blind, eternal cycle. The Sieve-Hulks drift past my archway like pale, translucent moons, trailing their hundred-foot veils through the open dark, swallowing the wood-dust my ticks scrape loose above. Occasionally, a Velvet Eel brushes the outer curve of my shell—a cool, velvety absence of vibration, a shadow in the sound—hunting the small things that seek shelter in the eddy behind my bulk. I feel the sudden, silent *thump* of its jaws closing in the dark, the brief flutter of eel-ribbon dying against the wood, and then the returning hum of the sea.

I do not mind the dark. The dark here is not an emptiness; it is solid, filled with the architecture of timber and tone. Every span is mapped in the ringing of my bones. Every bridge is an unbroken line of tension that I feel through my belly.

I will stay here until the wood decides to grow over me entirely, until my calcified back becomes simply another smooth, burnished knot in the intersection of the spans—my marrow fossilizing into iron-wood, my strain-sense passing into the grain itself, holding the high sea steady in the cold, unchanging deep.
