I would not be on the open terrace. To be on the open terrace is to be caught in the manic arithmetic of the floor, where you must either run on electrostatic fire or be swallowed by the next shelf rising out of the quantum grain. 

Nor would I be among the ribbon-things. Their life is all tension and hunger, a whip-snap existence carved out between the furnace-breath of the updrafts and the blinding radio-roar of the towers.

No, I find myself higher up, in the wet, ringing gloom where the curtain walls meet the ceiling of mist, wedged into the deep, shaded fissures behind the shoulder of an Anchor.

Here, the pace of the world changes. It does not slow down—nothing here is allowed to be slow—but it deepens, turning from a frantic rattle into an immense, heavy torque. 

At first, I might seem like an outgrowth of the Anchor itself: a thick, ridged plating of pale, semi-flexible silica and dense, fibrous gristle, tucked into the crease where the beast’s leathery flank presses against the newly extruded basalt. But I am not part of the Anchor, nor am I stone. I am the thing that lives in the shear.

My body is long, flattened, and segmented like an articulate wedge, built to withstand pressures that would pulverize bone. I have no eyes; in this violet dusk, eyes are an inefficiency. Instead, my entire dorsal surface is paved with hexagonal quartz plates that register the slow, crushing shifts of the amphitheater’s geometry. I feel the pylon pulses not as sound, but as an ache in the marrow of my plates—a deep, rhythmic iron-pull that tells me precisely where the local gravity is warping and where the next fracture will split.

I am an acoustic feeder, a creature of the glass-moss gardens.

While the Anchor heaves its slow, hour-long breath, drawing in the hot mist and venting plumes of dry, whistling steam, I extend my feeding apparatus down into the hanging thickets of hollow glass filaments that trail from its belly. These filaments ring continuously with every draft and vibration, tuned to the high, crystalline frequencies of the amphitheater’s birth. Inside their hollows, the green-black autotrophic slurry churns under pressure. 

I do not eat the slurry; I drink the *resonance*. 

My ventral side is lined with hundreds of fine, hair-like acoustic styluses made of hardened, metallic chitin. I slide them into the vibrating tubes of the moss, tapping directly into the harmonic current. The sound of the world—the hiss of the frictionless sheet-water, the rhythmic *tack-tack-tack* of the scuttlers’ vacuum seals, the whip-crack of hunting ribbons, and the dry, seismic *clack* of the newborn stone—enters me as pure vibrational energy. My internal organs are chambers of dense, oil-rich fluid that damp and translate these shocks, turning raw sonic force into a slow, warm metabolic burn.

When a fresh fracture opens far below and the chime-worms burst forth to shed their talc-spores, the sound is a sudden, sharp needle of high C that shoots through the basalt curtain. I catch that note. I channel it through my plates, absorbing the spike, filtering it, and feeding it back into the Anchor’s hide as a low, soothing counter-thrum. 

It is a mutualism born of sheer survival. The Anchor keeps me elevated above the hungry, growing floor, anchored to stone that it has already stabilized with its deep-driven roots; in return, I act as its harmonic dampener, smoothing out the violent acoustic spikes that would otherwise shatter the fragile glass-moss clinging to its skin.

I am stationary, but never still. Inside me, the oil churns. The mist condenses on my quartz plates, trickles into the lateral gutters of my shell, and is stripped of its ozone before draining away into the Anchor’s breathing vents. 

Sometimes, when a ribbon-thing tires of the hunt and banks upward into the mist to scrape its pearlescent flank against the Anchor’s skin, its trailing membrane brushes across my shell. It feels like a stroke of heavy, wet silk, charged with static. The ribbon feels the steady, dampened hum of my body—a single point of rhythmic coherence in a violent, unmade world—and lingers for a heartbeat in the vacuum of my wake before curling back toward the towers.

Down below, the world manufactures itself with furious, unthinking speed, snapping and sheeting and crying out in flint and steam. But up here, tucked behind the massive, heaving shoulder of the dampener, I hold the vibration. I keep the glass from breaking. I am the silence between the fractures, drinking the sound of the stone being born.
