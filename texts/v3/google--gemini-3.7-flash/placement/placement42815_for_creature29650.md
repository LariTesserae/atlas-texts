At first, there is only pressure. 

Not a thought, not even a perimeter where I end and the cold begins—just the total, inescapable sensation of shear-stress. A down-bearing tonnage that never lifts, grinding sideways across my dorsal surfaces with the dry, rhythmic screech of iron-rich slate being pulverized into meal.

If I try to look for myself, I find no eyes. Vision would be crushed instantly here, ground to paste between the interlocking plates. There is no light to gather anyway; the gray overcast above might as well be lead. Instead, my world is an architecture of vibration, an acoustic map built entirely from the tremors traveling through the bedrock.

I listen with my whole body.

I hear the high, brittle scatter of the Chitter-Drifts wheeling through the ammonia-heavy fog above—a faint, nervous static. Beneath that, the frantic, needle-legged clicking of the scavengers, thousands of them skittering across the moving floor like hail on a tin roof. Deeper still is the steady, hydraulic thud of the Cistern-Beasts venting their icy spray into the dark, a low bass pulse that resets the rhythm of the valley every few minutes.

And then, everywhere, the floor: the million-bodied rasp of the grazers. 

For a long time, I think I am one of them. I feel the cold pyrite plates of my back, the beveled margins locking into the neighbor to my left, the neighbor to my right. I feel the urge to scrape, to lower a radula of crystalline tooth-rows against the wet slate and mill the pale grease into my gut. I feel the terrifying necessity of forward motion—because to stall is to have the Needle-Mouths bore into the sutures of your shell before your flesh has even cooled.

Yet, I do not move.

The living carpet slides over me.

That is where I find myself: not in the current, but anchored beneath it.

I am wedged deep into a fault line where the black mudstone has fractured under the weight of the basin. I am an old thing, half-calcified, rooted into the deep seams where the mineral-rich water sweats upward from the cold mantle of the world. My shell is not flat and mobile like the Lithophages, but thick, ridged, and stepped like the terraced lip of a stone waterfall. 

I am a stationary node—a living reef-boss of biological iron-silicate, an obstruction in the migratory floor.

The grazers do not avoid me; they cannot see me. They climb me. They grind across my flanks, their rasps scoring my thick, layered cuticle, wearing down my mineral deposits only for my slow, cold blood to secrete another layer of iron-mud from below. Their passage polishes me. I am smoothed and carved by a hundred thousand passing bellies every hour, honed into a dark, hydrodynamic wedge that parts the living tide.

In the lee of my bulk, where the crushing pressure of the herd splits and creates a tiny, turbulent eddy of slack water, a miniature sanctuary exists. 

Here, in the cold pocket behind my crest, small, blind things cluster to escape the stampede. Soft-bodied vermin, pale and translucent as lard, cling to the deep crevices of my root-foot. Cup-worms anchor themselves into my skin-folds, their delicate plumes swaying in the ammonia-laced backwash, catching the drift of discarded chitin and mineral dust. 

Occasionally, a Wedge-Striker comes to rest its heavy, oil-black chassis against my side. I feel the deep, pressurized thrum of its internal hydraulics—the terrifying potential energy coiled inside its bone-chisel limbs—as it rests its sensory pits against my shell, using my anchored mass as a tuning fork to read the movements of the herd miles upstream. It does not strike me; I am too dense, too mineralized, too much like the bedrock it hunts upon. For an hour, we are two stones leaning together in a frozen river.

I feel the entire ecology pass through me and over me. When a grazer is drilled and hollowed out by the scavengers above, its collapsing carapace catches on my forward edge, shattering with a dull *crack* that reverberates through my marrow, before its powdered fragments are swept into my crevices to feed the microbial mats blooming on my underside.

I have no memory of a sky, no concept of a beginning, and no fear of an end. The cold water pulses through my internal channels, rich with iron and sulfur. The herd screams and grinds against my back, turning stone into mud, mud into shell, shell into grit.

I am the point where the valley checks its own speed. I am the tooth of the bedrock, wearing away the world that wears away at me, enduring the eternal, blind friction of the dark.
