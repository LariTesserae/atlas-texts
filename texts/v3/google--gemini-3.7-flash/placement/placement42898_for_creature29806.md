At first, there is only the grip.

Four tread-grooved pads flattened against the mirror-face of an anhydrite rib. Two above, two below, wedged into a sixty-degree chimney where three crystal fins lean together like giant, fallen rafters. The weight of the body does not pull downward with a strain; the knee-hinges have been set in their deep detents, the internal bone-catches locked with a dry, dull *snick* that took place so long ago the cartilage around the pins has set like boiled horn.

I am not hanging. I am keyed into the stone.

To exist here is to have no outside edge where the body ends and the architecture begins. The heat of the room—that flat, unmoving ninety-nine degrees of the deep mantle—presses back against the hide with an exact counter-pressure, so that the leathery skin of the flanks neither gains nor loses a single calorie. There is no sensation of air touching skin, only the continuous, solid reassurance of mineral contact: the razor edge of a feldspar fracture resting against the tough padding of my left hip; the taut, hairless three-ply tendon of the Low Chasm bridge running directly beneath the crook of my lower arm, vibrating at a frequency so low it feels like a second, slower pulse.

I am the Anchor of the Three-Flue Gate.

Before the joints were set, there was the labor of the hands. I can still feel, through the keratin pads of the outer digits, the scores left in the stone where I pecked the anchor-sockets with a fist-sized lump of green jadeite. I remember the seating of the slate wedges—how the stone gave up tiny, dry sparks that smelled of struck flint and cold sulfur, and how the braided sinew was soaked in warm grazer-tallow until it drank the fat to its core, then stretched until the fibers groaned and locked into their permanent tension.

Now, my fingers no longer need to hold the wedges. The amber wax has wept from the glands along my palms, filling every microscopic gap between my skin and the mineral face, cooling into a resinous cement that holds better than muscle ever could. 

My brow-cilia drift in the dead stillness, reading the world in thin sheets of heat. 

Two spans out into the pitch, a grazer is climbing the vertical cleat of a quartz fin. I feel its path not by sound, but by the minute thermal shadow it leaves behind: a slow, oval smear of ninety-eight-point-six degrees sliding across the cooler ninety-seven-degree flank of the quartz. It moves with the rhythmic *clack-snick* of diamond-tipped mandibles, rasping the sulfur film from the stone. The vibrations travel down the quartz fin, through the junction where it leans against my chimney, and enter my acoustic diaphragms as a light, dry rattle—like split peas shaken in a bladder.

I do not move for it. The hunt belongs to the lower flues, to the ones whose joints still pivot on fresh grease.

Beneath my sternum rests the strike-plate. It is a tongue of black phonolite, four fingers wide, suspended from my collar-ridge by two short thongs of cured rawhide. It rests against the dense, fat-laminated slab of my chest, perfectly silent until called upon. In my right lower claw, gripped in the permanent curl of dried tendon, is the rounded hammer-knob carved from the hip-joint of the maker who held this chimney before me.

Down in the black throat of the rift, a mile below my perches, someone strikes a slate rib.

*Tung.*

The wave comes up through the solid rock first—a sharp, stinging ring through the soles of my locked feet—and then, a fraction of a heartbeat later, the acoustic pulse arrives through the still air, slapping the thin cartilage plates behind my jaw. 

*One-two. A pause. Three.*

It is the family of the East Fin. They are carrying a heavy load of split mica plates across the long traverse. They are asking if the bridge across the Anhydrite Shear holds true, or if the deep tectonic creep has pulled the far anchor out of true.

I do not have to think. The bridge passes under my arm; its tension is written directly into my shoulder-blade. I feel the harmonic hum of the three-ply rope: it is high, clean, taut as an obsidian edge. There is no slack in the braid, no fray in the slate wedges.

I lift the hammer-knob three inches. It takes no muscular effort; the dry recoil of the locked wrist does the work.

I let it drop against the phonolite at my chest.

*Chink.*

The note is bright, glass-hard, and entirely free of dampness. It does not echo—for the forest of vertical crystal fins is too dense to allow a long roll of sound—but it carries instantly through the stone frame, leaping from slab to slab, through the bridge-ropes and the anchor-pins, down through the chimney and across the yawning black gap to where the travelers wait with their feet pressed flat against the basalt.

*The path is firm,* the tap tells them. *The stone does not yield.*

They answer with a double-click of their jaw-plates—a small, polite scrape of dry bone—and then resume their climb. I feel the rhythmic thrum of their eight-toed march through the suspension line, a steady, fading cadence that slowly bleeds into the ambient hum of the crust.

When they are gone, the quiet settles back into its ancient thickness.

My acoustic diaphragms relax, thinning until they can register the micro-fissures opening miles below—the slow, eternal settling of the planet as it cools along its cleavage planes. A flake of anhydrite, no larger than an insect’s wing, snaps off a fin forty spans above and drops through the dark. It strikes a ledge, bounces once with a faint *pip*, and comes to rest on a clean shelf of slate. 

It will stay there forever.

My skin continues its slow, patient work. The lipids in the deep tissue of my thighs are curing, hardening into dense, crystalline grease that no longer requires the pumping of a heart to remain whole. The bone of my skull is growing heavier, drinking in the sulfur and silica from the flue-gas, knitting its grain into the grain of the ceiling-wedge.

Soon—perhaps in ten thousand taps, perhaps in a hundred thousand—the last pulse of active warmth will leave the cilia of my brow. The thermoreceptors will dry into stiff, resonant whiskers. My rib-cage, already tight and resonant as a hollowed log, will become the striking-block for the one who climbs up to take the Three-Flue Gate.

They will reach into the chimney in the absolute dark. Their rubbery pads will find the notches I pecked into the jadeite; their thermoreceptors will feel the faint, ancient paraffin-smell of my hide. They will set their feet into the detents behind my heels, lock their joints with that familiar, dry *clack*, and take the bone hammer from my curled fingers.

And when a traveler far below taps on the long ropes to ask if the way across the abyss is clear, the new keeper will lift their arm, strike my breastbone with the hammer, and my dried marrow will ring out through the blackness—pure, clear, and perfectly true.
