At first, I would not be distinct from the joinery. 

To exist here is to begin as an ache in the grain—a point where two vectors of sap pressure meet at an oblique angle and fail, for several seconds, to decide which direction is down.

I am not one of the Caliper-Beasts, whose leg-tips ring like tuning forks against the green timber; nor am I one of the soft men of elder-pith, seated in chairs they will never leave until their fibers fray into dust. Those are completed functions. They have their tolerances stamped into them by the division of their cells.

Instead, find me in the shear-plane: the knife-edge boundary where a three-story tenement’s gravity shears against the horizontal pull of an open viaduct. 

There is an unbuilt corner where the wrought-fiber gaslamps point toward an empty pocket of air, their posts parallel to nothing. That is where I collect. I am heavier than the parchment-winged gliders, but less dense than the lignified beams—a creature or an instrument that has accreted from the cold, unoxidized sweat dripping off the mortises. 

My body is long, narrow, and many-jointed, like a folding rule left out in the cellar until the brass hinges have grown a skin of pale lichen. I have no eyes, because sight in this damp velvet is only an invitation to fall; instead, I possess a row of exposed marrow-pores along my flanks that drink the draft. I can feel the five-story clock towers swaying in the void three blocks away, their unanchored pendulums sweeping through arcs of cool mist, not keeping time, but keeping *pace*—measuring how fast the wood must divide to keep from snapping under its own weight.

I do not scuttle like the Ledger-Crabs. I wedge.

My purpose—or my hunger, which in this place is the same thing—is the correction of right angles. 

Where the city secretes a wall that is off by half a degree, the strain creates a high, silent frequency in the cambium. The sap travels too fast on the convex side; the xylem tubes on the concave side starve and grow brittle. When that happens, I crawl into the seam where the floorboard meets the void. I insert my tapered limbs between the wet, unvarnished tenons and expand, drinking the pressurized bitter fluid until my segments lock with a wet, wooden *clack*. 

For hours, perhaps days, I become the shim. 

While I hold the angle true, the world around me works with terrible, indifferent industry. The Plumb-Gnats drift past my flanks in green, razor-straight columns, their cold light catching the celery-scented steam that plumes from the vine-pistons below. I feel the Cambium-Eels sliding through the deep taproots beneath my spine, their rasping mouths vibrating through the heartwood like a distant drill. Above me, the Gasket-Ticks swell on the viaducts, their lard-colored hides turning translucent blue as they swallow the excess life of the joint.

In this position, held rigid between two tons of expanding heartwood and hundreds of feet of black, empty air, my consciousness is entirely mechanical. I feel the entire quarter breathing:

*A valve opens in the lower works—a wet, suctioning gulp.*  
*The draft shifts six inches to the west.*  
*A tenement facade two streets over buds three new window casings with a sound like snapping dry twigs.*  
*The ledger-pods tick twice, subtracting weight from the eastern pylons.*

I am aware of the emptiness beneath me: that immense, wet cellar where condensation falls forever with a slow, rhythmic *tock*. I know that nothing supports the city from below; it is hanging from its own intention, held aloft by the tension of its fibers and the blind, forward-rushing logic of its growth.

Eventually, the beam I am bracing thickens. New cambium layers creep over my cuticle, pale and slick as peeled birch. Cells divide around my joints, binding my limbs into the permanent structure of the floor. 

When the angle is cured and the wood can bear its own burden, I do not die, nor do I leave. I simply let the sap drain from my marrow-pores. I soften. My outer husk delaminates, flaking away in paper-thin curls that drift downward into the clearance, while my core remains embedded in the corner—a knot in the timber, a permanent memory of how to stand square against the dark.

And from the other end of the viaduct, where a newly budded archway is beginning to sag into the cold clearance, another part of me—blind, segmented, and shivering with the need to measure—begins to uncoil from the wet grain.
