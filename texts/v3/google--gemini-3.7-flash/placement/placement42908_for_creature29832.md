At first, it is tempting to belong to the light—to be the glare itself, or one of those ephemeral midges spinning out its entire, furious twenty-minute existence in the steam plume of a bypass cowl, scalded and frozen at the same instant. But the light is too wide, too careless. It belongs to the sky, and the sky here is only a void through which heat escapes.

Nor is it the black silt below, though there is a deep, gravitational pull toward those blind nemerteans weaving through the cold slurry. That silence is too absolute, too ancient; it existed before the first caisson was sunk into the granite, and it will be there when the iron has dissolved into orange mud.

Where I find myself is in the joint. In the narrow, violent threshold where the steel meets the peat, and where the mechanical pulse becomes a physical anatomy.

I am on the lower service tier, beneath the rust-bright grating of the central siphon-spur, where the catwalk hangs only six inches above the heaving skin of the bog. 

I am one of the tenders, but old enough in the basin that the distinction between my own body and the maintenance of the line has worn thin, like the leather over my knuckles.

I do not look at the sky. Through the smoked quartz slabs of my goggles, the world above the handrail is only a flat, blinding sheet of mercury that aches behind the temples. My eyes are kept down, fixed on the three feet of space immediately in front of my iron-shod boots: the greasy orange diamond-mesh of the walkway, the thick green collars of liverwort creeping greedily around the hex-bolts, and the dark, sweating iron of the six-inch slurry bypass.

I do not have a name that gets spoken; there is no medium for a name here. Without sound, identity is a matter of weight, footing, and the specific cadence of the tremors that pass through the soles of the feet. 

My world is entirely tactile, translated through three layers of grease-soaked steerhide, the bone of the jaw, and the heavy zinc plates of my harness.

At this hour—if the word *hour* means anything under a sun that merely circles the serrated granite teeth without warming them—I am leaning against the housing of the number-four intake governor. My forehead is pressed flat against the unpainted steel of the inspection cowl. 

Through the frontal bone of my skull, the basin speaks in clear, heavy syllables:

Deep down, forty feet into the drowned fissures, the intake impellers are turning at nine hundred revolutions per minute. The beat is mostly clean—a low, dense *thrum-thrum-thrum* that settles behind my collarbones—but there is an uneven, gravelly stutter on the third stroke. A stone has lodged in the primary baffle. It is not large enough to shear the bronze vanes, but it is tumbling in the vortex, grinding a slow, crescent-shaped groove into the seating ring. 

I feel the cold spray drifting off the relief exhaust, settling in minute, icy beads across the nape of my neck where the leather hood meets the collar of my oiled smock. It smells of boiled tallow, wet iron, and that sharp, sour tang of ancient moss crushed under high pressure.

In my right hand, I hold a short-handled copper drifting bar. I don’t swing it; I tap it against the bypass casing, three rhythmic strikes followed by two long, hard presses of the metal against the pipe. Fifty yards down the line, through the spray and the blinding glare, the iron carries the strike to the tender at the lower weir. 

I wait. My boots are braced against the grating; the caulks bite deep into the rusted lattice to keep the perpetual shudder of the ground from walking me off the edge into the deep sponge.

Through the soles of my feet, the answer returns along the catwalk stringer: *Hold.* Two sharp pulses: *Gate closing.*

I close my eyes behind the dark quartz. In the blackness, the roar of the basin does not exist as sound, but as an enormous, cold architecture of pressure. I can feel the entire three-mile basin suspended on its granite shelf—millions of tons of icy water creeping through billions of capillary moss-tubes, all of it gathered, forced, and narrowed into these rigid, vibrating iron arteries that shudder against my ribs.

I adjust the tension wrench at my hip. My fingers inside the thick hide mitts are stiff, permanently curled to fit the radius of a two-inch valve wheel. My skin under the wool is pale, damp, and smells of mineral oil and tea. 

When the gate below closes, the tremor in the steel changes pitch, rising from a gut-shaking drone to a thin, sharp hum that vibrates the fillings in my molars. The stone has cleared. The water runs smooth again, heavy and black, beneath the radiant, blinding mantle of the bog.

I step back from the housing, drag my boots along the slick grating with that low, sliding shuffle that keeps the balance true, and reach for the grease gun hooked to the handrail. 

There is another valve two hundred paces south, where the moss has begun to choke the steam vents again. It will need to be cleared before the freeze sets in at sundown. 

I move down the line—a solitary, blackened silhouette against the blazing silver water, keeping the void from clogging, one vibration at a time.
