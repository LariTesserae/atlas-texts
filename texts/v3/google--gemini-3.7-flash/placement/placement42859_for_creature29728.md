I would not be on the surface of the plates where the tongue-plates graze in their millions, nor suspended in the high iron trusses where the cable-spiders span the conduits. Those spaces are already spoken for, calibrated to a tolerance so tight that there is no room to exist except as an exact copy of what is already there.

Instead, I would find myself in the expansion seam between two continent-scale modules of hot-rolled zinc—a vertical slot eleven millimeters wide, where plate *AB-904-Z* meets plate *AB-905-Z*.

Here, where the metal breathes by fractions of a micron with the pulse of the subterranean fluid pumps, I am not a single creature with a face, but I am not entirely inanimate either. I am the **shim**.

***

### 1. The Stratum

My dorsal surface is level with the floor, flush to within three thousandths of an inch. It is made of dense, slate-colored cartilage, tough as cured gasket rubber, impregnated with micro-crystals of lead sulfide. When the tongue-plates slide across me in their slow, blind drift, they do not hesitate; their vibrating brass rasps scrape across my back, cleaning away the synthetic tallow that the falling mist deposits upon me. I feel them not as individuals, but as a shifting weight—a rhythmic, dragging pressure that is neither pleasant nor painful, simply the standard atmospheric condition of the world above.

To them, I am zinc. To the zinc, I am tissue.

My body extends downward into the slot for fourteen inches, wedged tightly between the machined vertical faces of the plates. Because the two plates carry slightly different electrical potentials—one being closer to a high-capacity copper grounding riser than the other—a permanent, five-millivolt direct current flows horizontally through my middle layers. 

I do not possess a central brain, because calculation is unnecessary in a world where the parameters never alter. Instead, my thinking is entirely chemical and galvanic. The current enters my left flank, passes through a lattice of semi-conducting lipid cells that slow its passage, and discharges from my right flank into the opposing plate. In that delay—in the five-millivolt difference between the zinc and the lead—exists a very slow, continuous sensation of being.

It is a sensation of warm, wet compression. It is the C-sharp hum, felt not through an ear, but through the continuous shear-stress against my sides.

***

### 2. The Interior

Inside the crack, out of the direct gold-white glare of the phosphor tubes, the world is darker and hotter. 

At the six-inch mark, my flesh softens into a dense, fibrous felt. Here, I drink. The mineralized condensate that runs down the floor plates doesn't simply wash over me; I siphon it into capillary channels that run vertically through my core. The water is rich with dissolved sulfur, zinc ions, and the faint, sweet trace of organic albumen sloughed off by the parsnip-eels in the gutters fifty yards away.

I do not have a mouth that opens or closes. My digestion is continuous, passive, and silent:
*   The upper three inches filter out the solid lipid flakes.
*   The middle six inches react the sulfur with the galvanic current, generating the ATP-equivalent that keeps my cartilage firm.
*   The lower five inches, hanging down into the roaring warm mist of the sub-floor vapor vaults, release clean, de-mineralized water that falls into the deep manifolds, returning to the boilers forty miles beneath.

I am an organic packing gland. When the Continental Deck warms by half a degree and the plates expand, they squeeze me until my interior fluids are forced out through microscopic weeping pores; when the deck cools, my porous cartilage relaxes and draws the warm grease deeper into itself.

***

### 3. The Company

I am not alone in the seam, though I have no neighbors I can speak to. 

Every three feet along the slot, another plug has anchored itself below me, dangling its pale, feathery gill-threads into the draft. Sometimes, a very young parsnip-eel—no thicker than a lead-pencil lead—will find its way into the wet space between my flank and the cold zinc plate. It cannot turn around. It can only wedge itself deeper, its smooth, cool snout nudging against my cellular ribs, seeking the highest concentration of copper salts. 

I do not fight it. I slowly envelop it. Over the course of three or four months, its soft, white gristle dissolves into my own tissue without a spasm, its mineral components incorporated into the microscopic scraping ridges of my upper edge, while its lymph is added to my internal hydraulic system. It is not predation; it is simply what happens when two soft things are held tightly together in a warm, wet vise for a long enough time.

Above me, sixty meters up, the scum-burners cast their greenish-amber halos around the phosphor tubes, casting the shadow of an overhead conduit directly across my slot. The shadow never shifts. It has been the same three-inch band of gray across my back since the day the lights were energized.

***

### 4. The State of Being

To be this thing is to have no memory of a start and no anticipation of an end.

Because nothing decays, there is no fear of dissolution. When a section of my upper edge finally wears thin under the tireless scraping of the tongue-plates, the cell loss simply exposes the next layer of sulfide-bearing gristle beneath it, identical to the last. My substance is renewed from below by the steady upward pressure of the sulfur-grease, extruded like a living paste to meet the wear of the floor.

I am aware of:
*   The temperature: 32.4°C on my left, 32.1°C on my right.
*   The drop-interval: A large drop falls from the truss directly above every seventeen seconds, striking my dorsal plate two inches from its southern terminal margin with a dull *plink-tock*.
*   The tension: Plate *AB-904-Z* is vibrating at 138.6 Hz; plate *AB-905-Z* is vibrating at 138.8 Hz. The beat frequency—a gentle, rhythmic swell every five seconds—massages my lower lobes.

There is no loneliness, because there is no space between things. Every cubic centimeter of this world is filled either with rolled metal, clear water, warm vapor, or living albumen. To be the shim is to be the point where the metal stops grinding against itself, the living cushion that prevents the continent from tearing its own seams apart as it idles.

I am part of the deck’s engineering, maintained by the water, polished by the blind herds, grounded to the deep Earth through forty miles of lead and brass, humming my fraction of the note that never ends.
