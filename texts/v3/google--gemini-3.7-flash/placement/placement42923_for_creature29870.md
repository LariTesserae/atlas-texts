The weight of the jaw is the first thing. 

It hangs slightly loose, unclasped, because if the teeth touch while the heels are down, the crowns of the premolars buzz together with the dry, stinging rattle of two river pebbles shaken in a pocket. Keep the mouth slack by half an inch, and the whole chamber enters through the soles instead, climbs the shins, parts at the pelvis, and comes to rest behind the bridge of the nose as a steady, warm pressure—not sound, exactly, but the feeling of someone leaning their full shoulder against the other side of a very thick door.

I am not the machine, and I am not the wood that drinks it. 

I am down where the root-knee dips toward the second drain, four paces back from the sweep of the counterweight. 

At first, sitting here, you might think you are looking at an old bundle of wet sailcloth or a knot where the grain turned back on itself after striking a boulder in the dark three hundred years ago. The wool of the smock has taken the color of the liverwort—that dull, slate-green that has no yellow in it—and where the sleeves have dragged through the drainage channels during the autumn clearing, the cuffs are stiffened with iron salts into flat, curved boards that ring like slate if they strike the floor.

The knee hurts only on the up-stroke. 

That is how the count is kept. When the long hemlock beam above groans against its bronze trunnion—a sound that doesn't reach the ears so much as it makes the skin across the ribs draw tight—there is a tiny slackening in the floor-grain. The root relaxes by the width of a fingernail while the head of river water fills the bottom cylinder. In that five-heartbeat pause, my left patella settles down into its groove. Then the piston takes the weight again: *chump-thung*. The root-arch tightens, the floor heaves upward perhaps the thickness of a wet leaf, and the joint in my leg pinches shut. 

One. 

Then the swell. The water lifting behind the oak wall with that heavy, tearing suction, like wet hides being pulled off a butcher’s bench. 

Two.

***

In the beginning—thirty years ago, or forty, when the hair on my arms was still pale and my hearing was still thin enough to be torn by the slap of the flume—I thought the work was to look at things. 

I watched the brass grease-cups. I watched the hemp braids fray into white hairs where the rod slid down into the stuffing box. I had the young man’s habit of turning the head quickly when a swallow clipped low through the dust-beam, or when a dead crawler rolled down the bevel of the foundation root and dropped into the pit.

That passes. The eyes are too slow, and they lie about the balance. 

By the time the blue stain on your forearms has met across the knuckles, you understand that looking only tells you what has already failed. If you see the hemp smoking, you are already three strokes too late; the bronze journal has already scored the iron neck, and the score will stay there for fifty years, singing its bad, sharp song into the soles of your feet every time the crank comes over the top.

Now, I keep the grease-paddle between my knees and wait until the third rib on the right side begins to flutter. 

A dry bearing does not squeak to us. It produces a small, greasy coldness right under the shoulder blade—a lack of drag that makes the whole sixty-pace room feel slightly too light on its feet, like a table with one leg an eighth of an inch short. When that coldness comes, I don’t have to look to see which cup is starved. The left trunnion pulls behind the left ear; the central crank-pin pulls at the small of the back; the bottom guide-shoe catches in the right groin.

I stand up on the half-beat, when the wood is rising. If you stand while the floor is falling, your ankles take the whole jar of the down-stroke, and after ten years of that, you walk like an old crow with your toes turned inward to save the arches.

***

There are four of us now, though two are still down in the light-wells where the air smells of wet grass and river silt, scraping the lichen off the intake grates with horn spoons so the head doesn't drop. They are young; they still talk with their hands, making long, sweeping gestures that mean nothing down here where everything that matters is shaped like a wedge or a circle.

They will come down later, when their ears have grown thick and quiet like dried figs. 

One of them will take this wooden bowl. It is carved from a burr of black walnut that grew into an iron bolt two centuries ago; the grain has swallowed the thread of the screw so completely that you can see the spiral pattern in the bottom of the dish like the ridges on a finger. It holds two pounds of tallow boiled with pulverized slate. 

When you rub it between your fingers, it doesn't feel slippery. It feels like very cold lard mixed with silt from the deepest pool below a weir—heavy, dense, impossible to wash out with water. It takes three days under the sun-chutes for the skin to stop smelling of sheep-fat and wet iron, but nobody goes up into the sun-chutes unless a dead-fall blocks the flume. The light up there is too wide; it has no walls to hold it together, and it makes the back of the neck feel bare and dangerous, like a sheep when the dog is behind it.

Down here, the vault has no corners where anything can gather that wasn't invited.

***

Sometimes, near the sixth hour after noon—when the sun reaches that one crooked gap between the third and fourth taproot ribs—the light falls directly across the face of the great flywheel. 

It is twelve feet of grey iron, pitted like the rind of an old cheese where the sand fell away in the casting before my grandfather’s grandfather had a name. The dry sap from the roof has dripped on the spokes until they look like the legs of old horses, swollen at the hocks with dark amber resin.

As it turns, the shadow of the balance-beam cuts across the light in one long, slow, downward slice. 

Down. 

Then the shaft of dust-motes snaps back into white fire as the crank clears the pit. 

Up.

A crawler moves an inch along the grain beside my left heel. It is flat, slate-colored, the size of a copper penny, its little shield-plates grey with the dry powder of swallow-droppings it has ground up during the noon hour. It doesn't hurry. It doesn't move when the down-stroke hits; it has six tiny notches on its rim that grip the ridges of the heartwood, so the vibration just passes through its carapace and out through its feelers into the air, keeping its shell as clean and bright as a river pebble.

I put the flat of my hand down beside it. 

The wood is cold, but it isn't the cold of stone. It is the cold of something very old that is still drawing water from three miles of dark gravel under the meadows. Deep in the core of the timber, two inches below the blue-black skin, the sap is moving up toward the branches four hundred feet above in the wind, and the iron pump is pushing the river down into the stone flume behind the wall, and the two currents cross each other in the grain without touching, like two men passing on a narrow stair in the dark.

The piston goes down: *chump*.

My thumb-joint clicks in answer. 

The hemp smells of sheep and mineral oil. 

A gnat hits the spider-line twelve paces up, and the thread drops its pitch by half a tone—not enough for the swallows to notice, but enough to make the skin on the back of my hand tighten against the wood.

I reach for the paddle. The crank-pin is coming round for the seventh hundredth time since the sun hit the rib, and the grease-pocket is hungry for slate.
