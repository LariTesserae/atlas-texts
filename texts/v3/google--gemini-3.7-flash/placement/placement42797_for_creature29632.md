I would not be in the flume lines, nor among the foragers diving into the wet rot of the gutters. 

I would be wedged into the northernmost shear-joint—the high, narrow crook where the primary suspension line leaves the living hemlock cambium and meets the first lashed rush-beam of the upper tier. 

It is the coldest station in the grid. Up here, where the vaulted ceiling of black rootlets narrows into a tight, weeping wedge, the draft does not circulate; it shears. It comes through the peat-crack like a drawn wire, smelling of ancient, unrotted moss and pulverized slate. 

At first, I might mistake myself for a piece of the joinery. 

My spine is curled hard into the concave sweep of the root-cleat, my flanks pressed flat between the bitter, wet sap-wall and a cured strut of elder-rib. My vellum hide is stiffening at the edges—the elderberry oil I secrete is slower now, more resinous, gumming my flank-folds with a tacky glaze that catches stray spores. My eyes, long absent, leave no ache, only the broad spade of my skull pressed flat against the wood, its concentric rings of micro-vibrissae splayed like moss-filaments against the bark.

I am a tuner. A keeper of the pitch.

My four needle-claws do not file themselves against peat for speed; they are ground flat at the tips, shaped to catch the fine cross-weaves of spider-silk sinew without severing them. Every few dozen pulses of my heart, I reach out with the inner digit of my left limb and hook the lead line—the thick, triple-braided hawser that holds the weight of forty dwellings and three thousand sleeping harvestmen above the empty shaft.

I pluck.

The vibration does not enter my ears; I have none to speak of. It strikes the keratin of my claw, runs the light bone of my forelimb, and sets my lower jaw into an intense, ringing hum.

*D-sharp below the threshold.* Too slack. 

A hundred feet above our heads, out in the open sky we have no memory of, a squall is tilting the crown of the hemlock. The taproot is leaning back on its heel, widening the tear in the earth by the breadth of a mite’s leg. If I leave the line slack, the next hydraulic heave will fill the lower flumes and the sag will drown the secondary nursery alleys in near-freezing mud. 

I tap out four dry, hard strokes against the cedar anchor-peg with my right heel: *Lift. Bind. Three turns.*

Across the empty vertical shaft, through the bitter, wet fog, the answer comes back through the floorboards—two sharp clicks from a team of three in the lower tier, levering a pine-needle spar to take up the drift. The silk tightens. The jawbone sings again, climbing to a clean, taut, glassy hum that sets the micro-hairs on my crown shivering.

Only then do I allow my body to remember its cold.

My heart is a frantic scarlet knot beating against the root-flesh, but it is slowing. The draft is stealing the heat from my limbs faster than my blood can push it out from my core. Below me, through the slatted floor of split rush, I can feel the low, seismic furnace of the shrew-cribs—that roaring, violent 104-degree radiance where the young sleep stacked four-deep in the furnace of living fur, twitching in grease-heavy dreams. 

I do not go down to them as often now. The scalding heat of the shrews feels less like relief and more like a tear in the skin; the transition from their boiling fur back into the draft makes my bone-claws brittle. 

Instead, I draw the thatch closer. 

With my spare digits, I reach into the trembling, tangled mat of blind harvestmen that clings to the underside of the anchor-cleat. I brush their hair-fine knees with the musk-gland behind my forelimb. They respond without hurry, shifting their long, jointed legs over my shoulders, weaving a moving, twitching canopy of dry chitin across my back. Their communal shudder breaks the draft. They do not warm me, but they keep the frost from seating itself in the seams of my joints. Under their weight, the air smells of dried grass, cold iron, and the dull, sweet rot of old cambium.

I live in the interval between heaves.

When the deep water comes—that sudden, silent swelling that forces the glass-laminate of swamp-melt through the roof—I do not look for grubs. I drop my chin flush to the living cambium and feel the hemlock drink. 

The taproot groans. It is a sound too vast and slow for the foragers in the alleys to heed, but up here, it is a tectonic shudder that travels straight through the crown of my head. The root is alive; it hates the tear we live in; it spends every season trying to swell shut, to crush our gridded streets back into solid, unbreathing wood, or to rip apart and drop our rush-bridges into the gravel sumps below.

My purpose is to feel where the wood intends to close, and where it intends to break.

Soon—perhaps before this flume-cycle completes its drain, perhaps a few hundred storms from now—the elderberry oil will stop flowing entirely. My skin will dry to the color and stiffness of birch-bark. My ribs, curved and light as needle-shavings, will not leave this corner. A younger builder will come up the rush-ladder with a gut-string and a blackthorn spine, unhook my stiffened claws from the primary line, and lash my dried pelvis directly into the corner-brace to stop the windward flutter.

My spade-skull will remain pegged to this cambium. My jaw will keep the tension when the tundra winds wrench the upper branches. 

I will feel the pitch rise. I will feel the city hold.
