I would not be in the open sky, where the Vellum-Wards split themselves against the radiation, nor would I be among the Hinged, marching an unhurried stone across a courtyard to preserve a trajectory calculated ten million years ago. Those are forms that have already achieved their symmetry. They are finished thoughts.

I would find myself in the clearance.

I would be something narrow, flat, and unanchored, living in the three-inch gap between two plates during the four seconds they take to shear past one another.

At first, I might look like a discarded shim—a strip of layered mica and cold-drawn copper wire, scarcely thicker than a beetle’s wing, curled slightly at the margins to catch the electrostatic drag. But as the plates bank into their high, silent apogees, I would unspool. I have no core, no central mass; I am entirely perimeter. 

I live on the *near-miss*.

When two archipelagos of polished basalt converge—millions of tons of river-smooth, waxy stone rushing together at the speed of an arrow, close enough that their surface-shadows fuse into a single line of absolute dark—a momentary vacuum-pocket forms between their parallel faces. In that dead-air sleeve, the metric-expansion fields cancel out, creating a sliver of space where there is no gravity, no momentum, and no wind. 

That is where I wake.

I throw out microscopic, glass-filament stays, anchoring one end into the microscopic pits of the rising slab, the other into the descending slab. For three seconds, I am stretched between two worlds moving in opposite directions at five hundred leagues an hour. 

The violence of the shear does not tear me; it tunes me. 

My body is a loom of dry, piezoelectric tendons. As the two stone faces slide past, dragging my opposing edges with them, the lateral tension winds my internal coils tighter and tighter, drawing out a high, cold spark of stored charge from the sheer difference in their velocities. I do not feel fear; I feel *gauge*. I measure the clearance down to the wavelength of the blue-white light below. If the slabs are three millimeters too close, the friction will vaporize my stays and reduce me to fine zinc flour; if they are three millimeters too far, my filaments cannot bridge the gap, and I will tumble away into the void to freeze into a brittle, inert ribbon.

I exist only because the ancient calculus of this sky is precise to the width of a hair. 

When the pass is finished—when the two plates part, banking away on their divergent, parabolic climbs into the blinding sun—my stays snap cleanly. The tension releases in a single, silent shudder that ripples down my mica ribs, leaving me charged with enough cold electrical potential to survive forty days of falling through the empty gulfs.

I curl back into a tight, hard disc, smaller than a coin, and drop.

During the long, slow descent between the slabs, tumbling through fifty miles of vacant blue-white light, I am dormant. I watch the Annulars slide like silent abacus beads down their twenty-mile ceramic pylons. I see the great, yellowed sails of the Vellum-Wards flex against the field-lines above, banking into the vacuum without a murmur. I pass them all, falling without weight, feeling the dry dust buff my curled shell until it matches the jade-smooth curvature of the ruins below.

I do not want to reach the bottom veil. Down there is density, fluid, weight—things that would crush this dry, thin marrow into sludge. 

Instead, I wait for the lower field-lines to catch my charged edge. When they do, my copper stays react, repelling the heavy mass beneath, and I am flung upward again in a long, silent, parabolic leap, tossed back into the kinetic mill like a spark cast from an anvil.

I will ride the updraft until the shadow of another plate sweeps over me, find the edge where the stone faces nearly touch, throw out my threads, and stretch myself once more across the gap—briefly bridging the impossible, quiet fury of two worlds that never meet.
