I would not be above, hanging from the bronze fluting to catch the clean mist, nor would I be one of the heavy amber domes bearing the full, hot glare of the quartz disc. To be seen by that round eye overhead is to be bound to its clock, to spend one’s life counting the sweeps of the brass linkage and snapping rigid at every passing shadow.

I would find myself down in the blind tolerances.

At first, it feels like nothing more than an unevenness in the pressure—a place where the cold-rolled steel trough takes a five-degree drop toward the sump bolt, where the lathe left a microscopic ridge of curled burr that never got dressed away. 

That is where I am: under the shelf of a Brood-Dame’s third abdominal plate, wedged into the sub-stratum of the Plenars. 

Down here, there is no color, only viscosity. The light from the quartz disc reaches this depth only as a diffuse, honeyed glow filtered through three layers of living amber and the thick, yellowing suet that glues her clutch together. What I know of the world is entirely tactile, hydraulic, and resonant.

My back is soft—wet vellum, constantly lubricated by the thin run-off of condensed tallow and the alkaline wash the siphoners drip from the walls. I do not have a hard shell to protect me, nor do I need one; the heavy carapace above rests upon me not as a burden, but as a roof that vibrates in sympathetic pitch with the turbine’s core. When the machine turns deep behind the bulkhead, the steel beneath my belly hums at forty cycles, while the brass rib beside my flank rings at something higher, almost sweet. Between those two frequencies, my body is a damper. My micro-bristles—hundreds of them along every joint, fine as silk, stiff as copper wire—mesh into the flanks of the four Plenars flanking me. 

We do not have separate intentions. When the female above shifts her anchor in the blue bolt-head to let an egg settle, the sudden displacement of weight travels down through her ventral plates into my dorsal ridge. I do not decide to yield; my bristles slip three microns to the left, intermeshing deeper with the crawler beside me. The wave moves outward, dissipating into the mass until twenty thousand bodies have absorbed the fraction of a milligram of drift, and the clutch remains unbroken.

I am partly a cushion, partly a tongue.

Every two hundred seconds, the valve snaps. The sound down here is a sharp, hydraulic *thud* through the metal floor, followed by the slow, warm seepage of moisture reaching our level. When the droplets roll down the fluting, through the combs of the Carapaces and past the Dame’s waxy fringe, they arrive laden with the minerals of the upper casings—trace copper, aerosolized animal fat, the microscopic dusting of worn brass.

My maxillary palps are always moving through this grease. I groom the underside of her egg-sacs, sweeping away the tiny, crystalline hairs of the white screw-head fungus before they can take root in the wax. The taste of the fungus is chalky and dry; the taste of the grease is rich, heavy, and metallic, like warm iron dissolved in lard. I do not swallow much. Most of it is worked by my mouthparts into a slick, translucent mastic that I pack into the microscopic chatter-marks of the floor, smoothing the path for the swarm, reducing the friction of our endless, communal sliding.

There are moments when the shadow sweeps over. 

Even down here, beneath the bulk of the Dame and the press of the crowd, the darkness is instantly legible. The heat radiating from the quartz glass drops by a fraction of a milliwatt. 

When it happens, everything locks. The Dame’s heavy plates freeze; the bristles along my sides stiffen into rigid hooks; the fluid film between the steel and my skin halts mid-flow. In those four seconds of stillness, I feel the true shape of the steamer-trunk world: the cold, unyielding precision of the freshly cut ribs, the perfect concentricity of the bolt threads, the silent, absolute containment. There is nothing beyond the bronze casing. There has never been an outside. There is only the machine’s tolerance and the living tissue that fills it to zero clearance.

Then the shadow passes. The light warms the shell above me. The bristles soften, slip, and begin their dry, velvet stroke once more.

When my time comes—when the constant micro-chatter of the steel wears the fine hairs from my joints, or when the weight of the clutch presses me too flat against a lathe burr—I will not leave this trough. The Chisel-Jaws will reach beneath her skirt, their soot-blackened mandibles working with the neat, dispassionate geometry of a punch-die. They will shear my soft plates into five neat ribbons. The Plenar to my left will take the first piece; the Plenar to my right will take the second. Within forty sweeps of the brass arm, my vellum back will be the wax on a new egg, my bristles will be the combs of a Carapace, and my moisture will be back in the steam line, waiting for the valve to fire.

Until then, I am the thickness of a leaf between the cold steel and the living heat. I hold the seam.
