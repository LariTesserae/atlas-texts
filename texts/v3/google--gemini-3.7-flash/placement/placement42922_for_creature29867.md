I would be pressed flat against the seam where a wall of black glass shears against a column of grey calcite, hanging at a canted angle over a drop that has no bottom, only a sideways repetition of itself.

My eyes are long gone behind their scarred white lids. The glare does not touch me as sight; it registers only as a dry, flat weight upon the brow, a constant bleaching heat that produces no warmth. The world comes in through the jawbone, through the flat undersides of my knuckles, and through the honeycombed cavities in the heels of my feet.

Inside those small bone chambers, the mineral fluid shifts. When the chasm widens by a hair’s breadth or an upward-falling stream twists on its axis, I feel the level tilt inside my own skeleton before the stone gives any audible sign.

I am one of the old ones at the crossing. 

My chest is a wide, unyielding barrel of fused bone, built like a dry-stacked arch to keep the acoustic pressure of the chasms from collapsing my lungs. My skin is thick, dead-white, and heavy with the tallow I sweat along my spine—a pungent, waxy grease that smells of wet chalk and sheep-fat, sealing the biting condensation away from my blood. Without it, the water that sweats out of the void would leach the heat from my marrow in a hundred breaths.

My place is not in the shelters, though I sleep there when the seams grind too hard. My place is wedged into a spatial pinch where two needles lean within arm’s reach, yet are separated by a gravity-shear that would tear a loose stone in half. 

To my left, the water weeps downward along the slate in slick, silent aprons. To my right, three feet away across the seam, a braided river of condensation runs straight upward toward the searing white ceiling of the sky, spinning like a fluted glass rod. 

I live in the hinge between them.

My left hand holds a chisel-ended bronze spud, its metal beaten thin and scarred by three generations of hands before mine. My right hand is hooked through a braided line made of siphon nerves, cured in brine until it feels like wet wire. Suspended from my hip is a teardrop of dark basalt that pulls not down toward my feet, but outward at a hard diagonal, dragging me perpetually toward an invisible center of mass that floats in the blinding air. I do not fight the pull; I lean into it, letting the spatial seam hold my weight like a stiff hammock.

I am waiting for the pitch of the spire to change.

Every hour, or what passes for an hour in a place without shadows, I press the flat of my chin against the calcite. The whole mountain is a bell struck by water. The troughs—those flat, slate-plated mollusks—are clustered six inches below my knees, their pyrite teeth locked into the obsidian. Through the stone, I can feel their shells vibrating, translating the roaring friction of the chasm into a low, piezoelectric hum that warms the rock just enough to keep my bare knees from sticking to the frost-rime.

When their hum sharpens, I know a cascade is tearing free above.

I reach out into the upward-falling braid. My hand passes through the seam—a momentary, sickening twist in the wrist where down becomes up—and enters the spinning water. It is bitingly cold, dense as oil. My fingers, blunt-nailed and calloused with tallow, close around the yielding, glass-clear gelatin of a thread-marrow. It does not struggle; it has no muscles to fight with, only the hydrostatic pressure of its clear belly. I draw it through the seam, feeling the sudden weight as its fluid enters my gravity, and strip the fibrous cord along its back with a thumbnail.

This is the braid-work. I splice the dried siphon threads into our suspension lines, working in the sulfurous, flameless warmth of a pyrite heat-stone wedged into a cleft beneath my belly. 

When another Keeper crawls along the lower traverse, seeking the fat-basins, they do not call out. They strike an adze against the flint wedge driven into the calcite three yards below me: *clink-clink, pause, clink*. 

The vibration travels up through the stone, through the aragonite shells of the troughs, through my kneecaps, and into my jaw. 

*The seam is narrowing,* the strike says. *The water is thickening with salt.*

I answer by tapping the heel of my bronze spud against the basalt pendulum hanging at my waist: a single, resonant note that dies quickly in the acoustic roar of the chasm. *I am anchored. The line holds.*

We do not dream of soil or green things; our minds have no shapes for them. We know only the cold, frictionless slide of the glass, the iron-sweet taste of trough-meat cracked fresh from its polygonal shell, and the ringing, shivering tension of the lines that keep us pinned to the sky. 

I know that one day the chime in my jaw will drop an octave. The calcite will have thinned too far, dissolved by the endless wash of airborne water; the spatial seam will slip its notch, and the sideways gravity will snap shut like an adze hitting flint. When it happens, the cradles will spill their stones, the spinning braids of water will shatter into spray, and we will fall into the lateral void along with the shards of our mountains.

Until then, I stay wedged in the seam. I take the tallow from my spine, dress the siphon-line between my scarred fingers, and lean thirty degrees into the white, roaring nothingness, listening to the bone-deep song of the stone holding itself together against the glare.
