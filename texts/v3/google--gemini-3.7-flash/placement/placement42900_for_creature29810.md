You do not start by knowing what you are; you start by knowing where the weight falls.

First comes the hydrostatic thump beneath the heels—a low, wet percussion traveling up through three inches of vulcanized gum-rubber, through the bronze outriggers bolted to the soles, through the shins, settling in the small of the back like the vibration of a ship’s boiler room. 

I am out on the South Flute when the noon-siphon engages. 

Everyone else goes down into the dead core before the meridian. When the sun stands perpendicular to the basalt, the mountain stops venting its heat into the sky and starts collecting it like a concave mirror. The air locks hiss shut in the rock galleries three hundred feet below; the heavy latch-dogs turn with a dull iron clunk; and the catwalks belong entirely to the steam, the beasts, and whatever is foolish or thick-skinned enough to stay between them.

I stay.

My hands are wrapped in strips of boiled sailcloth, stiff with sheep fat and gray molybdenum paste. They are not dexterous hands anymore—they are clamps. In the right fist is a long-hafted caulking iron, its spade-tip notched to seat against the high-pressure brass rivets of the condenser trunks; in the left, a pigskin bladder of cold tallow.

If you asked the men in the galleries what I am, they’d call me a *Seam-Tender* or a *Gasket-Calker*, though neither word fits the work. Machinery doesn’t stay bolted to the Spine on its own terms. An iron shoe sunk six feet into the rock will stay true for twenty years, but where the iron meets the basalt, the lithotherms crowd in like barnacles on an anchor chain. They taste the sulfur wash and the lukewarm water sweating off the pipes, and they shoulder against the footings with a hundred tons of blind hydraulic muscle. If a disc works its way under a main stanchion, the torque will shear a two-inch steel pin like a carrot.

My job is the gap. The narrow, scalded border where iron, living horn, and raw basalt try to occupy the same four square inches.

Through the smoked glass of my mask, the world is reduced to shades of tea and burnt copper. The sky above is not blue—it is a heavy, dark plum, almost black at the zenith, where the air is too thin to hold the light. Below, the mountain is a blinding, liquid glare. The moisture flashing off the plates makes a pale, shimmering halo two feet above the terrace, through which the backs of the great millstones look like sunken stepping-stones in a boiling creek.

I take a stride.

It is not a human walk. You cannot lift your feet high; the wind off the northern drop will catch a raised boot like a sail and lever you over the edge before you can drop your center. You slide. You keep your weight balanced over three points at once—heel on the crown of an elder plate, toe-flange resting on its neighbor, lateral bronze spar bridged across to a third. You feel for the dull, mineral firmness at the disc's center. You avoid the margins. 

To the side of my foot, a narrow seam yawns open by two inches as a three-foot plate cycles its vents. I catch the smell through the mask's charcoal box: not just sulfur, but the heavy, fatty sweetness of hot marrow, like a soup kettle that has boiled dry on a stove.

A ribbon of quicksilver flashes across my boot. A solder-skink, drawn by the shadow my canvas smock casts against the glare. For three seconds, it presses its belly against the vulcanized rubber of my instep, using my dead weight as a momentary sunshade while it snaps up two pale isopods that were clinging to the seam. I can feel the frantic, microscopic vibration of its ribs—hundreds of beats a minute, an engine running so hot and fast it will burn itself to charcoal in three seasons. Then the shadow shifts as I lean into the caulking bar, and the skink pours itself away into the steam, bright as spilled mercury.

Above me, the No. 4 condenser makes its stroke.

It is forty feet of black-enameled iron, balanced on a cantilevered A-frame that hangs out over a four-thousand-foot drop into the valley haze. The stroke takes twelve seconds to rise, twelve to fall. 

*Hhhhh-tunk.*

The sound is small in the thin air, but the iron catwalk shudders. A cloud of sweet, lukewarm condensate—stripped of salt and copper by the scrubbers—pours over my shoulders from the overhead drip-pans. It drenches the canvas of my smock, washing away the crusts of dried mineral sweat, soaking through the tallow-soaked duck to cool the skin beneath. For five heartbeats, inside the brass box of the mask, the air smells like river rain.

Then the steam clears, the sun catches the wet cloth, and the heat closes back in like a heavy wool blanket pulled over the face.

I kneel. The knee-pads are thick ash-wood, cupped to sit on the dome of a living shell without cracking the laminated rings. 

Beneath me, the plate shifts slightly. It knows the weight. It doesn’t have eyes, but its rim-pits register the infrared shadow of my bulk, and its anchor pads dig half an inch deeper into the basalt with a slow, wet, grinding sound like millstones turning in gravel. We are old acquaintances, this plate and I. Its center ring is notched where a dropped spanner scarred the horn twelve years ago; the scar has grown outward in three concentric circles of pale copper-green, pushed toward the edge by new growth from the core.

I lean down and drive the notch of the caulking iron between the plate’s rim and the flange of the intake pipe. 

A jet of scalding mud and bacterial foam hisses out against the tool, splattering the smoked glass of my mask with yellow freckles. I work the bar with the heel of my palm, rhythmic and steady, feeling for the soft, spongy resistance of the sealing-ring. The iron enters two inches, three. I squeeze the bladder, forcing a fist-sized plug of cold tallow and graphite into the void behind the tool to keep the sulfur-crust from binding the bronze sleeves.

The lithotherm groans beneath my knees. Not a voice—just the deep, hydraulic settling of two hundred pounds of oil and copper-blood re-distributing its ballast. The plate dips a fraction of an inch, the iron clears, and the intake flange settles back into its bed of vulcanized rubber with a dull, satisfying *chock*.

I don't look up at the Vane-Wings circling in the purple void above. If you look at them, you tilt your neck; if you tilt your neck, the seal on the rebreather’s collar lifts, and the thin air tastes like burning matches for an hour afterward. You keep your chin down. You watch the rock, the plates, the joints where the grease has gone gray and dry.

I am not the mountain, and I am not the machine that drinks it. 

I am the thing that keeps them from tearing each other apart. The wedge of tallow between the piston and the basalt; the slow, two-legged beetle that crawls across the armor while the sun is too white for the hunters and the air too thin for the engineers. 

When the sun begins to lean west and the shadow of the needle-peaks stretches out across the cloud-floor below, the airlock klaxon will buzz in the iron under my boots—three long, vibrating pulses. The others will come up the ladders with their wrenches and their copper wire, their voices loud and thin in the brass rebreathers, smelling of coal-smoke and pressurized bunkhouses.

They will find the flanges seated. They will find the intake lines clear of horn, the grease-boxes packed, and the catwalks cleared of dead isopods.

By then, I’ll be sitting in the shadow of the low-pressure accumulator, where the condensation runs down the iron columns in cold, finger-thick streams. I’ll have the mask unlatched just enough to let the lip-valve drink from the collector cup, tasting the dark, slate-cold mineral runoff that has passed through three miles of stone and fifty feet of copper pipe, waiting for the rock to cool enough to let the mountain sleep.
