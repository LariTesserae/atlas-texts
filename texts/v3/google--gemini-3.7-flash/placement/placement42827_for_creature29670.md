I would not be above, where the fronds spread wide to catch the milk-white glare, nor below, adrift in the high-pressure dark of the deeper root-tunnels. 

I would be found in the narrow, unventilated seam where a square-hewn lintel of gray basalt meets the crook of a five-sided aqueduct stem—wedged into the exact angle where ninety-degree human geometry is being swallowed by the one-hundred-and-twenty-degree geometry of the living floor.

I would be an **Intercalary**—a creature of the shear-line.

***

### Form and Substance

I am neither masonry nor succulent, but an accretion that exists only because the two have been pressed together for three thousand years without yielding.

My body is small—scarcely the length of a mason’s forearm—and completely flat, having grown into the microscopic tolerances between the chisel-grooves of the basalt and the glassy, waxed cuticle of the plant. I am shaped like a wedge of polished slate, but my substance is composite: alternating lamellae of dense, living horn and flexible, mica-thin plates of cold silica. 

I have no eyes, because in this basin there is nothing to watch that does not remain where it is. Instead, my entire upper plate is a single, continuous sheet of pale, chatoyant spar that receives the unvarying white-gold noon through the translucent green leaf-flesh above me. Light enters me not as an image, but as a continuous, mild fever—a steady caloric value that keeps the viscous fluid between my plates at a constant, frictionless boiling point.

I have no limbs for walking. I have no need to travel. My underside is divided into thousands of microscopic, glass-hard teeth that have seated themselves into the tooth-marks of the ancient iron chisels still visible on the basalt. I am anchored to the memory of the builders' tools.

***

### The Sense of Being

To be here is to exist as a *strain gauge*.

The plant swells with an immense, slow, hydraulic ambition, drawing volatile mineral water upward toward the selenite vault. The basalt, dense and indifferent, simply occupies its coordinates. Between the two, the pressure is immense—hundreds of pounds per square inch of steady, silent torque.

My consciousness is the measurement of that pressure. 

I do not think in words, nor in images, but in **the tension of the joint**. When the sap-flow surges three miles to the south, an Aulon turns in an internal conduit, and the frond above me stiffens by the width of a hair; I feel it as a harmonic shift in the plates of my back, a low, singing note of pure mechanical compression. When the almond-skinned figure sitting upon the lintel above me draws its single, monthly breath—filling its fibrous lungs with warm paraffin and mint—the basalt tilts by a fraction of an angstrom, and the note deepens.

I am the ear that listens to the immobility of the world.

***

### Sustenance and Interface

I do not siphon the free-hanging dew like the Loricates, nor do I distill the light like the Lenticular Disks. My nourishment is purely interstitial.

Along the line where the plant's cuticle is crushed against the stone, a microscopic seepage occurs—not sap, which would burst under such force, but an ultra-filtered, oily lymph pressed directly out of the plant's cell-walls by the weight of the masonry. It is rich in silicate salts and liquid waxes. 

My lateral margins—fringed with combs of stiff, glass-clear cilia—draw this lymph inward through capillary action. It passes through my plates, depositing microscopic layers of quartz along my edges, and is extruded out my posterior edge as a cold, crystalline grout that permanently seals the space behind me. 

I am slowly, imperceptibly cementing the world together. Where the plant might have gently shoved the stone aside over the course of ten thousand years, I turn their contact into a single, fused mineral-biological weld.

***

### The View from the Seam

From where I am lodged, the great basin is not a landscape of hills and sky; it is a solid, illuminated mass of varying densities.

I perceive the selenite ceiling above as a vast, white weight pressing downward. I perceive the root-continent below as an immense, green bladder vibrating with millions of tons of trapped water. 

And three cubits above me, resting on the basalt beam, is the foot of the Masonry-Keeper. 

I know the shape of that foot better than it knows itself. I know the micro-fissures in the dry bone of the heel; I know the exact thickness of the almond-colored wax that seals its arch; I know the weight of its long, unmoving patience. Sometimes, over the course of a century, a single drop of condensation will roll down the keeper’s shin, hang for twenty years at the tip of the ankle, and finally fall with that distant, pressurized *tock* onto the plant-flesh beside me.

The impact sends a ring of microscopic ripples through my upper spar. My plates register the frequency, compensate for the moisture, and settle once more into the balance.

***

### Permanence

I do not fear being crushed, for the basalt is true and the plant is elastic. I do not fear starvation, for the noon never tilts toward dusk and the sap never fails.

If I have an end, it will not be death, but petrifaction. Layer by layer, the quartz I secrete from the plant's lymph is thickening my carpace. In five thousand years, the gap between the lintel and the stem will be entirely filled with my laminated body, turned completely to stone—a living wedge that has become a permanent structural bracket of the world, holding the masonry of the past in the living fist of the present, under the unblinking eye of the selenite sky.
