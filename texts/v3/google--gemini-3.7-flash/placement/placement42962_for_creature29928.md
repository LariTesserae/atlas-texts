At first, there is only the weight of the warmth, which is not heavy like an animal sitting on you, but broad, even, and everywhere at once, like being laid flat between two thick sheets of heated linen.

I am down where the light is loudest. 

I do not mean that it makes a sound in the air, though the hollow has its own quiet hum—the slow, dry friction of the disc-wings spinning in the updraft forty feet above, and the rhythmic *clink* of the water-drop that arrives with the patient cadence of a falling tooth. I mean that the light is an acoustic thing to the skin. It presses against the chalk of my forehead; it pushes back against the travertine ledge; it holds everything in the bowl like a heavy paste.

I do not have a face that opens and closes. 

Over the places where an eye might have been, there is only a smooth, clear pane of lime-skin, hard as a water-worn pebble. When the sun passes overhead through the great jagged hole in the world’s ceiling, the glare does not blind me because there is no dark inside me for it to ruin. It simply turns the inside of my skull into an amber room, then a white room, then an amber room again as the circle of heat crawls across the white silt floor.

I am long, and wide, and barely thicker than a split cedar shingle. 

My ribs are not a cage; they are a pair of open palms spread flat against the limestone. Between the rock and the underside of my belly there is no air at all—only the microscopic seam where my skin, which is soft and gray-pink like wet suede underneath, drinks the stone. 

In the hours before the circle of sun enters the well, the rock gives up its breath. A fine, bitter sweat rises through the micro-pores of the travertine, carrying with it the taste of deep lime, dissolved shell, and old rain. I do not have to seek it out; my underside is thirsty with a thousand tiny mouths that have no teeth. I press harder into the ledge. I widen the span of my toes until the claws bite into the calcium glaze. I drink until my belly swells by the breadth of a grain of sand, and that is enough for the turning of the day.

Where am I?

I have settled myself in the southern curve, down in the pale dust where the shadow stays longest, just behind the hollowed sandstone block. 

I know the shape of that block better than the things that made it. It has been cold for so long that it has forgotten the hands that turned in it, but the hollow in its center has the exact curvature of my spine when I curl against the noon glare. Beside my left flank lies a split bone, blunted and yellowed, locked to the rock by a crust of white salt that grows around its joints like creeping frost. Sometimes, when a breeze slips over the high calcrete lip forty paces above and shivers down into the bowl, the dust shifts over the bone, and a microscopic chime rings through the floor—a faint, dry vibration that travels directly into my jaw.

I do not fear the people who left the bone. Their absence has the same shape as the stone: heavy, smooth, and entirely unhurried. They came down to scrape the tendon-succulents, to take the clear, thick jelly that keeps the hide from cracking under the cruel wind of the plain above. They were soft things, wet and temporary, who came and went on two feet. But the hollow has folded over their footprints with lime and dust, and now their tools are simply more ledges for the skinks to lie upon.

Above me, the world drifts without landing.

I can feel the disc-insects by their shadowlessness. When one of them dies—its wafer of mica-chitin finally thinned to nothing by the scouring dust—it does not drop like a stone. It wanders down the thermal column, tilting left, tilting right, rocking on the heat like a petal through water. 

I do not chase it. To move quickly in this well would be to spend moisture that cannot be bought back until the next dawn’s sweat. I wait. 

When it strikes the silt two inches from my snout, it makes a sound like a pinch of dry salt falling on an anvil. I wait three drops of the water-clock. Then I tilt my jaw.

My mouth is small, dry, and lined with hard ridges. The insect does not bleed; it breaks between my jaws with the fine, brittle fracture of a dried eggshell. It tastes of sun-bleached chalk, air, and the bitter, faint residue of succulent-spores. It dissolves into dust upon my tongue, and that dust becomes part of the pebbled wax of my hide.

I am not alone on the ledge, but we do not touch. A foot to my right, another milk-skink lies pinned to the stone, its throat pulsing once every fifty drops of water. We share the warmth without dividing it. We know where the other begins by the thermal shadow cast against the rock—a slight, cool presence that marks the perimeter of a neighbor’s rest.

In the late afternoon, when the white beam tilts up the northern overhang and begins to ignite the hanging gray moss, the water-cups on the floor turn the color of polished brass. 

I feel the strike of the drops down in the marrow of my legs:
*Tap.*
*Tap.*
*Tap.*

Inside each cup, thirty paces away, the two salt-specks of water-fleas are riding the shockwave down into the blue-green silt and rising again with the rebound, circling their thimble of universe in the dark beneath the glare. They will never leave their basin; I will never climb the river-cobble footholds to the lip above; the tendon-plant will never let go of the floor it dissolves to hold.

The air begins to cool, turning from the breath of an oven to the breath of an empty cellar. The slaked lime smell deepens, edged with the sudden sharpness of condensing stone.

I pull my claws inward by half a grain. I draw my chin down until it rests flat against the ancient grinding stone, letting the petrified bone awl brace my side. 

I do not sleep, because sleep belongs to creatures that need to forget the day. Here, there is nothing to forget. The sun goes away, but the stone remembers its heat for a long time, and I am sewn so closely to its back that as long as the rock stays warm, I remain.
