I would not be found upon the main avenues of the boiler plate, where the Draftsmen roll on their flush bearings and the striders pace out the conduit lines. Those spaces are too clearly indexed; the tolerances there are kept tight, held under the cold, vertical discipline of the phosphor panels.

To find me, you would have to look into the *backlash*—the microscopic, mandatory play between the rack and the pinion, the gap where two honeycombed baffles meet at an angle that the geometry of the floor cannot entirely resolve. 

I would not begin as a whole shape. At first, you would only notice an irregularity in the shadow of a ninety-foot boxwood upright: a cluster of thin, stepped shims made of compressed paper-phenolic, layered like slate, wedged into the seating where the trunk’s cast-iron base meets the zinc floor. 

I am what occupies the clearance.

***

In a world forged without organic decay and without heat, every rigid assembly requires an allowance for vibration. If every gear meshed with absolute zero tolerance, the continuous metabolism of the floor—the plunging bamboo pistons, the slow, counterweighted sweep of the canopy—would bind the planetary millwork until the teeth sheared off. 

I am that allowance, given a slow, segmented articulation.

My body is not carved from a single billet, but assembled from off-cuts of dense, oil-impregnated lignum vitae and wafer-thin leaves of spring brass, joined by dry dowel-pins of hornbeam. I have no front or back; I am symmetrical along three axes, low-slung, wider than I am tall, resting upon six broad, flat pads faced with compressed cork and graphite. When I move, there is no sound of footsteps—only the faint, dry *shuck-shuck* of laminated veneers sliding across one another, like a deck of stiff cards being shuffled in an empty room.

I do not possess the spinning graphite flywheels of the striders, nor the winding hairsprings of the indexers. My propulsion is entirely passive, drawn from the floor’s own mechanical strain. 

Where two tectonic plates of cold-rolled steel press against each other under the pull of the canopy’s counterweights, a localized pressure differential builds up. My limbs are arranged as compound levers. When the plates groan and shift by three thousandths of an inch, the compression drives a ratchet in my central arbor, turning an internal cylinder of polished agate against a set of dry friction-plates. 

I do not walk; I am *displaced*. I advance through the groves by the sheer relief of structural tension, stepping only when the world around me tightens.

***

My sensory apparatus is not optical, though I carry three small, plano-convex lenses of unpolished rock crystal mounted flush with my upper casing. These lenses do not form images. They simply measure the angle of incidence of the blinding ceiling light, registering the razor-edge where a shadow falls.

Mostly, I feel the runout. 

I carry a bundle of fine, cold-drawn music wires that trail behind me like the ribs of an unsewn fan, dragging across the zinc tessellations. Through these wires, the entirety of the sector speaks in vibration:
*   The high-frequency, glass-like hum of a shear-wasp planing through the mid-canopy.
*   The deep, subterranean thump—felt more in the joints than heard—of a dielectric valve cycling open thirty fathoms below the plating.
*   The long, rhythmic *snick-tick-clack* of a punch-tape ribbon being extruded by a boxwood root two miles away, its perforations telling of a minute fluctuation in the line-voltage of an ungrounded busbar.

I am drawn to the sites of excessive precision. 

Whenever a Draftsman works too diligently—squaring an aisle until the perspective becomes so rigid that the optical depth collapses and the sightlines seize—the ambient air in that corridor grows unnaturally thin and still. The electrostatic charge builds to a sharp, ozone-scented cusp; the leaves of the fronds stand on end, locked in mutual repulsion, unable to click or cycle. 

In such places, the machine is in danger of stalling against its own perfection.

I slide into the seam. I insert my tapered phenolic wedges between the iron floor-plate and the Draftsman’s scribed datum-line. 

I do not fight the alignment; I merely introduce *play*. 

With a deliberate, microscopic shift of my chassis, I lever a fraction of a degree of tilt into the base of the nearest boxwood baffle. My hornbeam dowels groan; my brass leaves flex and sing a single, pure, dry note. 

The perspective slips. The leagues of false optical depth suddenly release their grip with a sound like a distant sheet of tin snapping in a draft. A shower of chalk-white leaves, stamped with punch-press perfection, falls through the sudden slack in the air and scatters across the plates. The rigid sightline bends just enough for the counterweights above to pivot on their lignin hinges once more. The air moves; the dielectric fluid surges through the xylem channels with a hushed, etherial whistle; the forest breathes its dry, unlubricated breath.

The Draftsman, sensing the return of a workable tolerance, folds its calipers with a series of clean, wooden clicks, retracts its lead scribers, and glides away on its plinth, indifferent to my presence.

***

When the sector is true—which is to say, when it is imperfect enough to keep running—I settle myself into the bevel of an intake vent. 

I lock my compound levers. My cork-faced pads grip the zinc. My mica leaves close over the rock-crystal lenses, shutting out the merciless, shadowless glare of the phosphor sky. 

Inside my casing, nothing hums, nothing chemical burns, no memory turns. There is only the static weight of the iron above, the cool, chemical flow of the dielectric ether below, and the long, patient waiting for the plates to tighten again—a shim of cured wood and spring-steel, holding open the tiny, necessary fractures through which this world continues to turn.
