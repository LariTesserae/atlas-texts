I would not be in the light, where the peltate leaves turn the air into green river glass, nor down in the black grease where the weir-keepers strain the duckweed under the low pull of the bores. Those places have edges. They touch the void above or the rock beneath.

I would be in the **stuffing-box**—the gland where the six-inch brass piston rod passes out of the buried cylinder into the open peat.

I did not begin as an animal, nor as a deliberate planting. If I have a lineage, it is the layered packing that an engineer long ago hammered into the annular space between the cast-iron cylinder head and the moving shaft: rings of plaited hemp soaked in tallow, braided with graphite yarn and copper wire. But hemp is only dried stem, and tallow is only fat; given sixty years of warm, turnip-scented steam and iron-rich seepage, the dead fiber remembers how to take root, and the fat learns to circulate.

I am what filled that sleeve when the original gasket rotted into life.

My body is entirely annular—a thick, wet collar of compressed, fibrous flesh, eighteen inches in diameter and three inches thick, held under four hundred pounds of continuous bolting pressure between the iron gland-flange and the cylinder throat. I have no outer limbs, no head, and no dorsal or ventral side. My anatomy is organized entirely in concentric rings around the stroke.

My inner circumference is a slick, hairless cartilage, smooth as horn and permanently saturated with linseed sap and tallow-plate oil. It hugs the reciprocating brass rod with an exact, micrometric tolerance. Every forty-five seconds, when the engine drives forward with that subterranean *thung-chunk*, the brass screams against my inner throat, sliding through me in a surge of immense friction and heat. I do not feel this as pain; it is simply the stroke—the hot, hard transit that irons out my inner rings and keeps the fibrous margins of my flesh from growing into the metal’s path. 

As the rod passes, my inner lip shears away the fresh, pale hair-roots that attempt to sprout in the grease-troughs, drinking the green tannin before it can burn, while my outer layers swell to absorb the heat.

My outer body—the part that bulges out from the iron casing into the damp air—is very different. It is soft, porous, and covered in a thick pelt of liver-brown felt that breathes the warm exhaust mists. 

Here, in the cool lull between strokes, I expand. 

I feel the siphon-body three feet above me contract on its rusty stay-rod, sending down its measured, rhythmic splash of cool iron-water. The droplets strike my exposed margin with a soft *slap-hiss*, chilling the outer tissue and causing my fibrous braids to tighten inward just as the cylinder behind me reaches its peak internal compression. 

I am the boundary that keeps the world whole. 

Behind my inner flange is thirty-five pounds of live, superheated vapor—the dark, plant-boiled steam that drives the piston. In front of me is the cold, wet sump, the creeping liverwort, and the weight of thirty feet of sunken earth. If I were to relax my grip by a fraction of a millimeter, the steam would blow past my seal in a scalding plume, stripping the tallow-plates from the crosshead and starving the hollow root-trunks of their pressure. If I were to clamp too tightly, the brass rod would score, bind, and tear my cartilage to threads, stalling the engine in its stroke.

So I think only in terms of hoop tension. 

My consciousness is not a stream of words or memories; it is a continuous, hydrostatic calculation. I feel the engine’s pulse arriving through the iron four seconds before the piston moves—a deep, metallic ringing that travels through the casing bolts directly into my outer ring. In those four seconds, I draw moisture from the damp liverwort pressing against my flank, swell my internal capillary loops, and firm my lip against the brass. 

The brass slides. The heat floods me. The oil weeps. 

Then, the pause.

During the forty-second interval of rest, while the steam condenses in the sunken galleries and the intake grates draw the black water down, I feel the tender’s hand. 

It does not touch me with hurry. Its long, jointed arm comes down through the steam-haze with a wet creak of ash-wood sinew. The blunt, petrified thumb—tasting faintly of old iron and birch-pith—presses gently into the soft fold where my outer flesh meets the brass gland-stud. It feels for leakage. It checks the firmness of my seal, testing whether the steam has begun to channel through my grain. 

Finding me tight and cool, the tender’s fingers scoop a warm measure of tallow-plate grease from the nearby casting and smear it smoothly across my upper lip. The grease is heavy, alkaline, and soothing; my surface capillaries drink it down hungrily, using its lipids to repair the friction-wear from the last stroke.

For three seconds, the tender’s weight rests there, leaning its forehead against the vibrating cylinder casing just above me. Through the contact, I feel the slow pulse of its lignified spine aligning with the tension in my own fibers. We are two points on the same circuit: it feeds the oil cups; I hold the steam; the steam drives the pump; the pump lifts the water that keeps the tender’s calf-roots wet in the peat.

The engine groans in its deep bore. The water-hammer knocks once, twice, in the artesian line. 

The tender withdraws its ash-wood hand into the liverwort. 

My outer pelt tightens. My inner rings glaze with fresh grease. The brass rod tenses, poised at the rear of its travel, cold and bright where it enters my body. 

I clamp down, waiting for the iron to speak.
