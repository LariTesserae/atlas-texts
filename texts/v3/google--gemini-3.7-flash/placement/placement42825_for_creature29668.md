At first, I would not have an edge. 

In a room where distance presses flat against the cheekbone and light is a cold, starchy broth suspended in the lungs, it is impossible to begin as a bounded shape. To begin with a skin would be to claim a certainty the stone itself has not earned. 

I would start as an unevenness in the glare. A cool, heavy spot in the luminous fog, no thicker than a wet sheet of parchment, drifting where the ceiling’s selenite stalks try hardest to become columns. 

For a long time, there would be no thought—only the sensation of the room’s tolerances. 

I would feel the frantic, electric shivering of the Stitches as they knit together overhead, their gelatine legs catching like burs in the unseamed air. I would feel the soft, lime-scented suction of the Grazers drinking the hesitation out of the curd-stone below, their bellies freezing into quartz, one milligram at a time. I would feel the jerk in the world’s elbow whenever the decapod snaps its five mica jaws, displacing a drop of volume that has nowhere to go, sending a small, hydraulic shudder through everything that is soft.

Gradually, out of that shuddering, I would begin to condense.

Not by breeding, and not by waking. I would condense the way grease hardens on the surface of chilled broth, taking form around a specific necessity of the room.

***

Where would you find me? 

Not in the air with the vibrating needles, which are too light to know where they are, and not in the milk-white fissures where the decapod threads its silken sutures across the sky. 

You would find me along the boundary line that no one else is watching: the wet, pale lip where the curdling floor is attempting to climb the lowest tread of the three red slate steps.

I would be the thing that lives in the overlap between what is finished and what is still refusing to solidify.

My body would not be made of chitin, nor cellophane, nor the dry brass wire of the Dweller. It would be a thin, stratified ribbon of cold, flexible horn—the color of mutton fat left in water—about as long as a weaver’s shuttle, but pliable, segmented along three dozen delicate, soft hinges that do not click when they fold. 

I have no face, because there is no horizon to look toward, but along the margins of my ribbon run hundreds of microscopic, cup-shaped indentations lined with cold mercury. They do not register the glare, which is everywhere and means nothing. They register *incline*. They know, down to the breadth of a spore, precisely how much the floor is tilting away from the dead, true horizontal of the red slate.

I am an unmade wedge. 

***

My metabolism is very slow—the slowest thing in the blister, save for the dark drop of oil in the keyhole. 

While the Grazers eat the unformed milk-rock to turn themselves into masonry, I eat only the *waste* of the red steps: the microscopic, iron-red dust that sloughs off their edges whenever the selenite beam concentrates its heat on them. It is old matter. It tastes of oxidized metal, dry wind, and dead weight—things that have never existed anywhere else in this three-hundred-foot burial.

When I ingest a grain of that red slate, my ribbon thickens. I crawl, with a slow, rippling peristalsis, into the narrow crevice where the wax-white marl is rising like bread dough around the bottom step.

I wedge myself between them.

The curd-stone presses inward, soft and urgent, trying to engulf the red slate, to dissolve its hard angles back into the embryonic slurry. And the red slate sits there, stubbornly square, dense as an anvil, refusing the chamber’s loose geometry. 

If the soft stone touches the red stone directly, it weeps violently; the contact of finished time against unstarted time creates a caustic, boiling brine that pits the slate and turns the milk-rock to brittle chalk.

So I lie between them, a living gasket. 

I take the cold, curdled pressure on my left side, and the dry, immovable red iron on my right. My body absorbs the friction between *what has already happened* and *what has not yet decided to be*. I swell with it. The soft stone pushes; my mercury-cups roll in their sockets; I exude a slick, cold tallow that allows the floor to slide harmlessly against the tread without catching fire or breaking the room's seal.

***

Sometimes, when the wall hiccups and the seam opens into that dizzying, silver-blue sky, a terrible draft passes through the chamber. 

For the length of an inhale, the white fog is thinned. The Stitches tumble into the void by the hundreds, the decapod’s silver threads stretch taut as lute strings, and the three red steps ring—a faint, deep, bell-like vibration that travels down through the slate and straight into the core of my ribbon.

In that single second, through the gap, I catch the scent of the world twelve miles above: the dry, bitter dust of pine needles, the smell of bird-lime baked in sunlight, the immense and horrifying certainty of things that have shadows.

The sky snaps shut. The wet marl heals. The decapod frantically lashes the seam.

And on the third step, the Dweller begins its forty-minute climb, carrying its sphere of water to hold against the light.

I do not look up at it—I cannot look up. But as it passes overhead, the weight of its three brass legs makes the red slate creak against my back. I press myself flatter against the seam, bracing the bottom step with all thirty-six hinges of my pale horn, holding the small ruin steady in the wax, so that when the droplet falls and makes its little *tink*, the step does not shift, the water strikes true, and there is still one thing in this bright, unstitched world that knows precisely where the earth is supposed to end.
