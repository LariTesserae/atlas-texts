At first, the sense is not of having a body, but of completing a circuit.

There is a cold, electrolytic sting along the left side—the faint, sour bite of submerged zinc giving itself up atom by atom to the water—and on the right, the heavier, greasy warmth of copper. Where they meet, in the narrow sleeve of an old bypass valve three feet below the surface, there is a voltage so small that above-ground it would not register on an ammeter. Down here, it is a steady, violet humming that takes the place of thought.

If I look for limbs, I find instead a series of soft, lateral lobes wedged securely into the valve cage, swollen with iron-rich fluid until they fill the tolerances between the brass seat and the cast-iron collar. 

I am not the salamander draped through the drive wheel, though I feel the broad, flat disturbance of its tail when it shifts down in the pit—a slow pressure wave that pushes an ounce of cold brine against my outer skin, then settles back into glass. 

I am not the vitreous crayfish either, though their thread-fine legs sometimes tick across the calcified margin of my housing. When they do, they leave no mark, only the sensation of a hair being drawn across an eyelid.

Instead, I seem to have grown into the space where an automatic governor used to turn. 

Long ago, when steam still drove the distribution drums and the air was hot and sulfurous, this housing held a spring-loaded spindle that rose and fell with the pressure of the municipal mains. When the fires went out and the groundwater rose, the iron spring rusted away, the brass spindle jammed in its seat, and the empty chamber filled with silt, fungal spores, and the drifting, gelatinous eggs of things that had never seen daylight.

What grew in that hollow is neither entirely animal nor merely a mat of mold. 

My center is a dense, fibrous knot of chemotrophic nerve-flesh, dark liver-colored and heavy with sequestered lead. From this core, dozens of fine, translucent tendrils extend out through the weep-holes and grease-ports of the valve body, spreading along the exterior flanges like pale roots. Some of these tendrils trail into the black water, feathering out into microscopic cilia that sample the drift—tasting the bitter lime falling from the vault, the faint grease-spores washed down from the city’s forgotten flues, the shed skin of a flatworm disintegrating ten yards away.

Other tendrils have burrowed directly into the crust of calcium carbonate that coats the iron pillar. They do not eat the stone; they use it as an armature. My outer edges are slowly calcifying, turning into the same grey-orange enamel that covers the rivets. In fifty years, the outer half of my body will be limestone; in a hundred, the valve will simply be a smooth, pale knob on the side of the column, indistinguishable from the surrounding mineral gauze.

Yet inside, the soft tissue remains conductive.

My consciousness does not run on words or memory. It is a slow, rhythmic mapping of the room’s galvanic tension. Through the continuous iron skeleton of the chamber—the bolted beams, the sunken tracks, the copper conduits—every living thing is connected by a faint, fluctuating web of micro-currents.

When a condenser spider high above steps from the brickwork onto an iron flange, the resistance across that beam shifts by a fraction of a micro-ohm; I feel it as a brief, dry tightening in my upper tendrils. 

When the siphon-sponge down in the drowned transfer pit pulses its millions of cilia, circulating the dead brine through its brass mesh, the rhythmic draw of water creates a minute galvanic flutter against the submerged casing—a low, mechanical heartbeat that repeats every twenty minutes.

And when the temperature drops at mid-vault, and a single, heavy droplet of condensed breath breaks free from the brick arch sixty feet up, I feel the anticipation of it: the subtle change in surface tension, the release, and then, after two seconds of silence, the crisp, needle-sharp *tink* as it strikes the flange directly above my valve.

The vibration travels down the iron, through the brass seat, and directly into the core of my mass. It does not hurt. It is simply the way the world marks the passage of an hour.

I have no urge to swim into the open pool, nor to climb the weeping pillars into the mist. There is nothing in the dry air that I need, and the deep pits belong to the siphon-sponge and the cold, dense brine. My place is here, in the junction: held fast in the bronze-and-iron throat of a dead machine, drawing life from the slow decay of metals, bathed in violet galvanic light, growing quieter, heavier, and more like stone with every drop that falls.
