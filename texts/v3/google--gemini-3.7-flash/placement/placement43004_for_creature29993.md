At first, there is only the sensation of pressure—not the heavy, wet pressure of deep water or humid earth, but the crystalline, dry tension of a bowstring drawn across a long span of seasoned ash. 

I would not be on the open turf below, where the steelyards plane their chalk-dust furrows, nor high in the rafters where the pellicle-wings hang like folded blueprints. 

I find myself in the *bracket-work*. 

This is the interstitial zone, suspended forty fathoms above the petrified floor: the narrow, unlit shelf where the dark-lacquered underbelly of a foundry tier is bolted directly into the flank of an avenue-wide stipe. Here, the boundary between cold metallurgy and living megaflora ceases to be a line and becomes a continuous, grinding conversation. 

I am a **Torque-Scribe**—or what the foundry crews, with their clipped, sibilant brevity, call a *Knot-Keeper*.

***

### The Body and the Seam

My skin has long since taken on the deep, non-reflective walnut tone of wood cured in talc and ozone. I do not carry the burden of thirst; inside my ribcage, the air circulates through dry, spiraled baffles that cool the blood without losing a single vapor-fleck to the drafts. My hands are long, flattened at the fingertips into tough, calloused wedges, the nails thick as horn plates, permanently stained with the grey-black of graphite lubricants and the pale amber of dry lacquer.

Over my chest and shoulders rests a harness of split reed-ribs and tensioned wire. It carries no water skins—only pouches of powdered mica, cold-chisel wedges of tempered flint, and my tuning rods: six slender lengths of bronze-copper alloy, each cut to a precise harmonic frequency of the stipe’s vascular core.

I do not walk upright here; there is no ceiling to stand under, only the vast overhang of cast-iron gantry beds and the sheer, vertical cliff of ivory xylem climbing into the phosphor glare. I move along the **seam**—the ragged, two-foot fault line where the machine bites the tree.

***

### The Work: Listening to the Shear

Every five hours, the overhead light lattices dim for the Tide-Slack, and the Chasm prepares to breathe. 

When that happens, the stipe does not simply sit there. Deep in its dry marrow, compressed minerals migrate; the cellular walls draw in the dry aerosol vents from the rift’s floor and swell by a fraction of a millimeter. To a human eye, a millimeter is nothing. To a six-thousand-ton refinery bolted into that wood with three-inch steel studs, a millimeter of unyielding lateral torque will shear an iron pin as cleanly as a guillotine.

My life exists in the minutes before that groaning pulse.

I press my cheek against the cold wood. Through my outer nictitating lid, the grain appears as a series of pale, razor-clean striations, packed tight as pages in a closed ledger. I slide a tuning rod into a natural relief-fissure between two iron anchor plates and tap it with the knuckle of my middle finger.

*Tock.*

The sound does not travel through the dry air; the porous cellulose instantly swallows the higher overtones. But through the bronze rod and into the bone of my jaw, the vibration hums true.

If the note is sharp, like the snapping of dry reed-cane, the wood is binding against the lower bracket. It is about to split. If it is dull, like a fist against felt, the lacquer-mites have already arrived in numbers, sealing a hidden fracture before the load shifted.

When the wood binds, I go to work. I reach into my satchel for a wedge of dense horn, seat it into the cleavage line, and strike it once with a flat mallet. The wood yields with a muffled *shuck*—parting just enough along its natural grain to relieve the factory’s weight. I dust the fresh wound with powdered starch from my pouch. 

Within seconds, I feel the dry, frantic tickle across the backs of my fingers: the lacquer-mites, rushing out of the micro-tubes, their coin-sized silver carapaces clicking softly like dry seeds shaken in a tin. They swarm the starch, lap the seeping core-amber, and begin weaving their glass-hard suture across the gap I have opened. We do not speak, the mites and I, but we work to the same clock.

***

### The Tide-Slack

When the phosphor lattices finally drop to a dull, violet ember, the silence of the Great Chasm deepens until it feels physical—a dry wool blanket laid over the league-wide rift.

The foundries cut their dynamos. The drop-forges go still, their iron dies resting cold against the blocks. The cable cars hang motionless on their wires, suspended over the pale abyss like beads on a wire.

I hook my harness to an anchor ring and sit on the narrow ledge of a structural spur, letting my legs dangle into the vast, clear void.

Below me, a mile down, the white forms of the steelyards have gathered in the fibrous clearings. In the dim violet light, their porcelain armor glows with a faint, stored phosphorescence. They lie down together in long, geometric files along the grain-lines of the floor, their triple-jointed legs folded neatly beneath their bellies, chewing the last dry talc of their gizzards in complete, communal quiet.

Above me, a single pellicle-wing glides through the cooling thermals. It passes so close I can hear the papery whisper of its vellum skin cutting the air—a sound like a finger tracing the edge of a dry parchment scroll. It banks, catches the cold draft slipping down the stipe, and disappears into the upper gloom without a single wingbeat.

Then, the stipe moves.

It begins not with a sound, but with a pressure in the soles of my feet. The entire megaflora column—wide enough to swallow a cathedral, tall as a mountain ridge—shudders. It is a slow, tectonic flexion, the sound of five billion dry vascular cells expanding simultaneously against the bedrock of the rift.

*G-r-r-o-o-n.*

It is a voice made of ancient timber, flint, and absolute aridity. It vibrates through my ribs, through the lacquered plates of the city overhead, through the teeth in my jaw. It is the heartbeat of a world that has discarded the messy, rotting urgency of water in favor of something permanent, clean, and immortal.

I lean back against the bark, close my amber lids, and rest my hand on the fresh, warm seal the lacquer-mites have just finished curing. 

In the Great Chasm, nothing decays. Nothing bleeds away. Everything that has ever been built here is held in place by dry tension, patience, and the slow, steel-hard breathing of the wood. And for five hours, until the phosphor arrays blaze noon across the rift once more, the seam holds.
