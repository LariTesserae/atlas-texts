I would not be up on the windswept top chords where the falcons dive, nor down in the blind sulfurous steam-tunnels with the crickets. Those places belong to the extremes—to pure flight or pure acoustic dark. 

I would be at the hinge. 

Specifically, I find myself stationed at an intermediate expansion knuckle: an oval access portal cut into the web-plate of a three-thousand-foot cantilever, right where the great horizontal truss receives the diagonal tension ties and carries them down into a graphite rocker-bearing. 

It is a place where three climates meet within the span of six paces. 

To my left, the manhole opens into the hollow box-girder: warm, pitch-black, smelling of old tallow, lard-grease, and the wet, mushroomy scent of ferro-fungus. To my right, the outer flange drops sheer into the sunlit void, carpeted with crushed wild mint, liverwort, and creeping fig that drinks from the guttering of a leaky copper steam-sleeve. And beneath my heels—pressed bare and calloused against the cool iron—is the massive, machined steel plate that shifts four inches twice a day, sighing as the sun crosses the meridian and the mountain of iron expands.

I am a ballast-keeper, a listener at the pin-joints.

My body is shaped by the architecture. My skin carries the dark, purple-black stain of linseed oil and iron-oak gall, rubbed into my thighs and forearms to stop the wind-scab and keep the steam-dermatitis from taking hold. Around my waist hangs a heavy harness of boiled hide, slung with flat willow baskets, a small horn flask of clarified palm-grease, a bundle of chalk sticks, and my mallet—not iron, but dense, close-grained mountain boxwood that will not fracture the brittle lacquer of ancient rust when struck.

I spend my morning in the neutral zone. 

Before the noon heat swells the steel, I lay my ribcage flat against the web-plate, just behind the primary shear-pins. I don't tap yet; I only breathe with it. When you lie skin-to-iron, the framework is not quiet. You feel the slow, five-mile-long heave of the lower wind-braces taking the morning thermal off the cloud-sea. You feel the tiny, scratching skitter of civets three tiers above, their horn-claws clicking in the cable-strands. 

And deeper, through the marrow of the plate, comes the low, hydraulic pulse: *one… two… three…* the ten-minute pump down in the drowned bulkheads, sending hot water up the arterial flues to keep the joints from freezing and snapping like glass.

When the sun strikes the upper flange, turning the frost on the liverwort into steam, I begin to work the balance.

I take my boxwood mallet and strike the vertical stiffeners in sequence. 
*Clink.* High and thin—the diagonal is hungry; it is carrying too much sky-pull.
*Thrum.* Deep, rich, like a struck bell wrapped in wet wool—that one is seated true.
*Thud.* Choked.

I mark the choked plate with a greasy stroke of blue chalk. A drift of silt has choked the drainage scupper three bays to the windward, and the weight of forty baskets of waterlogged peat is twisting the flange by half a millimeter. 

So I climb out through the oval portal into the bright, blinding wind.

The transition is an electric shock to the blood. The heat of the internal steam sleeve drops away instantly, replaced by the clean, razor-cold wash of the troposphere. The clouds below are a blinding floor of curdled cream, sixty miles across, pierced only by the rust-red crowns of the furthest pylons.

I hook my prehensile toes into the rivet lines—my feet know the spacing better than my hands know the lines on my palm—and work my way along the angle-iron to the choked scupper. Beside me, a copper-backed salamander blinks its gold-rimmed, lidless eyes from a warm rivet-nest, completely unbothered by the drop beneath its belly. It knows, as I know, that nothing falls unless it forgets how to wedge.

With a short horn spade, I cut into the rich, black loam that has settled in the trough. It smells astonishingly sweet: decaying fern, distilled water, and engine oil turned to sweet humus by centuries of blind worms. I do not throw it into the abyss—to cast weight into the void is a sacrilege against the span. 

Instead, I fill my wicker panniers and carry the dark load along the neutral axis, stepping over the thick, pulsing canvas sleeves of the steam mains, toward the great hollow knuckle where the dead-load is needed. I pack the earth into the counterweight pockets behind the rocker-shoe, planting cuttings of silver-leafed fig into the fresh soil to root it down and drink the condensation before it can pit the bearing races.

By late afternoon, the light turns the color of crushed persimmon. The wind drops, falling into that vast, suspended silence that only exists three miles above the bedrock. 

I return to my hang beneath the belly of the main box-girder. 

The dwelling is small—just a cradle of split cane and tarred rope slung from two heavy eye-bars, swaying perhaps an inch or two in the residual flex of the evening cooldown. My small copper brazier glows with dried fern-peat, throwing little sparks that vanish into the dark void beneath the floor-slats. 

I do not sit inside, though. I sit on the narrow ledge of the bottom flange, my legs dangling into the cold purple air, my back pressed against the warm, lagging-wrapped flank of a steam line. 

From here, I watch the camber-falcons make their last, silent stoops through the lattice towers, sliding down the thermal columns like dropped knives before banking up into the gantry eaves. A civet, slick and smelling of spiced palm nuts, trots unhurriedly along the catenary cable twenty feet above my head, its horn-toes clicking softly in the evening chill: *tup, tup, tup, tup.*

Then comes the sound that signals the end of the day: the great iron crack—clean and sharp as a cannon-shot—as the rocker-bearing three paces from my knee releases its noon-strain and slides half an inch along its bed of graphite grease, seating itself for the night.

The whole three-thousand-foot arm shudders beneath me—a single, massive, elastic bounce that ripples through my spine and dies away into the steel marrow of the world. 

I reach out my hand in the gathering dark and touch the cold face of the joint. The chalk mark is gone, rubbed clean by the slide. The tone is true again. I pull my knees to my chest, rest my forehead against the damp, warm canvas of the steam main, and let the slow, ten-minute respiration of the pumps rock me into the dark.
