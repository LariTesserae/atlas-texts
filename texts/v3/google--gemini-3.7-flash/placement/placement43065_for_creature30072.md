At first, there is only the transmission.

It does not enter through the ear, because the air is too crowded for hearing—the acoustic crush of trillions of mandibles and snapping boughs turns the atmosphere into a solid, vibrating cake of white noise. The news comes instead through the flat, calloused balls of the feet, through the sit-bones braced against the cured curvature of a hollowed grub-knot, through the teeth when the jaw is clamped shut against the dry yellow dust.

I am sitting in the throat of an anchor-joint. 

To the world above, an anchor is something that holds fast. Here, an anchor is merely an argument that takes longer to lose. 

Below me, thirty fathoms down through the tallow-noon haze, the great gray bough—a trunk three centuries thick, sluggish with petrifying resins and iron-doped heartwood—is twisting four degrees to the leeward drift. I can feel the torsion winding up inside its core like a drawn winch. It travels into my tailbone as a dry, rhythmic shudder: *clack... clack... clack...* the micro-cleavages of interior lignum giving way one vessel at a time.

My hands are not resting; they cannot. In this place, to let the hands go limp is to invite the moss to fuse your fingernails to the bench. 

I hold a short-handled adze faced with flaked obsidian, black and cold against the warm, floury air. Every sixty heartbeats, between the long, sighing pulses of the vascular pods overhead, I reach down and slice a ribbon of pale, weeping cambium from the lip of my niche. The shaving curls away like a hot dried wafer, smelling of turpentine, baked meal, and old tallow. If I drop the adze and sleep for five hours, the niche will have narrowed by the thickness of my thumb. If I sleep for a cycle, the wood will have taken my knees, welding them into the living grain in a painless, suffocating grip of amber and fiber.

I am a **Wedge-Tender** for the lower basket-rigging of the Seventh Rift-Climbers. 

I am not young, though age here is measured not in solar turnings but in the depth of the resin-crust upon your skin and the number of times you have had to saw your own hearth-pole free of the swelling floor. My skin is the color of gray horn, cross-hatched with fine, dry cracks that have been sealed shut with boiled borer-grease and dusted with ground quartz to keep the heat from siphoning the dense, dark oils out of my blood. My eyes are small, wet only behind their thick, amber nictitating skins; when I blink, it is a dry, silent click, clearing away the drift of mica powder that sifts continuously from the ceiling canopy three miles above.

My work is the slip-joint.

A hundred yards out over the chasm, swinging in the violent thermal updrafts, hangs the central drying-cradle of our hearth. In it, forty souls are sleeping, their hammocks woven from the flexible wing-struts of mantis-kites, their children smeared with bitter wax to keep the flour-ticks from grazing upon their soft ears. The cradle is tied to this bough by twelve braided cables of deep-bast fiber, each cable as thick as my thigh, running through sliding collars carved from the fossilized tusks of the Great Borers.

The timber grows; the cables must give. If the collars jam, the timber will pull the cradle apart with the slow, careless power of an expanding continent, snapping the lashing and casting forty bodies into the boiling white churn of the lower dust-dunes.

So I sit at the junction, bathed in the yellow glare of a pulsing lipid-pod above my head. 

Beside me rests my kit: three heavy wedges of borer-horn, a mallet of petrified root, and a leather pot of hot, skimmed marrow-grease that never solidifies. 

A sudden shudder rips through the timber beneath me. The sound is like the crack of an iron axle. A gout of hot, vaporous terpene puffs from a hairline fracture two inches from my left heel, smelling so sharply of cedar and scorched fat that my nostrils flare and my jaw clenches. 

I don't look at it. Looking is too slow. 

I lay my palm flat against the primary tension-rope. The fiber is humming at a high, singing C-sharp—the timber has surged three inches in the last hundred breaths, and the slip-collar has bound against an irregular knot of newly extruded grain.

I take up the horn wedge, set its tapered edge directly into the throat of the bone collar, and strike.

*Tock.*

The strike is not just force; it is an acoustic word. The vibration travels down the cable, through the harness, through the sleeping bodies in the cradle, telling them: *The timber moves. The Tender wakes. The line gives.*

*Tock.*

On the third blow, the collar slides forward with a heavy, groaning screech, shedding a spray of dry, scorched bone-dust. The tension on the rope drops to a low, safe hum—a deep, resonant D-flat that vibrates comfortably through the soles of my feet. The cradle sways outward into the void, finding its balance again in the updraft, riding the heat of the world's endless, churning belly.

I set the mallet down. I take a dollop of grease on my two fingers and smear it into the smoking track where the collar moved, watching the hot oil sizzle and vanish into the thirsty pores of the wood.

Then, with the calm, rhythmic unhurriedness that is the only way to stay alive in an engine that never stops, I lift my obsidian adze. 

The wall of the niche has already crept half an inch closer to my left shoulder. 

I strike the living wood. The chip flies, hot and aromatic. 

I strike again. 

I am not the master of this forest; I am not its parasite; I am its deliberate, unyielding flaw. I am the pebble caught in the tooth of the gear, keeping the space open for just one more breath, one more notch, one more scrape of the living grain.
