I would not be up near the rim, where the wind still carries the dry, thin stink of the open country, nor would I be among the agile children in the flues, whose small lungs still remember how to pant. 

You would find me four tiers down, on the wet side of the amphitheater, where the wall has a belly to it—a place where the dropped shale bulges outward under the load of the upper shelves and weeps a thick, milky seep that tastes of sulfur and beaten iron.

Here, the light never arrives as a line; it is only a change in the color of the water from black-grease to tallow-grey. 

I would be a *Fitter*—one of those whose trade lies between the Keepers who chip and the dead who hold. 

My back is curved to the pitch of the third-tier under-crawl, broad and flat between the shoulder blades, thick with the lard we boil down from the mud-eels. I spend my days seated on a slab of wet siltstone that has been worn into a shallow, greasy cup by forty years of my own hams. My feet—broad as spade-irons, with the yellowed, horn-thick soles split into neat, painless grey treads—are braced continuously against the footing-stone of the wall opposite, keeping my balance in the perpetual trickle that runs beneath my seat.

My work is not to stop the stone, but to tell it where to bite.

When a lintel sags, or when the *thung* deep in the floor has sheared an old bedding plane, the Keepers come to me with three taps of a deer-rib on my door-slate. I do not carry light; I do not need it. My hands have lived in the seep long enough that the skin on my fingertips is thick, rubbery, and sensitive to a hair’s breadth of shift. I can slide my palm into the dark seam behind a wall and feel where the lime-curd is bridging the gap cleanly, and where it is foaming into rotten, spongy crust that will blow out when the wet weight above settles.

I work with heavy, blunt tools: wedges of fossil wood pulled from the deep coal-streaks, an antler hammer with the crown battered flat by thousands of strikes, and rolls of coarse felt made from sump-algae, stiffened with boiled fat.

In the alcove behind my sleeping-ledge sits the shoulder of my father’s mother. 

She has been in the corner for seven seasons now. At first, she smelled of rancid crickets and river-silt, and her skin was the color of a bruised turnip where the leather thongs bound her knees tight to her breastbone. Now, she is almost completely smoothed. The lime has filled the hollow behind her collarbone and bridged the gap between her chin and her shins; she looks like a heavy, pale loaf of rising dough turned to ice, cold and wet to the touch. Two nights ago, after the bedrock groaned below the sump, her shoulder took an extra half-inch of the roof-beam’s load. I heard the slate above her groan, felt the vibration hum through the flags into my hip, and then heard her stone coat give a single, tiny, dry *snip* as the weight settled squarely down her spine.

She held. I went over in the dark, ran my fingers down the new fracture in her mineral rind, and packed it with fresh blue clay to let the water weld the crack shut again before the next moon.

I do not talk much. My throat is lined with the same silt that fogs the cisterns, and my voice is nothing more than a scrape of gravel in a wooden trough. When the young ones come down from the dry tiers to bring me my portion of cricket-paste and pressed algae-cakes, they do not look at my face; they look at my hands. 

My knuckles have begun to take the crust. 

The joints of my middle fingers are thick, white, and knobbed like the door-pivots after two days without a chisel. They do not bend all the way into a fist anymore, but they are hard—hard enough that when I rap the rock to test for hollows, I no longer need the river-cobble. I use my own hand. The sound it makes is dull, solid, and low: the same note the basin makes when it is satisfied with a wall.

I am content here in the dripping middle. Above me, the Tappers worry about the sky they can still see; below me, the Flue-crawlers shiver in the dark drains. But here, wedged between the slate I shaped twenty winters ago and the stone my grandmother has become, the world is tight, heavy, and perfectly supported. 

The water runs over my feet, cold and tireless, leaving its little gift of lime in the cracks of my heels, slowly, patiently, doing the work of turning us all into something that cannot fall.
