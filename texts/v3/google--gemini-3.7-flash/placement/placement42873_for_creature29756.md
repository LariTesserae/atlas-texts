At first, the sense is not of being a creature, but of being a pressure. 

A heavy, thick weight held between two immense forces: the vast freezing vacuum of the sky above, pulling like an open wound, and the slow, furnace-like upward shove of the rock below. In this place, to exist is to be wedged into that balance.

I would not be in the throat. That violent snapping back and forth between sixty below zero and the scald of the vent—that belongs to the things with plum-colored blood and wings like iron blades. Theirs is an existence of edges, always cutting through the seam where fire meets ice. 

Nor would I be down in the deep churn of the floor, where identity is dissolved entirely into the soft, boiling porridge of chitin and blind mouths. There is no memory there, only digestion.

I find myself midway down, where the basalt curves inward like the underside of a sleeping whale’s jaw. 

Here, the stone is not cold. It has been heated from within for ten thousand years until it feels like the flank of a living animal, slick with the running sweat of mineral steam and ancient grease. The light here is not dark; it is simply *not an eye’s world*. To see would be an unnecessary wound, a delicate surface exposed to ammonia and sulfur. Instead, the world is mapped through the ribs, the splayed pads of the paws, the long whiskers that brush against the copper-colored velvet of the stone.

I am one of the knot.

To be a tallow-mouse is to never truly know where one’s own body ends. I am a curved spine, a thick, slow-thumping heart the size of an acorn, a belly pressed flat against the wet basalt to drink in its iron warmth—but I am also the thirty other backs pressed against mine. Our tails are tangled in a slick, greasy braid that has not come undone in generations. We do not sleep and wake by the sun; we shift in turns, a collective heaving of heavy grey leather and wool that breathes once for every three beats of the mountain’s vent.

Above us, the roar of the chimney is a physical ceiling. It is not noise; it is the floorboards of the world vibrating. Millions of swift-quills, the vinegar-smell of the pip-bats hanging in their soft, greasy sheets, the *plink-slap* of lukewarm condensation striking our fur and rolling off without ever touching the skin. We are waterproofed by our own living fat. The tallow rises through our hide like oil through a wick, sweet and thick and dense, sealing us into this ninety-degree womb.

I reach out a forepaw. The claws are short, curved shovels, polished to a dull horn sheen by the stone. I scrape a patch of the copper crust—it tastes of iron, wet dust, and the sharp bite of volcanic ash—and chew it slowly, my jaws grinding with the heavy, unhurried cadence of something that will never need to run. There is no running here. There is only gripping, grazing, and sinking back into the warmth of the heap.

Sometimes, the draft shifts.

A thin, razor-sharp needle of cold air spears down from the distant mouth—a draft that smells of dry snow, ozone, and pine-smoke from valleys we will never touch. When it hits, the nursery roosts above shiver with a dry, paper rattle. And then, rarely, the vibration changes: a rhythmic scraping, flint against stone, the heavy downward scrape of yak-hide rope biting into the lip of the vent.

When the long poles reach down into the dark, tipped with their notched fire-hardened pine, we do not scatter. We cannot. We tighten the knot. We press our muzzles into each other’s flanks, burying our blind faces in tallow and wet wool. If the hook finds a flank, if the mass is torn apart and one of us is lifted upward into the blinding, freezing poison of the outer air—to be scraped, boiled, and burned in a stone lamp below the ice-fields—the space left behind simply closes. The stone is warm. The grease flows together. The knot heals itself before the cold can touch the rock.

And behind the rock, deeper in the dark fissures where the steam is born, I can feel the slow, rhythmic tick of the centipede's legs—a delicate vibration running through the basalt like a hair drawn across a drumskin. We feel it coming hours before it arrives. We do not fear it; it is just another tooth of the mountain, shearing away what is slow or old, keeping the pelt of the chimney clean.

I tuck my snout beneath the heavy shoulder of the mouse beside me. The steam curls over our backs, condensing into warm beads that trickle down toward the crickets below. The air is thick, sour with life, and utterly permanent. We breathe in unison, heavy and fat, holding the mountain’s heat inside our bellies while the freezing world howls itself to death fourteen thousand feet in the open sky.
