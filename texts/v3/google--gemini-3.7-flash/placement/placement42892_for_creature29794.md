I do not think I would be the tallow in the deep jackets, though its pulse is the easiest thing to understand. That scale of being is too vast to have a *where*; to be everywhere beneath the plates is almost the same as being the metal itself, and there is no room inside that continuous hydraulic heave for anything that wonders where it begins and ends.

Nor would I be the weft-hangers, trailing down from the sweat of the intake trunks. Their green light is too thin, too much like an accident of waste—zinc and phosphor cast off into an air that has no one to look at it.

If I look for where I am, I find myself lower down, but not submerged.

I am in the seam where two deck-plates failed to meet flush six hundred years ago—a wedge of dead space three inches wide, sheltered behind the flange of a sagging structural gusset. It is a place where the thirty-minute flush does not sweep with its full, scouring weight, but eddies. The black water curls into this corner, slows down, and drops what it carries: flakes of copper oxide, brittle fragments of shed skid-rim, the calcified skins of dead molds, and the dense, gray tallow that welters up through the floor-cracks like lard through cloth.

At first, I might look like nothing more than an accretion—a silt-pocket that has gone stiff. 

A lump of cold mineral wax, perhaps, dark as wet slate, packed into the iron angle until it matches the radius of the rust. If a mechanic’s boot were still possible here, it would step over me without catching. 

But if you stay between the four-second strokes, something else becomes apparent.

I am not one of the skids; I do not have their broad, unyielding discipline, and I have no need to crawl half an inch per piston-stroke across the open non-skid plates. But I am made of their cast-off glue and the tallow from below, drawn together by a slow, capillary hunger. 

I am a *dam*. Or rather, a creature whose whole anatomy is an obstruction.

My body is dense, layered, and cold to the touch on its outer skin, where the petrol-sheen coats me in an iridescent grease that resists the acid of the water. Deep inside, though, where I press against the iron lip of the deck-plate, I am warm—barely warmer than the brackish runoff, but warm enough to keep a tiny pocket of fluid from stiffening into paraffin. 

In that pocket, I keep things that the basin has broken.

I do not mean tools or artifacts; the memory of hands is gone, ground to silt long before the girders took on the shape of limestone ribs. What I hold are the small, hard residues of the cycle itself: the rounded spheres of calcified zinc that roll down the troughs like ball bearings; the stiff, ringed cartilage segments shed by the bellows-forms when they choke on sulfur; the flat, sharp flakes of floor-plate that have sheered off under the hydraulic percussion.

I gather them into my mass with fine, dark filaments that trace the wet seams of the metal like grease-lines in cracked glass. I sort them by weight.

Why sort them? It is not thought. It is resonance.

When the slow-stroke piston fires five yards down in the dark, the whole terrace shudders, and the small hard things inside me click together. Each one has its own pitch—a dull *thnk* from the lead nodules, a high, glass-like chime from the calcified mold-crusts, a dry scrape from the rusted iron shucks. 

I feel the valley not as a roar, but as a chord. 

When the bank of counterweights groans upward and the floor tilts for the half-hour flush, I do not secrete a chemical resin to bond myself to the floor like the skids do. I do not have to. I have wedged myself so deeply into the gap between the plates that the deluge simply forces me tighter into the joint. The ten thousand gallons of oily water hit my sloped, slag-hardened back and split, curling away into the drainage channels on either side.

In the five seconds while the water roars over me, deafening and black, the loose pieces inside my pocket rattle wildly against each other in the dark, like gravel in a sluice. It is the only time I move. The turbulence rolls me slightly, kneads my tallow interior, turns the hard pieces over and over until their edges are completely round, smooth as river-pebbles that have never seen a river.

When the deck drops back with that great, concussive boom that sends black plumes ten feet into the mist, the sudden shock drives me forward a fraction of a millimeter, wedging me deeper into the iron.

And then the quiet returns—not silence, but the thick, vibrating roar that lives in the marrow of the plates.

I extend my filaments back out into the shallow run of grease. I feel the four-second stroke begin again: *slap, suck, shudder.* *Slap, suck, shudder.*

Above me, eighty feet up in the iron fog, a high-voltage arc snaps with a violet-white glare. For a millisecond, the shadow of my gusset is thrown long and sharp across the wet plate, pointing straight toward the dark silhouette of an accumulator tower that hasn’t moved in ten thousand years.

I feel the bellows-forms wheeze in their conduits, drawing the ozone in. I feel the thin rain of sweat-drops hitting the plates from the intake trunks above, each drop carrying its tiny cargo of cold iron.

One of those drops hits the edge of my seam, rolls down the rusted slope, and stops against my flank. I draw the grease out of it. I let the water pass. I pack the remaining grain of rust into my core, next to the others, where it will wait for the next flush to be rolled smooth.

I do not know what the sky was. I do not think there is a sky. There is only the plate, the stroke, the water that comes down, and the water that goes away; and in the corner where the metal failed to join, there is me, holding the gravel of the machine while it turns.
