At first, there is only the sensation of grain. 

Not the grit of sand that has tumbled down rivers or rolled against surf until its edges are round and forgiving, but the sharp, unspent friction of newly lifted gypsum. It is the feel of a world that has never been rinsed. Dust here is not debris; it is the dry currency of everything, settling into the micro-fissures of carapace and stone, clinging to the waxy blue bloom of succulent skins, filling every silence with a faint, velvet resistance.

To find oneself here is not to arrive from somewhere else. There is nowhere else; the chalk flats extend past the pale curve of the horizon until the glare folds them into the sky. So the emergence is quiet, starting not at the high roosts or down in the deep, churning quarries of the phalanxes, but in the thickness of the wall itself.

In the interstitial space.

Between the outer skin of a south-facing gallery—where the sun cures fresh alkaline paste to the hardness of fired ceramic within minutes—and the hollow, fluting lung-shafts where the chamber-tuners cling like velvet hinges, the architecture is not solid. It is cellular. It is packed with layers of dried aromatic wood, compressed lichen-chaff, and the bleached, needle-fine ribs of long-striders whose running days ended four centuries ago.

I would not be the runner. The runner’s mind is pure stride, a single line stretched taut across a thousand leagues of viaduct, measuring the world in the rhythm of bristle-rimmed pads against chalk. 

Nor would I be the silt-swallower, churning the raw floor of the basin into high-noon stone, because their awareness is seismoreceptive and vast, felt in the heavy bones of the shoulder, an unceasing, blind appetite for the horizon’s raw mineral crust.

No, identity here settles in the transition—where the material changes state.

I find myself clinging to the interior curve of a secondary draft-flute, where the terracotta shade begins to cool the baked air coming off the terraces. Not an arthropod with comb-mandibles, nor a heavy predator flattened along a lintel, but something smaller, flatter, and profoundly still: a wedge-headed, scale-tight creature whose hide is the exact grey-buff of dried secretion, living in the seam where the structural mortar meets the vascular roots of the wall-succulents.

Call it an *Anchor-tender*, or perhaps something that has no name in the dry whisper of the city because it never leaves the conduit.

My body is long, flattened like a dry leaf pressed between stones, ribbed with interlocking horn-plates that can wedge against the raw chalk walls with enough leverage to hold against the strongest desert draft. My claws are not meant for digging or sprinting, but for splicing: split-tipped, hardened like hornbeam, capable of teasing out the dry, fibrous hair of the lichen-mats and tucking them directly into the weeping seams of fresh mortar before the paste fully petrifies.

I do not eat dust, and I do not hunt the columns. 

My nourishment is the slow, concentrated resin that the succulents exude into the stone when the draft blows too hot. It is bitter, amber-dark, and thick as cooled pitch. I take it drop by drop from the root-tips that bore through my cell, licking the mineral salts from the bark with a tongue as dry and rough as a carpenter's file. In exchange, I clear the root-paths. When the salt crusts grow too thick and threaten to choke the capillary pull of the vines, I scrape them clean, letting the white shavings fall down the flutes to where the tuners can feel the change in tone.

From here, the city is not a labyrinth to be navigated, but an instrument being played by its own ventilation.

When the warm wind rises at mid-day, the entire thirty-foot wall hums. The note is low, somewhere between the rattle of dried seedpods and the deep, chest-felt vibration of a phalanx turning a corner three miles below. I feel the couriers pass overhead: not as footsteps, but as a rhythmic flutter in the air pressure, a thousands-long staccato that makes the silk-canopies tremble on their anchors.

I know when a lintel-stalker has made its drop, because the draft suddenly hitches—a single, split-second hesitation in the fluting of the shaft as the air currents reroute around two bodies instead of one, followed by the faint, sharp smell of unspilled, concentrated bile and the metallic scent of cleaved vertebrae. Within an hour, the runners carrying root-fiber will slow their pace near the arch, the builders will arrive with their churning jaws, and the gap will be filled, sealed, and smoothed. The dead become the lintel. The lintel holds the arch. The arch anchors the vine.

And in the deepest heat of the afternoon, when the sun stands motionless at the apex of the pale sky and even the runners settle into a gliding, low-energy cadence, I press my throat against the unweathered chalk.

Through the stone, there is a warmth that has never known a cloud. It is dry, ancient, and clean. There is no decay here, no moldering damp, no rot to soften the edge of the world. Everything that has ever lived in the basin is still here, holding up the ramp above it or channeling the draft below it, calcified into an eternity of useful stone.

I close my dust-membranes. The draft whistles through my fluting flute. The resin tastes of sun-baked scrub and iron. I tuck my limbs beneath my carapace and become, for another century or two, another unyielding knot in the mortar of the wall.
