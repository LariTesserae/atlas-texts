To understand where I am, I have to begin with the lower jaw resting flat against the wet stone. 

There is no distance here between the earth and the skeleton. The jaw is broad, thick-boned, sheathed in dense, rubbery tissue that acts as an acoustic plate, funneling the entire basin upward through the skull. I do not hear the storm; I am displaced by it. The baseline frequency is the machine—a subterranean three-cycle hum that sets the marrow in my ribs shivering at a pitch so low it tastes like copper at the back of the throat. Layered over that is the brutal, erratic hammering of water tearing over basalt, and beneath it all, the sharp, tectonic ticking of stone adjusting to freshly opened faults.

I would not be on the open gravel bars where the herds plow blindly through the scrub. That frantic, short-lived furnace of energy—the grease-soaked wool, the furious shoveling through sulfur-warm moss—is food, or it is movement to be cataloged, but it is too loud, too exposed to the tearing sheer of the wind. 

Nor would I be in the sky, tumbling in the violent elevator updrafts of the heat siphons, or churning in the blind, frothing coils of the flash-lakes.

I find myself in the blind spot. In the narrow, dead-air pocket on the lee side of the central atmospheric sink, where the forty-meter hull curves down to meet a submerged terrace of fractured rock.

Here, the gale is broken into a screaming vacuum overhead, shearing past without touching the stone at my flanks. The water does not strike; it drains in thick, glass-clear sheets around the hull’s zero-tolerance curve, foaming softly at my pads. 

My body is heavy, dense, and built entirely of low-center mass. If you were to touch the pelt along my spine, you would find no warmth escaping—it is a soot-dark, coarse felt, dense as compressed turf, weeping an oil so heavy that the freezing mist simply rolls off and pools in the basalt crevices. Beneath that barrier, the blood runs hot and thick, but quiet. The heart does not flutter like the scuttlers in the rock fissures; it beats in long, heavy strokes that sync, through sheer hydraulic entrainment, with the slow rhythmic pulsing of the pylon’s status rings.

My eyes are narrow, dark slits, indifferent to the slate-gray gloom. They see only the high-contrast edges: the white line where water breaks over an intake lip, the faint violet halo of an ionized cable, the steam rising from a grazer’s freshly churned trail. 

Most of my world is mapped through the pads of my four splayed limbs and the ventral plates along my belly. Through them, I read the basin like a live circuit. 

I feel the three-point cadence of a grazer six hundred yards up the braided river, its cleats slipping a half-inch on fresh ice. 
I feel the monofilament cables singing their microsecond adjustments—the sudden, sharp tightening that precedes the shedding of an ice sheet by three heartbeats. 
I feel the subterranean shift of the deep-core sink drinking in its thousand tons of slurry, altering the current in the flash-lake.

I am not the builder of the pylons. I do not understand the mathematics that keep them suspended, seamless and impossible, against the tear of the crust. But I am their consequence, their quiet tenant. I know the exact frequency at which the composite hull warms by half a degree before venting pressure. I know that when the blue status-ring dims to a dull iron-gray, the ionized column above it collapses, and the pelagic birds diving into the froth will misjudge the wind and slam into the gravel bar, broken and heavy with fat.

I do not hunt by pursuit. In a world moving at this speed, chasing is a waste of heat. 

Instead, I press closer to the hull. My soot-colored coat absorbs what little light bleeds from the clouds, making me indistinguishable from the composite stone and the shadow beneath the strut. I wait. The basin will do the work. The wind will sheer off a weak branch; a gravel bank will collapse under the weight of the water; a juvenile grazer, blinded by rime and frantic for sulfur-ferns, will take the path of least resistance around the pylon's base, seeking the very calm I am occupying.

When the vibration through my jaw tells me the weight has shifted—that the hoof has found air instead of basalt—I will not rush. 

I will simply rise from the lee, step into the spray, and let the sheer, violent momentum of this place deliver what is mine.
