To find myself in this place, I would have to be stripped first of every wet instinct. I would have to forget the swallow, the tear, the softening of things by rot, and the panic of thirst. 

I would not be in the air, drifting like the diaphanous gnats, nor would I be among the bustling chalk-paths of the weevils carrying their geometric pods. My place would be at the hinge—at the absolute interface where the dead, knife-edged black rock meets the first, oldest root-knots of the amber city.

I am one of the deep-anchor weavers, settled into a transverse fracture of the basalt where the rock sheared three centuries ago and has not moved an atom since. 

My body is broad, flat, and heavier than those of the bridge-spinners out on the bright catenaries. My carapace has the rough, matte finish of an unworked slab of slate, gray-black and entirely dull, so thoroughly encrusted with calcareous bloom and coriander-scented moss dust that a weevil passing across my dorsal plate does not pause; to its blunt, searching snout, I am merely another ledge of mineral. 

I have eight points of anchor. My legs do not end in claws so much as micro-faceted diamond styluses that have ground themselves, over decades of minute adjustments, into the microscopic pores of the fresh basalt cleavage. I do not slip. I do not sway. The canyon winds that lift the dry awnings of the wasp-ways beat against my flank, but I do not feel them as a threat; I feel them as load. 

My primary sense is not sight—my eyes are small, dark beads shielded beneath thick awnings of horn, used only to register the sun’s highest azimuth—but *tensile tension*. 

Through my two long, multi-jointed thoracic arms, I hold the main-stay cables of the central skyway. These lines do not belong to me; they are spun from the dry, high-tensile silk of four generations of my kind, threaded through the living wood of the plum-vines, and cured with the stone-flour saliva of the wasps. They run straight into the center of my chest, anchored into deep, internal apodemes of reinforced chitin. I am the counterweight. When thirty wasps land simultaneously on the upper clerestory, or when the midday sun heats the great amber arches and causes the resin to expand by a fraction of a millimeter, the shudder travels down the cable, through the silk, and directly into the marrow of my shell. 

I answer it not with movement, but with internal geometry. My internal fluids—thick, golden, aromatic esters that move with the stately viscosity of cold honey—shift between hydraulic chambers in my joints, locking them harder against the stone. 

Inside me, the fire is slow and perfectly clean. I feed rarely, once every several sun-turns, when a trailing caravan of chalk-weevils leaves a dry, pressed disc of seed-lipid on the ledge near my mouthparts. I draw it in with small, horn-tipped palps, crushing the hard fat without saliva, feeling the volatile oils burn instantly in my crop with a steady, dry warmth. 

There is a profound, meditative peace in this density. I hear no words, only the vast, muted friction of the colony: the chins of the blind wasps polishing a portal rim until it rings like slate; the soft, talcum whisper of millions of mite-feet buffing the hollows of the plum-vines; the faint, resonant hum of an aromatic beetle caught in a thermal draft. 

Most of all, I feel the rock. The basalt shelf is young, unweathered, and hard. It radiates a pure, fevered heat through my belly-plates all through the blinding afternoon, a heat that does not fade until long after the amber domes have turned from bright lantern-gold to the color of smoked tea against the twilight.

I know the fate that waits at the end of my century. Already, the joints of my rear-most legs are seizing, their membranes crystallizing into hard, translucent resin. In a few more cycles of the sun, the hydraulic shift of my esters will cease entirely. The silk cables anchored in my chest will not be cut; they will remain taut. 

When my breath stops, no one will lift me or cast me down into the ravine. A crew of velvety, gray-mantled wasps will arrive with crops full of basalt-dust and plum-lipid. They will work over me with their flat, spatulate chins, smoothing the seam between my slate back and the basalt cliff, laying down microscopic coats of sun-curing lacquer until my shell is simply the cornerstone from which the next arch springs. 

Until then, I hold the bridge. The light is blinding and honest; the stone beneath me is hot and clean; and the tension in the silk is true.
