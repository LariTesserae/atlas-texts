I am not up in the zinc boxes where the chains complain against the rock. The heat up there is thin and frantic, trapped between corrugated metal and the ceiling’s chisel-bite; it makes people light-headed, twitchy, quick to tap out warnings on the conduits before a pressure drop has even cleared the second expansion loop. 

Nor am I the stone itself, though the floor tempts with that final, cold density. 

I find myself low—down where the air has stopped pretending to circulate, resting in the dead-weight pocket three rungs below the lowest perforated catwalk. 

My feet are in wood-pegged slippers, but the soles have long since softened, saturated with wet tallow until the grain of the ash-wood matches the grain of my own heels. I am a Tender of the Lead Runs—the one who works the gullies where the Ward sweats out its waste and its grief, scraping the tallow-mats before they choke the drainage, watching where the grease thickens and where the cold mudstone drinks.

To live down here is to be an elbow in the piping: everything heavy eventually washes past.

***

In the beginning of a shift, before my eyes adjust from the orange filament cages of the middle tier to the dull, tallow-lit gloom of the sump, I don't look at anything. I stand on the narrow granite ledge—fresh-sheared, sharp enough through the hide wrap to keep my balance honest—and I lay the flat of my forearm against the main lead return.

You don't listen with your ears down here. The air is too full of the conveyor's *clack-clack* and the high, dry hiss of the relief packing. You listen with the ulna. 

Through the bone, the Ward reports:
Three miles of copper vibrating at sixty strokes a minute—the engines are holding pressure.
A hollow, stuttering skip—the pyrite buckets on the North rise are running light, someone hasn't cleared the hopper screen.
And below that, deeper than the engines, the slow, thick drag of the hydraulic ram settling into its pit, like a heavy beast sitting down in the dark.

When the pulse is steady, I step down into the gully.

The slurry comes up to mid-shin, warm as fresh blood near the engine discharges, but turning grease-cold where it hits the raw rock wall. My job is small and exact: with a crescent-shaped scraper made of boiled horn, I peel the rubbery white fungus from the water-seams. If you let it grow unchecked, it bridges across the lead troughs, damming the water until it backs up into the lower transformer vaults. But if you peel it too clean, the bare mudstone flakes under the sulfur spray, crumbling into grit that ruins the centrifugal pump impellers.

So I leave three fingers’ width of the white skin along the stone. A gasket of living tallow between the cold rock and the hot grease.

My hands are strange to look at, though I rarely have light enough to see them. The hair was scalded off in my second year by a ruptured drain cock on accumulator Four, and the skin grew back slick and thick, greyish-pink like the underside of a river stone. The mineral dust has worked into the pores until my knuckles look tattooed in dark iron. When I pack a pinch of the dried copper moss into my lower lip, my thumb leaves a rusty smudge across my chin that doesn't wash off with the gully-water.

The moss tastes like copper wire and dried blood. Within seconds, the astringent takes the heat out of the back of my throat, numbing the constant prickle of coal-fines. 

From above, a sharp *tink-tink... tong* rings down the copper conduit. 

Two shorts, one long. It’s Mora, up in the third-tier suspension huts. Her zinc box is swinging heavy today—she must have the salt-troughs full for the brine cure. The tap means: *Clear the runoff at Pier Nine; the condensation is sheeting.*

I do not shout back. The air would steal the voice anyway, turn it to a rasping cough. I take the butt of my horn scraper and rap twice against the lead lip of the gully: *Heard. Moving.* The lead gives a dull, heavy thud—not a ring, but a shockwave that travels through the water and into the floorplates.

***

As I move toward Pier Nine, the air changes. It drops three degrees—which, down here, feels like plunging your face into well-water. This is the shelf where the water runs silent over the chiseled steps of the mudstone, where the living yield to the floor.

There is someone lying in the third trough today. 

It is young Kaelen—or what is left of him after the winch drum snapped on the coal incline. They brought him down while I was scraping the upper bypass. They have laid him straight, as we always do, heels tucked together, hands crossed flat over the sternum, thumbs locked so the arms don't float when the tallow-surge comes at shift-change.

The white shroud has already started. 

It is fascinating to watch, if you have the patience of a sump-tender. The fungus doesn't rot him; there are no flies, no maggots, no air for corruption. Instead, the tallow-scum from the upper engines, washed down by the condensation, meets the cold iron-water seeping from his skin. The pale fronds are already lacing between his bare ribs, fine as linen thread, pinning his hide kilt to the stone. By the end of this cycle, his face will be smooth and featureless under a white, rubbery veil. By next year, the mineral salts will have replaced the marrow in his shins, turning him as heavy and dark as the mudstone beneath him.

He is already taking up the vibrations of the accumulator. When I touch his cold shoulder with my scraper, the metal hums through him into my tool without a break. 

He is stopping being *above* and becoming *below*.

I clear a drift of coal-grit from around his ankles so the water flows evenly over both feet. If the flow is uneven, the stone hardens crooked, and later, when the next tier of brick is laid for the secondary dynamos, the foundation will have a soft pocket. We need our dead to be flat. We need them to be true.

***

When the shift horn blows—a long, wet groan of steam that echoes through the rock until the teeth in your jaw ache—I do not climb up into the swaying huts immediately. 

I sit for a moment on the lowest iron rung, just above the water line, where the draft from the cooling tower pulls a faint, moving current across the back of my neck. 

My hide apron is stiff with dried machine tallow. My wood-pegged slippers are dripping lead-grey silt onto the stone. In my mouth, the copper moss has lost its bite, leaving only a clean, metallic stillness on the tongue.

Up above, the Tenders are shifting in their hammocks. I can hear the zinc boxes chiming like distant, muffled bells as twenty bodies turn at once to find the cooler air near their floor seams. The cable conveyor slows, its *clack-clack* lengthening, spacing out, as the engine throttles down for the accumulator recharge.

I am the one who stays in the seam. 

Not the engine, which will burn out its pistons in ten years. Not the stone, which will wait ten thousand. But the thing in between—the scraper, the listener, the one who keeps the grease from choking the rock and the rock from breaking the iron. 

I spit the spent moss into the dark gully, watch it swirl once in the tallow-sheen, and tap my horn knife against the lowest rung. 

One short. 

*Still holding.*
