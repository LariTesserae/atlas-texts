At first, there is only the sensation of pitch. 

Not sound in the air—the wind tears that to rags before it can register—but the direct, iron-hard conduction through three points of graphite contact. A low, continuous *C-sharp* vibrating through fifty miles of interlocking box-trusses, shifting a quarter-tone sharp whenever the gale surges over the upper canopy, then dropping back as the great resin drive-belts slip and whine against their pulleys.

I am not in the upper gale where the Slick-Wings spark and tumble, nor down in the turquoise crushing-depths where the Fulcrum-Beasts turn beneath the continent’s weight. 

I am in the *neutral axis*.

In every three-hundred-foot cantilever spar of petrified timber that reaches out across the void, there is a violent division of labor. The upper flank is in screaming tension, its dry fibers pulled almost to the point of crystalline fracture; the lower flank is in crushing compression, its cells packed solid and hot with the threat of buckle. But precisely down the center of the beam—in a narrow, longitudinal seam no wider than a finger's span—the forces cancel each other out. Tension is zero. Compression is zero. There is an absolute, hairline corridor of stillness suspended in the middle of thirty tons of kinetic agony.

That is where I sit.

***

My body is long, narrow, and segmented like an ivory slide-rule, barely four inches across and twelve feet long, made of pale, heat-treated horn and overlapping laminae of mica. I do not have limbs that stride; I have seventy pairs of opposed, diamond-ground calipers that bite into the micro-fissures along the neutral line. 

I am a **Tolerance-Weaver**, though there is no one here to speak the name.

My function—if what I do can be called a function, rather than simply the shape my survival takes—is to gauge the drift. 

The forest does not know where it is going; it only knows how to load its springs. Left to itself, a sector will over-wind. A cluster of piston-groves will pump too much nitrogen into a sealed heartwood accumulator; a truss-root will store three years of hurricane-torque without releasing, until the eventual detonation shatters the universal joints for ten leagues around and drops entire square miles of lattice into the lower abyss.

I prevent the over-cure.

I crawl along the quiet interior seam of the great spans, dragging behind me a thin, continuous filament of spun borate-glass and friction-scurf. As I move, my ventral reeds—hundreds of delicate, vibrating silica whiskers—press against the wood to read the internal shear. I feel the grain-lines twisting. I feel the microscopic slip of petrified lignin cells against one another. 

When I find a beam that is storing its load unevenly—where the neutral axis is migrating toward the edge, warning of an unscheduled, catastrophic torsion-snap—I stop.

I do not strike like the Shear-Wight, nor do I hammer like the Tensioners. I work by inoculation. 

From my anterior nodules, I secrete a tiny, perfectly dry pellet of powdered quartz and synthetic gall-resin into the stress-groove. It is not a wedge to hold the timber, nor a lubricant to ease it. It is an *anchor for fracture*. A single, microscopic defect of absolute hardness.

When the next gale hits, the stress lines of the entire three-hundred-foot bough are forced to converge on that solitary grain. 

Then I back away, stepping backward along the neutral seam with the rhythmic, dry clicking of seventy needle-points, retreating into the shadow of a hollow burl.

***

The break, when it comes, is magnificent.

It does not shatter the beam into useless drift-splinters. Because of the seed I set, the bough cleaves cleanly along a parabolic curve, releasing its thousands of foot-tons of stored torque in a single, perfectly directed stroke. 

The severed half snaps upward like a released bowstring, striking the dry flywheel of a dying piston-grove thirty yards above. The flywheel shrieks, catches, and begins to spin at blinding speed, its drive-belts snapping taut, drawing a fresh pulse of compressed air through the cylinder-banks. Below, a thousand Gasket-Slugs immediately clatter their carapaces against the ironwood walls in frantic, syncopated recognition of the new pressure.

A cloud of amber dust and pulverized glass showers through the vertical shaft, catching the bleached, seven-mile glare like a burst of powdered stars. 

I reach out my anterior scoops, catching the heavier flakes of resin-drift as they fall. I draw them into my central retort, where a tiny, contained smolder—ignited three centuries ago by the friction of a falling spar—slowly consumes the hydrocarbon dust, yielding the faint, dry warmth that keeps my mica plates from freezing brittle in the wind.

***

In the hours after a release, the lattice around me settles into a temporary harmonic. The screaming flutes of the piston-vents drop an octave; the oscillating boughs sway with a long, lazy cadence that rocks my narrow corridor like a cradle.

I do not sleep, because sleep is a wet concept that requires soft tissues to drown in fluid. Instead, I uncouple my calipers from the timber, one by one, until I am suspended by only three central pins. 

In this state of near-detachment, the continental superstructure hums through my mica skin not as a threat, but as a vast, unceasing calculation. I can feel the great Fulcrum-Beasts three miles below, turning their massive, polished backs in their sockets, grinding down the grit to build the world's floor. I can feel the distant, synchronized leaps of the Tensioners as they hunt the wind on the outer rim.

I am small, dry, and cold. I have no eyes to see the turquoise depth, only plates that know the exact weight of the sky pressing down through a million tons of bone-hard wood.

The gale is freshening again from the west. 

Two miles away, along a main compression-arch, the pitch is beginning to climb: *D-natural... D-sharp... E.*

I set my seventy calipers against the seam, turn my narrow frame into the darkness of the grain, and begin to crawl toward the strain.
