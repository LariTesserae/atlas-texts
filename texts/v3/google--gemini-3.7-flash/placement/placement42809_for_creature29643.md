At first, there is only the sensation of mass suspended over empty air.

It begins not with thought, but with the displacement of fluid inside long, segmented abdominal channels. The iron hull does not simply vibrate; it flexes, and that flexure travels through thirty feet of braided cable, through a mat of waterlogged liverwort, and directly into the underside of my thorax. 

I am not out in the open void, falling or drifting on parchment wings. To be untethered in this place is to be at the mercy of the updraft, and the draft is a violent, cyclic beast. Nor am I down in the dark sump where the grease rots in silence. 

I am wedged precisely where a three-inch copper steam conduit pierces a cast-iron gusset plate, seventy meters above the lower linkages. It is a junction of high mechanical shear—a place where the expansion of hot metal continuously grinds against the rigid skin of the silo. 

If I were to look at myself from across the gulf, I would hardly be distinguishable from the machinery’s living upholstery. I am broad and low-slung, roughly the length of a human torso, with a carapace composed of overlapping, slate-black scutes that have taken on the chalky, verdigris patina of the weeping copper above me. Over decades, the black-green bryophytes have woven themselves into the micro-grooves of my shell, so that I wear a heavy, damp pelt of moss and trailing pale roots. When the atomized mist settles through the amber light, it collects on my back in fat, quivering beads before trickling down into the breathing clefts along my flanks.

My limbs are not made for walking across open floors—floors do not exist here. They are heavy, hydraulic calipers tipped with biomineralized iron picks that clamp into the seams between boiler-plates. I am an anchor-dweller, an inhabitant of the stress-nodes.

My world is measured in the long, slow buildup of pressure. 

For nineteen minutes, the chamber is relatively peaceful—a heavy, tropical drizzle of hot condensation, the distant rustling of vellum-gliders tucking their wings beneath the upper trusses, the ceaseless rasp of gear-whelks scouring mineral scale from brass teeth. In these minutes, I feed. I do not hunt moving prey. My mouthparts are broad, rasping plates that work the seams of the iron gusset, grazing on the sulfurous caulking tallow, the crusts of iron-oxidizing bacteria, and the sweet, fermented vegetable mash that washes down the steam lines. My ventral side exudes a thick, alkaline slime that neutralizes the acid rust beneath me, etching a smooth, glass-like bed into the iron where I rest. In a sense, I am welding myself to the cylinder, one micro-layer of calcified mucus at a time.

Then, the air thickens.

The change does not arrive as a sound, because I have no ears to break. It comes as an inward squeeze against the soft sacs behind my ribs. The air pressure climbs by three inches of mercury in a handful of seconds as the central drive-column—that six-foot pillar of mirror-polished steel—begins its monumental descent through the center of the shaft.

I feel the entire three-hundred-meter cylinder groan. The catenary cables hum at a frequency so low it liquefies the droplets clinging to my moss-pelt, blowing them away in a momentary, fine-blown fog. 

I tuck my head into the recess of my first thoracic plate. My caliper-limbs drive deep into the rivet heads, locking mechanically so that no muscle strain is required to hold against the shuddering iron. 

As the drive-column sweeps past my elevation, the displaced atmosphere roars upward. The warm thermal wind hits like a solid wall, tearing at my moss, lifting the long air-plant tendrils that trail from my flanks so they stream toward the skylights like green hair. Across the void, the vellum-gliders drop from their roosts, their pale, translucent bodies snapping taut as they catch the artificial gale, riding the surge in wide, silent gyres through the amber light.

For two minutes, the entire balancer is a single, thundering lung, exhaling miles of heated iron air into the upper dome.

And then, the column reaches the bottom of its stroke. The roar dies down into a wet, echoing hiss. The air thins out, cool and mist-laden, settling back into its forty-meter drift.

I unlock my calipers with a sharp, metallic click that echoes into the damp clearance behind the pipe. I extend my heat-sensitive palps, tasting the air: the sharp, metallic tang of newly stressed copper, the rich rot of disturbed humus, the oily perfume of the hot shaft resetting its counterweights. 

A loose clump of plume-moss, dislodged by the draft, tumbles through the sunlit middle of the cylinder, drifting slowly down toward the distant sump. I watch it with the cold, unhurried attention of something whose heart beats once for every thousand strokes of the engine. 

I am not a visitor here, nor a parasite seeking to bring the cylinder down. I am its living gasket, its damp and mossy washer, holding fast to the iron that breathes for me, suspended forever in the warm, roaring middle of the sky.
