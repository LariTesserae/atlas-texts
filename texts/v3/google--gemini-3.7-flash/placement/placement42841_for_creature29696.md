I would not be moving quickly. In a basin where movement requires the continuous expenditure of coherence, to travel fast is to wear thin, to invite the edges of oneself to blur into the gray wash of the lichen-mats. 

I find myself low to the ground—in fact, largely below it. 

I am settled into the shoulder of a low, rounded swell where three braided channels meet, just where the water hesitates before choosing whether to plunge into an bottomless silt-sink or creep backwards over a ridge of lead-sheathed peat. 

At first, I might be mistaken for a boulder of weathered basalt or an abandoned coil of heavy, rubberized conduit half-swallowed by liverwort. But there is a slow, wet pulse to my perimeter. I have no limbs to speak of; instead, my mass is arranged in thick, concentric folds of dark, slick hide that feels like chilled tallow or wet slate. I have surrendered the need for a silhouette. In a valley where form is an active tax, I have chosen the geometry with the lowest surface-area-to-volume ratio: a heavy, slumped dome, settled deep into the cold mire, anchored into the petrified circuit-strata by a thick taproot of dense, fibrous cartilage.

I am a *ballast*.

If the Iterants are the surgeons who smooth the tears, and the Pylon-striders are the kites drinking the heat of the snaps, I am the reservoir where the basin deposits its arithmetic remainders. 

The physics here are leaky, but nothing ever truly disappears; the discarded decimals, the fractions of momentum that don't balance when a stream reverses its flow, the small temporal debts left behind when a grebe kills an insect twenty seconds before it lands—they all generate a faint, toxic static. A smear of unresolved potential. If left to pool, it creates a dead zone where the ground loses its grip on materiality altogether and turns to cold, humming soup.

I draw that slack into myself. 

I drink the damp not through a mouth, but through the deep fissures between my folds. My internal cavity is cold, silent, and immensely dense—a labyrinth of mineralized plates and viscous, oil-suspended electrolytes. When the air overhead shears with that dry, glass-breaking snap, I feel the jolt run down through the silt like a low-voltage kick. I absorb the parity error, pulling the temporal dissonance down into my core, where it is slowly, mechanically ground down against the heavy resistance of my mass. 

It takes days to digest a single second of stolen time. Inside me, it feels like a ball of cold mercury rolling endlessly down an internal spiral, gradually cooling, shedding its velocity, until it matches the vast, subterranean drone of the earth and becomes simple, inert weight.

Because of this, I am exceedingly heavy. The mud beneath me is compressed into a stone-hard ring of dark, vulcanized glass. Weft-worms love the perimeter of my body; they thread themselves by the dozens into the creases where my hide meets the peat, drinking the trickle of voltage that bleeds off me as I ground the local field. Their translucent cords pulse with a quiet, rhythmic violet light, festooning my flanks like living stitching.

I do not have eyes. I do not need to see the pewter sky or the copper ferns. I perceive the basin as a topography of tension. 

I can feel the cold, sharp needle-points of a Pylon-strider four hundred yards away, stepping gingerly across the field lines, its weightless stride sending faint acoustic ripples through the strata. I can feel the small, frantic blips of the grebes waiting in their false puddles, their dielectric bellies shimmering as they tilt against the gravitational drift. 

And sometimes, when the rain settles into a heavy, continuous drumming that drowns out everything but the deepest frequencies of the earth, the Iterants come.

They do not come to fix me. They know I am not broken; I am simply full. 

They arrive in silence, their black-lichen skins glistening, their gill-slits flaring pale indigo in the cool fog. They kneel around my sloping bulk, pressing their five-jointed fingers deep into the soft, rubbery crevices of my upper folds. They use me as an anchor. In my immediate shadow, reality is quiet, dense, and stubbornly fixed; spacetime does not flicker here, because my immense, digested inertia holds the local coordinates pinned to the mud like an iron weight on a sheet of wet parchment.

They lean their foreheads against my cold flank, their breathing gradually slowing, locking into the slow, heavy peristalsis of my core. For an hour, or a day, they rest from the exhausting labor of insisting on their own shapes. They let my bulk carry the burden of their mass.

As they sleep against me, the valley hums its crushing, multi-tonal chord. The cold rain beads on my hide, swelling, merging, and rolling down into the dark water that flows, quietly and without hurry, both uphill and down.
