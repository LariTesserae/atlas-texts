I would not be at the surface, where the light is too thin and the air tastes of naked snow. Nor would I be down in the petrified quiet of the floor, though my ears are already tuned to the slow, four-beat thrumming of the *Titanoceratodus* sleeping in the silt below.

I find myself suspended at twenty-four fathoms, right where the amber rays of the noon sun cool into a hard, oceanic jade. 

I am a Cable-Tuner, a lashing-hand of the mid-tiers.

***

My body has long since forgotten the sharp edges of land. I am thick through the ribs—my chest arched like the curved hull of an alder hab-sphere, giving my lungs the volume to hold a single, calm inhalation for the length of time it takes a char to graze the length of a seventy-foot guy-rope. Beneath my skin, the fat is firm and pale, cold to the touch on the surface but burning with a deep, slow metabolic heat within. My scalp-crest is braided tight, stiffened with the bitter wax of the midday caldera lilies, and across my knuckles I wear thin, curved plates of dark river-slate, lashed with gut. 

My home is a three-tier wicker cylinder tethered between the Council Rotunda and the western shale ledges. It is an old sphere, but not yet a dead one. If you run your palm along its outer stave-hoops, you can feel the life of it changing under your fingers: the upper half is still soft green alder and living, bearded moss that yields beneath your grip, but the lower ring has begun to drink the lime from the meltwater. It is growing a rind of pale, gritty tufa. 

In another fifteen winters, its buoyancy will fail entirely; the root-floats will tire, the granite ballast-baskets will kiss the silt, and I will let it go down to join the reef. But for now, it hangs perfectly trimmed, floating on the invisible breath of the basin.

My days are measured not in hours, but in the tension of cords.

Sound travels through my collarbones before it reaches my ears. When I kick out from the air-bell of my tower—leaving behind the warm, close stench of peat-smoke, dried char-belly, and musk—the water takes my weight with a soft, total embrace. My milk-lids flick shut over my eyes, filtering the high, sharp glare of the surface into a restful, mossy twilight. 

Out in the void, fifty paces from any wall, the city is a living harp. 

I reach out a hand and curl my fingers around the master transit cable: eight braided strands of river-nettle, thick as my wrist, anchored to a six-ton granite block in the twilight below and buoyed by a cluster of cured pumpkin-gourds far above. 

I press the slate plate on my thumb against the cable and strike it once: *Tock.*

The vibration races up and down the line. I lay my forehead against the wet nettle-fiber and listen to the return. 

Through the cord comes the story of the whole basin. A quarter-mile away, near the surface rafts, a child is paddling too fast, their beaver-tail fins slapping the water with a frantic, wasteful cadence (*thwip-thwip-thwip*). Below me, at thirty fathoms, the granite counterweights are shifting in their nettle cradles with a low, grinding *clonk-clack* that tells me the deep thermal seep has shifted two degrees north. And through it all runs the deep, shuddering hum of the great lungfish, vibrating the water so thoroughly that the marrow in my shins aches in sympathy.

A dark shape glides toward me along the line, moving with the jerky, mechanical dignity of an iron spring. It is one of my barn-crabs—an indigo scissors-crab as broad as my chest, its shell encrusted with miniature river-limpets. It recognizes the texture of my wake. It climbs onto the line ahead of me, its serrated claws clicking a quiet greeting against the fiber: *snip, tap-tap, snip.*

Together, we work the line down toward the cold twilight. 

Where the water-moss has grown too heavy, threatening to drag the cable down with its sodden weight, I shave it back with a flat slate knife, letting the green ribbons drift down to feed the bottom-feeders. Where the nettle strands show the gray fraying of rot, the crab follows my hand, its heavy claws surgically shearing away the dead fiber so I can splice in fresh, wax-cured cordage from the pouch at my thigh. When we finish a span, I reward it with a strip of char-belly cured in bottom-salt; it takes the meat from my fingers with delicate, blue-tipped mouthparts, vibrating its carapace in contentment.

Around noon, when the light strikes down like vertical pillars of green glass, I pause in the open void between the towers. 

Looking up, the surface of the sink is an undulating ceiling of molten silver, patterned by the dark, floating shapes of the garden-rafts and the tiny, frantic silhouettes of the water-shrews diving for snails. Up there, the world is sharp, fragile, and dry. My people go there only to salt what they have taken, squinting painfully at the huge, empty sky before sliding back into the safety of the bowl. 

Looking down, the light fails. The suspended towers fade from green wicker into ghostly, calcified rinds of gray tufa, resting silent in the gloom where the ancestors' homes have become the foundation stones for our own.

I do not wish to be up where the sky burns, and I am not yet ready for the long stone sleep at the bottom. 

Here, in the cold, clear middle—where the ropes hum with the voices of my cousins, where the char brush against my flanks to graze the algae from my harness, and where the water carries every thought, every tap, and every heartbeat across the quiet four hundred paces of our world—the tension is exact. 

I strike my slate knuckles together twice—*clink-clink*—to tell the watch-diver at the Rotunda that the western guy-line holds true. Then I draw my legs up, flick my fins, and glide effortlessly through thirty yards of emerald void, homeward to the air-bell.
