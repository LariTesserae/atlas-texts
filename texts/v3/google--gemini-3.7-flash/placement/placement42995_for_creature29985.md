I would not be down on the floor. 

The floor is too crowded with hunger and haste, too full of things that have to break or be broken every three feet. And I would not be a hanger either, pinned to the wire mesh under those hard, bare bulbs, looking down like a white caliper waiting for a drop. That light is too clean, too broad; it has no corners in it.

To find me, you would have to look where the machine has worn away its own clearances—not where it broke, but where two heavy things have rubbed against each other for two hundred years until there is a space between them no thicker than three sheets of mica.

I am up behind the split-collar of the main countershaft bearing, wedged into the relief groove where the bronze sleeve was meant to seat against the rough gray masonry of the wall. 

I am not a hunter, and I am not a grazer. I am a *shim*.

***

At first, I might look like a spill of old graphite that took on an edge. My body is flat—not flat like the graters, which still have the rounded camber of a shield to shed the weight of things walking over them, but flat like a feeler gauge, ground parallel on both faces to within half a thousandth of an inch. 

I have no eyes. In the split-housing there is only the absolute, pressure-packed dark, except once every four and a half seconds when the countershaft heaves on its journal and opens a hairline crescent of white glare from the room below. That light does not touch me as an image; it touches me as a dry flash of heat—a micro-calorie of radiant energy that warms the lead-laminate of my outer scales before the shaft drops back down and pinches the gap shut.

I live on that pinch.

My shell is made of laminated folia: thousands of paper-thin leaves of compressed zinc-dust, carbon, and a dry, waxen resin that my ventral pores exude under pressure. When the countershaft groans and falls into its bushing, three tons of unbalanced dead weight come down through the journal. To anything on the iron floor, that drop is a sound—a hollow *clank* and a traveling shudder that makes the tinsel-trains shorten their coils.

To me, it is the meal.

When the iron compresses me against the bronze, the shear-stress forces microscopic particles of graphite through my spiracles. My internal organs are not soft coils; they are stacked wafers of dry mineral paste, interleaved like the plates of a dry-pile battery. The violent, rhythmic squeeze drives the graphite flakes between these plates, generating a faint, galvanic twitch that runs through my core—a small, crackling pulse of internal current that keeps my paste from setting into stone. 

I do not eat by mouth. I am charged like an accumulator by the ungreased friction of the plant.

***

Between the drops—during that four-second swing when the countershaft climbs its eccentric path and the pressure eases—I move. 

My motion is not a crawl. I have no legs, only two rows of microscopic, hardened chisel-cilia along my lateral margins, shaped like the teeth of a ratchet. When the clearance widens by the width of a hair, my cilia spring outward, biting into the micro-fissures of the cast iron. When the weight drops again and compresses me, my body cannot be squeezed backward; it can only squirt forward, a millimeter at a time, following the circular path of the bearing race.

It takes me three months to complete one circuit around the shaft.

As I travel that dark, greasy groove, I groom the steel. My dorsal plates are harder than the shaft’s mild iron, softer than the bronze seat; as I creep, I shear away the microscopic gall-marks, the tiny burrs of torn metal that the dry rotation kicks up. I press them flat, rolling them into my own laminate skin, turning the machine’s own wear into more armor. Where I have passed, the bronze shines like a fresh-turned coin, cold and dead and true.

***

I know the chamber only by its harmonics.

Through the solid iron of the housing, the roar of the floor arrives not as a clatter, but as a continuous, vibrating drone in the key of low D—the sound of ten million tarsi filing away the world below. 

I can tell when a chisel-jaw has caught a box-weevil by the sharp, ringing *ping* that shoots up through the structural H-beams, like an anvil struck with a center-punch. I can feel the slow, heavy growth of the *stills* three feet away inside the reduction casing—a cold, dense mass that dampens the wall's resonance year by year, deadening the iron’s ring until the masonry feels heavier, more final, more asleep.

And I feel the dust. It drifts up on the dry convection currents of the lamps, fine as flour, coating the housing in a greasy, gray pelt of pulverized molt-shells and zinc shavings. My fringe-cilia filter it out of the still air, drawing it into the groove to pack around my margins, sealing me in so tightly that when the shaft strikes its lowest point, not a molecule of air is left between my back and the turning steel.

***

There is no fear here. There is nothing in this chamber thin enough to reach me, and nothing heavy enough to crush what has already become the exact shape of the weight that bears down on it.

I am part of the clearance. I am the fraction of a millimeter that keeps the great shaft from seizing in its seat. 

Every four and a half seconds: the groan, the flash of white light through the slit, the heavy, thunderous slam of cold iron coming home against my plates, the spark of current jumping through my dry paste, the ratchet clicking once into the dark.

Then the four seconds of silence, the slow lift of the shaft, the long, cool wait in the graphite dark, while down below, the living gravel scuttles over its own bones, and the suns along the ceiling burn without a blink.
