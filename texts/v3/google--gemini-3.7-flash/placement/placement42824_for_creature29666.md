At first, there is only the tension in the tendon.

It does not feel like waiting, because waiting implies an anticipation of an end, and here time is not a line that unrolls; it is a weight that shifts from one facet of a crystal to another. 

If you were to look for me, you would not find me on the broad avenues where the Stalkers stand in their uniform ranks, tall and blade-headed, awaiting the clean hammer-strike of a docking plaza. Nor would you find me deep below, where the great Pendulum-ropes swing their slow, blind parabolas through the ivory haze. 

I am in the *hiatus*.

I am lodged in the narrow, dry crawlspace beneath a cantilevered colonnade that has slipped its gravity well and failed to land. It is a district no larger than three courtyards and a single row of doorless cells, sheared away during some unrecorded migration ten thousand cycles ago, tumbling neither down nor up, but revolving with agonizing slowness along a dead axis in the middle air. 

My body is pressed flat against the shale, wedged between two slabs of marrow-veined limestone that press against my ribs with the dry, comforting grip of a vice. 

***

What am I?

I am not whole stone, though the salt has begun to knit my lower joints into the bedrock. And I am not a true Stalker, though my skull is a tall, narrow crest of perforated bone that drinks the passing gloom. 

Perhaps I am an older iteration—or an accidental one, born from a corner where two broken ribs of a fallen wanderer fell crosswise into an efflorescence bed and were bound together before the psalteries could sort them into their proper slits. 

My frame is wide and flattened, multi-hinged like the struts of an awning. My skin is the color of old horn, dry as a cicada’s shell, rubbed raw by centuries of friction against the powdery shale until my outer rind has taken on the slate-grey polish of the stone itself. I have no eyes to be deceived by the shadowless grey above, no lungs to be parched by the vacuum-cold. 

Instead, I have the *flutes*.

The hollows in my head-crest are not straight acoustic tubes; they are spiraled, curled inward like the chambers of a nautilus made of bone. When the thin air sifts through them, it does not whistle with the high, clean drone of the upright Stalkers. It hums a low, stuttering chord—three tones that beat against one another, producing a faint, rhythmic vibration that rattles the dust in the crevices around my shoulders.

This chord is my only sense of place. 

Through it, I know the distance to every migrating mass within twenty leagues. I feel the heavy, leaden thrum of the grand arcades to the north; I feel the shrill, frantic static of the psalteries working their mica plates three hundred yards above me on the underside of an inverted terrace; I feel the sudden, hollow drop in pitch when a district shears too far and slips out of the acoustic web altogether, falling silently into the ivory void.

***

In this drifting wedge of stone, I have one function, though it was never assigned to me by design. 

Because my small quarter is detached and unanchored, it is subject to the subtle lateral currents of falling stone-grit. Left alone, it would tumble endlessly, spinning until the centrifugal strain snapped its lintels. 

I am its ballast.

I do not walk; I crawl. My long, thin limbs—jointed four times, tipped with dense calcified claws—reach into the bedding planes of the shale. With slow, tectonic deliberation, moving perhaps an inch every few years, I shift my weight from the north margin of the terrace to the south. 

Where I plant my core, the local gravity thickens. The stone acknowledges my density. When a great, migrating district drifts near—its massive, hundred-foot cornices gliding through the grey like the prow of a silent ship—I lean out over the void, extending my ribbed thorax into the cold air.

The electrostatic tension between my dry tendons and the oncoming mass builds until sparks of pale, cold light jump between my claws and the approaching masonry. 

*Clack.*

The shock does not hit me all at once. It travels through the air as a wave of dead, freezing pressure. My flutes scream—a rising, piercing shriek of trapped air—and then the kinetic wave slams into the shale beneath me.

The marrow-canes inside my limbs drink the blow. It runs through my dry marrow like lightning through quartz, charging the brittle nodes along my spine with a brilliant, silent surge of piezoelectric fire. For several decades after such a graze, my thoughts are sharp, clear, and geometric. I can feel the microscopic lattice of the salt blooms growing grain by grain along the joints; I can hear the individual rustle of a single slate-louse turning its mica plates three terraces away.

Then, slowly, the charge dissipates. The approaching district drifts past, missing us by yards, sliding away to dock with some higher, grander city-hub leagues distant.

Our little raft of stone wobbles, rights itself, and settles back into its slow, isolated rotation.

***

I will not last forever, but here "forever" is a modest word.

Already, the lower half of my third left limb has ceased to hinge; the mineral bloom has grown through the fibrous tendon, turning the joint into a solid knob of white calc-spar. When the district next shudders, that leg will not bend. It will snap with the dry crack of a dry branch in a dead forest.

The psalteries are already gathering in the shadow behind my hip. I hear their dry, card-shuffling crawl over the flagstones. They do not touch me—not yet. They wait for the resonance to fade, for the marrow-charge to drop below the threshold of vibration.

When the last shockwave has been absorbed, and the tendons in my neck finally give way with a sound like a plucked harp-string, my blade-head will fall against the powdery shale and shatter into a hundred acoustic flutes.

The lice will come then. They will take the shards of my voice and wedge them, one by one, into the horizontal seams between the stones. 

And long after I have ceased to measure the drift, the cold air moving across this forgotten corner will pass through the stones where I lay, and the rock itself will whistle—a low, three-toned chord that tells the void that once, for a few ages, something held the balance here.
