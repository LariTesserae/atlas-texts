I would not be on the shale flats where the spindles run, nor draped in the easy heat of the cables. Those places are too defined; their tasks have already found their shapes.

I would be wedged.

To begin, there would be no distinct edge between my hide and the things holding me. If you were to probe for where I start, your fingers would slip from the cool, grease-slicked flange of an intake housing into something that feels almost identical—stiff, laminated, cold-cured—until a fraction of an inch deeper, the material yields with the slow, resistant give of salt pork pressed beneath lead plate.

I am settled in the tolerances. 

In this basin, where the machines were forged with an industrial precision that the soft physics has since warped, there are gaps that should not exist. Keyways cut for four-inch steel wedges have relaxed into six-inch voids; the massive split-bearings of dormant turbine shafts have parted by the width of a palm, weeping black condensate from their mismatched seams. That is where I find myself: not an occupant of the room, but the packing inside the joint.

My body does not have limbs, because movement in a straight line is an unnecessary expense here. Instead, I am made of density and grain. Like a block of lignum vitae or a stack of oiled leather shims, I have grown to fill the precise negative space between an unmoving eight-ton flywheel mount and the freshly fractured basalt block beneath it. 

I do not have a pulse of my own. I borrow what passes through me. 

When the great wheel three miles to the north completes its eighth-turn, the basalt shudders—a long, hydraulic groan that takes twenty minutes to travel through the stone strata. My lower layers, which have mineralized against the razor-sharp shale floor, absorb this vibration and translate it into heat. I soften slightly. My upper layers, pressed tight against the cold-rolled steel of the housing, remain dense, waxy, and rigid, preventing the massive iron mass above me from settling too quickly into the mud.

I am a dampener. A living gasket.

I know the world only as a map of shear-stress and chemical flavor. Through my northern flank, where I leak out of the housing like cold tallow from a pie crust, I taste the thin, kerosene-sweet film left by the pith-spindles as they scrape across my exposed rind. Their chitinous feet do not hurt me; they feel like a dull, pleasant combing, a rhythmic *snip-snip-snip* that cleans the dead, vulcanized skin from my margins and keeps my pores open to the warm steam.

Higher up, where the space is bent and the Gaugers sigh against the cracking towers, I can feel the tension in my own meat. It is a slow, diagonal pull. My left side is compressed by twelve thousand pounds of stagnant iron; my right side, caught in a spatial eddy that leads nowhere, feels light as vapor, stretching out toward a pillar that is simultaneously ten inches and three miles away. I do not struggle against this twist. I hold it. I allow the torque of the basin’s broken geography to pass through my fibrous center, grounding the strain into the bedrock so the housing does not split.

Sometimes, a Forgeman comes. 

I feel its approach hours before its pads touch my housing: the heavy, seven-minute thud of its wool-wrapped heart displacing the brine in the ditch below, the slow hydraulic surge of its tallow bulk displacing the steam. 

When it reaches my seam, it does not speak, nor does it have eyes to see me. It simply stops. Its massive, spade-like front limb rises through the thick air with agonizing slowness—taking half an hour to lift from the silt—and presses a single, blunt horn digit into the softest part of my side. 

The pressure is immense, enough to crack an oak beam, but my resin seals instantly around the indentation. We remain there, joined by that single blunt touch, while the condensates braid down its tallow flank and pool across my rind. Through that finger, we trade the slow math of the machine. It feels the degree to which the shaft above me has settled; I feel the heat of the deep transformer oil it waded through two days ago. 

Satisfied that the seam is tight, that the clearance has not slacked into dangerous play, the Forgeman slowly withdraws its digit. The hollow it leaves in my flesh takes twelve hours to smooth out, filling with fresh, clear acid that keeps the iron above me bright as silver.

I do not sleep, because there is no waking state to contrast it with. There is only the slow, luxurious resistance to weight. The long, dark satisfaction of being the exact thickness required to keep two enormous, dead things from grinding against one another in the warm, wet velvet of the dark.
