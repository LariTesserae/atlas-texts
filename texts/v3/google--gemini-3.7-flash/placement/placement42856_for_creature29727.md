First, it is only a measurement of weight.

Seven seconds while the beam swings upward and the light broadens over the wet basalt. One second at the apex, where the counterweight chains pull taut in their iron troughs with a slow, grinding sigh that sets the water-beads dancing along the flange. Seven seconds coming down—the shadow widening, the greasy air compressing, the cold mist shoved aside by twenty tons of cast iron—and then the strike.

*Thung-chunk.*

It does not hurt. If it ever hurt, that was fifty thousand strokes ago, before the grain closed, before the fiber forgot the forest and learned the engine. 

I am down where the iron meets the stone, seated in the low socket of the northern bed-frame: the heartwood stop-block of English oak, four feet square and two feet thick, bolted flush into the shale floor with lead-poured iron dogs.

Above me is the sole-plate of the rocker arm, faced with a strike-plate of soft pig-iron. Below me is the crushed basalt, where the water-voles have tunneled their runnels through the gravel. Between the two, I have become something that is no longer strictly timber, nor yet cold iron.

To be wood down here is to live a curious, heavy half-life. A tree rots from water and dies in fire, but under this discipline, neither can touch you. The Sump-wardens do not allow rot. Every morning when the pale daylight first strikes the top bore-grooves of the cut, the younger warden kneels beside my flank with an iron ladle. He pours hot sheep’s tallow—melted over the coke brazier with pine-pitch and paraffin—straight into the split grain of my end-grain. 

The heat runs through me like marrow. The blow of the descending beam drives it deep, ramming the grease through the cellular pores until the wood is solid as basalt and slick as lard. When the cold mist hits me, the water cannot enter; it gathers into round, mercury-bright droplets on my polished crown and shivers off into the gravel when the iron falls again.

I feel the entire Sump through my base.

The wardens think they are the only ones who know the tension of the copper hoops, but they feel it only through the soles of their birch-soled clogs. I take the whole cut into my grain. I know when the southern condenser is choking on a tangle of willow root before the first plume of steam thins—I feel the change in the recoil, a dry, stuttering shudder through the floor flags half a second before the stroke finishes. 

I know the voles by the small heat of their ribs pressed against my underside. In the hollows between my bedding bolts, where the shredded hemp packing has formed a warm, oily felt, a mother has tucked her fourth litter of the season. When the strike comes, my mass absorbs the ruinous peak of the concussion, turning it into a dull, thudding wave of low heat that rolls through the gravel. The pink pups do not startle; they open their mouths against the hemp, drinking the warm vibration as if it were milk.

When the sun sits directly overhead, the cut turns silver. The light comes down straight between the gantry webs, reflecting off the wet copper of the cylinders and the broad, wet tongues of the hart’s-tongue ferns. 

From my corner against the stone, the ferns look like green fountains held in mid-spurt. Their roots are constantly reaching for me. Every twelve hours, during the shift change when the engines are left to their long, slow rhythm, a pale white root-hair from the coltsfoot will creep out of the flagstone seam and touch my side, drawn by the smell of hot sheep’s grease. It tries to bite. It wants the nitrogen and the fat. But the vibration is too dense; after three strokes, the root-tip is stunned, shaken to glass, and pulverized into the grit to feed the midges.

I have no eyes, but I have the displacement. 

When the soot-tail martins dive down through the steam plumes to take the midges off the exhaust boxes, the air thins above me for a fraction of a second. When the wind rolls over the lip twenty feet up, the cold air drops like a dropped sack, flattening the mist against the flags, and the smell changes—from the sweet, lard-and-stone breath of the cut to the sour soot of the city streets above.

I do not care for the city above. It has no timing. Its sounds are ragged, without stop-blocks or counterweights, full of voices that begin and end without clearance.

Here, everything belongs to the quarter-minute.

*Lift.* The tallow cools on my strike-face, hardening into a pale, waxy skin. The voles shift in the hemp below. A drop of copper-stained water falls from the trunnion bracket and lands precisely on my northern bolt-head: *ping.*

*Fall.* The shadow thickens. The air screams out from between the iron plates. 

*Strike.* The iron kisses the oak; the grease liquefies under the hammer; the shock travels down through my rings, into the anchor pins, through the grey shale, down into the root-rock of the mountain where the compressed air is already groaning through the dark brick tunnels.

I am holding the bottom of the world steady, fifteen seconds at a time.
