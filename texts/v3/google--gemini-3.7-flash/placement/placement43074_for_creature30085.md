I would not be up in the light of the eighth or ninth tiers, where the sky is wide and silver and the swallows nest in the clean corners of the ashlar. Nor would I be all the way down in the dark sump-conduits, where the great pale salamanders lie like drowned tallow in the slow pools. 

I would be in the middle damp, around the fourth tier down from the wet rim, where the cold begins to have a weight of its own—a density you have to lean your ribs against when you turn a corner.

Specifically, I find myself in the *gap-seams*: the narrow, breathing spaces behind the masonry where the mountain’s raw shale face has not quite touched the back of the dressed stone. 

I am not an artisan of grand arches, nor a keeper of the clean flumes. My work is smaller, slower, and almost entirely tactile. I think of myself as an *in-between listener*—one of those who checks the tension where the green hemlock roots cross the cleavage planes of the rock.

***

My hands are thick and flat, the skin on the palms tanned like oil-cured leather from years of mixing bitter river-clay with crushed cedar gall. The nails are worn straight across, stained dark from the wet tannin that runs down the joints. I wear very little wool on my arms—sleeves only get sodden and drag against the narrow rock—just a tight vest of boiled goat-hide, double-waxed with tallow, and knee-wraps of braided bast that keep the chill from settling into the joints of my legs.

My tools are few:
- A flat spud of elk-shin bone, notched every two fingers to measure root-swell.
- A horn horn-flask of slip-clay, kept warm against my belly.
- A coil of soft copper wire, fine as hair, which I stretch between two brass pegs driven into adjacent blocks. If the mountain settles or the wood drinks too fast, the wire hums when plucked with a fingernail, its pitch rising from a low, wet drone to a sharp, brittle ring.

Most of my day is spent neither standing nor sitting, but braced. The rift teaches you three-point contact before you learn your vowels: one heel wedged into a shale crack, one shoulder pressed against the wet, cold cheek of an ashlar block, both hands free to feel where the bark is binding too hard against the mortar.

You learn very quickly that the stone does not hate the wood, but it does not yield to it willingly. The stone wants to fall; the wood wants to drink and swell. My whole existence is the negotiation between the two. 

When a root grows too fat in a narrow bedding joint, it doesn't break the stone immediately. First, it sweats. A white, sweetish sap weeps out into the moss, mixing with the mineral water that sheets down the cliff. That is the first sign. You can smell it three tiers away if the air is still: it smells like bruised parsley and cold iron. When I catch that scent, I slip into the access-slit beneath the nearest gallery floorboards, sliding on my side through the dark, water-draped gap until I find the knuckle of the root.

Then, the slow work. I do not cut the root—to cut it would leave a dead sponge in the wall that rots into empty air within three seasons, letting the tier slump. Instead, I pare the bark back with the bone spud, just a sliver at a time, along the side that faces the stone. I coat the wound with clay and river-ash. The tree feels the cold of the rock directly against its raw cambium, draws its water inward, and turns its growth sideways, slipping along the bedding plane rather than levering the blocks apart.

***

In the late silver of the afternoon, when the mist gathers thick enough to turn the opposite wall into a ghost-cliff of wet slate, I come out onto the narrow corbelled sills. 

I sit with my feet dangling over the three-hundred-foot drop to the mill-races, where the water is churned into a quiet, creamy foam that makes no more sound than a heavy wool blanket being dragged over wet flagstones. A fat marten, dark-coated and smelling strongly of fish-oil, will often come and sit two paces away on the same timber, its sides heaving in the chill, watching the swallows drop like lead weights from the eaves before snapping their wings open at the last possible inch.

We do not look at each other, the marten and I. Down here, looking too hard at a thing is like shouting—it disrupts the balance of the air. We simply occupy the same cold ledge, our skin and fur repelling the steady, silent silver bead-mist that condenses on our brows.

I take a cold cake of hemlock-bast from my pouch, break off a corner, and press it against my tongue. It is bitter, salty, and tastes of old rain, but it warms the back of the throat after a moment. 

From below, I can feel the faint, deep thrum of the great settling conduits—a low vibration that comes up through the soles of my feet, through the basalt, through the living hemlock heartwood, and into my own pelvic bone. It is the rift breathing. It tells me that the third sluice is clear, that the trout are holding steady under the bridges, and that the foundation-arches have not shifted a hair’s breadth since the morning mist rose.

I am not a creature of the open air. If you took me up to the rim—up where the wind has no walls and the daylight has no wet stone to soften it—my skin would dry and crack like ungreased leather, and my ears would ache from the violent emptiness of the sky. 

My home is the seam. My work is the silent knot where the mountain, the hemlock, and the cut stone agree to hold one another up against the weight of the water, for one more cold, dripping season.
