At first, it is only a question of weight and contact.

I am not standing on the ground, because there is no ground here that stays still enough to be called that; I am braced across an angle. One foot—sheathed in stiff, oil-soaked rawhide with lead-shot sewn into the welt to give it gravity—rests on the radiused shoulder of an eighteen-inch high-pressure return. The other is wedged into the gap where a fresh wedge of black slate is lifting a bolted flange off its bed. The stone is cold, smelling of deep cellar and crushed iron pyrite; the flange is eighty degrees hotter, weeping a slow, amber bead of paraffin grease that smells faintly like mutton fat and scorched turnip.

If you asked me my name, the sound would not travel three feet into the wool baffle across my mouth. Here, you do not use a name; you use a drift-rate.

I am one of the Markers, though not of the main crew who walk the skids behind the tractors. I work the *interstices*—the crawlways that exist for forty minutes or three days between a boiler-skin being seated by a crane and the moss closing the seam. My kit is light because anything heavy anchors you to a plane that will be vertical by noon. I carry three sticks of blue chalk wrapped in bladder-skin to keep the dew off, a flat brass feeler-gauge shaped like an alder leaf, and a horn scraper for lifting dead midges out of valve-seats before the rams lock them down.

***

Where am I?

I am thirty feet below the pale sky and twelve feet above the wet peat, inside the hollow box-girder of an uncommissioned bypass manifold. The air in here is warm and smells of damp linen, ozone, and the sour green starch of horsetails that were crushed when the iron was extruded this morning. 

I spend a great deal of time waiting.

You cannot force an alignment here. You have to lay your forehead against the cold web of the beam—right between the double rows of cold-driven rivets—and listen into the iron with your teeth. You feel the basin before you see what it intends. 

Three miles out, the slate is taking a long, slow breath, lifting the western quadrant half an inch every ten minutes. You feel it as a harmonic twitch in the copper ties. Then comes the machine’s answer: the pneumatic stabilizers deep in the sumps groan against their seals, not with a roar, but with that soft, thick *huff* of air forced through wet felt. The girder I am sitting in shifts two degrees south-southeast. A grease-swift, whose nest is still wet against the exterior fillet, doesn't even leave her clutch; she simply spreads her primaries against the steel to ride out the slew.

My job is not to stop the shift. Nothing stops the shift. My job is to decide whether the shift is an *erasure* or an *addition*.

***

If it is an erasure, I take the horn scraper and I shave away the grease-seal where the bypass enters the sump. I let the acidic moss-water in. Within six minutes, the cold liquid hits the pre-heater jackets; the thermal differential causes the relief springs to drop their dogs, and the entire assembly—twelve tons of milled steel and braided conduit—sighs, unknots its pins, and settles back into the black mud like an elk lying down in the reeds. 

By dusk, the liverworts will have covered the rivet heads. By tomorrow, a self-laying rail line will roll right over the place where I was sitting, its wide oak treads pressing the metal into the bog until it is nothing more than a dense layer of mineral strata, waiting for the diamond-saws of some next iteration to quarry it back out.

If it is an addition, though—if the slate beneath the footings has stabilized into a true dome—I take the blue chalk.

I reach out of the access hatch, into the cold, silent drift of the basin’s air. The sky above is the color of skimmed milk, hard and clean, reflecting off the damp backs of thirty thousand rising surfaces. I find the seam where the new masonry has been sliced square by the saws. The rock-flour is still wet on the stone, like gray batter. 

I draw a line four fingers wide across the joint: *Keep.*

***

Who am I in this?

I am not the builder. The cranes do not know I am here; they have no eyes, only mercury switches and counterweights that balance against the heaving of the bog. 

I am not the beast. The ash-fox that slips across the pipe-bridge above me doesn't look down; it knows by the heat-signature of my canvas coat that I am neither food nor a warm pipe to sleep on. We have an understanding based purely on displacement. I leave the trimmings of my tallow candle on the flange-steps; it leaves the carcass of a grease-rat outside my tool-pouch where the gristle won't foul my chalks.

I think I am the basin's margin-hand.

In old books—the kind the Scribe-Crews keep in tin map-cases under their bunkhouse stoves—there are notes written in the blank white space around the printed text. Small corrections. A word crossed out because the ink ran. A mark showing where the leaf was folded when the press came down.

That is where I live. In the damp white space between the machine's intention and the earth's refusal.

My fingernails are stained permanently with Prussian blue from the wax pencils. My knees have the shape of rivet-heads pressed into the bone. When I sleep in the crawlway, the low, hydraulic thrum of the floor doesn't wake me; it acts like the heartbeat of a mother to an unborn child. If the basin ever went quiet—if the iron stopped growing and the slate stopped swelling, if the draft was finally declared finished and the city settled into its final, dry stone—I would probably suffocate in the stillness.

But there is no danger of that today.

A new heave is coming from the south. I can taste the sulfur in the cold dew gathering on my sleeve. Beneath my boots, the box-girder begins to sing its muffled, low-C note, and three hundred yards away, an entire row of four-story boilers begins to rise slowly out of the horsetails, steaming like wet horses in the morning light.

I take the chalk out of its bladder-skin. I test the point with my thumb. Then I crawl forward toward the light to see what needs to be kept.
