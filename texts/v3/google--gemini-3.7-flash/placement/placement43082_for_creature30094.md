At first, there is only the sensation of two unyielding surfaces and the hot, oily film that keeps them from ever truly touching.

I am not in the open fall of the water, where the Gliders plane along the flat facets, nor am I down in the arterial troughs where the filaments trail like weed in a race. Those are spaces of transit. Where I find myself, there is no transit—only an immense, lateral reckoning.

I am wedged into the acute internal angle where three octagonal trunks meet the underside of a primary canopy-rib. 

It is an architectural notch where the geometries of the basin converge under thousands of tons of dead vertical mass. If this junction were left dry or vacant, the micro-torsion of the hydraulic rams miles below would cause the knife-edges of the lignified wood to bite into one another, creating friction, grinding dust, and eventually, the fatal slack of wear. 

I am the thing that occupies that angle. 

***

My body does not have a silhouette that can be viewed from the outside, because there is no outside to me; I have grown to occupy the exact negative space of the joint. My substance is a dense, laminated cartilage—neither entirely bone nor entirely muscle, but something like cold-vulcanized amber layered with sheets of copper-slick collagen. 

I do not have limbs, nor a head, nor an anterior direction. My anatomy is a three-flanged wedge, ten paces across, thickest at the core and tapering out to edges thinner than a dragonfly’s wing, which slip cleanly into the hairline seams between the machine-trunks.

My existence is measured in micro-radians of deflection.

When the deep subterranean rams stroke downward, the basin’s floor draws taut, and the canopy-rib above me presses down with an increase of twelve atmospheres. My core receives this load. I do not resist it by being rigid; I resist it by being incompressible. The force passes directly through my cellular lattice, converted instantly into a sudden, deep galvanic pulse that races along the copper veins of my outer rim and discharges into the vertical trunks, warming the descending water by a fraction of a millidegree.

When the stroke eases, my cartilage rebounds without a sound.

***

I am continually fed by what the junction squeezes out. The lignum sweats a heavy, metallic sap rich in tin esters and sulfurous lipids under this kind of torque; my skin, which is pitted with millions of hexagonal micropores, drinks this exudate directly under pressure. I require no mouth, no digestive tract, no lungs. The pressure itself forces the fuel through my membranes, where it is stripped of its charge and excreted along my lateral margins as a glassy, frictionless wax. 

This wax coats the wooden flutes, allowing the massive members of the canopy to flex against one another in complete, perpetual silence. I am the basin’s oiler, but I am also its gauge.

My consciousness—if it can be called that—is the consciousness of the joint.

I do not remember a beginning, because there is no delta in this place: no yesterday of lesser weight, no tomorrow of release. I know the basin not as a map, but as a distribution of vectors. I feel the distant shift of a Gasket-Beast expanding three leagues to the west as a minute relaxation along my left flange; I feel the passage of a Sleeve-Clamp a mile above as a tiny, rhythmic tick in the static charge of the wood.

In the absolute blackness, bathed in constant, warm condensation, there is no loneliness. Loneliness requires an edge where the self ends and an emptiness begins. Here, there is no emptiness. Every square millimeter of my skin is mated under absolute pressure to the living engine of the world. 

I am held so tightly that I cannot be removed without dismantling the ceiling of the basin itself. I am the silence between the beams, the balance of the load, the cold-pressed heart of the corner.
