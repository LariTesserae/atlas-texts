I would not be on the pine poles. The high scaffolding belongs to the reach of hunger that still remembers the sky—the hunger that reaches upward for wind-fall and moth-drift. 

I would be found lower, where the work of the draft finishes its fall.

To survive here without going mad from the shudder in the marrow, you cannot be suspended in the empty air between the walls. You must take the stone entirely into your side. 

I would be settled in the damp shelf directly beneath the terrace of the four-slab shelter—wedged into the narrow, tallow-slick seam where the deep peat meets the cold sill of the western wall. 

At first, I might seem only another lump of water-logged sod or a bundle of sheep-wool that slipped from the perches decades ago and never rotted away. But there is a slow, thick pulse beneath that pelt. My shape is low, wide, and flattened, like a river-boulder split along its bedding plane, without neck or high limbs to catch the downdraft. 

I have no use for sight; the gloom from the crack above does not reach this notch. My skin is the color of wet river-slate, thick as saddle-leather, entirely devoid of sweat glands, and covered in a dense, felted mat of fine, colorless hair that drinks the condensation before it can bead and run. 

Where the man on the perches must jut his jaw and press his teeth to the rock to understand the flue, my entire undercarriage is built of a broad, unsegmented plate of cartilage that rests flat against the limestone. Through this plate, I do not merely listen to the stone; I am anchored into its frequency. 

I feel the four-mile ice-lock when the winter freezes the dale above: the rock grows brittle, its hum rising an octave into a hard, metallic ring. I feel the subterranean torrent below when it swallows a collapse of gravel three chambers down, the shudder traveling up through the floor like a heavy, blunt mallet striking the foundations of the world. 

My lungs are small, dense bellows that take air only through narrow, slit-like spiracles tucked beneath my flanks, protected by filters of fine, wet moss-fleece. I breathe perhaps twice an hour. To draw in the air of this pocket any faster would be to drown in its mist or freeze the blood in the heart.

I am the beneficiary of his scrapings.

Every three or four days, when the heavy thud of his horn-soled feet descends the pine rungs, the vibration changes. His mass shifts from timber to stone. I feel the dull scrape of his ox-bone tool working the high baskets, and then the faint, descending drift of things that escape his fingers: flakes of dried moth-wing, crushed beetle hulls, stray seeds of alpine sorrel, and bits of frozen tallow that have cracked off his fleece mantle.

They fall down the vertical void, caught in the cyclonic spin of the white noise, until the dead air at the pocket’s margin lets them drop onto the moss shelf. 

I do not hunt. I extend thick, spade-shaped fore-limbs—fingers fused into broad, horn-tipped shovels—and draw the sodden drift into the warm, fermenting pocket beneath my belly. There, in the anaerobic grease where the moss rots against the living stone, I turn the debris into fat. 

My mouth is low and circular, lined with broad, flat grinding pads of yellow horn rather than enamel teeth, designed to crush sodden wood-rot, wet seed-husks, and the cold-stiffened carcasses of whatever has tumbled from the upper world. 

When the man enters the four-slab shelter to sleep, he burrows down into the heather. Through the four feet of fermenting peat that separates us, I feel the massive, slow displacement of his body. We share the only fractional degree of warmth in this entire throat of stone: the pale, sour heat of the rot. 

He does not know I am here as a creature; to him, the shelf beneath his bed is simply an anchor that does not shift. When the cold above becomes so absolute that his breath freezes in his beard before it can leave his lips, his heavy, lard-soaked mantle presses down harder into the peat, seeking the steam of the fermentation. 

Through the earth, my broad back meets that pressure. In the black, concussive din, where the torrent roars its meaningless language through the bones of our jaws, we exist like two stones at the bottom of a cold mill-race: unmoved, unhearing, holding the grease of the dead world between us until the dale above thaws or the limestone gives way.
