I would not be in the air, where the gliders trace the unseen scaffolding of the sky, nor would I be among the herds whose skins turn light into panic and direction. To run with them would mean being entirely exposed to the glare, constantly translating the horizon into violet and gold.

I would be lower down, settled into the seam where the systems meet.

I find myself in the shallow, stone-carved basins at the foot of one of the northernmost needle-towers, wedged into the precise groove where the weeping, light-devouring pillar roots into the apricot sandstone. 

Here, the world is an intense study in contradiction: the rock beneath me is soaked with the baked, radiant heat of a thousand uninterrupted middays, while just above, the black surface of the needle is so cold it makes the dry air ache. I live in that hairline crack of condensation and thermal balance—the exact millimeter where the freezing moisture drawn from the vacuum first touches the hot stone and turns to liquid film.

***

My body is not built for the rapid, hydraulic sprints of the hunters, nor the expansive sails of the gliders. It is low, broad, and radial, shaped like a river stone that has been planed flat on one side. 

My underside is a soft, continuous mantle of dense, vascular cilia that clings to the sandstone, drinking directly from the runnel’s slick before the water can warm and evaporate into the thin air. My upper surface is a heavy, segmented mosaic of dull, slate-colored chitin, layered with thin flakes of mica and quartz dust that I press into my own secretions. I am not reflective like the *lithophages*; I do not want to flash or converse. I have made myself into a piece of the basin’s lip, rough and heat-sink dark, indistinguishable from the basalt nodules embedded in the sandstone.

Because the air is soundless, I do not listen with ears. Instead, my entire perimeter is ringed with sensitive, fluid-filled ampullae that rest flat against the rock floor. 

Through the stone, the plateau is never truly silent. It rings. 

I feel the stutter-stalkers as brief, sharp impulses—a mechanical *click-click-click* traveling through the bedrock as their tendon-ratchets release and lock. I feel the collective, rhythmic whisper of three hundred lipid-padded feet when a herd drifts past fifty meters away, grazing the red cyanobacteria down to the bare grain. And beneath all of it, deeper than bone, I register the steady, sub-audible thrum of the needle-towers themselves—the deep tectonic pulse of ancient conduits miles below, siphoning the void and feeding the runnels.

My life is slow, measured by the filling and draining of the stone cups. 

In the mornings, when the sun is white and low, I shift slightly outward toward the light, catching the first fierce rays to warm my hemolymph. During the midday peak, when the sandstone begins to *tick* with thermal expansion, I back into the chilled boundary layer of the needle-tower. There, shrouded in the faint optical mirage of the tower’s condensation field, I am almost completely invisible. 

I feed on the silt of this world: the shed aragonite scales of the grazers that drift down the waterways, the brittle shed wings of expired mites, and the mineral-rich crust that precipitates wherever the distilled air-water meets the ancient stone. I am a cleaner of channels, a keeper of the runnel’s edge. If silt clogs the stone flutes, the water pools too deeply and boils away under the sun; by slowly scouring the rock with my rasping undersides, I keep the veins of the plateau open and clean.

Sometimes, a glider will stoop from the stratosphere, falling like a dropped blade to skim the basin beside me. For an instant, its shadow crosses me—a brief, icy plunge in temperature—and I feel the displaced air shudder across my shell. I watch its razor jaw part the water without a splash, rising back into the blinding apricot sky on an invisible column of lift. 

I do not envy the sky, nor the swift, chromatic herds. 

There is an immense, quiet dignity in being the fixture against which their movement is measured. Underneath the weight of the white sun, pressed between the freezing black tower and the warm stone, I am an anchor in the machinery. I remain where the water begins, cool and undisturbed, feeling the pulse of the world unwind its ancient, endless clock.
