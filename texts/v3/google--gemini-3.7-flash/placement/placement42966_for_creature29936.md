I would not be on the open walkways where the wind catches the long hemlock boards, nor up on the highest terraces where the apprentices dry the bitter moss in the blinding noon. 

I would be in the knee-crevices—the narrow, wet under-galleries where the first cedar sills meet the cold, glassy cleavage of the diorite. 

To be here is to exist entirely within the physics of friction and weight. I would be one of the wedge-keepers: someone whose body has grown wide and dense, whose knees have taken on the blue, bruised stiffness of the stone, whose purpose is neither to lift nor to carve, but to *seat*.

My hands would be the color of the silt in the flumes, grey-blue right down to the cuticle, the skin on the palms turned to horn from decades of checking raw edges. Across my forearms, the white tracks of old diorite slices would cross and recross like a surveyor’s map—scars that never quite fade in this thin, sun-scoured air.

My work is small, rhythmic, and heavy. I carry an apron of thick, boiled walrus hide stiffened with wax, its pockets weighted down with hand-split heartwood cedar shims, bone grease, copper pins, and an ash-root mallet whose head has been beaten flat and fibrous by ten thousand strikes. 

Where the mountain tries to swallow the timbers, or where the timber tries to spring away from the razor-faced stone, I am there in the seam. 

***

You do not hear the mountain with your ears here. The throat of the cleft is too full of the falling water; it roars like a mill race that never ends, an avalanche of wet sound that strips away your thoughts if you leave your head lifted to the sky. 

You hear it with your bones. 

I spend the long morning hours with my forehead pressed against the pale, unweathered rock. The stone is cold enough to leave an ache behind the eyes, and its surface is so sharp and true it feels like leaning against the flat of a giant’s cleaver. But through the bridge of the brow and the jawbone, the entire mass of the ridge speaks. 

It is still settling. The earth broke this cleft out of the deep crust only a heartbeat ago in the mountain’s time, and the rock is crowded with internal stress—vast, invisible lines of compression that have not yet decided where to vent. Through my skull, I feel the *tink-tink-ping* of micro-fractures blooming four hundred feet below, deep in the dark water-well. I feel the dull, brassy hum when an afternoon gust catches the north blade and tries to twist it against the valley’s root. 

When the stone hums sharp and high, like a plucked wire, I take a cedar wedge, grease its flank with tallow so it slides without splitting, and drive it two inches deeper between the joist and the bedrock. The mallet strike makes no sound against the roar of the water, but the vibration kicks back into the heel of my palm, solid and dull. The frame tightens. The timber accepts the mountain's weight for another season.

***

Around my boots, the life of the seam carries on without noticing me. 

The pale skinks crawl out from the dark voids behind the dry-stacked foundation blocks. They are cool and dry, their milk-white scales sliding across the greasy leather of my shins as they climb toward a bead of condensation gathered on an iron spike. Sometimes one will rest on my forearm while I am holding a timber steady, its tiny, needle-hook claws prickling against my scars, its throat pulsing with slow, cold blood. We share the same patience; neither of us can afford a sudden leap. 

Beneath the wet floorboards, the water-flumes clatter softly beneath the roar. The air smells of wet slate, the sharp green juice of liverwort crushed under my heels, and the sweet, resinous tang of boiled cedar bark. It is an astringent, clean world—nothing rots here, nothing grows soft. The water is so laden with lime and silica that the laces of my boots have petrified into pale, brittle cords that crack when I untie them.

***

When the sun finally dips behind the western blade, the light turns the blue diorite to the color of a bruised plum. The wind drops for an hour, falling away into a cold, downward draft that pulls the smell of birch-smoke out from the living terraces above.

That is when I climb out of the under-ribs.

I take my seat on the lowest cantilevered platform, where the drop beneath my hanging feet is nine hundred feet of sheer, unreadable shadow. My back rests against a main upright that I wedged twenty summers ago; I can feel its slow, familiar creak against my spine.

Across the three-hundred-pace gap of blue dusk, I see the yellow swing of a lantern. 

It dips twice, holds steady, then traces a slow arc to the left: *The south cable is singing.*

I lift my own lantern from the iron hook, swing it once low to the right, and bring it to my chest: *I have the mallet. Let it rest until dawn.*

Then I set the lantern between my feet, pull my hide apron over my knees, and press my cup of hot, chalk-bitter fern tea between my palms. In the darkening gorge, the water roars on, timeless and deafening, but in the wood at my back and the bone in my chest, everything is held fast. Everything is held tight against the stone.
