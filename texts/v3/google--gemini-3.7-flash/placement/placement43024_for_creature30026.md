At first, there is no boundary between my body and the weight of the water. 

There is only the compression—hundreds of fathoms of mountain runoff pushing down with the quiet, indifferent mass of a collapsing sky—and the low, hydraulic humming that lives inside the marrow of the rock. 

I do not begin with eyes. In this bruised indigo, light is not a tool for seeing; it is merely a measure of distance, a faint, cold stain that tells me how far away the open ceiling is. What I know of the world arrives through my flanks, through the broad, shivering planes of my skin that read the architecture of the current.

To my left, three miles up-drift, a terrace is spilling water like a burst dam. I feel the vortex before it has a shape: a sudden drop in density, a violent spiral of negative pressure that cuts across the avenue like a falling blade. 

I do not drift. To drift here is to be unmade.

My consciousness narrows down from the vast acoustic ringing of the plateau to four specific points of agony and anchor. Deep in the horizontal seam where two basalt megaliths grind against each other under the torque of the current, my lower limbs are wedged. They are not soft. They have forgotten how to crawl, forgotten the pulse of crawling. Over two hundred winters, the tissue has turned to dense, unyielding gristle, and the keratinized rings on my undersides have bitten into the micro-fissures of the stone until they are more mineral than bone. 

Beneath my grip, I feel the powdery grit of ancient grout-leeches—generations of crushed, iron-heavy carapaces compacted into the crack, acting as a greasy, leaden buffer between my flesh and the shearing rock. I am locked into the foundation. If the current took my mantle, it would tear my torso from my limbs before the hooks pulled free.

The water roars across my back. My skin has grown into an exact negative mold of the archway above me. Where the flume hits hardest, my mantle is calloused into a thick, cartilaginous shield, gray-white and pitted like pumice, deflecting the tearing down-drafts around my bulk so that they shear harmlessly into the dark avenue behind. 

I am the *pale mantle* of the eighth pylon, though I have no word for *eight*, and no word for *pylon*. I know only that this stone has a specific grain—a calcified, fossilized ring of ancient shell-rock that tastes of cold lime and iron—and that it rings in the key of low iron when the cleavers pass overhead.

Above me, the deep blue void trembles. 

*Clack. Clack. Thrummmmm.*

The percussive groan of a cleaver strikes the lintel fifty feet above my head. The sound travels through the basalt faster than it does through the freezing water; I feel the vibration rattle through my embedded hooks before the acoustic shockwave hits my skin. The beast is banking through the colonnade, thirty paces of upright, living slate, tilted on its side to shave the gale of the flume. It does not look down. It has no eyes to spare for the crevices. Its shadow clips the indigo twilight above me, vast and silent, and its passage drags a long, trailing wake that sucks at my trailing arms.

And then, in the turbulence of the leviathan’s wake, the drift delivers.

My upper four limbs, long and frayed like hemp ropes in the current, are extended into the roaring flume. Their sensory fringes taste the cold shear: the faint mineral snow of descending calcite, the sour musk of a gale-weaver colony that has snapped three pylons upstream, and then—the sharp, erratic flutter of panic.

A rib-fish.

It has been clipped by the cleaver’s hydrofoil, thrown out of its laminar glide, and spun sideways into the dead-water pocket beneath my arch. Its glass-needle spine flashes white as it tries to right itself against the cross-shear.

I do not deliberate. My body is a hydraulic spring that has been cocked for three centuries.

The trailing arm snaps shut not with the fluid grace of sea-life, but with the sudden, mechanical violence of a closing valve. The frilled suckers collapse around the fish’s flank, crushing its transparent keels against its ribcage. Before the vortex can seize us both, the limb coils backward into the dark, pulling the pale, spasming thing into the recess beneath the megalith.

The beak—black, heavy, chipped by a thousand meals of shell and bone—cleaves through the cold meat with a sound like a splitting pebble. 

The nourishment is cold. It tastes of mountain runoff, calcium, and void. It does not warm me; nothing is warm here. It merely replenishes the dense blubber of my mantle, feeding the slow, glacial growth of the callouses that keep the water from scouring me down to the bone.

When the meal is done, the current sweeps the fragments away in a fraction of a heartbeat. The avenue is clear again. The water roars on, polished, violent, and utterly empty.

I settle deeper into the seam. I feel the slow, imperceptible shift of the fifty-ton block resting its weight upon my anchored limbs—the plateau settling by another fraction of a millimeter as the eons wear its foundation flat. 

I will remain here until the stone forgets the arch, or until my hooks turn wholly to chalk. Until then, I am the keystone’s tongue. I am the tooth in the seam. I am the shape the water makes when it tries to hollow out the mountain.
