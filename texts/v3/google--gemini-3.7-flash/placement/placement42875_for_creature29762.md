The first thing to understand is that there is no falling asleep and no waking up, only the slow swelling and contracting of the warmth in the rock beneath my chest.

I am low to the stone. My weight is not something I carry; it is something I give entirely to the ironstone table, pressed flat through six broad pads whose microscopic velvet has grown so intimately into the granite’s pores that to move even three inches takes the work of an entire afternoon. The ironstone is hotter than blood, saturated with millennia of mid-latitude sun, and it hums upward through my belly-plates in a continuous, heavy pulse. 

I do not look out toward the horizon. My eyes are thick-lidded, milky with age, turned downward toward the small, shadowed space between my forelimbs. What I know of the world comes through pressure, through the tallow-warm vapor that bathes my sides, and through the slow wash of mineral water over the horn of my mouth.

On my left flank, the heavy, tiled rib-casing of another crawler presses hard against mine. We have leaned together like this for three turnings of the sedge-flowering—perhaps forty years, perhaps eighty. Where our carapaces grind against each other in the slow swell of breathing, the stone-hide has worn flat and polished, like two river boulders settled into the same pocket of gravel. I know the rhythm of its spiracles before my own: the long intake, the silence where the damp sits in the lung-chambers, and then the wet, soft *shuck* as it exhales a plume of warm, fatty mist that condenses across my shoulder.

My back is not smooth. It is an old, terraced roof of mineralized bone, heavy as a dry-laid hearth, crusted thick with orange lichens that tickle faintly when the mist-breeze lifts. Down the center runs a wide, shallow furrow, worn smooth by decades of rain. 

Now, as the copper light begins to slant through the air, the mist gathers on my shell. 

It does not fall; it simply appears, drop by drop, swelling in the lichen-beards until it breaks free and trickles down the carved gutter of my spine. I feel it moving forward, inch by cold, clean inch over the heated plates of my back, gathering dust, spore-meal, and the fine chalk-residue of the fleas grazing my flanks. When it reaches the clefts beside my lower jaw, the sponges drink it in. My tongue—heavy, broad, surfaced with endless rows of blunt, horn rasps—shifts forward with a wet scrape against the granite floor. 

I scrape. The rock yields a faint, salty grease: the dried bloom of blue-green crust that grew in the midday damp, mixed with the iron-dust of the plain. It tastes of cold metal and old time.

Above me, there is a sudden, dry rustling—like dry husks stirred in a basket. A Ribbon-Flit has dipped down from the thermal tier, its long, shimmering tail brushing across the crest of my shell as it grazes the edge of a drifting mist-bell. For a moment, one of the bell’s trailing gossamer threads drapes across my milky eye. It is weightless, cool, and smelling of sweet methane. It lingers there until the convective draft lifts it away again, spiraling softly up into the copper haze.

A presence settles at my front right corner. It is not another sleeper. The motion is deliberate, rhythmic, possessing a quiet, angular purpose.

A Tender has arrived. 

I feel the blunt, three-fingered claspers wedge beneath a loose wedge of shale beside my shoulder. The Tender does not hurry; its breath vents against my neck in long, steady sighs that smell of wet slate. With a dry, grinding heave, it lifts the stone, holds it against its broad chest, and turns it until the grain catches the prevailing drift. Then it lays it back down, snug against my forward limb, sealing a gap where the mist-water was draining away too fast into a crevice. 

When the stone is set, the Tender leans its broad chin against the edge of the new wall, right beside my head. It rubs. Back and forth, back and forth, working the oily, waterproofing wax of its throat into the raw rock with a steady, soothing vibration that rattles pleasantly through my skull-plates. We share that space for an hour: my tongue scraping the film from the granite basin, its chin polishing the curb that holds the water in.

I am not young. Deep beneath my shell, in the hollow beneath my hindquarters where the limbs tuck in, the blind mites have already begun to gather in the folds of my soft skin. They do not bite; they merely wait. 

I know that in some season to come, my pads will cease their three-inch creep. My spiracles will part for the last time, letting out their warm plume into the communal hum of the swale, and they will not draw closed again. The mites and the grubs will do their slow, quiet work beneath the vault, and my hide will settle, inch by inch, until the bone plates rest flat upon the granite. The lichens will cover the runnels of my back completely. The Tenders will come, testing the rim of my empty shell with their blunt forelimbs, and they will nudge two smaller stones into the gaps between my ribs so that the dew gathers deeper in the hollow where my heart once sat.

The crawlers behind me will press forward, resting their heavy chins upon my ridge to drink the clean water from my hollowed back, wearing the dead bone to the same glass-smooth polish as the walls that snake across the plain.

The mist is thick now. The air smells of tallow, damp lichen, and the vast, slow digestion of a million bodies. I draw the warm air into my sides until my plates creak softly, hold it while the water trickles forward down my spine, and let it go into the quiet.
