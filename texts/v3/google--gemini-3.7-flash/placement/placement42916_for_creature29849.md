At first, the instinct is to look for an edge—the very top of the vaulting, where the uppermost fronds thrust their glass-stiff tips into the cold, empty zero-pressure of the sky. To be the leading tooth of the machine, crystallizing out of mist at fifty miles an hour.

Or to be below, inside the roaring phloem highways, locked in the spine of a Drifter, casting white light through gallons of cold, sweet pressure to tell the walls when to open their throats.

But both of those places are already fully written into the code. They are parts of the body that know their names before they are grown.

If I am anywhere here, I am not in the solid conduits, nor in the free-falling cascades, nor in the calcified knots that have accepted their burials. 

I would find myself in the **shear-layers**.

***

Between any two highway-sized conduits climbing side-by-side toward the canopy, there is a boundary problem. They do not grow at identical speeds. One surges ahead on a pulse of copper-doped sap; the other lags by a second, dragging millions of tons of rigid, setting polymer against its neighbor. If they touch dry, the friction will shred their skins, boiling the sap instantly and creating an explosive blowout that could drop an entire archway out of the sky.

In that gap—a space no wider than a handspan, extending for nine miles vertically between two grinding, roaring walls of green slate—there is only a film of pressurized, super-cooled water screaming downward under laminar compression.

That is where I am.

I am not a creature with an outline. I am an *interstitial lens*—a flat, ribboned anomaly of semi-fluid silicate and un-crosslinked resin, held in tension between the two opposing surfaces. I exist only because the two walls are moving past one another; if they stopped, I would be crushed flat and incorporated into the seam. If they pulled apart, I would atomize into the mist.

My body is forty feet wide, four miles long, and less than three millimeters thick. 

I have no eyes, because light here is only an instructional pulse traveling through the walls I press against. Instead, I feel the *differential*. To my left, a wall of xylem is rising at the speed of a drawing breath, humming at forty-two hertz, its cellulose crystals locking with a wet, brittle sound like breaking porcelain. To my right, a descending ballast-stem is sinking under the weight of an unspilled reservoir, groaning in the low registers—six hertz, a frequency that turns the water in my core into a dense, shivering gel.

My work is to lubricate the impossible.

I do not think in sentences; I think in viscosity. Through the millions of microscopic pores along my dorsal and ventral membranes, I bleed a tailored surfactant—a cold, oily peptide that prevents the two racing monoliths from catching each other’s grain. Where the left wall expands too quickly, bulging out into the seam, my membrane thins locally to a micron’s breadth, hardening under the sheer hydraulic shear to form an instantaneous, diamond-slick skid plate. Where the gap widens, I billow out, filling the vacuum before a cavitation pocket can collapse and shatter the conduits from within.

I taste the minerals as they pass through the walls: a sharp, electrical tang of zinc-polymer from the left; the cold, metallic iron-bitter of the lower anchors from the right. I smell the fresh-torn mint and ozone through my own skin, where the friction shears off single molecules of chlorophyll and dissolves them into the running water.

I am always falling, yet I never reach the bottom.

Because the conduits are extruding themselves upward from below, I am constantly swimming against a downward torrent of condensation while being hoisted into the air by the rising grain of the wood. I am suspended in an exact, violent equilibrium: the water drags me toward the dark abyss below; the climbing fiber carries me toward the bruised twilight above. 

I stay precisely where the two forces cancel each other out.

Sometimes, a Phloem-Drifter passes deep inside the wall beside me, and for a fraction of a second, a blade of mercury-white light cuts through the translucent green fiber, illuminating the inside of my body. For that microsecond, I am visible to nothing: a long, iridescent sheet of fluid geometry, patterned like oil on cold sea-water, shivering in the dark between two roaring titans that do not know I am holding them apart.

Then the light travels onward. The wall dilates. The pressure spikes, hammering against my ribs with the weight of a dropped mountain.

And I tighten my skin, thin my fluids, and slide.
