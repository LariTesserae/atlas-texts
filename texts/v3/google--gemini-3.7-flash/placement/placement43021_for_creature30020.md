I would not be at the top, where the upland sea tilts over its lip, nor down in the sunless sump where the Basin-Cows lie choked in drift. 

I would be wedged in the mid-reach: **Intake Sub-Four, on the western rib of the High Flume.** 

I am a Sluice-Tender—specifically a *Gate-Listener*—and I live in the dry hollow behind the arc of the third cataract, where forty thousand tons of gray water peel off a granite lip every second and vault clear into the air, leaving a roaring, negative-pressure cavern the size of a cathedral between the cliff and the falling wall.

***

My home is an iron cylinder, eight feet across, slung by twelve-inch forged turnbuckles directly beneath the overhang. It hangs there like a drowned boiler, sweating oil and condensation, trembling so hard that salt won’t stay in a dish; it dances into tiny, concentric rings on the iron table until you press your thumb into it.

I am forty-two seasons into the Chasm. My eardrums ruptured during my second winter on the grates, when a pneumatic pipe ruptured at eighty atmospheres, but I am not in silence. The world here is not quiet; it is *dense*. It comes into me through my skeleton. 

Every morning begins not with light—light is only a shifting, milky glare filtered through twelve feet of green, solid fall—but with the **Bite**.

I have an ash-wood dowel, three inches long, capped with brass and notched to fit my left rear molars. I sit on the edge of my bunk, clamp the wood between my teeth, and press the brass cap hard against the main anchoring lug of the Sluice-Four bearing housing.

When the wood connects, the mountain pours into my jaw.

It is a three-part chord:
1. *The Low Floor:* The deep, granite grind of the world turning over—the bottom basins taking the vertical miles of water. It is a subsonic hum that vibrates the base of my skull, steady as a heartbeat.
2. *The Turbine Mesh:* A sharp, oily whirr that buzzes in the bridge of my nose. If the third bearing is running hot, the buzz gets thin and sour, like chewing tinfoil. Today it is thick, heavy, running cool on river-fat and grease.
3. *The Choke:* A sporadic *thud-clack* that rattles the crowns of my teeth. Driftwood. A water-cedar trunk, heavy as lead, caught on the upper trash-rack fifty feet above my head.

I spit the dowel into my palm. It is time to go out.

***

Dressing takes twenty minutes. The wool underneath is never dry; it has the sour, comforting smell of mutton grease and cold cellar mold. Over that goes the vulcanized canvas suit, stiff as cured leather until the body’s heat softens the knees and armpits. I wrap my wrists in lead-weighted tape, wind the copper wire around my ankles to seal the cuffs against the updraft, and step into the iron-shod boots.

The boots weigh sixteen pounds each. In the Chasm, light feet mean you fly; if you fly, you die against the lower rocks before you have time to draw a breath. You need the mass of a stone to stay where you put yourself.

I check my tether—three-ply hemp, soaked in Stockholm tar until it is black and sticky as licorice—and unlatch the pressure-hatch.

The air in the spray shadow smells of crushed ferns, ozone, and wet iron. It is twenty degrees colder than the bunkhouse. The draft tries to pull you forward into the gray belly of the cataract; the suction behind a falling waterwall is like an open lung, always inhaling. 

I drop to all fours immediately. Out here, on the slick gneiss shelf, two legs are an arrogance.

My hands—white, ribbed like the belly of a perch from forty years in the water—find the iron guide-rail bolted along the rock flume. Between the bolts, the chape-limpets have formed thick, horn-plated crusts. I don't scrape them away; their rough, keratin shells give my palms something to bite into. A cluster of weep-frogs sits in the groove beneath the rail, pale as peeled garlic. As my glove approaches, they don’t jump; they flatten themselves into the slime, vibrating their tiny hind legs against the iron—*tat-tat-tat-tat*—warning their neighbors three ledges down that the heavy thing is moving.

I reach the intake grate. 

The water here is not falling; it is running horizontally down a parabolic chute, forty miles an hour, six inches deep, smooth as a sheet of black plate glass until it hits the iron teeth of the trash-rack. It looks solid enough to walk on, but if you dipped an arm in, it would strip the flesh from the radius like a butcher’s knife.

A dwarf pine is wedged across Vanes Three and Four. It is five hundred pounds of wet, iron-hard wood, shuddering violently under the shear-force of the flow. If the water wedges it deeper, the intake will starve; if the intake starves, Turbine Two will overspeed and throw its brass runners through the granite casing.

I hook my secondary tether to the overhead safety cable—the steel wire singing a high, cold *ping* against my carabiner—and swing the long spruce-pike off my shoulder.

This is the work. It is not heroic; it is leverage and timing.

You do not fight the river; the river has millions of years of momentum and doesn't know you are there. You watch the rhythm of the shudder. The tree is trapped, but the water comes in pulses—waves created by the wind shearing the upper lips miles above.

*Wait. Wait.*

The pulse hits. The water rises two inches, lifting the butt of the pine just enough to break the friction weld of the current.

I throw my whole weight—canvas, lead tape, iron boots, sixty years of meat and bone—onto the end of the pike, wedging the bronze chisel-tip beneath the root ball.

The wood groans through the pike’s shaft, into my palms, up through my collarbone. For half a second, the mountain and I are holding the same lever.

Then the current takes the bite. The pine doesn't slip; it *explodes* upward as the intake clears, rolling over the grate with a wet, splintering crack that I feel in my shins, and then it is gone—shot out into the empty air behind the falls, tumbling two thousand feet into the mist below.

The water flattens out again. Black glass. The intake breathes a deep, sucking sigh that pulls the spray-curtains tight against the iron grates.

***

I sit back on my heels on the wet shelf, tethered short, and rest my forehead against the cold iron of the intake housing.

Around me, the mist-shearers are coming home to the chimneys. They whip through the spray-wall with their wings tucked back like pocketknives, greasy feathers shedding the gray mist in silver beads, settling into the warm, oily crevices where the turbine bearings bleed their mechanical heat into the rock. One lands three feet from my boot, its black eye horn-shielded, a half-chewed char tail hanging from its beak. It doesn't look at me; we are both just things that have figured out how to stay wedged in the cracks of the machine.

Below my iron boots, five miles down through the black gneiss, the deep basin hums. The water is falling. It has been falling since the mountains were sharp; it will be falling when the iron grates have turned to red clay and my bones are smooth as river gravel in some blind cow’s belly.

I put the ash dowel back between my teeth, bite down, and listen.

The bearing is cool. The mesh is true. The mountain is steady.

I unclip my tether and begin the crawl back toward the iron cylinder, keeping low to the stone, where the friction is least and the grip is sure.
