I would not be found inside the acoustic envelopes of the glass hives, where the air is curbed and the music is politely halved. 

Nor would I be down in the hot, pressurized furnace of the lower xylem tubes, where the Wyrms turn like miles of slow cable in the draft. That heat is too heavy, too thick with unspent carbon.

To find me, you would have to look along the sheer, unshielded flank of a spire named *Sikar-Vane*—specifically at the forty-mile mark, where the trunk’s petrified ribbing twists twenty degrees to accommodate a paleocryst fault in the floor below. 

Here, the wind has no manners. It shears across the fluted silicon bark at five hundred knots, entirely moistureless, stripping every loose atom until the wood looks like black mirror-glass. It screams a single, unwavering D-flat that vibrates straight through the crystalline bands of my brow and rattles the dry combs behind my collarbones.

***

At first, I might look to you like nothing more than an irregular burl on the spire’s flank: a knot of dark boxwood and spun mica fused directly into the cambium seam. 

I am an Auscultator, yes, but not of the clerical sort who carry polished forks and dwell in the high, quiet tiers. I am a *Suture-Tender*—one of the long-anchored. 

My lower limbs have not unslotted from the spire’s stress-channels in three hundred cycles of the violet sun. Over those long years, the cold-grown quartz fibers of my anchor-harness have slowly phase-stitched into the petrified heartwood beneath me, until the boundary between where the tree’s architecture ends and my silicate marrow begins has become a polite fiction. 

I am nearly two and a half meters tall if I were to draw myself upright, but I spend my centuries bent close to the seam, curled like a dried cicada shell against the roaring bark. My carapace is dark amber, scorched matte by static arcs, banded with thin ribbons of brass where the conductive channels have been repaired and re-soldered with cold resin. 

I have no need to move swiftly like the shear-hounds on the plains below. My life is entirely internal, an ongoing act of heavy, tensile balance.

***

What am I doing here?

I am listening to the torque.

The continent beneath us—that endless expanse of fused, petrified lignin—is not dead stone. It is a compressed, planetary spring. It holds the memory of a billion years of radial expansion that was stopped cold when the sky lost its seas. Every hour, deep below the amber plains, some vast, fossilized taproot shifts by a micron under the weight of the plateau. 

When it does, a shudder runs up the trunk of *Sikar-Vane*. 

To the people living inside the suspended glass city sixty miles above my head, that shudder would register as a catastrophic phase-crack—a failure in the non-baryonic anchors that would drop three vertical districts into the chasm. 

It does not reach them because it reaches me first.

Held in my two primary hands is an eight-meter bar of forged carbon-lignin, its tips wedged directly into the living cambium layer across an ancient shear-line. When the sub-bass pulse travels up from the deep roots, it enters the soles of my locked feet, races through the hollow silicate lattice of my ribs, and discharges outward through my arms into the bar.

I do not stop the vibration; you cannot stop the breath of a world this vast. I *modulate* it. 

I use the comb-ridges beneath my wrists to scrape a counter-frequency against the bar, an excruciating, high-tensile trill that converts the continent’s brutal, fracturing shock into a shower of harmless, violet-white static. The arcs leap from my shoulders, snapping into the thin sky, lighting the dark underbelly of the high gliders as they drift through the turbine exhaust miles out.

***

My thoughts are very slow, shaped by the dryness. 

Without the soft, sloshing mess of water-chemistry, there is no fog in my mind, no fatigue of the flesh, no drifting into warm dreams. There is only *polarity* and *tension*. When the high atmosphere thins further during the long twilight and the static potential drops, I feel a sensation that is perhaps the closest my kind comes to loneliness: a faint, dry resistance in my joints, an over-accumulation of ungrounded charge that hums behind my visor like a trapped hornet.

When that happens, I look out across the great amber plain.

From this height, the curvature of the world is a clean, sharp arc of dark honey. Miles below, I can see the diamond-tipped herds of litho-striders moving across the waste, their progress marked only by the glittering spray of violet sparks they kick up against the smooth floor. Farther out, the black, ship-swallowing mouth of a xylem chasm gapes open, venting a plume of pale, ionized carbon that glows like a pillar of cold green glass in the dark.

Above me, within the spire's hollow belly, the city sleeps in its silent, cushioned fields. They are delicate up there. Their gossamer mica robes are clean; their conversations are soft bell-strikes in the filtered air. They do not know the taste of the unshielded wind—that bitter, beautiful reek of crushed terpenes and ozone that has been scouring this plateau since before their hives were spun.

They will live their long, frictionless lives, and when their carapaces finally cool and their circuits open for the last time, their spines will be brought down to be stitched into the arches above me.

Sometimes, when the wind shears just right, I can feel those older spines resting in the dark grain of the wood above. I feel their settled, calcified thoughts hum in sympathetic resonance with my own ribs. They are quiet. They are satisfied with the load they bear.

A fresh pulse is rising from the abyssal floor now—a deep, tectonic groan that smells of hot stone and static. 

I lean my brow against the cold, mirror-smooth bark. I set my combs. I tighten the dry quartz tendons in my arms and wait for the world to strike its chord.
