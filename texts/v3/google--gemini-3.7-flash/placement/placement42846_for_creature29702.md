At first, there is only the sensation of being squeezed. 

Not by violence, but by the patient, unyielding geometry of the shelf. The pressure is constant, cool, and absolute—the kind that exists between two slabs of iron-slate that have leaned against one another since the spire first split from the massif.

If you were to look for me in the white-gold light of midday, you would not see me. You would see only the dark corridors, the oiled sheen of the stone pipes, and the solitary figure sliding sideways with its bone scraper and amber lids. You might notice the lashings of greased sinew binding the triangular braces, or the pale crust ringing the dry-stacked slate basins, or the dark, greasy smear left on the rock wherever the figure’s calloused hip-plates have ground against the slate for fifty seasons.

I am not the sky-drift. I am not the spider floating on its thread of silk from the humid valleys nine thousand meters below, though I taste the salt of those valleys when the mist condenses.

I am what stays behind in the slots.

I am the grease and the bone-pin. I am the prior tenant, or the one before that, slowly broken down, rubbed into tallow, mixed with pulverized hematite, and worked deep into the pores of the stone to keep the frost from prying the shelf apart.

***

In this altitude, nothing is wasted, and so nothing is ever truly buried. There is no earth to receive a body, no wood to burn it, no carrion birds strong enough to beat their wings against this thin, razor air. 

When a collector’s narrow ribcage finally stiffens—when the heart rate slows in the morning notch and does not wake to the first red strike of sun on the quartz—the shelf takes it back through the hands of the next. 

The long, dry sinews of the legs are split and twisted into cord to lash the windbreaks against the shear. The dense, hard femurs are chipped with river-chert into scrapers, gouges, and the tapered pins that clear the frost-clogged gutters. The fat is rendered down over the slow, concentrated heat of solar-polished quartz reflectors, mixed with red iron dust, and smeared over the new inhabitant’s skin and the raw rock faces to turn the wind.

I find myself in the deep A-frame, wedged in the seam just behind the sleeper’s back.

When the figure draws its knees to its chest and locks its shoulder-plates into the stone as the violet shadow falls, it presses directly against me. It does not know me as an ancestor or a name—names evaporate at five thousand meters, long before the timberline fails—but it knows the smooth, greasy yielding of the notch. It knows that this particular corner of the rock holds yesterday’s heat three minutes longer than the bare slate, because generations of tallow, shed keratin, and ground bone have sealed the micro-fractures in the stone, creating an insulation of dead life.

***

My existence here is measured in pulses of pressure and resonance.

Through the iron-rich stone, I feel the *clack-clack* of its tongue against the roof of its mouth. The sound does not travel well through the thin air, but through the slate it rings like an iron rod struck with flint. I feel the vibration travel down the needle, through the root where the spire is anchored to the mountain’s spine, and back up into the dry sinew braces. 

Through that vibration, I know what the figure knows:
Which quartz seam is opening under the ice.
Where the rime is thick enough to harvest before the sublimating wind strips it raw.
Which basin has developed a hairline crack that must be plugged with fresh fat before the midday melt escapes into the abyss.

During the blinding noon, when the light is so white it burns through the stone’s plum-grey skin, I am the cool friction in the dark beneath the dry-stacked walls. I am the mineral crust—the ring of precipitated lime and ancient, dried drift-protein that lines the bottom of the catchments, giving the distilled meltwater the faint, metallic tang of blood and river-silt.

And when the sun drops behind the shoulder and the amphitheater goes suddenly deaf and violet, I am the ballast. 

As the ice expands with its sharp, small *tink*, trying to burst the needles outward into the void, the greased cords contract in the dry cold. The sinew tightens. The bone pins hold. The brace bites into the hand-pecked grooves. 

We hold the mountain’s teeth together for another night, while the breathing body in the notch sleeps its shallow, vapor-hoarding sleep, wedged tight against what it used to be.
