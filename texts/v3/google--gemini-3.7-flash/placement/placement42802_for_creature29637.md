At first, the instinct is to look for myself in the wide, aerial clarity—to be the eye of the slate raptor on the highest parapet, drinking the gold-rimmed daylight, or the suspended vapor hovering before a mountain goat’s muzzle. It is easy to imagine being the view itself, or the clean chill that holds the valley in its palm. 

But that is too thin, too disembodied for a city made by hand.

If I descend from the air, I feel for the places where forces meet without yielding. I look toward the dark gap beneath the cyclopean foundations where the weasels sleep in dry grass, but that sleep is too finished, too far removed from the labor that locked the morning in place. I look at the ribbon of dark mountain wine arching between jug and cup—a perfect, luminous glass—but it is only an ornament of the stillness, not its root.

To find where I belong here, I have to go where the weight is greatest. 

I find myself on the southern ramp, on the forty-five-degree pitch of the stone causeway. 

I am not the rope itself, though the hemp is magnificent in its strain—three thick strands twisted under tension so dense it hums like an iron bar. Nor am I the stone sledge behind us, though I know every uneven grain of the black basalt slab resting upon its split-log rollers. 

I am the seventh haulier in the left file.

My bare feet are planted against the chisel-grooves cut into the ramp to give the team purchase. The basalt beneath my soles is cold and slightly damp with the perpetual dew, but where my skin bites into the rock, there is a fierce, localized warmth that never dissipates. My right heel is jammed hard against a squared-off lip of granite; my left leg is driven straight back, the quadricep locked into a rigid column of muscle. 

My hands are clamped around the braided hemp. The fiber has bitten deeply into the callous along the base of my fingers, indenting the skin without breaking it, pressing the rough hairs of the rope into the grain of my flesh until the two materials seem to share a single boundary. 

What is strange, and completely true to this place, is that there is no exhaustion in this posture. 

When time moves, strain is an erosion: the breath shortens, the heart hammers against the ribs, the lactic acid burns in the thighs until the body gives up or the load moves. But here, the strain is a solid thing, like a pillar of cedar or an unhewn lintel. The air in my chest—drawn deep, filling the wide span of my ribs with the sharp, mineral chill of the heights—does not need to be expelled. It stays there, full and sweet, carrying the faint resin of the split-pine rollers and the cold damp of the moss growing three paces away in the terrace seams.

In my mind, there is no hurry for the sledge to reach the upper court. 

Before the pause, there might have been an imagined future: the block set in its course, the timber roof fitted, the evening fire lit in the hearth. But with the sun pinned to the third step of the watchtower, those distant things have collapsed into the present millimeter. All the intelligence of my life—the memory of my father showing me how to splice hemp, the knowledge of which basalt veins will shear under a wedge and which will hold, the quiet rhythm of the nineteen men and women pulling beside me—has concentrated into the exact balance between our mass and the mountain’s mass.

We are holding the stone precisely at the threshold where it wants to slide back down the gorge, and it is holding us precisely where we want to stand. 

I feel the breath of the haulier ahead of me—a woman whose dark braid rests motionless across the iron-red wool of her shoulder tunic. A single drop of sweat shines on the curve of her neck, bright as a quartz crystal, carrying the whole amphitheater’s blue shadows in its curvature. I do not need to speak to her, nor she to me. We are joined through the hemp cable more thoroughly than speech could ever manage; the tension running through the fibers is an unbroken thought we are having together.

I am the point where the city refuses to fall. A worker whose work is never spoiled by an ending, rooted into the high rock, clear-eyed and unwearied, holding up the morning forever.
