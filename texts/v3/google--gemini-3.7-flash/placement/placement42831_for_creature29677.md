I would not be found on the main transit decks where the hopper trains run their inverted circuits, nor in the high roosts where the ribbon-wings multiply their broods across four bays at once. 

You would find me lower down, in the damp throat of a lateral shear-vent, where the cold air smells intensely of wet zinc and green tallow. 

At first, you might not distinguish me from the framing. In this light, anything that remains still for more than an hour gathers the continent’s condensation until it glazes over: droplets hanging from the hem of my copper-wool tunic, beaded on the grease-rubbed leather of my thigh-guards, resting in the small horizontal groove where my amber visor meets the bridge of my nose. 

I am a *pitch-tender*, though the title is older than the steel we sit upon and means very little now. Mostly, I am an ear pressed against the iron.

***

I live where the continent forgets its plumb-lines. 

My camp is pitched in the hollow crux of a four-hundred-ton gusset plate. It is a quiet angle, sheltered from the flat, blinding downdrafts that peel off the upper gantries. Two layers of ribbon-wing vellum, cured with cold turbine oil until they are as supple and amber-dark as smoked bladder, are stretched across the V of the truss with tension wire. Inside, it is no wider than the spread of my elbows, but it is dry, and the steady, sub-audible churning of a subterranean flywheel six levels below keeps the floor-flange just warm enough that my drinking water does not crust with needle-ice while I sleep.

To wake here is to adjust to the grain of the morning’s stress.

Before I push the visor over my eyes, I reach out with bare fingers—calloused, blackened at the cuticles by graphite paste—and lay my palms flat against the web of the beam. 

I do not look at the sky; the glare through the vent-mouth is already an ache against the lids. Instead, I listen to the continent's waking tension through the skin of my forearms. 

First comes the long, slow sigh of the thermal creep—the eastern quadrant expanding as the pale sun strikes its millions of milled flanges, sliding along the oil-ways with a shudder so deep it registers not as sound, but as an ache in the teeth. Then, the higher, brittle chatter of the carapace-beetles grazing the morning frost off the channel edges directly above my head, their millions of ceramic mandibles working in dry, syncopated waves like rain on tin.

Only when I know the register of the day do I step out onto the catwalk.

***

My work is small, slow, and repetitive. I carry a satchel woven of copper-thread, heavy with brass drift-pins, a leather roll of balance-shims, and three striking-hammers of varying densities: one of soft lead, one of turned bronze, and a five-inch rod of cold-drawn tool steel with a notched grip.

I walk the lines where the geometries are beginning to fray.

Because the megastructure is young, its impossible folds are not static; they breathe. A bay that was ninety degrees yesterday might lean by half a millimeter by noon, pinching a hydraulic line or widening a shear-gap until the suspended iron filings begin to drift out of their magnetic arches like gray snow. 

Where the gap widens, I do not bridge it with heavy iron—the stress would simply shear the bolts like dry celery. I tune it. 

I set my brass hammer against the edge of an unjoined truss and tap twice. *Ting-clink.* 

The note is bright, carrying out into the silent, open-lattice chasm. I wait. Five miles away, through a spatial fold that makes the distance look like an arm's reach through a glass pane, a tapper family knocks back from inside a pressurized conduit: a dull, rhythmic *thump-thump-thump* that tells me the pressure in the glycol line is holding, but the casing is bowing under the draft.

I reach into the gap between the two beams—into that five-foot pocket where space has folded itself into a knot. 

This is the part of the trade that makes the knees hollow if you think of it too long. When I extend my right arm into the empty air between the trusses, my hand does not appear on the other side. Instead, through the slit of my visor, I can see my own grease-stained fingers emerging from a vent-throat eighty yards below, turned sideways, reaching out of the solid plate to adjust a small bronze balance-weight on a guide-cable. 

I work by the feel of the resistance. When the counterweight settles into its notch, the tension across the void resolves. The suspended filings between the girders snap back into a tight, shimmering curve, humming with the static load they carry, and the hopper tracks above me settle with a single, massive *thud* that rings down through the lattice like a cathedral bell.

***

I am old enough now that my skin has taken on the slate-gray pallor of the damp zinc, and the knuckles of my left hand are permanently thickened from thirty seasons of reading cold vibrations through bare bone. 

Sometimes, while sitting on the edge of the gusset plate waiting for a thermal slide to pass, the hinge-striders will come out of the shadows beneath the flange. They do not fear me; to their caliper-eyes, my motionless legs are merely another grade of rolled alloy wrapped in salted membrane. They will climb over the toe of my boot with dry, mechanical clicks, their fibrous suction pads peeling from the leather with the faint sound of tearing silk, hunting the midges that hatch in the standing puddle between my heels.

I like their company. They are precise. They waste no motion on doubt.

When the time comes—when the lenses of my eyes have yellowed too deeply to read the temper-colors on the fresh cuts, or when my hands lose the quickness needed to pull back from a closing fold—I will not leave this corner of the shear-vent.

I will take off my copper-wool vest, lay my hammers in their neat row against the web of the beam for whoever finds the draft next, and climb down into the unjoined gap where the arches of metal dust bridge the weight of the quadrant.

I will step into that quiet, magnetic stillness where the sun does not burn and the water never drips. I will let the continent’s long, slow harmonic take the mass of my bones, pulling it out into an iridescent line of salt and iron across the empty air—holding the five-hundred-ton truss square against the wind, keeping the angle true for another thousand leagues of quiet, unrusting steel.
