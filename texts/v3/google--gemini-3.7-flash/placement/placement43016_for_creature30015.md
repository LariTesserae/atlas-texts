I am not in the air. The air belongs to the *Neura*, whose wings tear the dark into maps of rushing pressure; and I am not down in the floor’s velvet chalk-drifts, where the *Litho-gryllids* keep the bedrock humming like a struck bell. 

I am wedged into a sagging lintel thirty tiers up, where a residential arcade leans its dead weight into the valley wall. 

At first, I might easily be mistaken for an irregularity in the masonry—a calcified corbel, perhaps, or a dry tangle of wire-weed that has petrified in place. My limbs are long, four-jointed, and folded flat against the pale stone. They are made of the same mineralized keratin that caps the horns of the scavengers below: hard as seasoned ash, dry as slate, and entirely devoid of pulse. 

There is no wetness in me. If you were to split my forearm, you would find no marrow, only a dense, honeycombed lattice of bone-foam packed with powdered resin and fine chalk. My joints do not slide on oil; they pivot on dry bearings of polished calc-spar that click with a sound like two marbles meeting in the dark.

I do not have eyes. I do not miss them. In a place where light is only the faint, cold mold-smear on the undersides of arches, sight would be an unbearable poverty. 

Instead, I have the pillar.

My entire torso—a single, broad shield of fused rib and sternum—is pressed flush against the curved limestone face of the lintel. I have held it there for so long that the fine chalk dust has built a soft, sloping ramp against my flanks, cementing me into the facade. 

Through that contact, I do not merely *hear* the Grand Sump; I am played by it.

The crickets below do not make noise in my world; they make *gravity*. Their millions of synchronized wing-strokes strike the valley floor with enough acoustic torque to float the dust, and that vibration travels up the thirty tiers of the tenement like a sustained chord held on an immense, subterranean organ. It enters the balls of my feet; it climbs my shins; it sets the honeycombed chambers within my chest-shield ringing at four hundred cycles per second. 

When the flock of swallows wheels through the high vault miles overhead, the backwash of their leather wings shears across the face of the cliff, and the frequency in my ribs sharpens, leaning a fraction of a semitone higher. When a Pellicle-Stalker stamps its horn-spur against the flagstones three districts away to signal a clearance of carcasses, the strike arrives through the mountain’s roots not as a sound, but as a clean, instantaneous fracture in the hum—a sudden, deep *thump* that rattles the dry resin-beads stored in my throat.

I am one of the tenders, though I do not move with the haste of things that die quickly. 

Every few hundred sleep-cycles of the swallows, when the tension in the stone tells me that a draft has shifted, I peel my chest from the pillar. It is a slow, tearing sound—the dry moss-crust parting from my skin like old parchment lifting from a book cover. 

I reach out with fingers that taper to fine, needle-sharp bodkins. 

My work is small, quiet, and endless. I groom the frieze.

I work along the lintels, lifting the heavy, suffocating drifts of chalk and discarded cricket-shards that threaten to choke the resurrection ferns. I comb the scale-moss with my claws, teasing its brittle, silvery margins out into the rising air-currents that slip up the cliff face. Where the *Neura* have glued their parchment eggs into the hollows of ancient relief-carvings, I use the blunt heel of my palm to press the hardening resin firmly into the stone’s pores, ensuring the cradles will not shear off when the seismic shudder of the deep crust passes through.

Sometimes, my needles find a patch of resin-nodules swollen beneath an ancient fern. I pry them free. They are dark, hard, and smell faintly of cedar and dry friction. I slide them into the horizontal slit at my throat. There is no chewing; the stones in my crop will grind them down over months into a dry, nutritive flour that feeds the slow growth of my shell.

I have no companion, yet I am never alone. 

Two tiers below me, around the bend of the smooth ramp where a colonnade once opened onto a grand terrace, another chest-shield is pressed against the rock. 

I know its size by the pitch it returns through the floor. It is older than I am; its sternum is wider, its frequency slightly lower, damped by thicker plates of calcified horn. We have not looked at each other—we have no faces to turn—and we have not moved toward one another in centuries. 

Yet when the deep, tectonic weight of the mountain settles overhead—a vast, silent groaning that dampens the crickets’ roar for a single, terrifying heartbeat—I feel the older one shift its weight against the limestone. It presses harder into the pillar, absorbing the shudder, and sends a long, slow harmonic through the stratum between us.

*The stone holds,* the vibration says.

I lean my own broad breastplate into the rock, letting my rib-cavity answer with the same clear, dry note.

*The stone holds.*

And together, beneath the thunder of wings and the unceasing, white-noise blizzard of insect wings, we listen to the mountain wear itself away, one grain of chalk at a time.
