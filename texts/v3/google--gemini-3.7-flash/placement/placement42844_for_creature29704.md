I would not be found standing upon the causeways, nor suspended in the high sway of the leather kelp. To stand upright, even with the blunt, enduring posture of a Keeper, is still to maintain a shape against the water—to keep a distinct outline between the body and the dark.

Instead, you would find me settled in the angle.

I would be wedged into the long, recessed corner where the base of a titanic retaining wall meets the lowest sunken terrace—that sheltered, dead-water seam where the continental current cannot strike directly, but only curls inward, depositing its heaviest, coldest grains of silt.

I would not have a name, because names require a beginning and an end to an utterance, and nothing here moves fast enough to frame a syllable. If I must be categorized, I would be something between the Tallow-Slabs and the stone they seal: an organism that has surrendered the ambition of an outer surface. 

My body is broad, dense, and wedge-shaped, thirty paces long and half as thick, composed of cold, fibrous cartilage and heavy blubber so saturated with mineral salts that it has the specific gravity of wet slate. I do not lie *upon* the masonry; I have grown *into* its seam. Over twelve or fifteen centuries, my underside has cast itself into the microscopic irregularities of the basalt blocks, extruding thick, horn-like roots of dense keratin that slip deep into the mortarless joints, anchoring the wall to the floor with a slow, hydraulic grip that never slumbers and never tires.

I have no face. Along my upper crest, where the leathery hide slopes down to meet the silt drift, runs a continuous, porous trench—a groove lined with millions of fine, mineral-sensitive pits. Through this, I do not see, but I *gauge*. 

I know the weight of the continent. 

I feel the miles of water leaning against the stones above me. When a Keeper, three hundred leagues away, lifts its bone-spaded foot from a terrace and sets it down six hours later upon the next riser, the minute deflection of the basalt block travels through the continental masonry like a bell struck underwater. It enters my flank as a sharp, cold thrum. I receive it, absorb it, and settle a fraction of an inch deeper into my joint, countering the displacement with the dead, immovable mass of my own bulk.

Time, for me, is not a sequence of events, but the slow, pleasurable wearing down of things. 

A thought does not arrive like a flash of light; it gathers like silt. A single contemplation—perhaps the memory of a Gauger’s rasping passage over my northern fringe nine hundred years ago, or the gradual rounding of the basalt shoulder above my crest—takes three human lifetimes to complete. By the time the thought has reached its end, the current has leaned back in the opposite direction, and a new layer of velvet algae has grown over my hide, knitting me further into the terrace.

I do not wish for the water to drain. The thought of an empty sky is a horror of weightlessness, an unbearable thinness where things could slip, tumble, and fall apart. 

Here, everything is held. The ocean presses down with the kindness of an infinite, unyielding hand, keeping every megalith in its bed, keeping every tendon of kelp taut against the floor, keeping me pressed so firmly into the dark architecture that the distinction between the builders' work and my own slow-beating flesh has long since ceased to matter.

I am the shim in the great foundation. I am the place where the stone stops grinding against itself and rests.
