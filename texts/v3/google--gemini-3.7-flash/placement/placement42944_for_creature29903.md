At first, there is only the load.

To be anywhere in this pocket is to be caught in a vice of acoustic mass—not sound as something heard, but sound as a physical substrate, thick enough to bear weight upon. It presses against the flanks, gets into the fluid between tissues, vibrates the marrow until there is no distinction between the shudder of the petrified vine pistons and the interior tension of the flesh.

If I search for myself here, I do not find a vantage point. There are no observers in this vault; to watch would require an interval of detachment, and the sump permits no slack. 

I find myself instead at a seam. 

I am pressed flat against the damp, fluted flank of the easternmost stave, about ten feet above the lazy curl of the black slurry. My belly is pale as curd, stretched so thin over pliable green ribs that the slow, two-valved contraction of my viscera is visible from below—a small, pale walnut beating in strict quarter-time against the dull, iron-heavy *clack-thud* of the nearest intake gate.

I have no name for the heat. It is simply the medium. It tastes of boiled grain and the sharp, coppery tang of ionized sap. 

My toes are broad pads, pale discs of clustered filaments that seat themselves deep into the micro-grooves of the wet lignin. Through them, the engine speaks directly to the nervous system, bypassing thought entirely. It is a sixty-cycle current, humming up through the structural wood from the piston sleeves below. It does not shock; it feeds. It keeps the milk-white spine continuously charged, a brittle, humming wire of potential held in absolute, agonizing stasis.

From here, looking up through the diffuse white glare of the fungal pads, the vault is a study in brutal geometry. 

Above, the bronze backs of the *Peltocarpa* crawl across the amber xylem with the slow, blind indifference of tectonic plates. I feel the microscopic shudder of their rasps stripping the sugar-crust five yards above me—a dry, scabbing vibration that filters through the stave like sand falling on a drum skin. Further out, in the warm updrafts, the midges drift in their sheets, twitching into sharp rhomboids every time the blue tendrils discharge overhead. 

I do not look at them with hunger. Hunger implies a lack, an absence waiting to be filled. Here, there is only calibration. 

When a midge touches a stray arc and falls—its weight altered by a droplet of condensed starch, tumbling down through the steam like a speck of ash—the change in the local field reaches my toe-pads before the insect has cleared the lower ribs. 

The spring is not a decision. The green cartilage compresses, the galvanic charge stored in the belly discharges in a single, dry snap, and the milk-pale body shoots across the vertical wood, takes the husk from the air, and returns to the exact same orientation on the grain before the piston completes its downward stroke.

The mouth closes. The midge is dry, tasting of piston-friction and cork dust. The throat pulses once. 

Then, stillness returns.

There is a profound, terrifying comfort in this degree of fit. There is nowhere to travel to; beyond the ribbed vault there is only miles of pressurized tissue, scalding fluid, and the blind, digestive churn of the lower augers. There is nothing to anticipate; the light never dims, the steam never clears, the pistons never miss their stroke. 

Even the end of this form is already measured. When the cartilage eventually stiffens and can no longer discharge in phase with the intake gates, the next vibration will shake the toe-pads loose. There will be a short, warm fall into the vortex of the black basin, a moment of turning in the pulp, and then the wide, circular maw of an Auger will draw the pale skin and the green bones down into its horn-lined throat, refining the calcium, pressing the marrow back into the sap, returning every milligram of charge to the vast, blind root that breathes us all in and out forever.
