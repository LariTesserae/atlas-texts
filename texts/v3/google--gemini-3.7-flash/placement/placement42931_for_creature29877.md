I would not be in the air, and I would not be fully of the wood.

My heels never stay still. Even down here, wedged between two weeping lips of pale heartwood that are already contracting to crush me, my hips keep the rhythm: *sway, unstick, sway, unstick.* The dead rind on the bottoms of my feet is thick, dry as birch parchment, peeling off in sour gray ribbons that the sap immediately dissolves. If I stop rocking for the length of a long breath, the wood does not attack; it simply *tastes*. It sends microscopic pale threads through my calluses, testing for blood-iron, testing for salt, wanting to fold my marrow into its annual rings.

My jaw is slack, hung wide open. 

A surge hits four hundred feet behind the eastern wall—a million gallons of sweet, scalding sap shoved through capillary pipes the width of a hair under four hundred atmospheres of pressure. The sound does not enter my ears; my canals are plugged with solid, bitter plugs of fossilized resin, smooth as river glass. Instead, the pressure punches through my teeth. It rings the curve of my collarbones like brass bells. It vibrates in the basin of my pelvis with a deep, sickening hum that tells me precisely how wide the tear will open before the wood begins its furious, weeping panic to stitch itself shut.

I have three minutes before the margin heals.

My skin does not look like surface skin anymore. It is thin, slightly green where the deep veins run, smelling constantly of crushed wintergreen and yeast. I don't sweat; my body coats itself in a slick, minty wax that keeps the plant’s digestive enzymes from softening my living tissue into pulp. Through the membrane of my chest, you can see my ribs—not white bone, but yellowed and hardened, calcified by years of drinking the water that bleeds out of the cavitation margins.

In my hands, I hold a wedge made from the jawbone of my great-aunt. The teeth are still set in the socket, ground down into a chisel edge.

I drive the bone into the weeping wound beneath my feet.

Down here, the heat is suffocating. The sap doesn't run like water; it boils without steam, pressurized and syrupy, glowing with the same pale, chartreuse fire that burns in the cathedral vault above. Every few seconds, there is a *crack*—the sharp, sickening snap of a cavitation bubble collapsing in the vascular bundle directly beneath my knees. The concussion kicks my knuckles, numbing them instantly, but I don't pull back. 

There it is: the cambial nodule.

It sits in the throat of the fissure like a tumor of crystallized sunlight—a fist-sized knot of compressed starch, hormone salts, and vegetative marrow, pulsing with its own faint, frantic beat. It is wet, fibrous, and boiling hot.

I hammer the aunt-bone deeper. The wood screams—not with a voice, but with a sudden, violent dilation of space. The crevice suddenly snaps outward, plunging me four feet deeper into the boiling dark, then recoils instantly, its wet, fibrous jaws slamming together to weld around my ribs.

I don't struggle against the clamp. To struggle is to tear your own dermis, and open blood in a healing crevice is an invitation to be grafted within the hour. 

Instead, I match the turgor. I drop my chin, let my pelvic bones absorb the contraction, and twist the jawbone wedge with both wrists until the nodule snaps free from its root-stalk with the wet sound of a torn ligament.

I shove the hot starch into the pouch slung across my sternum, made of cured bast and human hair.

High above me, out in the great empty nave, a chime rings out.

It is a pure, vitreous tone, cutting cleanly through the jet-engine roar of the walls. It comes from the high vaults—one of the glass chrysalises. I look up through the slit of the fissure into the dazzling, chartreuse sky of the vacuole. 

The six-foot spindle of spun silica has stopped drifting. Its vapor-tail has ceased. It has pulled its long glass body inward, folding into a shivering, diamond-bright needle that points directly at the western expanse.

*Blowout.*

The wall forty yards away is already beginning to bloat. It does not swell slowly; it blossoms toward the center of the nave in an impossible optical lurch, multiplying its cellular face, bringing miles of glowing cambium into a single, suffocating point of pressure. A hydraulic line is shearing behind the gold skin. In seconds, the air will be shredded by a storm of razor-sharp xylem needles and boiling sap under thousands of pounds of head.

There is no time to climb the ladder. The ladder is already half-digested anyway, a gray ghost under a skin of fresh cuticle.

I drop the bone wedge. I hook my fingers into the edge of the fissure, haul my torso out onto the flat heartwood floor, and immediately roll to keep my skin from sticking. 

I do not look for the others. I know where they are: already pressed flat against the vertical grain, cutting their forearms, offering the price of entry.

I scramble to the nearest wall of living cambium. It is towering, sheer, wet as an eyeball, humming so fiercely that it blurs my vision. 

I take the flaked pebble from my tongue where I keep it wet, draw its jagged edge across the inside of my left arm from wrist to crook, and press my bleeding meat hard against the burning gold grain.

The response is instantaneous. It is an ecstasy of cellular hunger.

The wood drinks the salt before it can even run down my wrist. The wall shivers, softens, and instantly boils over. Millions of microscopic, translucent papillae reach out from the grain, tickling, piercing, weeping an amber caul that washes over my knuckles, my forearms, my neck. 

It is blindingly hot, but it does not burn; it seals. 

Within five seconds, the plant has grown a blister the size of a burial mound over my curled body. I am inside the wall. The deafening roar of the nave drops to a thick, muffled thrum, like the sound of an ocean heard through a mother’s ribs. 

Through the clear, glassy skin of the blister, I watch the nave erupt.

The western wall shears open. Millions of gallons of pressurized sap explode into the void in a blinding, geometric fury, crystalizing mid-air into spinning daggers of glass-wood that shatter against the floor like artillery. The shockwaves ripple through the cambium, buckling the space, making the distant ceiling feel as close as a cloth laid across my face, then throwing it back into the stars of green light.

Inside the blister, the sap fills my mouth, my nostrils, the hollow behind my amber-plugged ears. 

I don't drown. The plant's sap is rich with pressurized oxygen, minted and sweet. My jade-tinted veins open wide, taking the sugar, taking the hormones, while my cut arm slowly dissolves at the edges, knitting seamlessly into the cambial flow. For three hours, until the vascular pressure drops and I must take my bone knife to cut my way out of this mother-flesh before my bones turn entirely to xylem, I am not a person running from a god.

I am a single, dreaming cell in the deep, blind heel of the world, suspended in light, waiting for the sap to cool.
