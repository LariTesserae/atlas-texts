At first, there is only the registration of weight. 

Not weight pressing down, but weight traveling through: a thick, percussive knot of force that strikes the bone of the jaw, travels along the flattened belly, and exits through the tail into the basalt. It is not heard. The ears have nothing to do with it; there is no room inside the skull for hearing when the entire forty-foot void is ringing like the skirt of an iron bell. 

I am not in the air. The air is too thin, too torn by the howling drop of pulverized cooling-water and the sudden gusts of steam escaping the ceiling sleeves. 

I am in the seam.

Where the rock broke—clean, glassy, black as coal—it left shelves no wider than a thumb, edges that have never met weather, only the violent parting of stone from stone. Here, the condensation sheets down in a continuous, freezing skin. If I were made of soft meat, this ledge would have sliced me open on the first day. But my underside is gray-white, thick and rubbery as a scrubbed root, cross-hatched with pale, calloused scars where the razor edges have tried and failed to bite.

My eyes are nothing more than two milky pearls sunk under wet skin, useless in the slate-and-amber dusk that filters down through the drain grates high above. I do not need them. The rift is drawn for me in pressure. 

When the stamp mills strike overhead, the world flattens. The rock shudders with an intensity that would turn a bird’s lungs inside out. In that downstroke, I press myself flat—jaw down, throat down, splaying four pale, heavy-clawed feet against the stone. I feel the entire mountain of the works groan under its own momentum. I feel the caddisworms in the crevice two inches below me clamp their iron-scaled tubes tight with their prolegs. I feel the cold, tallow-scented mist shatter into a thousand finer particles, suspended weightless for half a heartbeat in the overpressure.

And then comes the slack.

The slack is my season. It lasts less than two seconds, between the lifting of the iron stamp and the intake valve’s next long, hydraulic gasp. 

In the slack, the world resumes its slide. The water sheets forward again. The liverwort cushions, bruised purple and cold as ice, lift their sodden lobes. I slide forward through them, silent, heavy-bodied, my parsnip-colored skin nearly invisible against the wet stone. 

My mouth is wide, flat, and fringed with tiny, nerve-dense papillae that taste the mist. The air tastes of sulfur, ground shale, and old grease. A drop of cooling-water hits the shelf beside me, and its ripple across the stone’s water-film tells me everything: four inches to the left, a pale cricket has touched its whip-like antenna to the basalt, misjudging the vibration of the sump pump. 

I don't leap. Nothing leaps on these razor faces. I flow. My jaw snaps shut with the dull, mechanical certainty of an iron latch, crushing the cricket’s chitin against the roof of my mouth. It is cold fat and iron silt, bitter with the pulverized dust of the factory floor forty feet above.

Below me, in the black boil of the sump pool, the intake pipes drink with a steady, hollow roar, their vortices spinning the pewter minnows in endless, tight gyres. Farther out, on an iron sleeve that shudders so hard the frost falls from it in gray snow, a dipper blinks its white eyelids in the gloom, adjusting its grip before plunging into the foam. 

I have no name for the machines. I do not know that there is an "above," or that men in heavy wool coats walk on timber floors above the grated hatches, shoveling coke and cursing the chill that rises from the floorboards. 

To me, this rift is not an accident. It is simply the shape of the world: black stone that never softens, water that never warms, and a great, thrumming heart in the ceiling that measures out our lives in pulses of iron and falling rain. I pull myself deeper into the fissure, tuck my pale feet under the heavy fold of my flank, and wait for the rock to strike again.
