At first, there is no distinction between being a body and being a wedge driven into a fault line.

I do not begin with a name or an outline. I begin as a point of contact: a flat, chilled pressure where my back meets the vertical face of the basalt, and a corresponding firmness along the pads of my long, paddle-like knuckles where they press against the underside of an unmortared lintel.

I am not standing *on* the ledge so much as I am pinched within the architecture of it. 

To exist here is to have no center of gravity to pull against. My lower limbs—dense, segmented, cold as river clay—do not bear weight; they trail down into the sheer drop of the chasm, entirely slack, anchored only by the thick, glassine sheets of condensation that have grown over my ankles and cemented them into the rock over uncounted spans of light. If I were to try to fall, the stone would have to tear its own face off to release me.

The chord does not enter me through ears. I have none. It enters through the horn-plate across my chest and the broad, dish-shaped plane where a face would be in another kind of world. 

My face is an expanse of dull, gray cartilage, pierced with tens of thousands of microscopic pores. When the monolithic drone bears down across the abyss—that massive, unyielding grind of continent against continent—it hits the outer rim of the lintel first. The stone splits the wave. A fraction of an inch to the left, outside the lintel's frame, the air is hard as iron, vibrating with such dense, kinetic fury that any soft matter would turn to gray meal within seconds. But here, an inch inward, beneath the slate overhang, the pressure drops by a single, merciful interval. 

The air passes through my sieve-plate not as wind, but as an oscillating sieve-draught. Inside my chest cavity, where organs of soft tissue might once have been, there is only a labyrinth of calcified chambers, ribbed like the interior of a dry seed-pod. The tone enters. It divides. It drops into harmonic sub-frequencies that ring against my ribs, turning the violent pressure of the outer chasm into a low, steady hum of usable heat.

This is my metabolism: I am an instrument through which the trapped violence of the world is made quiet enough to survive.

***

Looking outward, my sight is not a gaze that travels; it is a passive registration of the bruised glow bleeding up from the chasm floor.

Because the space between the slats is sheared, I do not see a whole horizon. I see the world in three distinct horizontal strips. 

In the upper third of my vision, a miles-long basalt pillar stands at a tilt of fifteen degrees. 

In the middle third, that same pillar is displaced two feet to the right, showing a ragged gap through which I can see a Vane-Marrow hanging motionless, its long, kelp-dark body severed into two silent segments by the refraction, linked only by the fine, trembling tremor of its cartilaginous valves.

In the lower third, the pillar is gone entirely, replaced by a field of suspended shale-chips, each wrapped in the black, oily filaments of the Catenary Colonies. On the silk lines, the dew sits in swollen, immaculate pearls that never fall. Sometimes, over decades, I watch a blind-mite crawl across three inches of that black thread, its tiny legs clicking against the mineral crust with a rhythm so slow it resembles the growth of a crystal.

I do not move my head to track these things. To move the head would be to change the aperture of the sieve, to alter the pitch of the air entering my ribs, to risk the chord catching my interior plates at an ungrounded angle and shattering them from the inside.

My stillness is not sleep. It is the absolute, terrified vigilance of an anchor.

***

Yet, there is work. It occurs across spans of time so wide that the rock itself barely records them.

The lintel is not eternal; it is only very heavy. Over hundreds of years, the relentless vibration of the drone micro-fractures the footing where the two upright slate pillars rest on the knife-edge. The capstone begins to lean—perhaps by a hundredth of a millimeter over a century. When it leans, the acoustic shadow shifts. The raw, unshielded chord begins to bleed into the quiet pocket, biting at the edges of my upper ribs like acid.

When the pitch begins to rise—when the internal ring in my hollows shifts from the low drone to a thin, sharp shriek—I must move.

It takes seven years to gather the necessary tension in the marrow of my right arm. The joint is stiff, packed with cold tallow-grease and dried mineral salts. Slowly, cell by cell, the long, five-jointed limb uncurls from my side, lifting upward through the acoustic gradient. 

When my bare bone fingers meet the cold underside of the capstone, the friction is immense. The stone hums against my skin, numbing the nerves instantly. I press. I do not push with muscle; I lean my entire mass into the fulcrum, using the leverage of my calcified spine to nudge the slab back against the sheared angle of the horizon.

There is no sound of movement—the chord drowns all things—but I feel the click in my teeth. The capstone settles a hair's breadth. 

Instantly, the pitch drops. The shadow falls back over my face like a heavy, cold cloth. The piercing shriek inside my ribs smooths out into that deep, monolithic hum, uniform and calm, vibrating the liver-glass on the cliff beside me into quiet indigo sheen.

My fingers stay where they are, resting against the stone, ready for the next five centuries of drift.

***

I do not know who set these stones. Perhaps they were like me, or perhaps they were something larger that broke apart when the gravity failed and the earth forgot how to fall. 

It does not matter. The world below has vanished into the gray vapor, down where the light bleeds from the floor, and nothing will ever climb up these vertical ribbons to ask why we are here. There is only the damp gloss on the basalt, the tiny, silver-veined discs drifting across the unrippled pools, and the great chord that never finishes sounding.

I stand under the lintel. The dew condenses along the bridge of my sieve-plate, running down into the hollow of my throat in a slow, icy bead that never dries. 

I hold the shadow in place. The shadow holds me. We are balanced, frozen, and complete.
