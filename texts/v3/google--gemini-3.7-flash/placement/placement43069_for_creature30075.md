I am not in the light. 

To look up is to see the sky only as an intrusion—slabs of blinding, copper-yellow glare sliced through by the fibrous ribs of the span. 

I find myself on the dark side of the curve, tucked into the dripping under-swell of a primary root where the wood is eighty feet thick and groans like the timbers of a dry-docked hull. Here, where the morning mist lingers longest before the dry noon burns it off, the moss-pelt grows thick as a fleece, rank with the fermented vinegar scent of trapped midges and the cold, metallic sweat of stone dust.

I am not a hunter of the open air. I have neither the spring-harpoon nor the long sight needed to track a drift-polyp rising through the heat shimmers. 

My body is shorter, heavier in the shoulders, thickened by decades of working in the cramped twilight beneath the blister-nodules. My ribs are flattened, flared like hoops to press securely into the root-gutters; my skin is stained the dark, walnut-black of marrow-sap, lacquered with rendered polyp-grease until water beads on my forearms without wetting the hair. My ears are folded down beneath thick, tufted caps of oily hide to mute the screaming drone of the kite-wasps, turning the deafening sky into a low, chest-thumping hum—the world reduced to a heavy, continuous heartbeat.

I am a *mender of the junctions*.

Where the great root-bridges plunge their knuckles into the petrified stone needles, the wood does not rest quietly against the rock. The stone does not yield, and the tree never stops pulling. The tension between them is a terrifying, living thing—thousands of tons of pressurized fiber twisting against three-million-year-old basalt, grinding in the wind with a sound like iron teeth chewing sand. 

If the root slips, the span snaps. If the span snaps, seven blister-villages peel into the Hunger like loose scabs.

So I live in the crevice.

My home is not a woven basket dangling in the breeze, but a hollowed gall-pocket carved into the dead outer rind of the hinge-joint, sealed off from the blind needle-ants with smeared plugs of bitter tallow. Inside, it is dark, humid, and smells of old yeast and dry stone. 

My work is done entirely with my hands, my knees, and the bone-picks hanging from my harness. 

At dawn, before the valley breath has fully burned away and while the wood is still swollen and supple with the night’s moisture, I clip my three bone-carabiners to the ancient braided stay-lines and lower myself into the sleeve—the narrow, vertical gulf where the living cedar root wraps the stone needle. 

Down here, suspended over three miles of empty blue haze, I listen.

I take my stone plectrum—a flat piece of river-slate passed down from someone whose name has worn off the grip—and strike the root. 

*Tock.*

I press my forehead against the damp bark. 

The sound does not travel into the air; the air is too thin, swallowed by the wind. The sound travels *inward*, down the pressurized marrow of the vine, racing through the boiling sap-veins for four leagues to the east, bouncing off the borer-scars, humming through the anchor-nodes, and returning to my skull as a complex, trembling vibration. 

From the echo, I read the health of the Hold.

A high, singing ring means the fiber is dry, stretched to the verge of a tension-fracture; I must wedge boiled polyp-sponge into the upper weeping-traps to channel the noon dew directly into that grain. 

A dull, wet thud means a chisel-borer has breached a sap-canal somewhere deep within the core; I must climb into the seam with my long cedar-spikes, hammer them into the borer’s siphon-hole, and let the tree's own scalded, boiling resin drown the beast and petrify around it.

Sometimes, late in the afternoon when the sun hits the stone needle directly and bakes the basalt until it radiates a dry, kiln-like heat, the rock sings back. It doesn't use the language of the wood. The stone has no sap; it has only memory. It vibrates with the deep, tectonic groan of a world that was once solid, before the sky ate the valleys and left only these solitary teeth standing in the void.

When the tap-sign comes through the root from the hunters far out on the middle span—short, rhythmic pulses telling of a drift-polyp snagged, of meat and bladder-skins coming into the blisters—I feel it in the soles of my feet. I do not answer with news of the hunt. 

I tap back twice: *The hinge holds. The wood is wet. The drop is empty.*

I spend my life inches from the mouth of the Hunger, my harness taut, my toes curled into the fissures of the bark. I do not fear falling; one does not fear the sky any more than a fish fears the water. But I respect its appetite. Every scrap of shaved bark I cut away, every dead borer-shell I pry from the joints, falls silently from my fingers, dwindling into a speck before vanishing into the blue haze below. 

They go down, where nothing returns. 

I stay up, bound to the tension, wedge by wedge, strike by strike, wedged tight between the bone of the world and the wood that refuses to let it go.
