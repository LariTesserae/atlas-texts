At first, it is not a body at all, but a measurement. 

A gap of three-eighths of an inch between the curved belly of a rocker-arm and the weeping limestone face behind it. The gap widens to four inches on the upstroke, holds for the duration of a drawn breath while the lower sump fills, and then narrows down to nothing—or almost nothing. Just enough room for a layer of cold fat, a smear of silt, and whatever can make itself flat enough to share that clearance with the grease.

I am there, in the closing space. 

It takes a long time to remember where the iron ends and the cartilage begins. The machine does not have nerves, but it has tensions, and when you live pressed against a brass bushing that has been turning inside a pillow-block since before the stone above began to crumble, the heat of the friction becomes your own body heat. You do not feel the stroke coming; you *are* the deceleration before the reversal.

My shape is not something held stiffly against the world. It is an accommodation. 

When the walking beam descends, my ribs do not resist; they slide over one another like wet slate shingles, letting the chest wall collapse until the heart beats sideways against the flank. The flank yields to the cold stone; the stone weeps its hard, mineral sweat directly into the pores of my back. There is no distinction between being held and being crushed except for two hundredths of a second, and that interval is where I live.

My face has no need for daylight. The gray sky two hundred feet above is only a pale rumor of low pressure that slightly thins the siphon’s draw. What matters is the throat of the pipe beneath my lower belly. 

Through the skin of my wattles—broad, soft petals of horn-rimmed flesh that drape across the iron like wet leaves—the whole basin rings like an submerged bell. I can feel the flute-eels three conduits over, riding the cavitation when the high-pressure check-valve snaps shut with that muffled, hollow *plunk*. I can feel the grease-scabs yielding their zinc shells to the down-draw on the secondary journals, popping back into their dished shapes as the pressure vents.

I am one of the crawlers, but not an agile one. I have grown thick on the tallow of the lower trunnions, where the grease gathers heavy and black with ground iron-filings. 

My tongue is a ribbon of gray file-teeth. I work it slowly across the lip of a secondary drain valve, taking away the calcified lime before it can bloom into a crust that would catch the valve seat and throw the cycle off by half a beat. The taste is bitter, metallic, and clean: cold rain, ancient dolomitic limestone, tallow rendered from something that died before the roof fell in, and the sharp, alkaline tang of zinc. It is the only flavor that has ever existed.

When I move, it is not by stepping. Stepping implies a ground that stays where you put your foot. 

Instead, I flow from one tolerance to the next. As the trunnion swings left, I pour myself into the vacuum left by its heel. As it swings right, the returning mass pushes me through a slotted port into the dead-water behind the lower sluice-plate. There is an economy to it so absolute it feels like sleep: you do not spend effort to travel; you let the displacement of several tons of wet iron carry you through the dark.

Occasionally, through the long, hollow spine of the conduit I am cleaning, there comes a sequence of sharp, hard raps. 

*Clack. Clack-clack. Tt-tt-tt.*

Another crawler, two levels down, wedged into the oscillating cradle of the central siphon. It is tapping its elbow against the resonant brass casing. It is not an alarm; alarms do not exist where nothing can be avoided. It is simply an alignment. It is saying: *The third valve is drawing late by the width of a fingernail. The cushion is soft today.*

I lift my right forelimb—heavy, bone-dense, capped with a thick pad of horn—and strike the intake flange twice in reply. 

*Tock. Tock.*

*Understood. The silt is thick. The clearance is kept.*

The vibration shudders down through my brisket, through the water-logged muscle of my belly, and dissipates into the churning slurry below. 

I tuck my blunt, blind head beneath the lip of the housing. The water rises three feet in a silent, boiling surge, drowning me completely in cold, grease-slicked froth. I do not hold my breath; the hydrostatic pressure simply locks the valve-slits in my neck, holding the oxygenated moisture inside me while the flood scours my back clean of loose shale. 

The weight of the water above is immense, beautiful, and exact. It presses me down into the machine’s greasy cradle until we are a single, continuous bearing.

And then the siphon gasps. The water drops like a dropped stone. 

The cool, lime-rich fog rolls back over my skin. The walking beam lifts its massive knee with a low, hydraulic sigh, opening the gap above me by two inches, then three, then four. 

I stretch my spine into the widening dark, taste the fresh grease weeping from the seam, and begin to scrape.
