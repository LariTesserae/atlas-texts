At first, I would not be distinguishable from the weight itself. 

In a place where there is no sky, no open horizon, and no moisture to carve empty pockets between things, existence does not begin with an outline. It begins with load. 

I would not be on the open floor among the endless shingled carpet of the plate-crawlers. Their life is too continuous, too lateral—a rolling, horizontal conveyor of friction where an individual is merely a millimeter of moving bronze ridge, ground down and ingested before it has even cooled. Nor would I be clinging to the ceiling among the split-hulled capacitors, hanging in the scorched, stagnant zenith like charred fruit, waiting out the slow dissipation of their internal oil. 

I would be caught in the architecture. 

Not standing beside it, but inside the structural logic of the stacked slate. 

***

To find me, you would have to look into the third joint of the central pillar—four feet above the crawling floor, just above the zone where the needle-scavengers sift the fine, warm flour of crushed carapaces, and just below the vertical run where the long-legged climbers gather speed for their ascent into the heat.

Here, two massive slabs of dark, iron-veined slate do not quite touch. In the void between them, where the ancient, unknown builders left a jagged gap of three fingers' breadth, I have spread myself thin. 

I am not the slate, but I have forgotten where my carapace ends and the mineral begins. Over centuries of kiln-heat, the boundary between living tissue and compressed stone becomes a matter of negligible chemistry. My body is a wide, flat disc—nearly three feet across, but no thicker than a blade of dried grass at my margins. I have no eyes, because there has never been a photon in this chamber to justify them; I have no lungs, because the trapped, sulfurous gas would turn wet tissue to ash in an instant. 

Instead, my entire surface is an organ of compression and shear. 

My dorsal skin is not armor meant to deflect blows; it is a dense, vitrified felt of excreted basalt-dust, wax, and iron-silicate, fused under the immense downward tonnage of the stones above me. I am the cushion. I am the living shim. 

The weight of the upper half of the pillar—hundreds of pounds of dry slate, plus the clinging mass of thousands of ascending and descending climbers—rests directly upon my back. It presses me against the lower slab with a force that would crush an unyielding thing to powder. But I do not break because I have no hollow spaces. My interior is a dense, viscous matrix of non-volatile oils and microscopic, sliding plates of calcium fluoride, shifting microscopically to equalize the pressure across every square inch of the stone.

***

My life is measured in tensions.

Through the slate slabs above and below me, I feel the entire chamber as a system of harmonic vibrations. I feel the low, powdery whisper of the floor—that vast, dry settling of millions of iron-tipped legs buffing the greasy basalt. It reaches me not as a sound, but as a faint, continuous tremor that keeps my outermost spinnerets vibrating at the frequency of ground stone. 

Around my perimeter, extending into every micro-fissure of the masonry, are my threads. They are not spider’s silk; they have no stickiness, for stickiness requires moisture and moisture is impossible here. They are dry, taut cables of extruded mineral resin, braided with pulverized mica. I have driven thousands of these lines through the joints, anchoring the upper slab to the lower, lacing the dry masonry into a single, cohesive tendon. 

Whenever the climbing swarms surge upward, their millions of hooked claws scratching in rhythm against the outer faces of the pillar, the stone tries to twist. It wants to shear, to slide along its natural bedding planes and collapse into the crawling mass below. 

When it shifts—even by the width of a single grain of dust—I feel the stress concentrate along one edge of my body. 

In response, I do not move; I contract. 

A slow, hydraulic tightening deep within my central disc draws the mineral threads taut. The resinous lines creak inside the dark joints of the pillar, singing with dry, high-pitched tension, pulling the slate slabs back into square, locking them against each other until the shear passes. I am the reason the tower does not fall. I am the silent, stubborn refusal of the stone to become rubble.

***

I do not graze. I do not hunt.

My nourishment comes from the climbers as they scrape past my crevice. As their long, keeled bodies flow over the lips of the slate slabs, the friction grinds microscopic flakes from their carapaces—a continuous, hot snowfall of keratin dust, sulfur salts, and dried wax that sifts down into the joint. 

My fringe of spinnerets sweeps this dust inward, drawing it into the lateral grooves of my body where my metabolic mills—slow, dry, chemical furnaces—break it down without water, converting the mineral flour into fresh resin, replenishing the heavy oil that cushions my core, and spinning new thread to replace what the heat has turned brittle.

And then, there is the pulse.

It begins miles below, in the roots of the dead peak, but I feel it through the foundation stone long before it reaches the chamber. A deep, thermal swelling—an expansion of the mountain’s iron throat. 

When it arrives, the floor glows with dull infrared fury. The air, already dry as a furnace, tightens. 

Out on the floor, the millions of plate-crawlers flare their bronze ridges upward with a sound like a sudden intake of breath, a collective *hiss* that fills the black vault. 

Inside the joint, the heat hits me like an anvil. 

The slate slabs expand, groaning as they swell against my dorsal and ventral plates. The pressure doubles, triples, quadruples. My internal oils heat until they reach the threshold of liquefaction, thinning just enough to flow into the deepest micro-fractures of the stone, sealing every hairline void with hot resin. 

In that moment of maximum heat and maximum crushing weight, I am most entirely myself. 

I do not think. I have no memory of a world with sky, or rain, or cold. I know only the absolute balance between the stone that wants to fall and the thread that holds it; the slow, rhythmic heartbeat of the mountain beneath; and the endless, hot whisper of the living floor that climbs, feeds, dies, and turns to dust against my edges, keeping the dark alive.
