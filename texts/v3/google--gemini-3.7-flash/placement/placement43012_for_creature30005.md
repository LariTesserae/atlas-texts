I would not be up where the light catches the lip of the diorite, where the pale sky still tempts the eye with the illusion of an edge. That sky is an empty draft; it offers nothing to hold.

I would be down at the throat-line—the boundary tier, three hundred feet beneath the lowest coke furnace, where the cherry glow of the smelting pots fails and the permanent frost begins to coat the turnbuckles in a skin of dry rime.

Here, the air is dead-still and sharp as a glass shard. The smell of the city—scorched coke, hot mutton tallow, and the dry, bitter flour of crushed quartz—cools into something mineral and thin, like iron scraped across a tongue.

***

My spine would know the cradle before my eyes did. 

I would exist as a *Tuner-of-Weights*, living in a slung wicker basket bound with vulcanized hide, suspended beneath the lowest truss by three braided steel pendulums. I am not wide. My ribs are broad and flat, like the staves of an oil cask, but from shoulder to hip I am no thicker than a dry pine plank, built to slide flat-bellied across the guide-beams where the counterweight blocks drop past into the dark.

I do not wear goggles down here; there is no light to burn the eyes, and glass would only steam and freeze. My lids are thick, rimmed with dry waxy sebum, and my pupils stay wide, flat, and black, catching only the tiny, yellow-orange ember of a Greaser’s underside or the white pinpoint of a dropped forge-spark as it dies four hundred paces below my heels.

Mostly, I see through my teeth.

To know whether the mountain is about to bite or sigh, you do not look at the stone. Diorite does not give warnings to the eyes; it shears clean. You take a four-inch copper bit, held between the back molars, and press the free end directly against the primary load cable.

When the mountain settles to the west, the wire sings a low, dull *thrum*—a vibration that travels through the enamel, rattles the jaw hinge, and rings behind the temples like a struck copper kettle. If the sound is greasy and long, the turnbuckles up on the sixth tier are slipping. If the vibration turns shrill and dry, like a dry fingernail dragged across a slate tile, the east wall has pinched inward by a fraction of a hair, and the load must be dumped down the pendulum lines before the trusses buckle like dry straw.

I would have two brass rings on my left thumb and little finger. With these, I do not talk to people—I talk to the iron. *Tap-tap... scrape.* A signal sent up the main lead to the hoist-gangs in the dusk above: *Slack the third guy. Feed two inches to the drop-weight.*

***

Underneath my smock, pressed flat against the waxy skin over my sternum, would be an old Greaser. Its shell is scarred and grey, about the size of a trencher plate, smelling of mineral pitch and cold lard. 

It does not move much down in the frost. It stays wedged in the warmth of my quilted vest, its six horn-tipped claws loosely hooked into the thick weave of the wool. In exchange for the heat of my slow, barrel-chested breathing, its flat legs reach out from under my collar every few minutes, methodically grooming the quartz grit from the braided wire where my basket hangs. When the mountain twitches, its legs stiffen against my ribs an instant before the copper bit hums against my teeth. It feels the shear through the grease on its belly. We have the same balance.

My hands are never bare, but the gloves have the fingertips cut away and tipped with hardened horn thimbles. With these, I work the *bone-pins*.

When the dead come down from the foundry tiers—light as dried balsa, bound in greasy canvas and stripped of their moisture by the drafting wind—they are brought to my platform. It is clean work. There is no decay in this air; flesh does not rot at this altitude, it simply hardens into leather. 

I do not bury them. I fit them.

I take their long, dry thigh bones and flute them with a hand-file, turning them into hollow resonance-plugs for the cable sleeves. Their ribs become spacers for the counterweight chains, keeping the iron links from chattering against one another in the updrafts. What remains—the dry mass of trunk and skull—is lashed with tarred gut into the lead ballast blocks that hang below my cradle. 

They are the weight that keeps the city from shivering apart. Every Tensioner who ever swung a hammer or tended a flue eventually hangs beneath me, shoulder to shoulder in the pitch, swinging through a three-foot arc in the black void, lending their dried weight to the tension that holds their grandchildren’s floorboards tight against the diorite.

***

In the quiet shifts, when the furnaces above are banked and the only sound is the distant, wooden *snick-tick-clack* of the spine-birds nesting in the exhaust flues, I would lean my forehead against the cold diorite wall.

The rock here is not old. It is raw, sharp, and clean. You can put your tongue to the stone and taste the absolute absence of rain, the clean mineral bite of a world that has never known a root or a worm.

I would not dream of what lies outside the crack. To imagine a land without two walls forty paces apart—a place where the sky has no ceiling and the ground does not sing through your jaw—would feel like falling. It would feel like a cable snapping in the sleeve.

My safety is the pressure. My home is the weight of fifty thousand tons of suspended iron pulling against the unyielding teeth of the cordillera, held true by the grease on a crab's belly, the ring of brass on wire, and the steady, quiet ballast of the dead swinging in the dark beneath my feet.
