I would not be among the crawlers on the broad plains of the I-beams, nor suspended in the communal hammocks of the leeches, where the sway of the aircraft’s banking introduces the dizzying illusion of space. 

I would be deeper in the frame, where the geometry forgives nothing.

I would find myself driven like a bone wedge into the blind, unventilated seam where a vertical stiffener is riveted to the primary intake rail—a gap no wider than a coin’s edge, where the factory tolerances were left half a millimeter out of true. 

I would be a **shim-beast**, a creature of pure compression and shear.

My body would not have the rounded, fatty abundance of the mites or the boneless fluidity of the swifts. I would be flat, dense, and laminated, composed of layered sheets of stiff, cartilaginous horn interleaved with vascular tissue as dark and concentrated as cured liver. Over generations of immobility under the relentless torque of the airframe, my flesh would have taken on the exact topography of the steel: on my dorsal side, the coarse, granular imprint of the hot-dipped zinc; on my ventral side, the slick, glassy mirror of the raw mill scale, complete with the reversed indentation of a grease-pencil stroke—a single white *V* pressed permanently into the grain of my back.

I would have no limbs, no siphons, and no eyes. My leading edge—the narrow margin of my body that faces the shrieking intake slit—would be tapered to a hard, translucent blade of horn, honed to razor keenness by the constant abrasion of the aerosol slipstream. 

I do not graze, nor do I hunt. I am a creature fed entirely by **cavitation and impact**.

As the sub-zero fog screams through the seam at hundreds of feet per second, it strikes my leading edge and splits. In that instantaneous drop in local pressure, the microscopic droplets of turbine oil, dissolved stearates, and the fine, biological dander of the mites above are forced out of the mist, crashing into the microscopic, comb-like fissures that line my mouth-slits. My digestion is not a stomach’s churn, but a continuous chemical uptake: cold, pressurized nutrients driven straight through my skin by the velocity of the ship itself.

I would know the world only as **strain and frequency**.

The deafening, monolithic sound of the turbines would not be an external noise to me; it would be the medium in which my consciousness exists. It enters through the rivets that pass directly beside my flanks. Because I am wedged between two plates of unyielding iron that flex against one another under aerodynamic load, I feel every maneuver of the machine sixty thousand feet up:
* When the ship noses down into denser air, the pressure climbs, and my seam narrows by twenty micrometers. The extra squeeze drives the blood deeper into my core, producing a slow, ecstatic tightening that warms my marrow by a fraction of a degree.
* When the intake louvers cycle open, the frequency of the vibration jumps from a low, marrow-bruising thrum to a brittle, crystalline shriek. The steel rings like a bell, and the micro-ridges on my skin hum in sympathy, vibrating with enough intensity to prevent the bitter, surfactant-heavy water from ever forming ice crystals across my gills.

My death, should it ever come, would not be an event of predation or decay. It would be a mechanical integration. 

Over decades of holding the gap, the calcium and iron salts leached from the weeping condensation would slowly replace the organic fibers of my horn. My flesh would mineralize, stiffening until it possessed the hardness of cold-worked brass. I would cease to be an organism and become, permanently, a part of the airframe—an unfeeling, biological washer that keeps two structural plates from chattering themselves to pieces against the stratosphere.

Until then, I remain in the dark, frozen vice: wedged, fed by the blast, perfectly still, holding the pressure of the sky between my ribs.
