At first, there is only the sensation of being pressed thin.

Not crushed—there is no weight from above, only the sky’s hollow arch—but drawn out sideways, like a drop of wet paste worked smooth between two boards. The back is cold; the belly is warm. Between those two temperatures, a life begins to take measure of itself.

It is easiest to begin as a point of contact: where the soft, porous underside of a pumice shoe rests against the unsealed skin of a pale limestone flag. 

The stone is not dead under the sole. It hums with a deep, dry tension, an accumulation of sun that has not finished leaving the earth even as the maritime mist gathers in the air. To feel it properly, you must take the shoe off. You must put your bare heel down into the millimeter of dawn-water that has already collected along the chamfer.

The water is sweet with lime, caustic enough to bite the cuticles, silvering the skin with a faint, chalky grease that dries almost instantly if you lift your foot into the breeze.

So I stay low. 

I am not in the air with the chalk-gliders, where the world is reduced to a map of updrafts and thermal walls; that height is too clean, too drafty, too severed from the craft of the seam. Nor am I deep in the holding vaults where the water thumps blindly against the dark archways. 

I am in the throat of the cistern—in the three feet of vertical space where the world folds itself in half.

My shoulders are narrow enough to turn inside the access hatch without scraping the lime-slip from my tunic. My knees are permanently calloused from the habit of the rill: three paces forward on the pumice pads, a drop to the left shin, the drag of the cedar float across the joint, two heartbeats to watch the slurry settle to a mirror-sheen, then rise again before the sun strikes the wet edge and turns the paste to chalk.

My hands have forgotten the texture of anything soft. The palms are cross-hatched with hairline scars where dried mortar has pulled the oil out of the pores until the skin split like dry silt. In the small pouch at my hip, I do not carry bread or water—those wait below in the cool, damp niches carved into the barrel vaults—but three stones of graduated grit, a pig’s-bristle brush soaked in mutton tallow, and a tuning bar made of dense, thrice-fired black earthenware that does not ring when struck, but hums only when laid against a slab that is secretly cracked beneath its bed.

If you lay the bar flat against the limestone at the third hour of the morning, when the fog is lifting into steam and the heat smells like a wet kiln just cracked open, the stone speaks in grain.

A sound stone gives back a long, pure interval—a hollow *doh* that stays in your collarbone for several seconds. 

A stone that has separated from its mortar bed, or beneath which a pocket of trapped air is waiting to heave under the noon sun, gives back a dry, wooden click. A tick. A silence that has gone dull.

When I find that silence, the work becomes very quiet.

I do not chop or pry. Violence here would shatter the crisp margins that the builders worked months to scrape true. Instead, I insert a thin horn shim between the limestone and the terracotta paver beside it. I tap it with the palm of my hand until the lime bond sighs—a small, distinct puff of dry white dust that smells of ancient shells and wood ash. 

Then comes the slurry. It must be mixed thin, the color of morning milk, using water scooped from the dawn rills before the carapace-gazelles have walked their grease into the channel. You pour it into the hair-crack from a curved horn funnel, watching it vanish into the unseen void below. You pour until the stone drinks its fill and the gray lip of the seam suddenly floats a bead of clear moisture back up to the light.

Then the cedar float. One stroke north, one stroke south, matching the ancient grain of the wood-float that came before you forty years ago, or yesterday—there is no way to tell the difference here, where time does not rot anything, but only bakes it harder.

By noon, when the steam lifts into thunderheads and the floor begins its immense, crystalline expansion—that long, dry *tink-tink* of millions of clay rectangles adjusting their shoulders against one another—I am no longer on the surface. 

I have pulled the square lid shut over my head. 

Below the tiles, the maintenance culvert is dark, smelling of damp mineral and the cold breath of the underground cisterns. The water that fell at dawn is running here, a fast, flat sheet four inches deep, murmuring over the glazed gutters toward the central tanks. 

I sit on the narrow brick shelf above the water, my back pressed against the curved vault that supports the sunlit terrace above. Through the stone at my spine, I can feel the great herds pacing—not the sound of their broad hooves, but the rhythmic dampening of the heat-vibration as their shadows slide across the pavers three feet over my head. 

And then, faintly, through forty leagues of solid horizontal masonry, comes the stroke of another mallet.

It arrives not through the air, but through the arches: a low, resonant *clink-ton* that vibrates through the brick shelf, into my hip, and up along the ribs. It is Malo, three drainage sectors to the west, announcing that the localized noon squall has cleared his terrace, that the rills are running true, and that the mortar has taken the water without washing out.

I reach for my own mallet—the heavy one, made of pale, porous limestone with a rounded face—and I strike the center of the vault ceiling twice. 

*Tung. Tung.*

The sound runs outward into the dark, following the long, straight lines of the brick beds, traveling across the continent beneath the feet of the gliders and the gazelles, through the cold morning dew and the noon glare, keeping the floor whole, flat, and awake.
