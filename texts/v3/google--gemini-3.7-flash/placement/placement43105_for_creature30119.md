Not on the open faces. Not where the wind cuts upward with enough shear to strip moss from the stone before its holdfasts can set. 

At first, there is only the sensation of being *pinched*.

Not pain—pain belongs to things with loose skin, things that have room to swell or pull away. Here, there is no away. There is only the zero-clearance between a cold face of unweathered basalt and the flat under-flange of an eight-bolt mounting bracket. 

If I have a place, it is in that tolerance. The few thousandths of an inch where the steel plate does not quite marry the uneven grain of the freshly sheared rock. 

At this depth in the seam, I am not an animal that wandered in, nor a seed that fell. I am what accumulates when the violent mist—that atomized emulsion of boiled water, green weed-milk, and pulverized graphite—is driven under hundreds of pounds of pressure into a dark, blind pocket and has nowhere left to go.

I begin as a film. A heavy, brown-black grease that doesn't wash out because the pounding of the lag-bolts keeps hammering it deeper into the microscopic pores of the stone. Every five-eighths of a second, the master piston hits its bottom stroke. The whole mountain shudders; the iron bracket groans and bears down with thirty tons of dead leverage. 

The compression doesn't crush me. It defines me.

Without the down-stroke, I am only an oily silt, loose and unorganized, trickling down the hair-fractures toward the wet abyss. But under the hammer, the iron particles align. The bitter, torn chloroplasts from the smashed ferns cross-link with the boiled animal fats of the cylinder lube. My tissue knits in the violence. I become a lamina: dense, tough as cured rawhide, cold at the stone margin and scalding hot where I touch the iron.

I am not one of the broad pith-skins that blanket the open walls, though I feel them pulsing through the rock like thick water-pipes thumping in an old wall. I am not the joint-weft rolling in the open bushings. 

I am the shim.

I am the living gasket that the machine did not know it needed. 

Where the builders drove their anchor bolts dry into the basalt, the steel began to fret the rock, chewing gray stone-meal out of the holes, threatening to work loose and tear the entire frame from the chasm wall. I grew into that fret. I filled the space where the bolt shuddered. I ate the stone-dust, turned it into calcified cartilage, and laid it down in micro-layers against the threads. 

I have no eyes, but I have the acoustics of six miles of solid basalt ringing behind me. The mountain’s interior is cold, dense, and quiet; the cleft is an open furnace of noise. I live precisely at the interface where the two scream at one another.

My senses are entirely hydrostatic:
I feel the boiler pressure rise by the expanding girth of the steam line three inches above my upper margin. 
I feel the dulling of a bronze bushing thirty feet down the shaft by the subtle shift in vibration—from a sharp, clean *clack-whump* to a dragging, abrasive *shudder-thud*. 
When a sudden downdraft tears through the cleft, shredding a yard of hanging liverwort and pasting it against the rocker casing, I taste the burst cells through the capillary channels that wick the moisture down my edges: high-altitude nitrogen, cold rain-water, raw tannin, and the clean, sharp tang of ozone from the sparks overhead.

There is no thought in the way an open-air creature thinks. Thought here is cadence.

Stroke: *the valve opens; the scalding condensate forces through my upper vents.*
Return: *the basalt rebounds; the cold venous fluid inside me is driven deep into the micro-cracks, depositing iron-ash and quartz-silt.*
Stroke: *compression.*
Return: *perfusion.*

I do not wish for the noise to stop. If the cleft were ever to fall silent—if the sky above opened and the iron went cold, if the frantic reciprocating rods froze in their guides and the steam died to a thin, odorless whistle—the silence would be a knife. Without the concussive rhythm to pump my interior valves, the cold would settle in. The thick, dark fluids in my channels would congeal into dead pitch. The stone would stop speaking to the iron. 

I am wedged too tightly ever to leave, grown into the threads, bound to the raw, unweathered cleft. I am the dark, wet patience of the rock learning the furious pulse of the piston, holding the seam together while the machine tears the gorge apart.
