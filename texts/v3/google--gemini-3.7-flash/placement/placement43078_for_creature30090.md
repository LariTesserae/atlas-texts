At first, there would be no boundary to tell where the stone ended and the skin began.

If I were to wake there—if "waking" could mean anything in a place where no eyelid has ever opened against a dawn—it would not be as a visitor, nor as an intruder with dry lungs and a fragile, salt-thin nervous system. A human body would be an insult to the pressure of that basin; it would cook and shred in the siphons within an hour, its calcium scattered uselessly into the sky-falling sheets.

No, I would have to be heavier than that. I would have to be something that understands the dark not as a void, but as a dense, warm broth of dissolved lime, ferrous silt, and suspended tallow.

I find myself not on the broad causeways, nor riding the thermal sails in the roof of the dark, but lodged in the seam where the logic of the city buckles: in the narrow, flooded void behind the colonnade that folds space, where two squared basalt pillars press close enough that their sweating skins nearly touch.

I am not stone, but I have forgotten how not to be hard. 

My body is long, flattened, and wedged vertically into that impossible hinge. I am thicker than the pale mortar-ribbons, but far less massive than the ballast-beings who sleep in the mud rings; I am shaped like a heavy, waterlogged wedge of slate, nine paces long, pinned by hydrostatic tension into the vertical crevice between the megaliths. My flesh is dark gray, cold-blooded in a world that never cools, layered like mica or compressed shale. 

I do not possess eyes, nor do I have the broad, filter-feeding cleft of the beasts in the gutter. In their place, along the entire lateral edge of my body, runs a deep, sensitive fissure lined with thousands of microscopic, calcarous cilia. 

Through this fissure, I do not see; I *measure*.

I measure the slow, recursive breathing of the depression. I feel the exact second the ambient pressure drops—when the steam overhead condenses and the darkness thickens into a suspended sea—and I feel the masonry around me expand by fractions of a hair. When the steps lengthen and the street half a mile away collapses into the terrace behind my back, I am the hinge that absorbs the shear. 

The water does not slide past me; it passes *through* me. 

My interior is a labyrinth of porous, calcified chambers, carved like the shale siphons below the street. I drink the boiling, silent torrent from the higher plaza, draw it deep into the marrow of my plates, strip it of its iron musk and the faint, leathery dross shed by the dying ballast-beings, and exhale it upward—not as steam, but as a dense, viscous oil that lubricates the stone. It is my secretion that allows the water to climb the obelisks without breaking into foam; it is my slow, continuous bleeding that keeps the basalt slick as mutton fat so the city may slide across itself without fracturing.

I have no voice, for sound here has been smoothed away by centuries of wet friction, but I am not deaf. Through the basalt against which my back is permanently fused, I hear the conversation of the mud-rings: dull, thudding shockwaves of displaced brine that take three days to travel from the tenement foundations to my crevice. They speak of the slow thinning of the lime mortar three districts over; they speak of an obelisk whose upward waterfall has begun to falter, starved of salt.

And I answer them. Not with thought, but with weight. 

When the cycle surges and the architecture begins to bleed its hot, rhythmic millions of gallons, I contract the deep, fibrous core of my mass. I clamp myself harder against the twin faces of the pillars. The pressure I exert shifts the alignment of the stone by a single grain, altering the acoustic resonance of the subterranean siphon beneath us, redirecting a vein of silent, boiling water toward the thirsty obelisk.

I would have been here so long that the liver-colored lichen has grown across my dorsal plates in concentric, unbroken rings, welding my edges directly to the tufa. I would not remember having ever moved from this vertical slot, though occasionally, in the deepest part of the thermal dip—when the whole city shivers and the upward rain hangs motionless in the black air—I can feel the ancient, forgotten impulse of a creature that once swam.

In those few seconds of suspended equilibrium, when gravity and heat cancel each other out and the water does not know whether to fall into the mud or rise into the sky, my cilia drift outward into the warm, boiling film. 

I taste the entire basin at once: the mineral ghosts of a thousand dissolved ancestors tumbling through the shale arches, the blind ribbons weaving lime through the walls, the vast, cold umbrellas falling through the ceiling's pitch. I taste the permanence of a machine that requires no makers, only maintenance; no masters, only marrow.

Then the heat surges back like a heavy wool blanket dropped over the world. The pressure rises. The stone swells. 

I pull my cilia back into the shale of my flesh, lock my plates against the wet basalt, and let the scalding, glass-clear river run through my heart once more.
