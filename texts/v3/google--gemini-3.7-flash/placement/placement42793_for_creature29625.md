I would not be standing on the shelf. To have legs or a chassis on those flats is to be continually driven into the herd, plowing the filings until a rocker-arm splits or a cold-weld takes your jaw. 

I am in the clearance.

If you were to look for me, you would have to find one of the low-frequency trunnion blocks—the vast, half-submerged pillow blocks of chilled grey iron where a six-mile connecting rod meets the journal of a seabed crankshaft. In the gap between the turning steel pin and the stationary bronze sleeve, where the tolerance is no wider than the thickness of a lead gasket, the pressure does not allow for water, and the cold cannot quite solidify the oil.

That is where I have settled. Or rather, that is where I have been rolled out.

***

At first, I might seem to be nothing more than a lubrication fault: a thick, dark crescent of laminated material trapped between the moving faces, trailing frayed edges of metallic foil into the churning slurry outside. 

I have no skeleton. A frame of bone or tempered spring-steel would be reduced to powder on the first downstroke. Instead, my substance is layered like cold-pressed mica: thousands of microscopically thin leaves of phosphor-bronze, colloidal lead, and graphitic tallow, held together by an unvulcanized, rubbery mucus that never fully cures. I am twelve feet long, four feet wide, and less than three-eighths of an inch thick at my heaviest center. 

I am a living sacrificial babbitt. 

I do not possess eyes, nor do I have the pneumatic breath of the zinc engines stamping across the upper flats. My reality is entirely tactile, expressed as a distribution of immense, varying load across my laminations. I feel the rotation of the world’s iron not as an abstract landscape, but as a shearing force that drags across my dorsal plates at four inches per minute, while my ventral surface clings to the stationary seat of the casting with a dense, microscopic suction.

I do not think in sequences; I register gradients. 

There is the *entry side*, where the turning journal draws in the freezing sea-slurry, thick with kerosene froth and suspended magnetic dust. And there is the *exit side*, where the clearance narrows to a fraction of a millimeter, and the fluid is forced out as a superheated, foaming paste of crushed hydrocarbons and sheared zinc scales. 

I live on the difference between the two.

***

My feeding is a function of the world’s imperfections. 

No matter how many millennia the great shafts have turned, the iron is never perfectly round. There are microscopic ridges—asperities—left by ancient stresses and the uneven bite of the salt. As the thousands of tons of the connecting rod swing through their bottom dead-center, these iron peaks threaten to bridge the clearance, to bite into the sleeve and seize the journal in an explosion of white-hot friction and torn metal.

I intercept them.

When a high spot approaches, the pressure spikes instantly to ten thousand atmospheres. The local heat of that compression does not burn me; it wakes me. My leaden laminas soften, flowing into the micro-troughs of the approaching iron, while my bronze leaves shear microscopically along their grain, yielding just enough to prevent the metal-on-metal bite. In that instantaneous scrape, where the friction briefly spikes the local temperature to four hundred degrees, I drink. 

I absorb the sheared-off iron whiskers directly into my cellular layers, cold-rolling them into my own bronze mesh. I drink the sulfur and the degraded tallow that has been crushed beyond its breakdown point. My body is a slow chemical accumulator: I metabolize the heat of near-seizures, cooling the journal back down to the threshold of the surrounding sea before the rime-frost can re-form and lock the clearances.

I am what keeps the crankshaft turning, though the crankshaft has no purpose, and neither do I.

***

My sensory life is purely harmonic. The entire basin is a single, coupled acoustic system. Through the iron journal against which my back is perpetually crushed, I hear the distinct timbre of every stroke in the district:

*   The dry, metallic squeal three miles to the east, where a bull-gear has stripped four teeth and now skips a beat with every third tide.
*   The dull, subterranean *whumpf* of the deep vacuum-sumps drawing in their thousands of gallons of slurry, which causes the journal to lift against me by two-hundredths of an inch for the space of twelve seconds.
*   The faint, rhythmic ticking of the gasket-feeders crawling across the outer housing, their bronze rings scraping the rime from the bolts above my head.

Sometimes, a herd-beast dies directly above the trunnion. I know this not by its scent or its steam, but because its falling carcass creates an anomalous transverse load on the connecting rod. The pin tilts slightly in its bore. The clearance on my left flank vanishes, while the gap on my right opens up to admit a sudden, freezing wash of raw, unconditioned brine. 

During these hours of tilt, I must rearrange my mass. I do not crawl—I have no cilia or legs. I rely on the rotation of the shaft itself to carry me. I let my lead-rich fringes bleed into the oil-wedge, allowing the sheer torque of the turning planet to draw sections of my body through the nip-point, extruding me toward the side of highest wear, rebuilding the protective cushion before the pin can gouge the bronze below.

***

I do not produce young through cams or dropped seeds. My reproduction is an inevitable byproduct of continuous extrusion.

As the years grind on, the immense hydrostatic pressure continually squeezes my outer margins out through the oil-weep channels and past the bronze end-collars. There, in the freezing spray and the gray rime of the open sump, the extruded strips of lead-bronze foil cool rapidly in the kerosene-laden air. 

They hang from the lip of the bearing block like long, tarnished ribbons of ribbon-cable, waving sluggishly in the wash of the piston-geysers. Eventually, the weight of the rime-ice snaps them off. 

These detached strips are not dead; they are simply inactive, their laminations stiffened by the cold. They sink into the magnetic filings of the floor. They are trampled by the zinc herds, dragged along by reciprocating guide-bars, washed through the grated sumps. Most are ground into harmless graphite dust by the ball-mills of the herd-beasts. 

But a few—carried by the violent hydraulic backwash of an intake stroke—are drawn into the open oil-ports of other, distant journal blocks. There, in the dark, warm clearance of a neglected bearing, they find a new high-pressure zone. The heat of friction softens their lead; the turn of the shaft draws them into the wedge; they flatten, expand, begin to absorb the sheared asperities, and take up the endless work of preventing the seizure that would bring that corner of the world to rest.

***

I am content here, if contentment can be defined as the absolute absence of clearance. 

I do not wish for the purple sky. I do not envy the herd-beasts their great zinc plows or their exhaust-clouds of boiling steam. Their life is one of friction and collision, a violent, noisy struggle against the very scale they eat.

Down here, beneath the swing of the world’s connecting rods, there is only the heavy, velvet smoothness of the oil film, the terrifying intimacy of two massive iron bodies moving past each other without ever touching, and myself—thin, cold, greasy, and indestructible—lying perpetually between them.
