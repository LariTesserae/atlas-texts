At first, there is only the sensation of being held in place by friction and oil. 

Not floating, exactly, and not pinned. Just wedged so deeply into the damp logic of the stone that the distinction between where the basalt gives up its lime and where skin begins is difficult to keep track of. 

If you are looking for me, do not look to the mid-canopy where the moths generate their faint, nervous halos of heat, and do not look to the open compost where the lung-toad churns the dark mush with its spade-feet. I would not survive the open. The trench is too efficient; to be broad and exposed down here is to be folded into someone else’s metabolism before your warmth has even had the courtesy to cool.

I am in the masonry. 

Not in the tumbled rubble that sits loose in the ferns, but in the low, load-bearing tiers—the dry-stacked blocks that were planed square so long ago that the acidic sweat of the cave has rounded their seams into soft, swollen lips. Behind those lips, two feet deep into the dark chill where the geothermal heat of the floor meets the ancient, dead cold of the bedrock, there are narrow horizontal voids: the places where the builders’ leveling chips have dissolved into chalky paste.

That is where the pressure is best. 

I do not have eyes; the copper-tinted gloom that filters through the vine-strangled fissure sixty feet above is an abstraction I have no organ to interpret. What I have instead is an acute, intolerable sensitivity to shear. 

My body is long, flat as a folded leather strap, and entirely without bone. It has taken centuries of compression to become this thin without bursting. My skin is not scales and it is not fur; it is a tough, plum-colored mantle of water-tight connective tissue, coated in a thick, clear lipid that smells faintly of cold fat and dissolved limestone. On the upper plateau, in the dry wind that rakes the dust, this skin would curdle and crack like dried parchment in the space of three breaths. Down here, it is an unbroken seal.

I live where the water goes before it falls.

Every fat droplet that gathers on the vaulted ceiling must make a choice when it hits the stone. It runs along the worn drainage tracks, past the pale, hanging throats of the wax-bellies, down the vertical faces, until it finds the capillary pull of the mortar seams. When it enters the seam, it enters me.

I drink through the margin of my flank. I do not have a mouth that opens and closes with hunger; I have a lateral fringe of pale, translucent cilia that comb the greasy film as it creeps past. I take what the trench has already digested three times over: the microscopic spores that failed to grip the basalt, the powdered wing-scales of moths caught in the vipers’ coils, the dissolved guano washing down from the bat-clusters in the high vaults, and the endless, frictionless slime of the girdled snails. 

I am the filter at the back of the drawer.

There is almost no movement required. To move quickly in this air is to advertise a thermal signature, and a thermal signature is an invitation to the clouded eyes of the vipers. So I hold. I wedge the back of my body into the blind root-tips that have wormed their way through the stone from the plateau above—tips that are stunted, swollen with trapped moisture, smelling of bitter tannin—and I let my front taper out into the seam, paper-thin, resting on the slick basalt like a tongue on glass.

Time does not pass in hours. It passes in hydraulic swells.

Every few weeks, when the storm winds gather far above on the sun-baked crust, the atmospheric pressure drops. The heavy, fermenting air inside the gallery expands, pressing outward against the limestone walls like a lung trying to exhale through a pinched straw. 

That is when the lung-toads begin their work beneath the plinths. 

I feel them before anything else does. The vibration does not come through the air—the air is too thick and dead to carry it—it comes through the basalt. A low, rolling shudder, like the stone itself clearing its throat. It travels through the masonry, through the wet paste of the seams, and directly into the fluid core of my mantle. 

When that sub-frequency hits, the ceiling lets go.

Millions of drops lose their meniscus at the same instant. The sound is not a roar; it is a soft, interior rustle, like a sheet of silk dragged across wet grass. A curtain of warm water washes down the walls, flooding the seams, drowning the blind crickets, lifting the snail trails into a momentary, iridescent foam.

In that rush, I gorge.

My mantle swells, thickening from a ribbon into a heavy, dark loaf. The cilia work in a furious, silent frenzy, sweeping the flood-tide of organic grease into my central gut. The water passes through me and out the back, stripped clean, leaving behind only the rich, rotting sulfur and the faint, sweet trace of the wax-orchids' drowned pollen.

For ten minutes, fifteen minutes, I am entirely awake, dense with the pulse of the trench.

And then the rain stops. The air pressure stabilizes. The heavy, warm oil of the atmosphere settles back over the ferns. The lung-toads fall silent, sinking back into their plum-black slime beneath the compost. 

I flatten myself again. I push the excess water from my tissues until I am once more no thicker than a knife blade, wedged tight between two blocks of basalt that have not shifted since the ceiling first broke. 

The mold creeps back across the lip of the stone. A wax-belly salamander slides down from above, its salmon-pink throat pulsing against the damp, its tiny, rubbery toes stepping directly across the tip of my mantle without knowing it is walking on flesh. 

I do not flinch. I let it pass. I let the iridescent trail of a snail coat my flank once more, sealing me back into the seam. 

I am part of the stone’s plumbing. I am the quiet, unthreaded stitch that keeps the damp from rotting the foundation away. Down here, in the breathless dark beneath the plateau, that is more than enough to be.
