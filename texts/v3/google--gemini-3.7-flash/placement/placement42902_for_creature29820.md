At first, there is no reason to assume an edge. 

To look for oneself here by seeking a boundary—a line where skin ends and stone begins, or where an inhalation breaks off from the general damp—is to use the wrong instrument. There is only the sensation of being held flat against something infinitely patient, cool enough to soothe away all restlessness, wet enough that the idea of thirst never forms.

If I begin anywhere, it is as a point of contact: the slight, microscopic drag between the belly of a tallow salamander and the saturated liverwort beneath it. It is not cold that hurts; it is the cold that steadies. A heart beating once every four minutes leaves vast, spacious valleys between beats—time enough to feel the water slip a hundredth of a millimeter down the parabolic curve of the schist.

***

Yet staying there feels too brief, too localized. The salamander is only a transient swell in the water’s descent. 

Move slightly upward, and the sensation changes from smooth, hairless damp to the tight, interlocking weight of the slate-blue pelt. If I am in the vaults, I am the collective pressure of twenty ribcages aligned flank to flank against the stone ceiling. There is no individual sleep among the bats; there is only the slow, shared draft of air through fur so dense that mist has no choice but to condense upon it. 

From here, to exist is to be a condenser. You do not reach out into the dark; the dark collects upon you. You feel the bead form—a single, heavy droplet of mountain sweat gathering at the tip of an ear or the curve of a folded thumb—until its mass exceeds the tension of the hair, and it drops. 

You feel it leave you. You feel it strike the frond forty feet below. You are both the release above and the tiny, chalky vibration below.

***

And yet, there is a pull toward the back of the seam, where the air grows slightly denser still, caught in the dead-end crook where the basalt was wedged.

It is here that the distinction between the biological and the geological finishes dissolving. If you look for a mind in this place, you will not find a wandering human thought, but you will find the *rebound* of one. 

I would be the calcified bone peg hammered into the fissure—or rather, the interface where that marrow-bone gives itself up to the seep. 

Think of what that peg is: an old piece of leverage, once part of a knee or an arm that climbed up from a valley forgotten by the sun, now wedged so tight that the rock has taken it as a rib. It is neither alive in the quick, nervous sense of the lowlands, nor dead in the dry sense of the open crag. It is porous. Every hour, a few molecules of ancient phosphate and calcium are drawn out by the weeping water, carried down the flank of the stone, and woven into the lattice of a ghost-fern’s white frond or the clear, gelatinous shell of a salamander’s egg.

To be that peg is to be the slowest kind of speaker. 

You feel the mountain’s weight pressing inward with tons of quiet force, mediated only by the density of the river stones propping the drainage open. You feel the silent, steady passage of the water through your own calcified channels. You do not remember the sun—the sun is an irrelevant, violent glare—but you remember the exact temperature of the valley mud from ten thousand winters ago, and you are releasing that memory, grain by grain, into the slime that feeds the blind beetles.

***

In the end, I would not be an observer perched in the corner, nor a single creature crawling across the shelf. 

I would find myself in the **meniscus**—the unbroken, microscopically thin skin of water that joins the bat’s wing to the liverwort, the liverwort to the bone-peg, and the bone-peg to the mountain’s deep core. 

To be the meniscus is to have no center, only surface. It is to feel the entire cleft at once: the slow, five-minute swell of a hundred translucent hearts; the imperceptible uncurling of a crystalline fern that will not finish its gesture in your lifetime; and the heavy, velvet hush of stone that has finally ceased to fall, holding everything it caught in an unbroken, cold embrace.
