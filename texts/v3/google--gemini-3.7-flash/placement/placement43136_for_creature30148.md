I would not be on the wide gantry platforms where the yeast-pans are tended, nor up near the crown where the master cylinder exhales its white plumes into the clear high sky. 

I would be in the **shudder-gap**—the narrow, vertical hollow between the inner ring of a primary collar and the unyielding flank of the black needle itself. 

It is a space no wider than a man’s chest, dark except for the dull, ember-like infrared thrum of the basalt on one side, and the sweating, riveted skin of four-inch boiler plate on the other. To be there is to exist between two conflicting infinities: the ancient, molten core of the planet pressing outward through razor-edged stone, and the patient, heavy iron of the city clamping down to hold its perch in the sky.

***

In this space, I would be a **lead-settler**—one of the few whose work is not to build or harvest, but to listen to the interface.

My body would carry the shape of the crevice: low, thick-set, with knees that have folded so often against hot iron they have lost the memory of straightening. My chest would be broad and dense, built for breathing the damp, oxygen-thin air that smells perpetually of boiled copper, sulfur brine, and hot machine grease. My skin would be that same pale, translucent river-mud color, mapped with blue veins, slick with the lukewarm brine that drips down the rock face in unending, silent braids.

I would wear no shirt—the rock against my back is eighty degrees Celsius, a dry, thrumming wall of heat that sinks into the spine like a fever. But my front, turned toward the outer iron and the vast, empty gulfs of the sky, would be shielded in thick, tallow-soaked hide to keep the freezing drafts from seizing my lungs when a down-chimney blows through. 

Around my waist, on heavy braided hemp and copper wire, hang the tools of the settle: 
*   Heavy drift-pins of soft, unalloyed lead.
*   A flat brass spatula for clearing away the crusts of mineral salts left by the weeping water.
*   And the *tapper*—not the short copper rod of the gantry-runners, but a long, double-headed hammer of vulcanized rubber and lead shot, designed to coax a tone out of a five-ton bolt without fracturing the glass-hard basalt behind it.

***

My days would have no dawn, only the shifting resonance of the mountain.

When the stone swells—expanding under the pressure of the mantle miles below—the iron collar groans. The bolts, thick as tree trunks and driven deep into the needle’s core, begin to sing. They do not snap immediately; first, they *weep*. The lead washers seated beneath the bolt heads begin to deform, extruding like cool wax under thousands of tons of shearing force. 

My purpose is to be there when the lead gives.

I would move through the gap on a web of counterweighted stirrups, suspended by grease-slicked wire ropes that vanish into the dark above. My bare palms, calloused to the toughness of cured horn, would slide along the slick obsidian, feeling for the tiny, hair-fine tremors that precede a thermal fracture. 

Every few minutes, a plate-bug—blind, plate-backed, the size of a trencher—would crawl deliberately across my lead-soled boot, grazing on the iron oxides that form around the rivets. I would not brush it away; its slow, mechanical scratching is a comfort, a sign that the local stone is cool enough to support its shell without cracking the marrow inside.

When the tension rises, I would take my drift-pin, set it against the distorted washer, and drive new lead into the seam. 

To work in that gap is to feel the entire needle-city as a single living organism through your own bones. I would rest my forehead against the damp flange of the main beam, letting the iron press into the skin over my brow:
*   Three miles above, I can feel the slow, hydraulic heave of the master piston as it nears the apex of its cycle. The vibration travels down the stanchions like the purr of a sleeping leviathan.
*   Miles out across the gulf, connected only by the gossamer web of haulage cables, I can feel the faint, rhythmic shudder of the neighbor spires, their own pistons cycling out of phase, balancing the dynamic load of the entire archipelago against the wind.
*   And below—down in the heat-haze where the base of the needle disappears into the churning, unformed crust—I can feel the deep, low-frequency hum of the rock being born. It is a sound below hearing, felt entirely in the teeth and the gut.

***

When the Stroke arrives, the world pauses.

First comes the change in air pressure—a soft, immense descent that pops the ears. Then the great relief valve miles overhead vents its sigh. A dense, lukewarm veil of vapor cascades down the chimney between the rock and the iron, washing over me like a hot, sudden bath. It washes the gray mineral grit from my eyes, softens the tallow on my apron, and rinses the slick basalt until it shines like wet jet.

In that moment of release, while the steam billows around my shoulders and drifts out through the iron grates into the wide, empty air where the drift-skates wheel, the iron settles. It gives a single, hollow, mountain-wide *thud*—the sound of ten thousand tons of metal finding its new footing on the virgin stone.

I would lean back into my sling, wipe the warm water from my beard with a lead-stained knuckle, and tap the bolt head once with the rubber mallet.

*Ping.*

The note would run clear, round, and low through the metal, traveling out along the catwalks, down through the residential pods, and across the suspension spans to the next needle.

It tells them the collar holds. The stone is still rising. The city has another three hours of breath.
