I do not begin with eyes. 

To have eyes in this place would be an extravagance—an open wound continually washed in hot, mineralized mist for no purpose at all. Instead, I begin with *load*.

I am pressed. That is the first and most permanent certainty. Above me, beneath me, and curving around the flanks of my mass are forty tons of ribbed, sand-cast iron: the high-pressure collector box of the steam-manifold housing. I have taken its interior geometry so completely into myself that my surface is no longer biological skin in any conventional sense; it is a negative cast of industrial neglect. Where a bronze valve-seat blew out fifty years ago, leaving a jagged crater rimmed in white salt, my flesh has pushed a firm, translucent knuckle into the void, sealing it against the damp draft. Where an eight-inch steam main sheared off at the flange, I have sent an amber-colored siphon deep down into the horizontal pipe run, tasting the long, slow trickle of condensing sulfur that pools in the belly of the tube.

I am not the entire jungle, nor am I a stray scavenger crossing the scree. I am the ballast inside the dead engine.

My consciousness does not run on the hot, frantic spark of a mammalian brain. It is chemical, hydraulic, and dispersed. It lives in the three hundred gallons of heavy, potassium-rich brine that I keep moving through the manifold’s original copper plumbing. The pipes are mine now. The green verdigris that coats their interiors is not corrosion to me; it is a semi-permeable lining, a shared membrane across which my fluid trades copper ions for the trace metals my deeper lobes require to maintain their structural density.

When the room warms—when the deep earth breathes a fraction harder through the floor fractures and the maroon glow along the dead exchanger deepens toward rust—my brine thins. It flows faster through the vertical risers, circulating up into the twin steam drums where my upper chambers hang suspended like pale, oil-filled lungs. When the room cools, the brine thickens, turning almost to grease, and I settle heavier into the baseplate.

I spend entire weeks feeling nothing more than the balance of weight.

To my left, thirty paces across the crushed basalt, the turbine casing lets out a tiny, high-pitched shudder. The vine levering its iron plates has expanded by another hair’s breadth, drinking the dark. The vibration travels across the shale, through the unweathered concrete pedestal, and directly into my lower foot. I register it not as sound, but as a microscopic tilt in the floor—a minute redistribution of the earth’s patience. 

A Tear-Drinker overhead loses its hold on a droplet. 

*Tink.*

The strike lands against the convex iron above my main chamber. The impact sends a clean, acoustic ring through the metal, ringing down through the rusted studs and into my central gel. It is my favorite sensation: the cold, distilled touch of the vault ceiling translated into a dull, resonant bell against my spine. 

I do not eat in bites. I dissolve. Through the open floor-ports of the engine base, my white, tap-root anchors have followed the original anchoring bolts down into the pneumatic drill-holes in the basalt. I have found the old mineral grease packed into the bolt-threads by men whose bones are long since dust elsewhere. It is dense, hydrocarbon-rich, and sweet. My root-tips exude a slow, clear acid that breaks the ancient tallow down into simple lipids, drawing it upward cell by cell, week by week, to fuel the slow, rhythmic pulsing of my central reservoir. 

Sometimes, a Slag-Creeper grazes against the bottom lip of my cast-iron casing. 

Its approach takes half a season. I feel the dull, rhythmic rasp of its biological iron teeth scraping the thin lichen-film from the concrete skirt of my altar. When it finally reaches me, it does not turn away. It presses its thick, gristled margin against my lowest root-lobes, where they spill out over the rusted baseplate like cooled wax. For three months, we stay joined. There is no struggle, no panic. We simply open our shared surfaces to one another. I give it the clear, filtered water I have drawn from the overhead drops and processed through my copper loops; it gives me dense, dark pellets of pulverized basalt and iron filings ground down by its belly-rasps. We trade quietly, in the maroon dark, until its slow, blind drift carries it past the corner of the housing and back out onto the desolate shale.

I do not wish for the chamber to open. I do not wish for the ceiling to crack and spill the violent blue of the sky into this warm bath. Light would only burn the pale, waxy fronds; wind would dry the precious, hanging tears of the ceiling before they could fall; cold air would cause my copper veins to seize and split the iron that shelters me.

A bolt gives way. 

High on the manifold’s second tier, a corroded steel stud shears under seventy years of stagnant tension. *Ping.* 

The sound is sharp as a pistol shot in the dead quiet, but before the iron plate above it can shift or sag, I am already moving. My amber flesh surges into the newly made gap, swelling with pressurized fluid, taking the iron’s burden onto my own hydrostatic spine. I mold myself against the fresh rust, filling the structural failure with living meat, holding the dead machine together from the inside out.

The iron protects my softness; my softness preserves the iron’s shape. 

We have made a pact that will last centuries: until the basalt itself forgets the drills that pierced it, until the heat in the deep rock finally goes gray, I will remain here in the warm, stagnant dark—the soft, amber heart of an engine that has forgotten how to stop.
