I am not high enough to see where the cable-towers vanish into the gray ceiling, nor low enough to hear the final, unbroken roar where the valley’s cold runoff gathers into the black sump. 

I live in the slack. 

In every vast, counterweighted system, there is a zone of neutral tension—a mid-tier shelf where the pull from above and the drag from below cancel each other out into a steady, shuddering hover. My shelter is hung directly into the crotch of a four-way shear-truss, three hundred feet below the main ore-line, where an obsolete secondary intake pipe has split open and curled back like the peel of a dried fruit. The metal there has stopped sweating pressurized water and now only weeps the slow, brown moisture of old rust and rain.

If you looked for me from the tramway above, you would see only a bulge in the moss, a heap of wet felt and split wicker bound with tarnished copper wire, indistinguishable from the clumps of strap-fern that choke the flange-plates.

***

At first, in the half-light before the morning shift of the automated diverters, I am mostly ears and teeth.

Before my eyes adjust to the green-tinted dusk that passes for dawn, I wake by the jaw. I sleep with my cheek pressed flat against a clean patch of rolled iron—a strip I scrape clear of tallow-ticks and lichen every three days with a flattened copper chisel. Through the bone of my lower mandible comes the itinerary of the valley:

*Six miles west:* the four-stroke cadence of a tri-valve pump clearing a high reservoir. Its rhythm is slightly ragged today—a hesitation on the second stroke, meaning the intake screen is clogged with dead ivy or the fat of a boiled eel. 

*Two tiers above:* the soft, rhythmic scrape of a gantry fox trotting the top lip of an empty sluice, its horn-grooved pads ticking like fingernails across the zinc.

*Beneath me, deep in the hollows:* the low, oceanic hum of the great suspension rods stretching under the morning’s condensed weight. The air is cold enough that the steel shrinks, pulling the hanging platforms up two inches, groaning against the pins.

I know my name only as a specific sequence of three lead-taps on an unlagged cold line—*ting-ting... tong*—the sound of an old, heavy socket wrench struck against the outer wall of the 40-inch drain. No one has tapped that sequence in five seasons. The family line that lived on the suspension bridge to the north lost their anchor-cables during the heavy freeze three winters back; their wicker teardrops fell silently through the mist, swallowed by the moss before they ever hit the iron below.

Now, I am simply the one who keeps the ninth bypass clear.

***

My hands are not hands anymore in the way the valley’s ancestors might have understood them. The skin of my palms is thick, yellowed, and split with permanent gray fissures packed with grease and pulverized slate. My thumbs do not oppose easily; they have thickened into wedges, good for prying open the inspection plates on frozen valve-chests. I wear a smock made of three layers of sailcloth, boiled in river-fat and rubbed with kerosene until it is as stiff as sheet-lead and sheds the perpetual mountain drip without gaining weight.

My work does not begin with an intention; it begins with an imbalance.

Around midday—when the high cloud thins just enough to turn the mist from slate to cold pearl—a shudder runs through my truss that is half an octave too sharp. The vibration doesn't dissipate into the moss; it rebounds, ringing back through the rivets with a dry, metal-on-metal chatter that sets my teeth aching.

A wedge has slipped.

I take my kit: a coil of braided hemp soaked in tallow, a pouch of dried fern-fluff, two flat chisels of soft mountain iron, and a pig-iron mallet with its face worn into a dish.

Moving through the mid-tiers is not walking. It is a slow, rhythmic folding of the body. You do not trust the moss; moss is water disguised as footing, and three hundred feet of air waits beneath a two-inch pad of it. You place your feet only where the rivet-heads break the surface—their round, rusty domes give grip to the calloused arches of bare feet. You reach for the angle-irons, hooking fingers into the lightening holes, your belly dragging against the cold, wet belly of the beam.

The trouble is at the third counterweight of the Southern Sluice.

The counterweight—a solid cube of cast iron forty feet on a side, slick with black slime—slides in vertical guides greased with rendered fox-tallow. But a mass of creeping ivy has wedged itself between the guide-rail and the shoe, dried and compacted by the immense pressure into a hard, fibrous wedge that has thrown the block two degrees out of true. The iron is screaming—not loud, because the ferns drink the high frequencies, but as a sickening, hot vibration that cooks the moisture out of the air within three yards of the rail.

If it jams completely, the next dump of aggregate from the tramway above will snap the suspension cables. The gantry will whip upward like an unstrung bow, and sixty spans of walkways will fold like wicker baskets.

I crawl into the gap beneath the guide-shoe. 

The space smells of old scalding, boiling grease, and the bitter, crushed-celery stench of bruised saxifrage. The heat off the friction-point is the only warmth in the valley, but it is a dangerous warmth—it smells of metal ready to shear.

I do not cut the ivy away with a blade. A blade would dull on the grit embedded in the vine in two strokes. Instead, I drive the iron chisel between the shoe and the rail, using the short, heavy strokes of the lead-headed mallet. *Tink. Tink. Tock.*

With each blow, the whole mountain-spanning labyrinth seems to answer. Siphon-frogs miles away pause their drumming. A gantry fox, startled from its boiler-nest two tiers up, appears on an overhead catwalk, its gray brush hanging low, watching me through the weeping fog with eyes like yellow oil.

The chisel bites. The compressed vegetal mass gives way with a sound like a tearing boot. The forty-ton block groans, drops a fraction of an inch, and settles squarely back onto its greased runner with a heavy, wet *thump* that ripples through the deck, sending a shower of dislodged condensation raining down through fifty feet of empty steel beneath my heels.

The heat subsides. The cool, wet mist immediately rushes back into the seam, quenching the iron with a soft, dying hiss.

***

I sit in the inspection sleeve for a long time afterward, my knees pulled up to my chin, letting the cold water drip onto the crown of my head. 

I reach into my pouch and pull out a strip of dried eel-meat, chewing slowly. It tastes of river-mud and the bitter zinc of the troughs it swam in before the turbine purge washed it into my nets.

While I chew, I look down through the lattice.

Down through the third tier, through the fourth, through the hanging gardens of black strap-fern and the dripping webs of tallow-ticks, down to where the light dies completely. Somewhere down there, in the dark joints where the great foundation trusses meet the mountain walls, my grandfather is still at work.

He is not buried; he is working. His body, bound in fern-leaves and copper, was wedged forty years ago into the expansion gap of the main western aqueduct. By now, the cold water and forty million tons of shifting iron will have done their work. He will be flat now—hard, black, smooth as anthracite, sealing the gap between the plates so the cold mountain runoff doesn't scour the pivot-pin to rust.

My own knees click when I bend them now. There is a hardness forming in my left hip, a mineral stiffness that comes from sixty years of breathing sulfur-steam and drinking water that has run over twenty miles of galvanized zinc. 

One day, the *ting-ting... tong* on the pipe will go unanswered for good. Someone—perhaps one of the children from the north spans who still know how to splice hemp, or perhaps no one at all—will find where the ninth bypass has begun to chatter. They will find me here, curled into the dead space behind a valve-chest.

They will not weep. Weeping adds salt to the iron, and salt brings rust faster than the mist. 

They will simply wrap me in the heavy, sodden fronds, bind my wrists to my ankles with copper wire from an old dynamo, and wedge me into the track where the fourth counterweight groans.

And I will take the weight. I will settle into the seam, cold and greasy and dense, while the rain falls through the suspension towers and the foxes hunt the fall, keeping the valley level between the mountains and the dark.
