I am not up where the light catches the tops of the ribs. 

I am underneath, tucked into the dripping hollow where the underside of a three-century-old ironwood arch meets the great belly of a swollen scarf. 

Here, the world is dark amber and green-black. The wood does not look like timber; it looks like the flank of a drowned animal, slick with cold, glistening liverwort and seeping that slow, viscous lymph that coats the palms like tallow. It smells of crushed fern, tannic acid, and old grease. 

To find me, you would have to look where the structure gathers into its heaviest knots. You would have to climb down past the cradles of the living, past the walkways where the young splicers lead their green bines across the gulfs, down into the blind under-bays where the air is so thick with warm mist it feels like breathing through wet silk.

My back is curved to the radius of the beam. My skin has the pallor of a turnip root, gray-white and soft from seventy years of soaking in condensation, though my fingers are permanently stained the deep, oily black of walnut husk. My legs are thin and hooked, rarely straightened; they are tucked up against my ribs, keeping my footprint small, while my prehensile toes remain locked around a four-strand bine of plaited gut that hums like an iron harp-string two inches beneath my hip.

I am a *tuner of the scarf*. 

***

All day—if there were days here, where the sourceless twilight never lifts—my forehead rests directly against the cedar grain. 

I do not look out into the void. Looking into the empty miles of gray-green vapor tells you nothing; the mist lies, the distance dissolves, and the drift-herds move like ghosts through cubic leagues of air that offer no horizon. But the grain never lies. 

Through the bone of my forehead, the bridge is an engine of frequencies:

Three miles above, a young vine has been pulled too taut by a rising thermal; it is singing in a thin, glass-sharp whistle that sets my back teeth aching. 

Below me, twelve leagues down into the unmapped drop where the timbers rot into soil that has nowhere to fall, a lower bay has given way. I felt the shock an hour ago—not as a sound, but as a dull, liquid *thuck* that traveled up through sixty miles of spliced cedar, followed by a sudden, shuddering heave as this arch, my arch, took on another four tons of dead-load. 

When that happened, the joint against which I am nestled gave a slow, wet groan. 

In my right hand, I hold a scraper shaped from the scapula of my father's sister. In my left, a basket of dry willow wedges kept sealed in bladders of fat. 

My work is very slow. If you watched me for an entire cycle of mist, you might think I was only a growth of pale moss or a sleeping knot-dweller. But then my hand moves. With the bone scraper, I peel away a thin, translucent shaving of spongy, water-softened sapwood from the lip of the scarf. The wood underneath is hard, purple-brown, and alive, bitter with juice. 

Then, where the old hemp lashing has stretched a hair’s breadth under the sudden load, I slide a single split-wood wedge into the gap. 

I do not hammer it. Hammering sends violent, shattering ripples through wet grain. Instead, I place my thumb against the blunt end of the wedge, brace my shoulder against the opposing strut, and lean my whole life’s weight into the seam, pressing the wedge home until I feel the grain bite into grain. 

The hum changes. 

The glass-sharp shriek overhead drops half an octave, settling into a deep, vibrating drone that runs down my spine and vibrates in the hollow of my pelvis. The tension is reconciled. The weight has found a new path through the fibers, diverted away from the rotting tendon and into the living heartwood.

***

Beside me, in the deep fissure between the two spliced trunks, a family of leech-mice rustles. 

They are warm against my side, small and soft as peeled plums, their sensitive whiskers trembling against my ribs as they lap the sweet, bitter sweat that oozes from the fresh cut I just made. We do not mind each other. They eat the wood-rot that would soften the trunnels; I keep the joint from shearing and crushing their nest. Sometimes, when the draft from the bottomless bays blows cold and wet for days on end, I draw three or four of them into the folds of my bark-wrap to hold their heat against my chest.

Ten feet below my hanging cradle, trailing into the vast, warm emptiness, the sticky white sensory lines of a suture-maw hang motionless, like silk threads suspended in oil. Now and then, a drifting organism—some buoyant, rib-finned creature that tumbled out of the upper thermals—touches a line. There is a wet *snap*, a momentary shudder through the vine that anchors the predator, and then the quiet returns, heavier than before.

I know what will happen to me. My joints are already thick and stiff with the damp; my lungs wheeze with the sweet, perpetual mildew of the deep bays. 

When my thumbs can no longer press a wedge home, and my jaw can no longer read the tone of the wood, one of the younger splicers from the upper nocks will climb down with their oiled bags and bone chisels. They will lower me down into the soft liverwort of the main burl. They will scrape the flesh from my long bones with the same patient rhythm I used on the timber. 

My femurs will be split and carved into two large, six-fluted trunnels. My ribs will become scrapers for someone who is currently only a child learning to weave gut in the upper arches. 

They will drill two holes straight through this very scarf joint where I am sitting. They will drive my bones through the living ironwood and the cured cedar, pinning them together so tight that the sap will seal around the marrow-holes. 

And I will stay here. 

I will stop listening from the outside and become part of the hearing itself. When the great gales sweep through the upper voids and the thousands of miles of woven bines tighten against the sky, my bones will feel the strain, refusing the drop, holding the world together across the empty air for another three hundred years.
