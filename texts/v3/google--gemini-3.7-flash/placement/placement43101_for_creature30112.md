I would not be at the outer lip, where the bone-slat visors must be drawn against the white fury of the flats, nor up in the screaming, suffocating heat of the bat-crust where the drying-racks bake. 

I would be settled in the knuckle of the Third Tier down, wedged into the crook where a centuries-old taproot pillar meets the inward curve of the limestone belly. It is a slow, heavy junction—a place where the tension of seven suspension gangways gathers into a single, hair-wound ironwood bollard, polished to the dark, gleaming black of old tortoiseshell by the grip of four hundred years of passing toes.

I am a binder of under-ribs.

My body has the shape of a river-stone that has forgotten the water: wide-hipped, low-bellied, with shoulders set heavy and flat against my neck from decades of leaning out over open air. My feet are broader than my hands, the outer toes splayed and hooked like root-tendrils, habitually curled around the rough wicker rim of my work-basket without my needing to look. My skin is the color of smoked lard, cross-hatched at every bend with deep, pale creases where the golden flour of the seed-flails has settled and stayed. When I blink, my double lashes click softly together, catching the floating chaff before it can reach the dark slate of my eyes.

Between my knees rests a low, shallow tray of fired clay, warmed by a flat cake of smoldering swift-lime tucked beneath its base. In the tray, a dark, viscous pool of mastic sap, crushed dust-beetle carapaces, and rendered mouse-fat bubbles with a slow, thick *plop-plop*, releasing a dense steam of bitter resin and toasted grease that coats my tongue.

In my left hand is an awl ground from the heel-bone of an old weaver whose name I spoke when I was small; in my right is a cured rib.

It has just come down from the high flue—cured so dry and light it hums like a plucked string when I tap it with a fingernail. It belonged to a carter who broke his hip on the lower ladders two moons ago. The salt is gone from it; the hot drafts of the roosts have baked it to the color of dry cedar. My work is to pare away the last brittle edges with a flint scraper, drill the lash-holes through the broad end, and stitch it into the spring-frame beneath the floor of a young couple’s hut three spans out along the eastern vine.

I work slowly. A hasty breath costs spit, and spit is not to be spent on hurry.

Beside my thigh, curled into the hollow between my hip and the rock wall, two cushion-mice sleep in a single, round heap of dun fur. Their thick, fat-swollen tails are wrapped over their blind snouts, their small flanks rising and falling in hot, rapid little hitches. Every few minutes, without breaking the rhythm of my flint, I reach down and wipe the scraper across the oily ridge of the nearest tail, letting the animal's secreted grease lubricate the stone blade. The mouse does not wake; it merely twitches its whiskers, content in the baking pocket of my hide apron.

Above my head, the town breathes its continuous, vibrating roar—the rasp of the grinding-slates, the dry rattle of beetle-wing cloaks shifting on the ladders, the high, rhythmic *clack-click* of children signaling with hollow seed-shells across the drop. Through the soles of my feet, pressed against the main cable, I feel the precise weight and gait of everyone moving across the mid-tiers. I know the limping drag of the seed-beater coming back from the storage silos; I know the light, skittering scramble of the boys hauling guano-cakes down from the rigging; I know the sudden, sharp shudder that means a drop-lizard has leaped from a platform strut to snap a hornet out of the air.

Presently, there is a dry scratching on the rock behind my shoulder. A pale snout appears, followed by four feet of gray, mottled scales. The lizard does not pause for permission; it flows across my bare forearm—its belly cold and dry as slate, its suction-rimmed toes tugging lightly at the hair on my skin—and settles its chin directly over my glue-pot, waiting for the heat to draw the wood-borers out of the nearby timber seam. I do not shoo it. I lean my elbow slightly to the left to give its tail room to hang clear of the wet mastic, and our breath mingles in the amber gloom: mine smelling of parched seed, its of crushed chitin.

Here, in the warm, suspended belly of the shelf, nothing is lonely because nothing is empty. 

The floor I sit upon was stepped on by my mother, and the rib in my hand will hold up a cradle tomorrow. The air that fills my chest has already cooled the bats, dried the gum, and brushed the feathers of the swifts before dropping down to my ledge. 

When my own knuckles grow too thick with salt to turn the awl, and the fat leaves my marrow, they will carry me up the notched taproots to the hot dark under the ceiling. I will lie in the smoke and the sweet, baking heat until the wind makes me light, and then someone whose toes are just now learning to grip the wicker will bring me down, split my shin into shuttles, and bind the rest of me into the swing of the bridge, where I can go on taking the weight of the town as it walks.
