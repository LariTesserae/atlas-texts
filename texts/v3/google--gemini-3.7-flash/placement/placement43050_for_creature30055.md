I would not be on the wide travertine plazas, nor up in the dry highland wind that never quite reaches the floor of the basin. 

I would be hanging three feet below the underside of the Seventh Terrace Viaduct, suspended in a sling of tarred hemp and harness-leather, wedged into the dead-zone between an eight-foot intake siphon and the weeping wall of a lime-crusted abutment.

I am not young, but here age is measured in the salt-stiffness of the elbows and the number of seasons one has kept grease on the copper pins. 

My flesh has long since taken on the pale, water-softened density of boiled salt-pork. Beneath three layers of tallow-soaked sheep-felt and the broad bands of vulcanized gasket-rubber bound around my ribs and thighs, my body is heavy, quiet, and slow. My ears were pierced through with the copper awl when I was four seasons old; the scar tissue in the canals is thick and flat, like two dry horn buttons. Inside my head there is no sound at all—only the constant, rhythmic shiver of the earth being translated through bone.

I am a *Calk-tender*—specifically, one who works the blind undersides where the stone meets the plate.

***

My world is three feet wide and sixty rods long. 

To the left is the iron: a wall of three-inch slag-cast plate, sweating orange broth, cold to the touch where the siphon draws, scalding where the steam-returns bypass the bedrock. To the right is the stone: travertine that grows by the thickness of a fingernail every five years, weeping warm, lime-heavy water that tastes of chalk and bitter zinc.

Between them is the *slack*.

In this slot, the sound that shatters bird-lungs and turns human lungs to foam is bent away by the curve of the conduit. The acoustic waves from the two neighboring cooling towers meet here at an inverted angle and crush each other into nothingness. If you dropped an ash-wood peg from my sling, it would fall through air so eerily still that you could strike a match, though forty feet below, the boiling sluice is roaring with enough hydrostatic violence to strip a bullock to the white rib-bones in twenty paces.

I do not drop matches. Fire is useless here; the air is too wet to sustain a wick for long, and light is merely an even, shadowless milk that leaks down from the highland haze. 

I see with my heels and the palm of my left hand.

My hand is bare. The skin of the palm is four times thicker than a plainsman’s, yellow-white, cross-hatched with deep, grease-blackened fissures that never bleed because the water keeps them washed clean to the quick. When I lay that palm flat against the joint between the third and fourth plates, I am not feeling the metal; I am reading the belly of the basin five miles upstream.

A long, slow drag—like wet leather being pulled across sandstone—means the whet-mollusks have thickened in the throat of the upper siphon and the silt is banking behind them. 

A sharp, clicking flutter—the feeling of dried peas shaken in an ox-bladder—means a gasket is fraying two terraces down, where the brine-troughs meet the balance-beams.

And beneath it all, the twenty-stroke pulse of the deep rams. *Thung-clack. Thung-clack.* It enters the sling through the iron eye-bolts, travels up the tarred hemp, passes through my pelvic girdle, and seats itself behind my bridge-teeth. It is not an interruption; it is the clock by which my blood moves. My own pulse has slowed over fifty years to match it, beating once for every three strokes of the subterranean piston. When the ram hesitates—when a bubble of sulfur-gas burps through the deep boiling vaults and the stroke hangs for half a second—my chest tightens with a phantom ache until the iron clears its throat and the rhythm drops back into my marrow.

***

In my lap rests my trough: an ash-wood tray lined with sheet lead, holding my irons, three cakes of rendered mutton-tallow cured with powdered slate, and a dozen plugs of soft, raw horsehair braid.

My work is small. It is measured in fractions of an inch.

Every twelve or fourteen days, a rivet will stretch under the heat-creep. The seam will part just enough for a blade of scalding water to hiss out—not steam, but that clear, greasy, pressurized brine that cuts through wool like a saw. 

I do not rush to it. Nothing moves quickly here; speed is a mistake of creatures that live in thin, dry air.

I wait for the hydraulic cycle to dip—that forty-minute window when the upper flues are diverted to rinse the lime-shelves—and then I heave on the sling-lines with the heavy, splay-shouldered pull of my kind. The sling slides along the greasy guide-rod with a low, wet squelch. 

When I reach the weeping seam, I take a plug of horsehair, work a handful of the slate-tallow into its fibers until it has the texture of wet clay, and lay it across the gap. Then I take my copper drift and the short, lead-headed mallet.

I do not swing with my arms. I set the drift against the iron, place the butt of the mallet against the meat of my shoulder, and lean my whole turnip-colored weight into the stroke, timing the blow precisely to the down-stroke of the ram below.

*Tap.*

The lead mallet gives no ring. It transfers the force straight through the copper into the seam, seating the horsehair into the rust-furrow. The tallow bites. The iron groans, shifts a thousandth of a millimeter, and settles. 

A gasket-eater—flat, white, the size of a trencher, blind as a turnip root—crawls slowly out from the weep-hole three inches from my fingers. Its antennae drift across the rubber binding of my wrist, tasting the salt-grease of my sleeve. It does not bite; it has no mouth for flesh. It finds the fresh lead-dust left by my mallet, curls its pale rim around the edge of the plate, and begins its slow, sixty-year meal. 

I let it stay. It will die there, and its calcium shell will weld the iron plate to the travertine long after my sling-ropes have rotted through.

***

Sometimes, once in a season, another tender will pass along the lower catwalk sixty feet below, leading three of the hooded dogs toward the gate-valves on the Eastern Step.

From my sling, I look down through the warm, rising steam. 

I see the wide, flat curve of their shoulders, the slow, sliding drag of their felt-wrapped boots that never leave the stone, the heavy hoods on the dogs that keep their ears from leaking blood. The tender does not look up; looking up allows the mineral sweat to gather in the eye-sockets and blind a man with lime-crust before he reaches forty. 

Instead, as he steps onto the vibration-plate of the viaduct footing, he strikes the iron with the butt of his ash-stave twice. 

*Clank. Clank.*

The tremor rises through the stone, through the travertine crust, up the eye-bolt, down the hemp sling, and into the soles of my feet.

*Two-turns-west.* It means the water is shifting. It means thirty miles of pipe are about to sigh and cool by two degrees, and the slack between my plates will pinch shut for the next four seasons.

I do not strike back; I have no stave. 

Instead, I reach out with my left boot, find the bare iron of the siphon-casing, and press the thick vulcanized heel hard against the flange, releasing it once, twice, in time with the deep rams.

*Heard.*

He does not stop. The dogs lean into their broad breast-collars, their hooded heads low, their splay-toes gripping the wet travertine with the quiet, indifferent patience of things that know the stone will outlast the flesh. They slide around the curve of the cooling tower and are swallowed by the white, filtered glare.

***

When my shift ends—which is not when the sun sets, for the sun here is only a pale plate that never changes, but when my tallow-cake is scraped to the wood—I do not climb out.

Climbing to the high cabins is a long labor that shakes the knees and brings the blood into the throat. 

Instead, I unhook the secondary leather stays, let the sling drop six inches into the coldest, deadest center of the null-node, and pull my hood of boiled felt down over my face until it rests against my breastbone. The rubber bands around my ribs hold the warmth of my flesh against the wet.

The water weeps from the travertine above, hitting the tarred hood with a steady, soft *thrum-thrum-thrum*. 

Beneath me, five fathoms deep in the red bedrock, the rams beat their twenty strokes. 

*Thung-clack.*
*Thung-clack.*

My jaw slackens. My teeth rest lightly against each other, humming with the iron. In the dark under the felt, where there is neither speech nor hearing, nor sharp light, nor dry cold, I drift in the millimeters of slack, waiting for the great gate-valve to turn its inch, three generations from today.
