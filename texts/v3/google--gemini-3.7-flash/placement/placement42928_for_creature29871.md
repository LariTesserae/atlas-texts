The first thing to understand is that I would have no use for a name that could not be struck with a single, dry blow against a six-inch drainage collar. Names here are weights, and anything with too many syllables gets blurred into the soup before it reaches the corner of the dry-goods alley.

I would be wedged—not sitting, not floating, but settled with three pounds of lead shot in the belly of my apron—into the crook where a secondary steam-bypass punches through the lintel of an old apothecary. 

It is a good corner. The brickwork has bulged here, creating a slow eddy where the upward drift from the lower flues pauses to swirl before rolling toward the high rafters. The water is ninety-two degrees against my ribs, thick as mutton broth, carrying the sour-sweet tang of boiled marrow-fat and stale sulfur. If I let my knuckles drift outward, the fine green fur along the ridge of my shoulders stirs like pond weed, catching the amber glow cast by the bacterial sheen on the pipe five feet above me.

My hammer rests against my hip. Its haft is ash, seasoned until it turned black in a vat of machine grease, bound with three turns of copper wire so it will not slip when my palm is slick with paraffin.

***

In the beginning, you think you are looking for things with your eyes. That is a mistake of the upper dry.

Here, my eyes are only good for six yards of amber broth before the tenement facades dissolve into tea-stained wool. I use them mostly to watch for the carp—not because they matter, but because when four or five of them drift out of the parlor windows with their dorsal fins laid flat, it means the lower bypass has belched an oily bubble of coal gas, and the thermal current will shift in thirty heartbeats.

Instead, I keep my cheek pressed against the iron flange.

Through the bone of my jaw, the district speaks its whole anatomy. It is never silent, but it never screams. 

To my left, three blocks deep in the sediment: the walking beam takes its long, blind breath. *Hhhhun-tock.* It takes two minutes for the counterweight to drop through the dense slime of the pit, dragging its great iron knee toward the pavement. The stroke displaces twenty tons of red-brown water; I feel the rust-cloud before it arrives—a gentle thickening of the grain between my lips, a faint metallic prickle on the soft skin behind my ears. 

When the beam bottoms out, there is a soft, deep shiver that rattles the small bones in my middle ear. That is the dead elder’s grease doing its work in the sleeve. If the shiver is smooth, like lard rubbed between thumb and forefinger, the basin will stay warm through my sleep. If it chatters—even a single, dry *tink-tink* in the wrist-pin—it means the lime scale has cut through the tallow, and someone must take the pig-iron and the bone chisel down into the black.

I am not the one who goes down today. That is for the younger hands whose lungs can hold the stale gas for fifteen strokes of the beam. 

My work is the ring.

***

I unhook the hammer.

I do not swing from the shoulder. The water fights anything wide; it turns a blow into a slow, foolish push. I strike from the wrist, three inches of travel, catching the flange on its crispest corner.

*Clang.*

The note does not travel as sound in the air; it expands outward through the cast-iron skeleton of the four blocks. It punches into the water, rattles the window-leads of the flooded bakeries, and runs straight up the downspouts into the Dry Pockets where the sleepers turn in their eelgrass slings.

One beat. Two.

From the far gantry—half a mile away through the weed-choked gloom of the boiler-house square—comes the answer. A lighter iron, tapping twice on a high-pressure valve casing. 

*Clink. Clink.*

The lantern in the maintenance chamber is still burning. The leaded glass holds. The oil is clean.

I tuck the hammer back against my greasy hemp sash and draw my legs up. My soles—hard as horse-hooves, yellowed with decades of standing on lime-encrusted iron rails—find the mortar joint between two soft bricks. I lever myself out of the nook with the slow, hydraulic indifference of an eel leaving a drain.

***

You do not kick. If you kick, you stir the silt from the lintels, and then you spend an hour coughing out red grit that tastes of 1880.

You pull. Hand over hand along the rusted decorative iron of the balconies. My fingers are broad, flattened at the tips, the nails thick and grey like horn spoons. Every three feet, I run a thumb along the seam where the lead caulking meets the brick. The water here wants to get into everything; it wants to turn mortar into white paste and let the masonry slide into the street. Where the caulking has rotted out, I reach into my pouch for a lump of cold tallow mixed with powdered slate and iron dust, thumbing it into the gap until the stone stops weeping.

It is quiet work. A tallow-crab, its back encrusted with red boiler scale until it looks like a loose cobblestone, creeps over my knuckle, its blunt peg-legs seeking the mineral sweat on my skin. I do not brush it off. We share the same grease.

***

When my chest begins to tighten—not with pain, but with that heavy, sour weight that says the air in my tissues has turned to soda—I turn upward toward the third-floor roof-line.

The dormer window has been knocked out. In its place, the brickwork forms an inverted funnel, black with soot on the inside. 

I pull myself up through the window frame, my shoulders sliding through curtains of pale liverwort that feel like wet chamois. The water grows warmer here—almost hot, trapped under the ceiling by the rising steam. 

My head breaks through.

*Chup.*

The surface has the skin of old tea. Above it, the air of the Dry Pocket hits my face like a warm, greasy rag. It is ninety-five degrees in the dome, thick with methane, sulfur-bloom, and the heavy, sweet reek of eight Loomers sweating in their hammocks.

I hang by my elbows from the drowned sill, letting the water support nine-tenths of my weight. In the dim, olive-drab light that filters up from the floor-hole, the attics look like the inside of an upturned boat. The plaster has long since turned to grey mush, but the lath holds, furred with dry salt crystals that sparkle when someone shifts in their sling.

Old Marta is awake. She is sixty years deep; her skin has the translucent, yellowish gleam of a tallow candle that has sat too close to a hearth, and her eyelids are so thin you can see the dark circles of her blind pupils through them. 

She doesn't look down. She doesn't have to. She feels the displacement of my chest in the pool.

"The four-inch main," she says. Her voice is not a voice at all, but a wet, raspy croak, rusted at the hinges from fifty years of breathing damp gas. "The one by the tallow-trough."

"Clean," I say. My tongue feels thick, coated in paraffin. "The beam took two strokes while I held the flange. No chatter."

She grunts. Her hammock creaks—dry hemp against iron hooks. 

"The water is sweet today," she murmurs, turning her face back toward the damp plaster where the salt blooms. "Tastes of linseed. They must have broken into the old paint-cellar under the livery."

"Aye," I say. "Linseed and warm iron."

***

I do not climb out of the water. To pull your whole weight into the dry air is an agony of gravity; your limbs feel like lead ingots, your ribs creak under the strange, unsupported burden of your lungs, and the thin air tastes of nothing.

Instead, I let my chin rest on the rim of the floor-hole, my body dangling down into the tea-colored warmth of the flooded parlor below. 

Down there, forty feet in the amber gloom, a blind carp drifts through the skeleton of a chandelier, its pale tail stirring a drift of golden sulfur-dust from the crystals. Below that, the great beam begins its long, creeping return, rising against the weight of the drowned city, sucking another gallon of boiling brine up from the belly of the world.

I close my eyes. 

Through the skin of my belly, through the grease-soaked cloth of my apron, through the three inches of tallow that keep my blood from turning to soda, I feel the stroke begin. 

Up. 

Slow. 

Smooth as lard. 

We have another fifty years.
