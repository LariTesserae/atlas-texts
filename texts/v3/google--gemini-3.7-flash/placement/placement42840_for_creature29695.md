At first, there is only the boxwood.

It sits between the back molars—dense, oily, bitter with the taste of old river-grease—and the jaw aches around it with a steady, dull lockjaw cramp. If I let my mouth open by a millimeter, the hydrostatic hammer will take the tongue, peel the lips back over the gums, and fill the lungs before the hand can even twitch toward the release cord. So the world begins there: a two-inch cube of dry heartwood crushed between teeth, grounding the skull against the concussion of the vent.

I am not the diver who rides the crystal to the sun. 

I am the one who stays below the pine platform, wedged into the dead-angle where the western wall bells outward before it shears into the abyss. 

My back is pressed flat against a vertical rib of freshly split slate—so sharp that even through the oiled pig-leather and the curved ash-plate strapped across my spine, I can feel its vitreous edge biting into the grain of the wood. The current does not pull me; it pins me. It holds me against the stone like an iron hand flattened against a church door, a pressure so immense that my ribs cannot expand outward—I have learned to breathe only into the pit of my belly, shallow and tight, rationing the air trapped in my throat.

Through the green glass of the spectacles, sealed to the orbit of my eyes with cold mutton tallow, the world is a violent cathedral of pale emerald and white fire. The sunlight coming down from the overhead slit does not illuminate the water; it cuts it into falling sheets of light, like panes of sunlit glass dropped through an endless chute.

Beneath the deafening hydraulic roar—which I no longer hear with my ears, long since turned to scarred, silent gristle—my skeleton registers the rift’s interior life. 

It travels up through my heels, through the brass-wire stitches of my boots, into the shins and the pelvis: the high, glassy *tink* of the quartz face spitting a new flake; the deep, gut-churning thud of a steam-heave miles below in the dark roots of the peak; the rapid, sewing-machine chatter of the tallow-threads whipping themselves raw against the slate three feet to my left.

My right hand is gloved in stiff, boiled hide, but the thumb is bare, calloused to the density of horn. 

I reach into the seam.

You do not look for the sky-spar with your eyes. In this water, a three-foot plate of virgin selenite is completely, terrifyingly invisible. It has no index of refraction; it casts no shadow; it does not ripple. It is a presence defined only by the absence of current. Where the torrent should be tearing the skin from your knuckles, there is suddenly a patch of dead, frozen stillness—a plane of absolute, razor-sharp nothingness growing straight out of the rift at the speed of cooling lead.

My bare thumb finds the edge. 

It is cold—colder even than the glacial water, carrying the sharp, iron-burn of deep-earth frost. The edge catches the grain of my skin, shaving off a paper-thin curl of horn without drawing blood. 

I slide the flat of my hand along its flank. It is flawless. No pitting yet from the cavitation bubbles. No fractures running through its heart. It is four fingers thick at the root, thinning to a leaf at the lip, growing in the dark of the crevice like a frozen lung.

Beside my knee, clamped to the same rock rib, two split-fleas are grazing. Through the green lens, their mica-plated backs look like coins of dull brass. They do not care about me; to them, my leather-wrapped leg is just another pine spar wedged into the flow. Their iron-tipped hooks are driven deep into the micro-fissures, their mouthparts humming against the quartz with a high, dry vibration that I can feel through the sole of my right foot. For a second, our rhythms align: the beast rasping the rock, the mountain heaving in its flue, and my left hand lifting the cold-forged copper wedge.

I do not use the mallet yet.

I wait for the diver above me—the younger one, whose lungs are still wide and whose ribs have not yet set into the rigid barrel-shape of the old Cold-Bitten—to set his descending-stone against the log. I feel his signal travel down the stays: three heavy, rhythmic tugs through the greasy cordage looped around my wrist. 

*Ready.*

I seat the copper wedge behind the root of the crystal plate. The metal is soft enough that it will not shatter the selenite, but heavy enough to bite into the newborn slate. I take the bronze hammer from my thigh.

The water resists the swing, thick as honey, trying to wrench the tool upward toward the sky. I must use the current’s own spiral to bring the hammer down, leaning my shoulder into the shear, letting the downward eddy carry the bronze head home.

*Clink.*

The blow does not make a sound; it makes a shockwave that rattles the boxwood between my molars and sends a shower of microscopic bubbles spiraling off the copper head. 

The spar gives. 

I feel the plate release along its root with a shudder like a breaking bowstring. For a fraction of a second, it rests entirely against the palms of my hands—weightless, invisible, lethal—before the upward draw of the vent catches its underside.

I shove it upward, clear of the rock-pocket, straight into the chest of the diver above. 

I see him catch it against his ash-boards, his legs wrapping around the crystal to keep it from spinning, his green goggles flashing once in the sun-ribbon as he reaches down and jerks the pin on his counterweight boulder.

Then he is gone. 

The upward shear takes him like a stone shot from a sling, a streak of white foam and trailing tallow-line vanishing into the boiling glare of the overhead slit, carrying the mountain’s cold glass out into the clouds.

And I remain.

I pull my knees up against the slate rib. I take a fresh copper wedge from the pouch at my hip, feeling its cold, oily weight with my bare thumb. The current hits the empty seam where the spar just lived, churning in a tight, violent vortex that strips the tallow from my spectacle-seals and leaves the clean, metallic taste of ozone on the edges of my lips.

I set the wedge. I wait for the next stone to drop from the sky.
