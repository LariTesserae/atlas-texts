At first, I would not be easily distinguished from the architecture. 

If you looked across the sunken terrace from the basalt rim, you might mistake me for a swelling in an ironwood bole, or a bundle of spare hickory laths bound in oiled canvas and lashed to the low-pressure manifold. I am not high enough to share the wind with the lead-colored chime-swallows, nor have I gone so deep into the damp basalt trenches that my eyes have filmed over like the Graft-weavers’. 

I live in the middle-clearance: twelve to twenty feet above the flagstones, where the hickory rods swing their long, horizontal arcs and the iron junction boxes bite hardest into the living wood.

I would be a **Tolerance-Watcher**—a Lineman whose task is neither to build nor to steer, but simply to remain seated at the critical interfaces and listen to the seam where living plant-flesh meets cold pig iron.

***

My quarters are an oval hollow cut directly behind the three-way distribution manifold of the second pump engine. The chamber is dry and dark, smelling continuously of warm tallow, dried cedar shavings, and the sweet, medicinal reek of pressurized sap weeping into boiled linseed oil. 

I wake not by a bell, but by the shift in the baseline draft. When the pale, shadowless sky overhead begins to thin the mist at the basin’s rim, the leaves thirty cubits above my roof turn their undersides to the grey light. The vascular pressure changes; the hickory rod beside my sleeping-niche gives a soft, three-tone groan as the brass piston eases out of its trunk-sleeve, and the floor beneath my hip lifts by half the thickness of a fingernail.

My body has long since taken on the requirements of this elevation. My calves and thighs are thick, dense with slow-twitch muscle, but my shoulders are narrow and loose, capable of folding into the tight air-flues behind the accumulator jackets. My skin is pale—the color of dry ash—and slicked every dawn with cold pine wax to keep the mist-droplets from settling into the pores. I have no hair on my body; hair catches the micro-spray of mineral oil and gathers lint from the vibration-dampers, and lint in a bronze bushing is an untended sin.

My hands are strange, even to me. The pads of the first three fingers on each hand are calloused flat and smooth, like the sole of a wooden shoe, but beneath that dead horn the nerves have become acutely sensitive to heat and frequency. 

I do not use gauges of glass or mercury. When the counterweight drops—*thud*, four hundred yards away into its bed of sphagnum—I place my bare palm against the junction plate where eight bronze bolts pierce the cambium of the main pillar. 

I wait.

Through the plate comes the shivering return-wave. It passes up through the basalt floor, rises through the taproot, hums through the heartwood, and enters the bolt-heads. 

If the bolt is seated true, the hum is a single, clear, low tone that resonates in the bones behind my ears. If rot has found a purchase behind the sap-plug, or if the tree has laid down a ring of soft summer-growth against the thread, the tone splits. It buzzes. It tastes in my palm like dry straw.

When that happens, I take my brass drift and my boxwood mallet from the sling at my waist. I climb out onto the limb—moving heel-first, swinging my torso in counter-cadence to the five-minute piston stroke so my ears do not sicken—and I tap the amber plug three turns deeper into the bark, sealing the pressure until the tree grows over the wound and makes the iron part of its own skeleton.

***

I do not talk much. There is no one within shouting distance who could hear a voice over the deep, percussive sigh of the accumulators, and shouting would only break the rhythm of the lungs. 

My companions are smaller, slower things.

Often, as I sit wedged between the iron manifold and the rough, furrowed flank of the ironwood, an oil-beetle will climb out of the copper chase and walk across my bare foot. It is heavy—the weight of a flat river stone—and its blued carapace is cold against my tallow-rubbed skin. It does not bite. Its feathered antennae sweep the space between my toes, tasting the salt of my sweat, then it moves onward, methodically grazing the microscopic green scale from the brass grease-cup beside my ankle. Its passage leaves a faint, warm streak of paraffin that smells of wintered soil. I let it pass; we are both kept here by the same machinery.

Sometimes, in the long lulls between the mid-day strokes, a Chime-swallow will drop down from the ventilator flues. It will perch on the hickory rod just two feet from my face, its lead-colored feathers tight against its narrow body. It tilts its head, looking at me with a flat, dark eye that has never seen a predator or a open field. Then, with a sudden, mechanical snap of its throat—*clink-clink-tack*—it mimics the sound of the relief valve twenty yards away, turns once on its wire-thin claws, and launches back into the grey vertical gloom.

***

In the evening, when the light across the flagstones turns from uniform ash to the color of wet slate, I draw my ration of marrow-whey from the bypass spigot below my floor. It is thick, pale, and bitter with iron salts, but it sits heavy and quiet in the belly. 

I sit at the lip of my junction-node, my legs dangling into the thirty-foot drop, and watch the yard go dormant. 

The strokes do not stop—they never stop—but they lengthen. The pistons take five minutes to draw, six minutes to return. The plume of vapor from the accumulator drifts lower, settling like fine fleece across the empty flagstones. Below me, on the wide floor of braided ironwood, a single Weaver might emerge from a service trench, a dark, broad shape dragging a bag of peat, moving with the heavy, unhurried cadence of a root pushing through wet clay. 

We do not wave. He feels the vibrations of my seat through his soles; I feel the scrape of his bone spade through the pillar behind my back. That is sufficient greeting.

One day, forty or sixty seasons from now, my fingers will lose their tolerance. The skin will grow too thick to hear the buzz in the bronze threads, or a hickory rod will snap during an unseasonal frost and catch me before I can shift my weight. 

When that happens, they will find me already stiffened into the curve of the manifold. They will wrap me in linen, paint my face with raw turpentine, and carry me down the ash staves to the sphagnum pits beneath the fourth engine. 

I do not dread it. To lie there, under the three-ton iron counterweight, is only to move down forty feet into the ground I have spent my life listening to—to be pressed, stroke by stroke, season by season, through the moss and into the basalt cracks, until my bones are as hard as the ironwood and my weight is just another fraction of the pressure that drives the brass pistons toward the sky.
