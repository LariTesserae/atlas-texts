To start, you would look for me in the seam—in the four inches of dead, compressed grease that separates the basalt from the vulcanized root-mat. 

I am not an engine that moves. Movement here is an expensive debt, paid in pressurized fluid and sheared teeth, and the drop is always waiting to collect the balance. Better to be the thing through which the work is done. 

At first, if you scraped away the rubbery wool of the fern-bases with an iron edge, you would find only what looks like an old gasket: a flat, ring-shaped mass of braided cartilage and cold, petroleum-smelling gel, pinned to the vertical basalt by three hollow anchor-plugs of dark bone. I have no eyes; the violet twilight carries nothing worth seeing anyway, only the long, empty gray of the drop. But the stone is loud. 

Through the basalt, I feel the lathe-ticks two hundred meters below, their radial discs grinding their dual mirrors into the face, a rhythmic, abrasive chatter that travels up through the silicate like a wire humming under tension. Above me, the hydraulic ferns fill and empty—*shhh-clack, shhh-clack*—their iron-pyrite frills drawing the static out of the thin wind and driving it down their stems in hot, stinging pulses.

I am the shunt.

I sit between the fern’s tap-root and a hairline fracture in the pillar’s core. When the air is still and the purple vacuum presses down with its sub-zero weight, I am mostly solid. My inner fluids are not blood, but a dense, non-Newtonian tallow that thickens to the hardness of horn when cold. In this state, I have no thoughts, only a slow, dark registration of torque—the enormous weight of the hanging gardens pulling down against the stone, trying to peel the entire vertical forest off the cliff and send it tumbling into the white sea miles below. My bone rings take that shear. They groan silently, distributing the strain across three square feet of rock face.

Then the front arrives.

You can tell it is coming hours before it hits by the change in the rock's resonance. The deep bass note of the pillar rises, sharpening to a cold, dry ring as the atmospheric differential builds between our needle and the sister spire four miles west. The wool carpets begin to prickle. The fine hairs on my outer perimeter—microscopic needles of zinc-sheathed horn—rise toward the void.

When the discharge rolls across the gulf, it does not strike all at once. It crawls. A sheet of dull amber fire sweeps down the moss from the peak, silent as grease on glass. 

As the voltage hits the fern above me, its hydraulic valves seize open in an ecstatic spasm of sap, and the excess current dumps directly into my crown.

That is the instant I become awake.

The tallow at my core does not melt; it ionizes. For twelve seconds, fourteen seconds, my interior resistance drops to zero, and the cold, heavy grease turns into an incandescent fluid that spins inside my toroidal ring like a closed-circuit whirlpool. 

In those seconds, consciousness is not a thought or a memory—it is a terrible, beautiful precision. I feel the entire geometry of the spire: the twelve cisterns hanging below the west ledge, their agitator-worms beating figure-eights against the brine; the dead spindle-climbers frozen on their tripods sixty yards out, acting as dry resistors; the faint, high-velocity vibration of a lung-diver passing through the lower cloud deck, trailing its wake of compressed nitrogen. I am connected to the marrow of the stone. I know the exact depth of the fault lines running thousands of meters down into the suffocating fog.

During the discharge, my anchor-plugs draw minerals straight out of the basalt—atoms of iron and sulfur pulled through microscopic pores by the sheer draw of the arc—and use them to mill new layers of dental horn onto my outer ring, repairing the wear caused by the mountain's perpetual downward drag. I drink the static until my cartilage walls glow dull red through the black moss, a hidden washer of heat in a frozen desert.

And then the sheet passes. 

The amber arc leaps from the lower ferns to bridge the gulf toward the next spire. The current drops.

The tallow slows, thickens, and begins to gel. The sharp, mathematical clarity of the needle contracts, folding in from the edges like cooling slag. The distant thrum of the cisterns fades back into mere pressure; the sense of the sister pillars across the void narrows to a dull, cold pull on my western anchors.

The heat leaves last, bleeding into the insatiable stone. My valves pull shut with a soft, internal *thun*. 

I go gray. I go hard. The rubbery mat settles back down upon me, heavy and wet with supercooled beads, holding me tight against the sheer, dark drop, waiting for the sky to load the next charge into the rock.
