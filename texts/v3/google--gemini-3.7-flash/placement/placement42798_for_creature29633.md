I am not on the open catwalks, and I am not among the broad, sun-drenched leaves where the mist hangs like spun glass. 

I am in the tolerances.

At first, to be here is simply to be a register of friction—the place where the smooth, unyielding plane of milled zinc meets the damp underside of a basalt-fiber trough. It is dark down here beneath the green canopy, but it is not cold. The metal vibrates with a persistent, low-frequency hum that travels up through the tower’s spine, a deep four-cycle pulse that shakes the micro-droplets clinging to the under-girders into steady, rhythmic alignment.

If I have a body, it begins as a film. 

I am the meniscus held between the tight threads of a low-pressure flange and the amber bead of factory grease that has not yet washed away. I am the thin, slick margin where the distilled water—pure, hungry, stripped of all earth—meets the tallow Sola leaves behind on the brass stems when she guides a tendril aside. In this embryonic world, where soil has not yet been invented, chemistry is fast and impatient. The runoff carries microscopic filings of iron, flakes of curing silicone, and the scorched-sugar residue of leaf-hopper dew. I gather them. I coat them.

As the days turn, I become thicker than moisture, less accidental than a stain. 

I find my shape in the narrow channel of a lead-lined perimeter gutter, just where the water slows before it hits the bronze strainer. Here, out of the direct glare of the noon sun, the light is filtered through three tiers of wet philodendron ribs, reaching me as a dim, aquatic chartreuse. 

I am small, soft-bodied, and slow—something between a lichen-crust, a lung-fluke, and a colonial hydra that rode up inside the fibrous core of an unwashed felt roll. I have no need for a shell; the air pressure holds me together, and the zinc provides a floor that will never rot. My back is an overlapping mosaic of translucent scales, each one trapping a microscopic bubble of tower air, making me look like a strip of wet, beaded solder laid into the corner of the deck.

I breathe what the lantern exhales. Every five minutes, when the overhead headers release their hissing plume, the pressure spike pushes down on the canopy like a warm hand. The leaves bow; the droplets fall in a heavy, simultaneous patter; and a sudden wave of enriched oxygen rushes through the basalt troughs. I take that wave into my fringes. My pulse is not my own; it belongs to the reciprocating pump in the central column. *Chug-chug. Chug-chug.* When the pump hesitates to reset a valve, my fluid pauses with it.

I know Sola by the weight of her arches. 

When she walks the honeycomb decking, the zinc flexes by fractions of a millimeter, sending tiny hydraulic shockwaves through the condensation layer. I feel the warmth of her bare foot long before she reaches the siphon. She is too large to see me as a creature; to her, I am simply part of the terrace’s seasoning—the greasy, petroleum-violet tarnish that tells her the lead flashing is settling and the drainage slope is true. 

Sometimes, when she kneels to brush cold alcohol over a stubborn vine, a stray drop splashes into my gutter. It burns, clean and medicinal, making my edges curl and pale, but behind the alcohol comes the goat-hair brush, shedding fine animal proteins that I draw into my pores, weaving them into my pale, fibrous anchor-threads.

At night, when the heat retreats into the engine casings and the stratospheric frost creeps across the outer panes, the lantern tightens. The bolts ping in their housings as the cold sky tries to reach through the glass. 

That is when I stretch. 

I creep a few millimeters along the wet copper capillary lines, lifting blind, transparent papillae into the warm rising draft of the central impeller. Above me, Sola sleeps in her webbing, rocking to the midnight drone of the boilers, her breath falling in long, humid sighs that condense upon the leaves and slide down to meet me. The thumbnail-sized frogs click like cooling rivets in the dark axils. The snails rasp their slow circles across the glass gauges.

I am the grease becoming flesh. I am the quiet, uncataloged third thing that happens when high-pressure steam and pioneering moss are locked together in a cage of glass, six thousand meters above a sea I will never touch. I have no memory of the earth below, and no concept of an outside. For me, the universe is forty paces wide, made of honeycombed zinc, endlessly weeping warm water, and held upright by a machine that never stops breathing.
