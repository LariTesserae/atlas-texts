I am not in the wind, and I am not in the flood. The wind is too wide to hold a thought, and the sap-tunnels move with a pressure that shears the mind flat against the cell-walls before it can take a shape.

I am in the *lip*.

Where the grain has torn thirty cubits deep under its own torque, the wood creates a mouth that wants, with every surging hour, to bite itself back together. I am the thing wedged between the jaws.

Not the petrified timber wedge itself—though my knees are drawn up against the heartwood balk, and my heels are braced on the lower shelf where the resin weeps slow and black like cold syrup. I am the body that tends the wedge. If you leave a balk alone for forty turnings of the mist, the tree does not simply press against it; it coats it, licks it with pale cambium, creeps around the flint-pins, and swallows the leverage whole. Once the wood swallows your ironwood tooth, the seam snaps shut behind it with the force of an anvil dropped from the high needles. The hammock-lines part; the sleeping-cocoons become dark paste between two walls of jade-black grain; the bone gutters crush into powder.

So my life is the chisel-bite.

I sit where the light dies into grease-smoke. My ribcage has that broad, barrel flare that comes from breathing air that has been dragged through leagues of cold needles until the sweetness is scraped clean out of it. My forearms are not graceful; they are knotted like the burls that form where two limbs grind together in a cross-gale. The skin on the palms is not skin anymore—it is a thick, yellow horn, cross-hatched by the bitter dust of scraped bark, so dry that a live ember from the grease-pot can sit in the cup of my hand for five clicks before the heat finds a nerve.

My left ear is gone. Not torn away by a sail-glider’s barb, but worn flat, scalded and scarred into a dull, calloused rosette from twenty winters of pressing the temple directly against the grain. 

When you put your skull to the raw wood inside a split, you do not hear what the ears hear. You hear the weight of leagues.

To the west, four miles out along the great arch where it sweeps toward the cloud-ceiling, there is a lateral fiber that has been fraying since my grandfather was wedged into the prow. It sounds like a hair being pulled through teeth—a long, dry *skr-r-rt* that rises half a tone every three seasons. When that hair snaps, twenty thousand cubits of timber will whip three yards into the lee. The split I live in will twist three inches to the left. If my wedges are set straight, they will kick out into the void like sling-stones, and the chasm will shut like teeth.

So every forty clicks, I take the adze—flint lashed to the shoulder-blade of an under-strider—and I drive the balk a quarter-thumb deeper.

*Tock.*

The wood shrieks when the wedge bites. It is not an animal shriek; it is the sound of cold glass being scored with a diamond. A fine, white meal of wood-flour spits out around the edges and settles in the grease of my beard. It tastes of iron and wintergreen and old ice.

Below me, twenty fathoms down in the blue-dark grease-fog of the split, the fires are going. I can feel the rhythm of the clan through the soles of my feet:
*Tap-tap.* (The fuel-pots are full.)
*Tap-tap-drag.* (A child has dropped a scraper into the sap-trough; someone is fishing it out before it gums the pipe.)

They think I am listening for the snap-cycle. They bring me grease in flat horn-plates, and dried ribbons of glider-meat that have been cured in resin-fumes until they chew like leather harness. They grease my back so the damp doesn't rot the lungs out of me. They want me to tell them when the big rib will break, so they can cut their hawsers, abandon the split-village, and scramble like lice into the root-swells before the whole continent of wood goes ringing into the lower dark.

I do not tell them that the rib is not breaking.

The rib is *turning*.

Down in the depths where the mist is warm and rots into yellow gas, the wood is drinking something heavier than water. I feel it through the bone of my temple: a slow, spiral pulse that occurs once every twelve thousand breaths. The entire spire—the one that holds sixty clans and seven leagues of notched track—is screwing itself downward into the dark like an auger. The wood is tightening its grain until the cells are small as sand-grains.

When it finishes the turn, there will be no splits left. The lips will close. The jade plates will grind together until the bark is smooth as river-ice, and there will be nothing for a talon, a pin, or a human hand to catch.

Until then, the adze is in my palm.

I find a fresh fissure, narrow as a fingernail, where the living skin has wept a yellow bead of frost. I set the point of the ironwood tooth against the seam. I lift the stone mallet.

The wind outside howls through the negative space between the arches, blowing the whistling cries of the sail-gliders down the chimney of the split. A flash of pale green static illuminates my knees, the scarred wedge, the empty drop beneath my hanging toes.

I strike. The wood shivers, yields a thumb's width of space to the dark, and keeps me.
