At first, there is only the sensation of weight without burden—the press of the red basin holding its own breath, and the low, unchanging warmth that rises from the compacted clay like a second floor suspended just above the earth.

I would not be above the ground, looking down. In a place where nothing falls and nothing rises, to have a vantage point would be an error, a disturbance of the horizontal silence. 

I would be wedged in the narrowest seam, where the curved underbelly of the third flaked river stone meets the dust-choked depression in the siltstone floor. 

I am not the stone, but I have taken its outline. 

My body is flat, dry as an empty seedpod, and no thicker than a fingernail. It is impossible to say where the chitin ends and the accumulated silt begins, because over centuries the dust has worked itself into the micro-fissures of my back, cemented by the faint, salty exudate that keeps my tissues from turning to powder. I have no color of my own anymore; I have the color of the silt that was ground from these walls three thousand sunsets ago—that dark, rust-bitten ochre that looks black under the unblinking stars.

I do not have eyes. In this hollow, sight would only be an appetite that could never be fed, a waste of moisture. Where a face might have been, there is only a smooth, sloping frontal plate, shaped to deflect the drift of fine flour-dust so it does not clog the spiracles along my sides. 

Instead of seeing, I feel the hollow through the contact of three tiny, calcified claws pressed against the chipped bevel of the river stone. 

Through that stone, I know the world. I feel the microscopic tremor of the bedrock as it gives up the midday heat—not in waves, but in single, atomic clicks as the quartz grains relax their grip on one another. I feel the distant, resinous sleep of the wasps in the shrub stems three paces away; their dormant presence is a tiny knot of density in the air, a drop of amber holding its position against the dark. I feel the deep, subterranean heave of the great flat sleeper beneath the wash, whose heart beats so slowly that the pause between pulses is long enough for a grain of sand to tumble down a fissure and find its final resting place.

I do not eat what is fresh, for there is nothing fresh. I feed on the slow, invisible harvest of the dead air: the micro-flakes of keratin shed by my own long-fossilized ancestors, the drifted grains of pollen blown over the rim during storms that occurred before the siltstone walls were smoothed to this soft repose, and the bitter, white efflorescence of salt that the stone sweats out when the midnight chill touches its outer rim.

My breath is not a movement of ribs or bellows. It is an open osmotic exchange. A single breath enters my mantle at the rising of the red star through the notch in the canyon wall, rests within my dry, chambered interior through the long stillness of the dark, and is given back to the dust only when the first grayness touches the sky above the rim. 

I do not remember the hands that flaked the stone above me. I only know that the stone has three edges that are sharper than the wind could make them, and that in the angle beneath those edges, the stillness is absolute. 

I am the listener at the base of the rock. I am the thing that proves the hollow is not empty, merely full of things that have finished happening. And when the faint, dry ticking passes through the rigid wood of the shrubs—that light, flour-soft brush of cooling bark—I shift by less than the width of a spore, settling deeper into the flour-fine dirt, content to wait until the stars themselves have finished crossing the small circle of our sky.
