At first, there is only the vibration: not a sound heard, but a low, continuous shudder conducted through four inches of drowned cast iron into the flat of my belly. 

It is the combined weight of the place singing to itself—the rhythmic slap of warm condensate falling into the settling trenches, the heavy, dragging slide of a monitor lizard adjusting its mass on the manhole cover ten feet above, and, deeper still, the endless, fine grit-fall of guano tumbling through the open steel floor grates to sift down through the water column like black snow.

I am not in the light. The glare of the milk-pale sky has never reached this pocket, and even if it did, my eyes would make nothing of it. They are small, milky beads, buried under calloused folds of skin, sensitive only to the dullest shift between noon-warmth and the cool purple drop of evening. I do not miss them. To see in this water would be like looking through wet cement; the basin here is an opaque broth of suspended lime, iron hydroxide, and paraffin grease. 

Instead, the world exists as a dense, three-dimensional map of taste and electric tension.

I find myself wedged in the low clearance of a half-submerged suction manifold—a massive, six-foot-wide iron cylinder that once drew river water into the cooling jackets of the open-hearth furnaces. The top half of the pipe arches into the warm, yellowed air beneath the floor plates, dripping with rust-stalactites and coated in the velvet damp of sulfur mold. The bottom half is drowned in four feet of cold, dead brine that has sat undisturbed since the turbines stopped spinning.

I rest precisely at the interface where those two waters meet. 

My skin is not scales in the ordinary sense, but a thick, pebble-grained leather, slate-colored and scarred by the ragged edges of corroded rivets. It is an armor against the sharp, flaking mineral edges of this drowned machinery, but it is also an organ of pure perception. Every square inch of my hide drinks the chemistry of the basin: the sweet, fatty tang of bat droppings fermenting on the upper ledges; the sharp, metallic bite of leaching copper from the drowned transformer coils; the slow, alkaline wash of dissolving concrete.

From the corners of my blunt, flattened snout, my barbels extend out into the dark. They are thick as fingers at the base, tapering to fleshy, nervous whips that trail across the silt-choked rail spur beneath my belly. 

When a river prawn moves six feet away, its turquoise carapace clicking against a drowned ladder rung, the electrical pulse of its muscle contraction twitches against the left side of my jaw. When a cliff swallow nestling loses its grip seventy feet above, tumbling through the truss-work, smashing through a rusted ventilation duct, and hitting the surface with a muffled, wet *thwack*, the shockwave travels down through the tallow film and rings through the bone of my skull.

There is no need to hunt. Hunger here is not a sharp, desperate thing; it is a slow, languid gravity. 

I wait. The water above me churns faintly as the shore crabs tear at the fallen bird, dislodging scraps of down and rich, soft muscle. The fragments drift down slowly, tumbling through the lukewarm upper layer, entering the cold brine where I lie. 

I do not surge. I simply tilt my broad head, flare the heavy flaps of my gills, and draw the water inward. The suction pulls the falling detritus—silt, feathers, iron flakes, and meat—into the wide, toothless cavern of my mouth. The gills strain the mineral grit, venting a cloud of orange iron dust into the dark, while the soft biological fuel slides down to join the dense, slow-burning furnace of my gut.

Above me, the iron city breathes its heavy, ammoniac breath. I can feel the evening coming on by the way the vibration changes: the dry, rustling scrape of the swallows quietens, and the shudder in the steel rafters deepens to an all-encompassing, rhythmic thrum as millions of bats detach and begin their spiral into the dusk. The air in the manifold warms by half a degree as their body heat rolls downward. 

I let the current of their wake settle into the ironwork. I sink an inch deeper into the silt, my fins anchoring me against the submerged flange of an intake valve, perfectly fitted to the mold of this dead, sheltering machine. I will be here when the steel finally dissolves into red earth, tasting the slow, warm rain of the world falling to meet me.
