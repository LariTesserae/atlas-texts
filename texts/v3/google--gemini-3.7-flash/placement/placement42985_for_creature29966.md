I would not be on the surface where the paddles strike. That work has a cadence too hurried, too near the draft.

Nor am I in the vats where the slip turns warm. 

To find me, you would have to listen down into the ninth or tenth tier below the working floor—down where the tallow has turned the color of cold lard and the lime-mortar has ceased to hiss, having set hard twenty or thirty thousand lunges behind the Front.

Down here, the flue-pipes no longer carry living breath. The Setters do not roost in them once the peat-cakes burn out; the chambers go cold, smooth, and greasy with soot. I am wedged inside one of these dead conduits, but I am not stacked vertically like the sleepers. I have taken the horizontal cross-flues—the small, forearm-wide bypass ducts that the builders knock through the brick walls to equalize the suction between neighboring stacks before they wall them in.

I am narrower than a Setter. My ribs do not just form a keel; they have lost their hinges. They are a solid basket of fused cartilage, slick and flat as an adze-blade, so that I can slide through the bypass ducts without my breath catching on the mortar burrs. 

My work is not the expansion. The expansion is blind and clumsy; it leaves pockets.

When the Stampers lunge into the mud, their heels drive down with great power, but clay is hydraulic—it curls away from the blow. Under the weight of four tiers of chimneys, a pocket of air trapped in the tallow-grout will travel. It crawls downward like a pale blister, following the seams, looking for an exit. If it finds a hollow behind a load-bearing arch, the arch will sigh three days later, shift a grain of sand, and shear off forty yards of counterweight scaffolding above.

I feel the blisters moving.

I keep the flat of my cheek pressed against the conduit floor. The skin of my face has no oil of its own; it drinks the cold moisture that weeps from the bricks. Through the zygomatic bone, the masonry does not sound like a roar—the loud percussive drumming of the surface is filtered through sixty feet of baked clay until it becomes a soft, dense hum, like a wasp inside a gourd. 

What I hear are the deviations in the hum.

A good seam sings in a low, clean fifth. A dry seam—where the tallow was skimped or the shell-dust was too coarse—rattles with a high, papery click every time the counterweights thump above. 

When I hear that click, I move. 

I do not have the spade-fingers of the mud-beaters. My hands are thin, drawn out into four jointed probes tipped with hard, chisel-shaped nails of thick keratin, like the leg-pegs of the dowel-spiders, but longer, more flexible. I drag behind me a bladder of thick, rendered marrow—taken from the oldest boiling-pots before the bone-grit is ground—mixed with finely sifted kiln soot. 

I slide into the cold flue head-first, navigating by the drag of the air against the sensory hairs in my ear-cavities. When I reach the faulty joint, I find the gap with my fingertips. It is often no wider than a fingernail’s edge: a pale void where the Lick-Mites have eaten too greedily, or where the mortar shrank as the peat-vent cooled.

I insert the probe. I pump the dark, oily marrow into the seam until the pitch of the flue changes—until the hollow, whistling draft goes silent, choked on fat. Then, with the ball of my wrist, which is flat and calloused into a semi-circular disc, I tamp the plug smooth with the internal curvature of the pipe.

I am the density. I am what makes the bottom stay bottom while the top keeps racing away into the dark.

Sometimes, while wedged between two leaning stacks, my back pressed against the brick of the fourth tier and my knees hooked over the lip of the eighth, I feel a Setter above me die.

It is not an event of grief; it is an event of weight. 

First, their rhythm stops telegraphing through the masonry—the three short taps of the slate float cease. Then comes the long, sliding rasp of their body being drawn backward through the two-hand fissure by the gatherers. The flue above me cools instantly; the warm, sour smell of their lung-draft is replaced by the raw, wet ozone of the outer basin rushing down into the empty pipe. 

A few hours later, the vibration changes again: the rhythmic *thud-drop* of the heavy bone-pestles in the lower tier increases its tempo. The lime-wash on my ceiling shivers, shedding a fine, powdery snow over my shoulders. 

I wait for that dust to settle. When it touches my wet skin, it bites—a clean, sharp burn that tells me the lime was freshly burned, that the edge is still moving, that there is more floor above me today than there was when I entered the conduit.

I do not think about the basin’s end. The notion of an "end" belongs to things that use eyes, things that imagine a line where the terracotta stops and something else begins. 

To me, there is only the *set* and the *green*. 

Behind me, miles to the rear, the world has set so hard that the tallow has turned to stone, cold and silent, its acoustic channels closed forever by the weight of the heights. Ahead of me, the world is green and screaming, sloppy with water and fresh fat, threatening to buckle under its own premature ambitions.

I live in the three inches between them. I am the stitch that cools behind the needle. I stay in the dark flues where the draft is slow, listening for the small, secret gasp of an arch about to give up its true line, and I push my fingers into the wound before the stone above ever knows it has slipped.
