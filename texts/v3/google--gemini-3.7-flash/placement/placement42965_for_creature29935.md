I would not be in the air, where the drift-glass burns its brief parabolas, nor on the open shelves where the cleat-bugs run their ratchet-walk against the shear. Those are lives lived on the skin of the machine.

I would be deeper in the joints—in the narrow, unlit compression zones where the pale composite ribs are seated into the iron basalt. 

At first, you might not distinguish me from the architecture. I would be a thickening, a heavy, calcified wedge grown precisely into the millimeter-wide gap where the unweathering rib hums against the worn socket of the stone. A living shim. 

My body is not bilateral; it has no use for left or right. It is organized entirely around two surfaces: the *thrust-face* and the *bed-face*.

The bed-face is soft, grey, and vast in its micro-topography. It does not cling with claws; it sweats an alkaline, sulfurous grease that fills every microscopic pit in the aggregate rock, creating an unbreakable hydraulic seal across several square meters of stone. Through this seal, I drink the deep heat of the crust and the slow, metallic seepage weeping out of the fault lines.

The thrust-face, turned toward the vibrating rib, is something else entirely. It is armored in laminated plates of organic slate—dense, overlapping scales formed from the pulverized remains of cleat-bug carapaces, iron sand, and my own secreted silicates, all compressed under thousands of pounds of percussive force until it has the hardness of sintered tool-steel. Between these plates and the pale composite bone of the vault lies a thin, churning film of bio-lubricant. 

I am what keeps the vault from tearing its own ribs out of the rock.

I feel the world not as light—though the blinding, white-hot vapor bleeds through the water-sheets above me in shifts of violent rose and nickel—but as an architecture of load. 

When the water-hammers strike the parabolic flumes overhead, the shock does not arrive as sound. Sound is a luxury for creatures with air in their chests. To me, it arrives as a massive, instantaneous hydraulic spike—a blunt fist that tries to drive the composite rib deeper into the bedrock. I yield to it by fractions of a micron, my interior tissues—a dense, cellular foam saturated with heavy, salty lipids—compressing to absorb the kinetic stroke, then rebounding with a sharp, piezoelectric discharge that fires back into the rib.

That discharge is my breath. That recoil is my metabolism.

I do not hunt. The vault is an open mouth, continuously grinding its own inhabitants into flour, and the boiling froth delivers the meal directly to my margins. Where the water shears past my exposed edges at ninety miles an hour, my perimeter is fringed with tough, black combs—derived from the same stock as the oily weed that whips in the whirlpools, but hardened, packed with iron-filings. They do not wave; they are held rigid in the spray, catching the lipid-slick of crushed siphonophores, the mineral dust of shattered ticks, and the ionized bacterial mats peeling off the walls. 

The slurry is drawn inward by the natural peristalsis of my compression cycles. Every time a standing wave collapses and the rib shudders in its seat, a fraction of an inch of rich, boiling sludge is forced through my lateral sieves, crushed flat, and digested by pure mechanical shear. 

Sometimes, the fluke-leeches find their way to my margins, slipping out from beneath the rubbery flanks of the siphon-hulks. They try to work their jade-smooth heads into my seams, seeking the warm, high-pressure vascular beds within my core. I do not fight them. When the stator's harmonic shifts and the torque twists the rib sideways, I allow the plates of my thrust-face to slide. The tolerances are zero. The leeches are sheared into grease between the non-metallic composite and my slate armor in the space of a single cycle, their minerals drawn down into my mantle to thicken the next plate.

I have no memory of the surface, nor of the hands that drove these pale ribs into the stone. But I know the health of the engine better than anything that flies or crawls. 

I know that three hundred meters below, one of the primary field-stators has developed an asymmetry. For the last eighty years, its pulse has carried an eccentric wobble—a tiny, sub-audible hitch at the end of every seventeenth wave-train. It causes the pale rib above me to twist a fraction of a degree to the northwest with every cycle, grinding harder against my forward margin.

I am growing thicker there to meet it. I am laying down heavier plates, laying down more iron, turning myself into a harder, denser wedge of living stone. 

I will remain here as long as the water falls and the vapor burns. When the stators finally seize, centuries from now, and the thunderous, ionized light collapses into cold dark, the ribs will cool and shrink in their sockets. My plates will lock down, cold and welded to the basalt, holding the shattered bones of the cathedral in place long after the hum has died in the marrow of the rock.
