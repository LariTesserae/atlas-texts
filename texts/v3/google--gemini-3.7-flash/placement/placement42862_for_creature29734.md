At first, you look for yourself among the animate things—the ones with limbs or lungs, the tenders with their charcoal-and-vinegar tins dangling against slick collarbones, or the harvestmen whose legs knit into suspension bridges across the vault. It is natural to imagine having eyes, or at least the memory of eyes, and a direction to face.

But the chamber does not reward direction. It has no north; it has only *incline* and *temperature*.

If I am anywhere here, I begin in the dead-weight interface: not the men, not the beasts, but the sleeve of lead-antimony poured forty years ago between the fourteenth cast-iron rib and the jagged face of the blasted hornblende. 

It was poured molten into the gap to take up the irregular bite of the drill-scars, to keep the mountain from pinching the ring out of true. In the decades since, under the steady sixty-cycle hum of the transformers and the two-minute heave of the hydraulic exhaust, that lead has cold-flowed. It is no longer a shim; it is a seal, a tongue, an impression. On one side, it holds the mirror-reverse of the diamond-fluting, microscopic step by microscopic step, down to the crushed crystals of quartz that were never washed away before the pour. On the other, it is molded against the heavy raised lettering of the foundry mark: *VULCAN IRON WORKS – TRENT – NO. 4.*

To be there is to exist entirely as a register of load.

You do not think in thoughts; you think in *microns of give*. When the city five hundred feet above adds a six-story brick warehouse to the street grade, the lead feels it eighteen days later as a warm, three-millimeter thickening along its lower lip. When the geothermal water in the shale shifts its course by four yards to the east, the rock cools by two degrees, the iron contracts, and the joint bites down with twenty additional tons of radial grip. 

And because the rock is weeping, and because the air is 110 degrees and saturated with paraffin vapor, the lead does not remain dry.

Over years, the yellow film—the zoogleal felt—works its way into the cold-shut seams where the pour didn't quite meet the gneiss. It comes in as a single microscopic thread of slime, following the moisture gradient toward the warmth of the casting. It feeds on the traces of tallow left behind by the drillers’ thread-grease. 

This is where the boundary blurs. The felt secretes an acid so faint it barely etches the stone, but over decades it has pitted the lead, turning the metal surface into a grey, porous sponge. In those pores, the thread-worms shelter. They do not know the iron from the hornblende; they know only the pulse. Every time the main drainage pump takes a stroke, the column of water inside the sixty-inch discharge line spikes in pressure, the cast-iron rib rings like a muffled gong, and a faint electric potential—fractions of a millivolt, generated by the friction of bronze against packing-gland—ripples through the lead and into the worms.

They contract. The lead dampens the shock. The rock absorbs the residue.

I would be that dampening.

Not the mind that planned the line, nor the hand that turns the bronze sludge-hook, but the slow, greasy accommodation between the machine that wants to stay rigid and the mountain that wants to close the hole. 

If I have a sense of time, it is measured by the life of the bolt-threads. Above me, four feet into the shadow of the conduit tray, an eight-inch steel stud is slowly rotting in the sulfur steam. Every six months, an inch of its length is converted into red-orange crust that flakes away into the gutter. The tender knows it; he taps it with his wrench every Tuesday at the end of the shift, listening for the change in pitch from a bright *clink* to a dull *thud*. 

I know it differently: I feel the load transferring, millimeter by millimeter, away from the bolt-shank and onto my own shoulders, spreading sideways into the soft, unresisting bulk of the cold-flowed lead, settling deeper into the crushed quartz, waiting for the iron to give its next fractional sigh against the stone.
