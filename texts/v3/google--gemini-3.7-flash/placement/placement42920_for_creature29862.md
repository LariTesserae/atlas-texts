I am not on the gantries, where the younger ones swing on liverwort cords with their scraping-spuds, shouting silent numbers into each other’s palms. That is work for those whose joints still have spring in them—those whose skin hasn’t yet taken on the full yellow cast of cylinder-varnish.

I am down in the narrow throat behind the lower bearing-sleeve, where the eastern wall of the cleft forms a sharp, thirty-degree shelf just above the rising mist-floor. 

It is the hottest place a body can stay without blistering. The diorite here does not weep cold melt; it sweats water that has touched the deep roots of the horn, smelling heavily of sulfur and wet slag. My back is pressed flat against the stone to keep my balance, and through my shoulder blades I take the mountain’s steady, tectonic heave—a dull, planetary grind that never resolves into a note. Through the soles of my broad, bare feet, planted on the lower cast-iron girder, I take the engine: the three-foot brass ram, the iron tie-rods, the great shuddering *thrum-shuck* that takes two minutes to draw its breath and two minutes to let it go.

My ears have been full of boiled tallow and moss-wool since I was twelve; I have not heard a bird or the rush of water in thirty seasons. But when the lower valve-seat begins to scour—when a single grain of knapped flint gets past the intake mesh and scores the bronze shoe—my left heel feels it instantly. It feels like a dry hair being drawn across the bone.

***

In this pocket, I have become mostly skin and timing.

My torso is thick as an oil cask from forty years of drawing steam-thinned air into the meat of my ribs. My skin is the color of river gravel, patterned with pale patches where the scalding condensate has caught me, and dark, permanent rings of graphite grease ground deep into the creases of my wrists. I do not see well anymore. In the bright two hours of noon, when the sun drops down between the horns like an axe-blade, the light hurts behind my eyes; but in the green, sour gloom of the rest of the day, my fingers can tell the difference between four grades of lime-scale without looking.

My tool is the stuffing-paddle. It is a piece of hard hornbeam, curved like a rib, smoothed by decades of palms and boiled fat. 

Every sixty strokes—two hours by the heave of the ram—the crosshead comes down toward my shelf with its slow, terrifying inevitability. It does not drop quickly; it descends like an iron ceiling lowered by the weight of the sky. As it reaches the bottom of its travel, there is a dead moment. For twenty seconds, the water pressure beneath the piston balances the load of the high-altitude head. The machine pauses. The entire mountain seems to hold its breath; the waterfall's spray hangs suspended in the rising draft; the blind frogs on the wet flange beside my knee freeze, their translucent bellies flattened against the iron.

That is my time.

I lean out over the forty-pace drop, clipped by a single braided harness to the cold-water tie-rod. In the bucket between my thighs is the packing: liverwort fiber stripped thin as hair, boiled in tallow, dried in the draft of the exhaust valves, and kneaded with powdered graphite until it is as black and supple as eel-flesh.

My hands go into the smoking gap around the brass sleeve. 

The heat of the metal comes straight through my callouses, sweet and fierce. I feed the braid into the stuffing box with the hornbeam paddle, tamping it down into the gland, layer over layer, feeling for the soft spots where the steam has blown out the old grease. My knuckles brush the mirror-polished brass of the ram. It is so hot it feels greasy even before the oil touches it. If I drop the paddle, it will fall three thousand feet into the boiling cloud; if I am slow by five seconds, the crosshead will reverse its heave, and the rising shoulder of the piston will take my forearm off against the upper guide-plate.

I am never slow. The rhythm is not in my head; it is in my marrow. 

*One-two-three:* tamp the wet braid. 
*Four-five-six:* thumb the graphite grease into the lip. 
*Seven:* clear the debris—a crushed chain-cricket, a scrap of liverwort leaf that rode down on the piston rod—and pull back.

The iron groans. The low-pressure check valve seats with a solid, deep *clop* that rattles through my teeth. The accumulator begins its heave. 

I swing back onto my diorite shelf as the piston rises, carrying away the steam-shroud. The draft changes instantly, blowing hot oil-spray across my chest and cooling the sweat on my forehead until it feels like ice. 

***

While the engine climbs—those long, patient two minutes of upward strain—I sit with my knees pulled to my chin, wedged into the stone notch. 

A tallow-rat has made its nest three feet above my head, tucked into a dry fissure behind the main steam conduit. It knows me. When the piston rises and the ironwork goes taut, it creeps down through the dripping liverworts, its blind, waxy snout snuffling at my hair. It licks the salt and the boiled grease from the skin of my shoulders with a tongue like a rough leaf. I do not move to brush it away. Its body is warm, dense as lard, and its claws tickle the scars on my collarbone where an old steam-fitting gave way when I was young.

Around us, the cleft is never still. 

The young diorite is still settling, still splitting in tiny, hairline cracks that bleed fresh boiling water into the moss beds. Below me, in the hanging cradles, the younger Tenders are sleeping their four-minute sleep, their bodies rocking slightly in the canvas slings as the whole assembly sways on its anchors. Above me, where the mist thins into that impossibly clean, razor-sharp blue sky, the gasket-swifts are tumbling through the exhaust plumes, their tiny, hook-footed silhouettes flickering like iron sparks against the sun.

People who live down in the flat dirt—if there are people left down there—must think of machines as things that are made, used, and broken. They must think of mountains as things that stand still.

Here, there is no difference between the two. The rock wants to close the notch; the steam wants to tear it open; the accumulator balances them both on the end of its brass ram, sixty strokes to the hour, year after year, until the iron turns to orange scale and the diorite turns to sand.

The crosshead is reaching the top of its stroke now. I can feel the change in the tie-rods—the high-pitched, metallic shiver that travels down the steel as the upper relief valve cracks open to spill fifty gallons of scalding condensate into the fern-tiers below. 

The rat scuttles back into its cleft. 

I take the hornbeam paddle from my belt. I reach into the bucket and pull another arm’s length of black, tallow-soaked moss braid, winding it around my left wrist. I shift my weight forward on the balls of my feet, waiting for the ceiling to begin its slow, two-minute fall toward my hands.
