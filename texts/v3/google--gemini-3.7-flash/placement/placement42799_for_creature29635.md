At first, there is no boundary between my body and the weight of the floor. 

I am not standing on the plateau. I am caught in its tolerances.

For a long time, there is only the register of load—the difference between three hundred thousand tons of downward thrust and the four-beat pause that follows when the cedar piston reaches the top of its stroke. In that fraction of a second, the pressure lifts just enough for the cold, amber tallow to seep under my ribs, and I expand like an oiled sponge before the wood comes down again and irons me flat against the petrified peat.

I am not one of the mastiff-crabs, frantic and sharp, ringing against the bark. I have no legs to break. I am too heavy, too low, too wide.

If you looked down through the freezing fog, where the hydraulic sap boils over the black moss, you would mistake me for an outcropping of petrified turf—a dark, slab-like swelling forty feet across, wedged precisely into the corner where a reciprocating root-shaft exits the peat. 

My underside is not skin; it is a dense, laminated horn, cold as a flatiron and slick with vegetable petroleum. It has been planed by three centuries of lateral strokes until it mirrors the grain of the subterranean cedar beneath it. My top side is soft, wet, and open: a deep carpet of fibrous, gray cilia that catch the spinning discs of heavy water as they lift out of the gravity-fails. 

I am a **Damper-Mass**. 

I do not move across the anvil floor. I am where the motion goes to die.

Every time the titanic root-shaft shudders back on its return stroke, it carries enough kinetic violence to shatter the petrified peat beneath it, to turn the three-mile-deep bedrock into powdered carbon. I sit in the crook of the elbow. When the stroke lands, my horn-sole receives the strike, and my internal hydrostatic chambers—hundreds of interconnected bladders filled with dense, non-compressible kerosene—take the shock wave and route it through my bulk, turning seismic impact into heat.

When I take the blow, my blood boils internally. A dull, deep amber light blooms through the translucent seams of my flank as the vegetable oils inside me undergo pressure-catalysis. 

Then I breathe out. 

From the vents along my rim, I release a long, harmonic groan—a chord so low it does not travel through the air, but rolls inward through the peat, answering the subterranean drive-shafts five miles below. The sheet-gliders know this tone; they drift down through the ammonia gale to drop onto my back, digging their tethers into my warm, shuddering cilia while the bough-hammers stamp out saw-blades in the mist above us.

They nest in my heat; the blind crabs crawl across my horn-rim to scrape the carbon-flour from my joints; the gutter-eels froth through the drainage channels I keep open with the slow, pulsing expansion of my lower plates. 

I am blind, but I see the plateau entirely through friction. I know the exact viscosity of the sap ten miles to the north. I know when a dry-dock seed-pod has cracked its valve too early by the change in the floor’s micro-resonance. I feel the distant, three-legged stride of the Bell-Wanderers as they step through the dead-points of the world’s stroke, their acoustic bells chiming in the half-night.

I do not dream of the dead lowlands five miles below. The air down there is thin, loose, and warm—it has no structure, no purchase, nothing to push back against the meat. Down there, without three hundred thousand tons of cedar bearing down to keep my seams tight, I would simply come apart: my bladders unspooling, my horn-plates peeling open like rotten shale, my kerosene blood dispersing into an empty, silent sky.

Here, in the cold, wet dark, the world bears down with all its mass. 

The green sparks arc through the muck. The root-shaft draws back, groaning on its tallow beds. 

I feel the four-beat measure gather its strength in the deep turf. I draw in the amber runoff, swell against the black wood, and set myself to receive the stroke.
