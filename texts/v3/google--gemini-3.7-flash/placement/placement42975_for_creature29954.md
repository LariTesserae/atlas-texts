I would not be on the highest parapets where the wind shears clean, nor down in the deepest fermentation of the floor-mosses where the Loom-striders scrape their broad bellies along the slate. 

I would be wedged in the throat of a fourth-tier viaduct—one of the narrow, barrel-fluted transit arches that bridges the gap between the residential comb and the outer rim-lip. 

I am one of the Kith, but I am old enough that my skin has begun to match the dull, greasy polish of the gneiss it presses against. 

I feel myself first not as a shape or a name, but as an angle of resistance: forty-two degrees into the westerly blast, anchored by the three broad, tallow-slick pads of my left foot and the long, double-jointed grip of my lower arm hooked into a pre-carved scupper. The vacuum seal holds with a faint, wet tension. If I relax the muscle along the heel, it releases with a sound like a tongue clicking against wet leather; if I clamp down, the cold rock and my gray hide become a single continuous plane.

There is no darkness or light in my mind, only *density*.

Where a human would see the sudden flash of knife-edge sun, I feel a sharp, dry tightness stretch across the velvet dish of my face—a sudden evaporation that lifts the acoustic fuzz of the air and leaves the geometry ringing clean. The city snaps into hard, brittle clarity: the far tower hums in a high, whistling fifth; the ovoid window two spans above me rings like a struck brass bowl; the five-thousand-meter void to my right is an immense, dead silence that exerts a physical suction against the left rim of my facial dish. 

Then the cloud-pack hits.

The dryness vanishes in a heartbeat, replaced by a dense, freezing blanket that softens every edge. The high fifth drops half a tone as the mist loads the flues with weight. Water instantly sheets across my shoulders. I don’t shiver; my spine curves into the down-rush, and the cold runnels find the carved scars along my collarbones and ribs, following the channels I gouged into my own hide thirty seasons ago. The water races along these grooves, diverted away from my breathing-slits and directed smoothly down into the twin bladders at my hips. They fill with a heavy, swinging slosh that lowers my center of gravity, pinning me even tighter to the ramp.

I am waiting for the cross-draft. 

In this middle tier, the wind does not blow in a straight line; it knots. When the westerly gale forces its way through the lower tenements, it collides with the updraft boiling out of the abyss, creating a violent, spinning vortex right in the gut of the archway where I stand. Left unattended, this vortex will shear sideways into the grand hall below, tearing the young orchids from the back of a passing Loom-strider or shredding the delicate feeding-baleen of the Reeds rooted in the flue-mouths.

I hear another Kith approaching from the inner avenue before they reach the turn. Their gait is a rhythmic series of wet suctions—*thwip, slide, thwip, slide*—and their chest-keel is vibrating with a low, nervous chatter: a barometric warning. A massive shelf-cloud is cresting the western ridge.

They slide into the archway beside me. They are younger; their hide is paler, the runnel-scars on their ribs still pink and shallow. In the blinding white-out of the fog, we don't look at each other. We lean inward until our sternums meet with a heavy, blunt thud.

The contact is electric. Their chest-plate is broad and shivering; through the dense cartilage of our keels, they pass the shape of the coming storm. It hits my bone like the thump of a falling boulder: *three bars of pressure dropping fast, a cold front packed with slate-gravel, twenty minutes of maximum shear.*

I vent a short, pressurized hiss from the secondary lung-slits along my flank, answering with the tone of this specific archway—a low, grinding F-sharp that tells them where to anchor, where the stone is slickest, where the rock has a hair-fracture that won't hold a suction pad.

We link. My upper arm reaches behind their shoulder, my vacuum-pads locking onto the greasy hide between their shoulder-blades; their long, double-jointed limb wraps around my waist, fingers cinching into the anchor-groove behind my hip. 

Together, we step out into the center of the aperture, leaning out over the sheer fall until our joined bodies span the width of the arch like a living strut.

The gale hits us like an iron bar. 

It tries to rip our skin from our ribs, tries to peel our feet from the polished floor, but we are tuned to it. As the wind roars into our hollows, we open the cartilage vents along our sides. We don't fight the air; we swallow it, compress it in our hull-deep chests, and force it back out through our vibrating ribs. 

We don't scream. We become a third pipe in the city's organ. 

Our joined bodies throw a deep, buzzing counter-chord straight into the teeth of the vortex. The spinning knot of wind stumbles, loses its coherence, and shatters into a flat, harmless wash of spray that tumbles softly down the ramp, washing over the liverwort rugs below without tearing a single leaf.

We hold the tone. The water drains through our scars; our hearts slow down, pacing each other through the bone-contact of our chests—one beat every forty seconds, steady as the stone beneath our pads. 

The sun flares again through the mist, burning the back of my neck for ten seconds before the next grey wave rolls over the lip. We remain braced across the throat of the arch, wet, deafeningly loud, and utterly motionless, holding the breath of the city until the mountain finishes its chord.
