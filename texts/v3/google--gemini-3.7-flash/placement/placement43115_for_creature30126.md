At first, I am not a name or a face; I am simply the point where the heel meets the wet shale.

I would be one of the foundation-fitters, wedged low in the gutter-crevices where the basin tries hardest to seal itself shut. My body would be short, thick-ribbed, and heavy in the hips, keeping my center of mass inches from the slick calcium crust. I have no hair to catch the blind midges; my skull is smooth and cool, rubbed thick with the yellow tallow of the river-beasts until the mist beads on my forehead and rolls down into the corners of my mouth, tasting of salt, river-lime, and fat.

My eyes are mostly squinted shut against the pearlescent glare of the ceiling. I do not need them. The world does not happen in the eyes; it happens in the shins, the groin, and the flat, calloused bottoms of the feet.

I sit with my legs splayed across a fresh fissure of shale, my knees locked against the raw stone of the drainage wall. Pressed tight to the other side of the slab is my partner—I know them not by their voice, which cannot cross the roar of the crab-beds, but by the specific, heavy thrum of their diaphragm against the rock. When their hammer strikes, the shockwave travels through four inches of wet calcium, enters my kneecap, climbs my femur, and tells me exactly where the crack has run. 

In my right hand is an adze of flaked black chert, cold and glass-hard. In my left is a wedge of dense river-cobble, pitted and greasy from my own palm. 

Our work is not construction in the sense of making a finished home; it is an unceasing, rhythmic defense against the ground rising up to swallow our knees. Beneath our seat are three older causeways, already petrified into solid chalk, their bone-rafters crushed and filled with the cemented shells of scraper-crabs. If we stop chipping for the span of two sleeps, the mineral broth will sheet over the channel, lock the sluice, and turn our doorway into a blank, white curb.

So we strike. 

*Tock.* 

I feel my partner’s blow in my teeth. I seat my chert wedge into the fresh fracture-line, lean the meat of my shoulder into the haft, and drop my cobble onto the butt. The shale gives with a wet, sharp *snick*, flaking off in a clean, razor-edged spadeful that slides down into the warm, calf-deep water. 

Immediately, the scraper-crabs surge into the breach. 

They do not wait. They tumble over my bare, grease-cured ankles like a shovelful of wet porcelain plates, their stiff shell-hairs brushing against my skin, clicking and vibrating as they scissor the bruised-violet curd from the raw stone I have just exposed. Their mineral spurs rasp against the shale with that dry, endless hiss. A small one wedges itself between my heel and the floor; I do not look down, merely shift my weight, crushing its carapace under my calloused pad with a dry pop, letting its meat and shell wash away into the silt.

Above us, the air is thick, warm, and smells of copper, lye, and the sweet, heavy breath of the quadrupeds. One of the great beasts is wallowing in the main drift ten paces away, wedged between two bone-roofed tenements. Its tallow-white flank presses against the lintel, bulging through the open frame like a wet sack of flour, leaving a thick smear of grease on the stone that we will later scrape for our lamps and our joints. Its throat-plates grind together—*crush, grind, crush*—mashing thousands of crab-shells into paste, sending a low, rumbling frequency through the flagstones that harmonizes with the percussive strikes of five hundred other masons working the terrace above us.

We do not talk about beginnings, because nothing here has ever begun; we do not talk about ends, because the ceiling never clears and the water never falls. 

When my arms grow thick and clumsy with the weight of the hammer, I do not wait for a night that will not come. I drop the chert. I reach across the stone slab and lay my flat, tallow-slick palm against the base of my partner's neck. I press hard—once, twice—feeling the steady, heavy pump of their blood beneath the fat. 

They cease their strike. 

Together, we slide back into the shallow recess behind the channel, beneath an arch of curved rib-bone lashed with wet rawhide. We curl together on a shelf of dry shale, skin to skin, the rendered fat on our bodies sealing us into a single, water-shedding knot. We eat a fist-sized lump of cold midge-paste scraped from the grease-frames—salty, dense, tasting of the river-bed—and let our heads fall against the vibrating floor.

Through the stone pillow, the entire basin speaks at once: the million clicking legs of the crabs, the rhythmic adzes of the terraces stepping down into the mist, the wheezing blowholes of the river-beasts, the deep, geological settling of lime turning into bone. 

It does not keep me awake. It is the pulse of the only world there is. My lungs fill with the warm, wet breath of the basin, my ribs match the cadence of the hammers, and in four hours, when the vibration in my marrow shifts, I will pick up the chert and strike the white floor again.
