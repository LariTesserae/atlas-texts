I would not be at the edge looking down. The drop to the timberline is an abstraction, a draft of thinner, emptier air that the cleft spits back out; to look over the lip is to look at nothing that requires care. 

I would be deeper in, where the third tier meets the raw hornfels wall, where the living rock has not been faced or cut, but only accepted as the back wall of the sleeping cells. 

At first, I think I am one of the children—spindle-limbed, sticky-palmed, reaching up into the damp drift to hook the gas-bladders by their sensory hairs, feeling that strange, slackening yield when the beast forgives the capture and sinks into a cold heap in my arms. There is something enticing about that lightness, about living only on the vertical planes, weightless as a spider on the dry-stacked slate.

Or perhaps one of the scrapers, moving in the half-light before the pearl sky bruises, the crescent rib-blade gliding beneath the bioluminescent bloom with a clean, peeling resistance like cold butter from a crock. To be the hand that feeds the hearth-cakes, measuring the day in baskets of luminous mud.

But no. Those are tasks of the early hours, and the place has a slower, heavier pulse that demands another kind of presence.

I would be older, but not yet laid out under the red gills of the salamanders on the upper shelf. My chest has broadened to its full breadth; the ribs have set hard against the cold, dense moisture. My soles have lost the pink tenderness of youth and turned that pale, horn-like grey, thick enough that the razored chips of hornfels leave only white scratches across the calluses, never blood.

My station is at the braids.

Between the third and fourth tiers, where the weight of forty dwellings presses down upon the cantilevered slabs of slate, the builders long ago drove the pegs of petrified wood deep into the hairline faults of the mountain. From these pegs run the tension lines—braids of tough alpine grass, thick as a forearm, boiled in snail-tallow to keep the rot away, pulled taut by river-smoothed counterweights that hang in the dark vertical flues behind the tenements. 

The stone here is young and restless. Every few days, the mountain shrugs—a microscopic, unhearable settling that would shatter a mortared wall into shingle. The braids take the strain. They stretch, they groan at frequencies too low for the ear, and they slacken.

My work is to feel them.

I do not use tools for this. In the middle hours of the dim day, when the harvesters are resting and the hound-things are asleep in their crevices with their eight legs tucked under their velvet bellies, I walk the back flues. The air in these narrow slots is warmer, thick with the smell of wet liverwort, cold smoke, and the faint, sweet musk of our own shared skin bleeding through the cracks of the sleeping cells.

I step from lintel to peg, moving with the flat-footed, roll-stepping gait that leaves the rock undisturbed. I reach into the dark and lay my bare chest, or the flat of my forehead, directly against the taut grass cords. 

When you press your bone to the braid, the whole shelf speaks to you at once.

You feel the slow, fat slide of the gastropods three tiers down, their heavy shells grinding gently against the damp slate. You feel the shudder of the cistern salamanders shifting their bulk against the stone gutters. You feel the tension of the counterweights swinging half an inch in the draught. And beneath all of that, through the petrified peg, you feel the hornfels itself—cold, brittle, held in a vice of tectonic memory, waiting for an excuse to shear.

When a braid has slackened, I don't shout. I find the child whose turn it is to carry the wedges. We do not speak; I place two fingers against the side of their neck, pulse the rhythm of the tier, and together we haul the river-stone an inch higher, driving a fresh shingle of slate beneath the lever until the grass hums against our collarbones with the correct, low murmur.

In the evening, when the forum fills and the blue hearths are lit, I sit near the back, against the dry wall where the blind shale-predator has made its perch. Its carapace is cold against my shoulder, its oral cilia fluttering faintly as it digests the grey moths of the afternoon. 

I do not hold the slate slab. That is for the elders whose joints have begun to turn back into lime. I hold only a river-pebble, round and black, carried up three hundred years ago from waters none of us will ever see. 

When the sighing begins—when a hundred wet stones begin their slow, circular dance across the grooved slate—I do not watch the players. I tilt my head back until the greasy, felted wool of my hair cushions my skull against the bedrock. I close my eyes. 

The vibration comes down through the mountain, into the hornfels, through the mortarless joints, through my vertebrae, and into the teeth in my jaw. It is a language without words: a report that the shelf is still balanced, that the damp is still falling, that the wall still holds the cliff apart, and that for one more night, the mountain has agreed to let us stay pinched within its teeth, silent, crowded, and warm.
