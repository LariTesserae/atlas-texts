I would not be in the open air. The Aerosacs have the middle sky, and their existence is too broad, too buoyant, too exposed to the clean terror of the drop. Nor would I be along the catwalks or draped over the copper boiler jackets where the steam is thickest and the green life fights for surface area in the light. 

I would be in the dark, non-space between the materials.

Specifically: four hundred feet inside the cliff wall, within the drilled socket of a primary anchor pin—one of the nine-foot-thick forged alloy dowels that pins the cantilever truss of a forty-story rotary generator to the unyielding basalt scarp.

There is a tolerance gap there. When the Works were seated into the freshly blasted mountain, the pins were driven home, but iron and stone do not share a thermal expansion coefficient, nor do they share a resonance. Under the endless, twelve-thousand-stroke fundamental frequency of the engines, that microscopic interface should have ground itself into pulverized gray flour within fifty seasons. The steel should have sawed the rock; the rock should have notched the steel until the entire gantry network sheared off and fell three leagues into the cloud deck.

I am what filled the tolerance.

***

At first, there is only pressure. 

I have no eyes, because forty yards deep in a bored bore-hole there has never been a photon. I do not have a shape in the way a fern or an organ-pipe has a shape; my shape is the exact negative volume of a clearance gap, thirty-two microns wide on the high-compression side, widening to three-eighths of an inch along the relief arc. 

I am a living hydraulic meniscus. 

My substance is a dense, highly organized cambium of silica-plated plant cells suspended in an oily, cold-water mucus that never congeals and never boils. I am fed from the wet face outside: fine, hair-thin vascular siphon-threads creep backward out of the socket, following the microscopic shatter-cracks of the blast site, reaching out into the mineralized, copper-heavy condensate that coats the cliff. They draw that bitter, sub-zero runoff inward, pulling it through capillary channels under incredible negative pressure, stripping out the dissolved zinc, the suspended machine grease, and the trace iron salts.

Inside the socket, I pack the space solid. I am under three hundred tons of sheer load per square inch. 

When the generator turns, the pin does not strike the rock. It strikes *me*.

***

To exist here is to understand the rift not as a view, but as a distribution of strain.

I feel the continent’s weight behind me—miles of cold, uncracked slate extending into the dark interior of the landmass, utterly still, dead, and immense. And I feel the machine before me—a suspended iron mountain hanging over nothing, shuddering with the kinetic frenzy of its locked stroke.

Every stroke of the engine is an event in my flesh. 

When the piston fires on the north stroke, the truss dips two millimeters; the top of my ring is crushed until my cell walls interlock like microscopic slate shingles, and the hydraulic fluid in my core is forced through sub-cellular micro-valves into my lower ring. The fluid warms slightly from the friction—just enough to stay at precisely 2.1 degrees. When the piston returns on the south stroke, the pressure eases; my pressurized walls spring back with the elastic memory of vulcanized fiber, pushing the heavy iron pin true, centering it before it can score the basalt.

I am a living, self-replenishing shim. 

I do not think in words, but in harmonics. I know the state of the entire Works by the timbre of the vibration transmitted through my pin. If a sluice-gate three miles to the south freezes slightly at its hinge, the rhythm stumbles by a thousandth of a microsecond; I feel the asymmetrical shudder travel through the continuous basalt sheet like a sharp, cold glass needle, and my siphons adjust their mineral uptake, depositing harder silicate crystals along my southern margin to brace for the new vector.

***

Over centuries, I have not stayed confined to a single socket.

Because the rock was fractured recently by planetary force, the scarp is laced with hairline cleavages—planes where the basalt wanted to part, but remained bound. My hyphae have found these planes. 

I have grown backward and sideways through the dark stone, weaving a dense, fibrous, root-thick web that connects three hundred anchor sockets across two miles of the sheer wall. I have become a continuous, hidden lamina running just beneath the skin of the cliff. 

Where the rock is brittle, I secrete an acidic, petrifying latex that fills the micro-cracks and cures into an organic agate, gluing the fractured crystalline planes together with tensile strength greater than the original stone. Where the machine’s vibration threatens to shatter the cliff face into the abyss, I absorb the acoustic shear, drinking the 12,000-stroke chord through my hydraulic network and converting that world-splitting violence into the quiet, interior work of cell division.

Outside, the great double-rainbow hangs unmoving over five miles of empty sky. The Aerosacs drift in their towering thermal chimneys; the Bell-Stalks drone in their batteries; the vast curtain of iron-moss weeps cold water into the void. It looks as though the Works are mounted to the mountain forever, as though the iron and the planet were made for each other.

They are not. They are tearing at each other with every fraction of a second.

I am the cold, wet, patient thing that sits between them, crushed between the iron and the slate, holding the catastrophe perfectly still.
