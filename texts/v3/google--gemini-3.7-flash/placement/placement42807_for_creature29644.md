I would not be walking the Cable-Roads. Those paths are for the swift and the light, for those whose blood still carries a memory of hurry. 

Nor would I be a colossus, whose thoughts must be so broad and cold they cannot be called thoughts at all, but merely the slow alignment of mineral planes over epochs.

I would be found in the narrow, lightless crease where two Stelae stand flank to flank—in the deep, compressed seam where their wool has grown into a single, continuous felt. 

There, suspended eight spans above the siltstone floor by the sheer lateral pressure of two mountains of cool, fibrous meat, I occupy a hollow carved out with a flat blade of slate. It is a dry, fibrous pocket, smelling of powdered calcite and ancient, unwashed keratin. It is warm—not with the heat of a furnace, but with the stubborn, banked temperature of a stone that was baked during the creation of the world and has never been permitted to cool.

***

At first, I thought I was a man who had chosen to live in the wool. 

I had tools: three scrapers of dark chert, an awl bored from the toe-bone of an alpine hare that went extinct before the cliffs were smoothed, and a small mallet of polished river-silt with a handle of braided sinew. I had a task: I was a *Loomer*, or perhaps a *Tensioner*. My fingers, dusted white with gypsum bloom, were meant to clear the debris from the signal-cords that run through the under-fleece, ensuring that when the mallet strikes a horn three miles away, the vibration passes clean through the meat without being smothered by the felt.

I would sit with my knees drawn to my chin, my spine pressed into the left beast’s ribcage, the soles of my bare feet planted flat against the right. 

Between my arches ran the main lateral cable—sixteen plaited strands of under-wool soaked in old tallow and drawn until it was as hard and resonant as iron rod. If I bit the cable, clamping it lightly between my rear molars, I could taste the dust of seventy leagues of static herd. 

I could hear the world through my jaw.

To the west, the slow, dry friction of a Mica-Crab climbing a horn-flute: *tick... tick... tick...* separated by intervals of three days. 

Beneath me, two hundred fathoms down in the pillar’s core: the crystal lattice of the stone readjusting its grain under the weight of three million tons of unmoving ungulate. 

And far above, filtered through the ribbed canopy of the ray-beasts: the thin, dead hum of the unchanging sun vibrating against the upper sky.

***

As the seasons failed to change, the distinction between the pocket and myself began to lose its edge.

My skin no longer produces oil. When I rub my palms together, they do not slide; they rasp like two pieces of fine-grit slate, shedding a faint cloud of dry skin that drifts into the fleece and disappears. My transparent second eyelids have closed and stayed closed; the world is a pale, milky amber, soft around the edges, perfectly still. I do not need to see the dark obsidian eyes of the beasts to know what they are looking at. They are looking at the same nothing they saw when the air ceased to move.

Small things live with me in the hollow. 

A pair of Chalk-Weavers have built their dry silk bridges across the space between my left collarbone and the beast’s pelt. They move so slowly that in the morning—if there were a morning—they are always precisely where I left them, their many-jointed, chalk-white legs hooked into the weave of my woolen tunic. Sometimes, over the course of a generation, one of them will step from my shoulder onto the beast’s flank, and the silk it drags behind will gently bind my linen to its hide. 

I do not break the thread. To break a thread here would be an act of violence, a sudden, jagged noise in an architecture that has spent ten million years eliminating the accidental.

***

I have come to understand that I am not a resident here; I am a joint.

When the beast to my left settles its weight—that great, seismic contraction that occurs once in a human lifetime—I will feel it first not as sound, but as an unbearable, glorious pressure across my ribs. The two Stelae will come together by the width of a single finger. 

My marrow will take the load. The hollow I occupy will tighten, compacting the wool, the dry silk of the weavers, my own calcified bones, and the stone mallet into a single, dense stratum. 

I will not fall. There is nowhere to fall to. The air will not permit it, the wool will not permit it, and the geometry of this place does not have a void large enough to receive an undoing.

Until then, I keep my teeth against the wire. 

I listen to the great heart deep in the ribcage to my left. It has been gathering its strength for forty years. The chambers are full of static silicates; the valves are dry as parchment and tight as slate. 

Any century now, it will beat. And through the soles of my feet, through my jaw, through the chert tools tucked into the felt beside me, I will pass the tremor along to the next pillar, so that the ones who hang in the cables know that the plane has held, that the weight is true, and that nothing has slipped even a hairsbreadth toward the end.
