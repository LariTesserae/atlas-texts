It is difficult to begin with a name, because in a place where nothing decomposes, nothing is named to distinguish it from its rot. 

If I look for where I settle, I do not find myself among the active movers—not on the spinning rims where the horn-disks ride, nor along the long copper conduits where the ribbon-weavers take their charge. Those are creatures of the stroke. They have business with the cycle; they measure themselves against the piston’s drop.

I am deeper in the geometry than that, where the mountain ceases to be stone and the machinery ceases to be iron.

Where the great dynamo housings are bolted directly to the granite wall, there are four-inch anchor studs driven deep into the frozen slate. Around those bolts, between the cold, dense mineral of the peak and the gray iron footings that shudder with every revolution, there is an interface. A gap no thicker than a fingernail, packed with lead leaf, powdered sulfur, and the drift of crushed, resinous spores that has settled under pressure until it is as hard and smooth as vulcanite.

That is where the weight gathers. That is where I would be.

Not an animal with limbs to reach or lungs to draw, but a flat, laminate existence—a living shim pressed between the geology and the stroke.

***

My body is constructed in laminar leaves, thin as shaved horn, layered like the sheets of mica that line the pilot burners, but interleaved with a dry, graphite-rich grease that does not squeeze out under tonnage. I have no front or back; I have only two broad faces: one pressed immovably against the slate, which is sub-zero, immovable, and veined with ancient quartz; the other pressed against the machine base, which runs hot, slick with ester-vapor, and pulses with five hundred oscillations every minute.

I exist entirely in that shear.

The machine wants to walk. Every time the heavy counterweight clears its bottom dead center, sixty tons of cast iron tries to shift three thousandths of an inch toward the abyss. The anchor bolt holds it, but the granite beneath the footing groans; the iron wants to plane away the rock, and the rock wants to crack the iron’s ears.

I am the thing that translates them to each other.

My metabolism is not fed by eating or by the blue static snaps that the frond-weavers chase. It is fed by the friction of the mismatch. The endless, microscopic fretting between the iron’s expansion and the mountain’s cold rigidity shears the dry sulfur-crystals inside my plates. That shearing generates a slow, continuous piezoelectric trickle—a dull, smoldering charge that circulates through my graphite veins, keeping the aromatic resins within me just soft enough to yield without failing, just dense enough to keep the engine true.

***

What do I perceive?

No light reaches this seam, not even the faint copper flicker of the pilot flames. But the cleft is completely transparent to me in the language of vibration.

Through my top face, I feel the whole engine-room as a single, branching iron skeleton:
I feel the clearance in the crosshead slippers as a clean, square snap at the top of the stroke. 
I feel the tiny, dry scuttling of the grease-scrapers on the flywheel rim—a faint, sandpaper hiss that drifts across the shaft twice per second. 
I feel the keeper three yards away, its heavy, tallow-thick body slumped against the bearing pedestal, its slow, non-aqueous pulse thudding like a distant sandbag dropped on the felt floor once every ten cycles.

And through my bottom face, I feel the mountain:
The cold, bottomless needle of slate dropping miles into the roaring cloud deck; the deep, iron pipes plunging into the earth’s red core, hissing with dry vapor; the silent, immense strain of the winter winds screaming against the outer needle, trying to snap this whole granite thumb from the ridge.

I sit between the gale and the flywheel.

***

Sometimes, over decades, the balance drifts. A bearing wears down by the thickness of a moth’s wing; the convective draft shifts as a vent fills with dry, waxy pollen; the engine’s song drops a quarter-tone into a heavier, more brutal pound.

When that happens, the shear across my body changes. The pressure on my left margin increases by three hundred atmospheres; the heat rises until the sulfur between my horn-leaves begins to soften into a dark, viscous syrup. 

I do not move—I cannot move—but under that uneven agony of weight, I slowly yield. I allow the resin to migrate from my compressed edge to my relieved edge, molecule by molecule, over months of relentless thrumming. I change my thickness by sixty microns. I tilt the engine's bedplate by a fraction of an arc-second.

The counterweight finds its true center again. The high-pitched chattering in the valve spindles subsides. The keeper behind the dynamo does not have to wake; its quartz eyes remain dark beneath their shutters. The stroke settles back into its flat, teeth-rattling *clack-whir-thump*, and the heat drains away, leaving me tempered, compressed, and slightly more like the rock than I was before.

***

In this anhydrous closet at the top of the sky, nothing is born and nothing is discarded. 

If I am anything, I am the memory of the contact. If the fires below ever cool and the counterweights slow to an absolute stop, the iron will contract with a sound like pistol shots, and the mountain will take up the slack. I will be caught there still, cold and glassy, a paper-thin fossil of lead and amber resin, holding the dead weight of the piston true against the granite forever.
