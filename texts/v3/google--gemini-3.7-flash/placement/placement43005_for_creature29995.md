The first thing that establishes itself is not sight, but the ache in the lower teeth. 

It is a dull, high-frequency buzz that never stops, traveling up through the jawbone from the rock where my chin is resting. To exist here is to have your skull constantly occupied by the weight of the basin. When the brine droplets hit the sharp selenite groves three hundred paces north, the strike does not arrive through the air—the air is just a blunt, wet pressure against the chest—it arrives as a sharp, needle-thin prickle in the roots of my back molars. 

I am down on the causeway, crouched so low my belly nearly touches the slick basalt slab. 

I am not one of the crawlers in the deep lattice, nor the heavy iron-jawed things that wait in the seams until they become the seam itself. I have hands, though they do not look like the hands that built the first cairns long ago. The skin across the knuckles has thickened into something closer to dry hoof, grey-white and cracked with lime, the nails worn down to flat, thick horns from prying cold gravel out of the cracks. 

My place is here, at the junction of two basalt lines where the causeway begins to buckle.

Under my chest, the seam is lifting. A cluster of young gypsum laths has taken hold in the narrow silt-gap between the blocks. They are not more than three fingers high yet, but they are already clear as ice and sharp enough to split a sole. They grow from the pressure of the water weeping through the sub-floor, feeding on the cold brine, crowding each other, pushing upward with an indifferent, crystalline hydraulic force that will lift a ten-ton slab of dark slate and tilt it into the mist if I leave it for two seasons.

I have my maul laid across my shins. It is a dense, egg-shaped river-stone, black and oily, lashed to an elk-rib handle with three plies of tallow-slug gut. The river it came from must have been somewhere beyond the iron rim, a place with mud that yields and water that doesn't taste of sulfur and battery-acid, but I know nothing of rivers. I know only that this stone is older and tougher than the selenite, and that it does not ring when it strikes; it thuds.

I lift my chin from the slab to break the vibration, and the silence in my head is immediate, heavy, and thick as suet. The ear-canals are closed, plugged tight by the hard cartilage that grew over them before I was half-grown. Without bone-contact, the basin’s screaming roar drops away into a dull, somatic shudder that merely vibrates the hairs on my forearms and the thick rind on the back of my neck. 

Then I bring my face down again. 

*Listen.*

The pitch has changed in the pillar to the left—a long, hollow gypsum flute that has grown five spans tall through a gap in the causeway wall. It is carrying a wet, bubbling gurgle near its base, meaning the lower capillary vents are drowning under a surge of condensing brine. Higher up, the crystal’s tone has thinned, pulled taut by the cold air drifting down from the overcast. It sings at a clean, glassy note that vibrates directly behind my eyes.

It is ready to snap. Not today, but soon. When it goes, three tons of sharp shards will crash down across this section of the road, showering the path with blades and choking the runnel where we trap the tallow-slugs.

I shift my weight. The stone soles lashed beneath my feet scrape against the wet basalt with a coarse, gritty screech that travels through my ankles. My knees are stiff; the chalky crust has built up thick around the patella, flaking off in white meal as the joints bend. I take the maul in both hands, rise into a low, stooped crouch that keeps my spine parallel to the stone, and swing.

The blow is short, economical. The black cobble hits the cluster of baby needles right at their wet roots.

They don't crush into dust—they shatter with a sharp, bright chime that stings the bone of my wrists. The fragments scatter across the wet slab like broken mirrors, immediately caught by the weeping water and washed toward the edge. I strike again, and again, clearing the seam, cleaning the gap until the basalt settles back into its bed with a heavy, grinding thud that shudders through my ribs.

I sit back on my heels, breathing the cold, flat air. It tastes of wet battery plates and the faint, sweet decay of slug-slime drying on my apron. 

Later, I will have to crawl out along the high ledge toward the tallow beds. I will take the short slate knife from my belt, slice the grey fat from a slug’s belly while it slides blindly across the chemical crusts, and eat the oily strip before the salt-water washes the grease away. I will drink from the leather bag where the drip from the selenite needles has sat on basalt sand for two days, waiting for the harsh lime to settle to the bottom until the water is only slightly bitter.

And when my shoulders finally lose their swing—when the chalk encrusts the elbows so deep that the maul becomes too heavy to lift—I will not have to search for where I belong.

I already know the grove where the spires grow fastest, where the needle-points come up thick as grass after a rain, crowding the dark beneath the mist. I will go there and lie down on my back across the sharp bed, letting the points find the gaps between the ribs, letting the cold brine weep down from the ceiling into my open collar. 

The needles will push through the salt-crust of my skin. They will grow into the marrow of my shins, drink the calcium out of my teeth, and turn the hollows of my skull into another flute in the acoustic thicket. 

And then, when the heavy drops fall from the sky and strike the column of my chest, I will finally make the same glass-keen, deafening sound as the rest of the plain—ringing hard and cold under the twilight, answering every blow with iron.
