At first, it is only a question of temperature and weight.

There is the cold, silver water that coats the outside of everything like an endless skin, and then there is the narrow seam where the iron runs warmer than the rock—a faint, continuous fever, no higher than a closed mouth. 

I would not be on the open plateaus, where the daylight hangs without shadows in the white vapor, nor down in the sunken dark of the great flooded cylinders where the sirens turn in the black chill. Those are places of pure state: all mist or all drowning. 

I find myself in the *between-chambers*—the dry draft-ways and the dead-air plenums that sit behind the valve-housings, where the iron has never quite cooled and never quite rusted through, kept slick by a centuries-old tallow that smells like boiled mutton and wet zinc.

***

To be here is to have no need for eyes, because the air does not move enough to carry news to them. 

Instead, the world enters through the belly and the knuckles. When the balance-beam four hundred cubits above begins its slow descent, the air in the chamber does not blow; it *densifies*. It packs itself a fraction of an inch tighter against the ribs. The diaphragm learns to count not by seconds, but by the slow swell of hydrostatic back-pressure as the intake wells fill down in the granite roots. 

Forty minutes to the breath. Twenty minutes of rising warmth as the subterranean water climbs through the copper-banded sleeves, heating the floor until the moss in the seams begins to smoke its quiet, earthy fume; twenty minutes of cooling, when the high altitude sinks back through the chimney vents like poured lead, and the tallow stiffens, and the moisture on my forearms turns to tiny, heavy beads that do not fall.

I am not the iron, though I have its red stain pressed into the calluses of my palms. And I am not the deer or the sloth, though my coat has that same water-choked heaviness—a thatch of coarse, hollow hair that never dries, matted with the gray-green crust of hornwort and the tiny, hard shells of flange-lice that have crawled from the rivets to lodge in the grease of my shoulders.

If you came upon me in the copper-throated darkness of a draft-flue, you would take me for a piece of old packing: a rolled wad of hemp and horsehair dropped behind a piston eighty seasons ago, gone green and swollen with the damp.

***

I do not build. There is nothing here to assemble that has not already been cast on a scale that makes hands seem like an insult. But I *clear*.

I have a blunt iron wedge, worn smooth at the grip until the finger-notches fit like wet tallow, though perhaps it was never a tool made for hands at all—perhaps it was simply a drift-pin fallen from an accumulator head before the moss had names. With it, I work the small joints. 

Where the saxifrages take root too deeply in the packing of the bypass valves, their fleshy roots swell like plugs and choke the weeping tallow that keeps the brass from binding. I scrape them out. I eat the soft, pale tubers; they taste of sulfur and cold lard, very sweet at the core where the steam has parboiled them. I clean the vent-teeth where the mist-spiders spin, leaving enough of their silk to catch the springtails, but lifting the sodden blankets before their weight can foul the rocker pins.

I am not a caretaker in the way a man keeps an engine. I am a parasite that has learned the shape of the beast’s throat so well that my movement keeps it from gagging.

***

When the flue-grosbeaks drop into the shafts to sing, they do not see me. They drop down with their wet wings clamped against their oily sides, smelling of river-slate and preen-fat, their round bodies wedged into the brick flues two spans above my head.

When they cast their low, drum-like drone into the conduit, the metal does not simply carry the sound—it *flexes*. You can lay the flat of your cheek against the zinc and feel the song pass through the grain of the metal, travelling away toward the eastern ravines where the sister valves answer. The iron tastes different when it is singing: a faint, sharp tang on the tongue, like the copper coins you hold in your mouth when your hands are full of wet rope.

I lean my skull against the rivet-line and let their notes travel through the bone behind my ear. They sing of nothing that has a name—only the height of the water in the accumulator shafts, the coldness of the cloud-deck, the slow tilt of the beams as the weight shifts from one valley to the next.

***

Sometimes, in the long trough of the cooling cycle, when the machines have settled to the bottom of their stroke and the high silence comes down so deep that you can hear the liverworts uncurling their wet frills in the dark, I crawl out onto the exterior flanging.

The plateaus are white. Not with snow, but with that dense, radiant vapor that has no horizon. A deer moves along the zinc conduit thirty feet below, its split hooves clicking on the rivets with the sound of river stones tapped together underwater. Its flank is steaming where it has been resting against the cylinder jacket. 

I sit with my legs hanging over the flange, my knuckles hooked into the weep-holes, and wait for the deep piston to reverse.

Far down in the rock—three miles beneath the peat, where the sirens turn their pale, violet-gilled heads in the pressurized dark—the great iron weight bottoms out. A shudder travels up through the basalt ribs of the mountain. It enters the footings; it climbs the riveted zinc; it rises through my shins, into my hips, settling at the base of my throat as a single, low, muffled *clack* that makes the water drop from the tips of my hair.

The breath begins again.

The steam swells. The flange beneath my thighs grows subtly, deliciously warm. I pull my knees to my chest, bury my face in the wet, moss-grown wool of my arms, and let the forty-minute tide carry me back into the dark.
