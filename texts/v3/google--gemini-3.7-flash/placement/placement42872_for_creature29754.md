The cold does not come from outside; it is simply the ground state of matter here, like the density of basalt or the thickness of tallow. 

At first, there is only the vibration in the collarbones. 

Before any thought of limbs or a face, there is the hum. It travels up through the seat of the pelvis, through the two flat stones that serve as an anchor, and into the marrow of the spine. It is thirty miles of bird-wings beating high in the clefts, smoothed by distance and sheer wall-bounce into a single, dry, purring engine that never idles down. You do not hear it with the ears after a while; it replaces the sound of your own pulse. Your ribs are tuned to it. If it stopped, the chasm would drop like a severed rope.

I am not in the high walls. Up there the air is too thin and wet with the spit-glue of the brackets, smelling of grease and frantic, half-feathered heat. Nor am I down in the deep scree where the slate-biters roll the dead; the blind beetles have no need of anything that pauses, and to sit among them is to have your hems shredded and your heels scraped raw by mandibles that cannot tell ankle from splintered martin-wing.

I am wedged where the stone changes temperament. 

Behind me, four feet into the cleft, the dust is dry as slaked lime. It smells of sixty human winters that were never winters—just long, motionless accumulations of guano ash, shed skin-flakes, and the dry, pepper-like droppings of crickets that crawled in here to die out of the damp. In front of me, twelve inches past my bare toes, the condensation begins: that fine, dark sheen where the rock turns purple-black and weeps its silent, oily sweat toward the floor.

I sit on that exact seam. 

My thighs are broad and flat, covered in two layers of skin: my own, which is the color of lard rendered three times and thick at the knuckles, and an apron made of thirty-two swiftlet breasts, greasy side down, held together with the dried gut of the big blind crawlers. The apron is stiff as sheet-horn; it smells of old lard and cold stone. When I lay my palms flat upon it, there is no heat to lose. The body down here has long since given up trying to broadcast warmth into the shale. We keep it balled tight behind the breastbone, like a glowing coal carried in a horn lantern.

My hands are wide, the nails squared off from prying cobbles and splitting the wing-bones for their narrow marrow-gutters. When I want to speak to the ones behind me in the dust, I don't use breath. Breath is too precious to waste on the roar. I tap the side of my thumb against the flat edge of a basalt slab resting between my knees. 

*Clack-tick.*

It is the stone that carries it. Three hundred paces down the shelf, an old woman lying curled in the skink-skins feels the strike in her hip-socket. She knows: *The fall is heavy today on the west flare.* 

She doesn't move yet. There is no hurry. Nothing down here rots fast; the cellar air preserves everything in a dull, damp pause. A fledgling that strikes the rock at the third hour of the gray light is still sweet at the tenth.

My eyes are wet and round, like peeled onions, resting in deep bone sockets beneath an overhanging ridge of brow. I see very little of what the upper world might call a shape. I do not see the sky—that white slit far above is only a place where the darkness goes dilute and milky, like water in which a tallow-candle was washed. But I see the *gleam*. I see where the moisture catches the gray; I see the sudden black interruption when a heavy mass drops through the violet air; I see the orange-white shimmer of the cricket sheets when they roll like a thrown net over a fallen nest-bracket.

Mostly, I am the watcher of the edges.

Every twelve or twenty falls, something comes down that is not a bird. 

Sometimes it is a slab of the upper lip itself—a chunk of limestone the size of a hay-wain that has lost its grip on the shale, whistling down through the roar to burst against the talus like a sack of dry meal. When that happens, the vibration knocks the crickets three inches into the air and leaves the beetles spinning on their backs, clicking their spade-legs in fury until the dust settles. 

Sometimes it is something older: a dry, brown limb of a surface tree that grew out over the void until its root-ball rotted, tumbling down through the gray wash, stripped clean of its green leaves by the long fall. We bring those in carefully. We do not burn them; wood is too hard, too strange, too full of ancient sun that has no business in the clefts. We scrape the bark into fine tinder to catch the spark from the chert-strike, and we use the straight heartwood to make the long prying-tines that let us reach deep under the lizard-carpets without waking the pale-whips.

The pale-whips are the only things that do not make a sound on the stone. They are the white silence that moves under the purr.

A shadow shifts on the shale thirty paces out, just where the dry dust yields to the slick black floor. A skink—fat as a child's thigh, its skin the dull, greasy violet of bruised lard—is working its way up the slope, nudging blind through the scree, following the scent of cold blood. It moves by inches, its short legs paddling in the loose stones with a sound like dry peas sliding in a bladder.

I lift the bone-pointed tine from beside my knee. 

I do not strike it. I wait for it to reach the basalt pavement where the vibration is clean. When its heavy, cold chin rests upon the stone, I strike the basalt once with my knuckles: *thud.*

The lizard does not startle—it has no startle in it—but it stops. Its pale throat pulses twice, tasting the stone. It turns its broad, flat skull toward the alcove, its clouded black eye catching the milk-light. 

We look at one another across the ten feet of stone that separates the dry bone-dust from the weeping rock. Neither of us blinks. We are both made of the same cold tallow; we both eat the same slow, falling sky; we both know that forty feet below us, in the deep horizontal cracks where no light ever washes, the white ropes of mold are slowly turning our dropped scrap into stone again.

I lower the tine. 

I take a pinch of the dried liverwort from the pouch at my belt, tuck it into the hollow behind my lower teeth, and let the bitter, iron-tasting juice run down into my throat. Then I lay my head back against the cold, clean fracture-plane of the wall, close my onion-eyes against the milky gray, and let the great, acoustic engine of the birds grind me back into the balance.
