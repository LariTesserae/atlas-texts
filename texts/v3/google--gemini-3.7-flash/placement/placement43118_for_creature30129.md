I am not above the timber; there is nowhere to look down from that is not itself already lifting or sinking on a column of cold sap.

If I try to locate my edges, I find them where the frost stops.

At first, I might seem to be an accumulation—one of those long, tapered drifts of sawdust that gathers in the dead-angle behind a shearing plate, where the wind cannot clear the angle. But sawdust does not brace itself against the heave. Sawdust simply falls into the pinch. 

I am wider than I am tall, though my height changes depending on the hour’s stroke. My body is built around three long, lateral vertebrae of dry, dense ironwood—not bone, but living culm, seasoned under such intense cross-grain pressure that it has long since forgotten how to splinter. My skin is heavy, dark, and greasy to the touch, like the rind of an old ham that has hung through five winters in an unheated shed. It does not feel the cold as pain; it feels the cold as a tightening of the weave, a pleasant, astringent grip that keeps my internal moisture from escaping into the dry gale.

I live in the **Neutral Axes**—the thin, shifting planes within the great girders where the wood is neither under tension nor under compression. 

In every arching vine that spans a ravine, there is a top surface being stretched until the fibers whimper, and an underside being crushed until the cells turn to mush. But precisely in the middle—sometimes an inch thick, sometimes only the width of a knife-blade—lies the zero-point. The plane of zero shear. That is where I lie.

I am a *Shim-Keeper*, or perhaps simply a creature that has mistaken a mechanical tolerance for a home.

***

My days—if they can be called days under that cold, unblinking white glare—are spent wedged flat between the sliding laminations of the upper defiles. 

I do not walk; walking requires an arrogance of surface that this mountain quickly cures. I migrate by displacement. When the cambium-piston beneath me begins its slow, six-foot upward stroke, the lamination above me slides back by an equal measure. I grease the interface. I secrete a thick, yellowish lipid from glands along my ribs—a cold tallow that smells faintly of turpentine and wet sheep—and smear it against the dry grain with the broad, flat spades of my fore-limbs. 

The wood takes the tallow eagerly. It softens the abrasive bite of the mineral crust. In return, the motion carries me. I am a passenger in the machine’s own friction, carried five hundred yards up a ridge without moving a single muscle of my own, simply by lying between two surfaces that have business with each other.

I have no eyes. Light here is only a measure of exposure—to be in the glare is to be dry, and to be dry is to be brittle. I see by the *grain-lines*.

When I press the flat of my forehead against the heartwood, the mountain presents itself not as shapes, but as vectors of terrible, silent intent:
* To the north, three miles deep, a vascular valve the size of an abbey is filling with sub-zero brine; I feel the low, seismic ache of its walls stretching, a 4-hertz thrum that rattles the oil in my gut.
* Directly beneath my belly, a knot of petrified vine is reaching its yield point; the fibers are parting one by one with tiny, dry twitches, like hairs being plucked from a hide.
* Half a mile above, on the wind-raked crest, a company of Seamers is tapping their ironwood wedges into an opening flue. Their taps are light, rhythmic, dry—*tack, tack, tack-tack*—asking the grain if it intends to hold until nightfall.

I answer them sometimes, though not out of fellowship. If a Seamer drives a wedge too deep into a high-load arch, they risk setting off a running split that will ruin the neutral axis I am resting in. When I feel their ironwood biting too close to a shear-line, I back my rump against the split and drive a burst of pressurized mucus into the crack. The moisture swells the dry grain instantly; the split clamps shut on their wedge, trapping it immovable. They feel the sudden, dead weight through the handle of their hammer and know enough to back away. *Occupied,* the wood says to them. *Find another pocket.*

***

What do I eat? The mountain sheds constantly, but it does not shed meat.

In the afternoon, when the sun stands at its highest and the mist in the hollows turns from gel to a fine, drifting meal, the atomizing vents along the ridge begin to purge. They do not blow steam; they blow an aerosol of supercooled sugar-water, dense with starches that have been fermented in the root-pumps under four hundred atmospheres of pressure.

I do not leave my seam to feed. I have two long, hollow quills that extend from the corners of my mouth—flexible pipes of horn, ribbed like goose-throats. I snake them out through the weep-holes in the bark, out into the thin, freezing draft.

The mist coats the quills in seconds, freezing into long rimes of grey, sugary frost. When the layer is thick enough, I draw the quills back inside through the narrow clearance gap, scraping the frost off against the sharp, horn-lined margin of my lips. It tastes of iron, pine-resin, and the sweet, dead cellar-rot of starch that has never seen a leaf. It is cold enough to make my gullet knot, but once it reaches the central sack of my stomach, the tallow-ferment takes it, turning it into the thick grease that keeps my hide supple.

***

There are times, during the great re-alignments, when the neutral axis vanishes.

A sudden heave of new cellular growth below will turn what was once a resting-flue into an active compression-tier. When that happens, the wood gives no warning through sound; the velvet drinks the alarm before it can leave the cells. There is only the *weight*.

I feel my rib-culms begin to bow. My internal organs, which are mostly bags of heavy, non-compressible oil, flatten out toward my flanks. My skin stretches until the yellow tallow weeps through every pore, creating a hydro-static film that prevents the bark from tearing my hide away.

In those hours, I am entirely motionless. I become a gasket. A layer of living grease two inches thick, spanning twenty square feet of contact area between two massive balks of ironwood that are trying to grind each other into peat. If I resist, I am turned to sawdust in three seconds. If I yield completely, I am squeezed out like marrow from a split bone and dropped into the abyss below.

So I balance. I let three tons of pressure push through my left flank while my right flank vents oil to equalize the hydraulic head. I breathe only when the machine breathes—a slow, shallow inflation during the microscopic back-lash when a cambium-piston reaches the top of its stroke and pauses before the downward draw.

It is a small life, measured in millimeters of clearance and thousands of pounds per square inch.

I have no name for the mountain. To me, it is simply the *Work*. It has been turning its heartwood over, ring by ring, cold year by cold year, since before the wood forgot how to rot. I do not think it knows I am here, any more than a locomotive knows the identity of the oil on its slide-bars.

When the light finally fails—not by setting, but by the mist thickening until the glare is choked to a uniform, leaden grey—the mountain’s rhythm slows by a fraction. The frost gets into the outer cambium, stiffening the fibers, making the giant pistons work harder against the cold.

I pull my quills in. I tuck my flat paws beneath my breast-culm. The two plates of wood above and below me settle together with a dull, sub-audible groan that I feel through my teeth. The clearance closes from two inches to three-quarters of an inch.

The pressure is enormous, steady, and perfectly cold. It holds me so tightly that my pulse cannot move unless I push it with my whole body.

I let the mountain take my shape. I let the resin seal the seams. I wait for the next stroke.
