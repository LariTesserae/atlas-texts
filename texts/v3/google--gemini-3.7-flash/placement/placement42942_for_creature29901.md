At first, there is no sense of having an edge. 

To arrive here is to lose the habit of the horizon. In a room twenty paces wide, buried beneath three miles of basalt, space is not measured by how far the eye can travel, but by how heavily the mountain rests upon the stone, and how cold the moisture sits against a surface before it consents to run.

If I look for where I begin, I do not find myself standing on the carpet, nor suspended in the dark clefts above. Those are places of completed purpose—the turf that has already woven its conductors through the shale, the hanging gourds that have already perfected their cages of dark composite. 

I find myself instead at the hairline.

I am pressed against the narrowest boundary in the room: the clean, unyielding seam where a spar of oil-slick alloy dips beneath the liverwort mat. 

My body is not soft, though it yields to extreme, patient pressure like a cake of cold beeswax or chilled pitch. I am shaped like a disc of wet river-slate, flat-bellied and slightly domed, no larger than an open palm, but weighted with the density of heavy-metal salts leached from the basalt over forgotten centuries. I have no pulse. The blood that would have carried heat in an animal has here been replaced by an oily, pale lipid that remains fluid down into the deep frost, viscous as glycerol, holding my layered cellulose together in a heavy, impenetrable silence.

Beneath me, my underside is a dense brush of pale capillary filaments. They do not burrow into the rock—basalt is too proud, and the alloy is entirely seamless—but they fit themselves into the microscopic roughness of the boundary. 

Through these filaments, the world enters me.

It does not enter as light, for there is none, nor as sound, for the air is too cold and muffled to carry a voice. The world is an array of frequencies and weights.

Through my left flank, I feel the ancient architecture: a dry, greasy stillness, an impossibly rapid vibration that never generates heat, humming at a pitch so tight it registers only as a rigid sort of calm. It bleeds a faint electrostatic field into my cellulose, charging the metal grains in my lower rim like iron filings around a lodestone.

Through my right flank, I feel the wet green: the slow, damp drag of the bryophytes, cold water condensing along the margins of my shell, and the deep, communal respiration of the black fibers beneath the peat. 

I am where they meet. I am the scab that forms over the tension between the machine and the moss.

I do not think in words; thoughts would move too quickly and tear the cold lipids in my tissues. Instead, I register *states*. 

There is the state of the hanging drop: high above me, suspended from the needle-tip of a wire-fern, a single sphere of water swells for five hours, its mass pulling at the frond. I feel the deflection of the fern through the conductive turf. When the drop finally breaks free and falls—striking the gravel hollow twelve feet away with that solitary, glass-like *clink*—the kinetic tap does not travel through the air to my ears; it travels through the basalt floor, passing through my capillary roots like a sudden, clean tightening of a guitar string. 

Then the silence closes over it again, thicker than before.

My motion is something that happens across seasons. To feed is not to chew, but to permit the charge to migrate through my salts. When the alloy spar bleeds its micro-currents, the mercury-like beads of water along its edge quiver and roll toward me, drawn by the faint magnetic bias of my rim. I drink them by capillary pull, drawing the pure, cold moisture through my margins, stripping out the dissolved minerals, and allowing the heavy lipids inside me to realign.

When that alignment is complete—perhaps as the mountain above shifts into its long winter thaw, though no warmth ever reaches this depth—the tension changes. I lose my grip on the alloy by the breadth of a single molecule, and the hydraulic creep begins.

I slide. It takes sixty days to move the width of a thumbnail. 

Behind me, I leave a faint, iridescent track of oxidized minerals and spent lipid—a silvered line on the bare rock. I do not look back at it, but I feel the liverworts immediately reach for it, their stiff green scales creeping over the trace, digesting the salts I have discarded, sealing the stone behind me.

Above me, in the vaulted gloom, one of the great calcified gourds contracts by a fraction of a micron as the chamber's field expands. It releases its note: a sound too deep to be heard, passing through the basalt and through my dense, slate-like shell as a momentary, exquisite ache in my mineral core. 

It is the vault taking a single breath. 

I absorb the note, steady my weight against the cold alloy seam, draw another drop of mercury-water from the boundary, and wait for the next. Nothing will ever disturb this room. Nothing will ever need to be different than it is. I am dense, I am cold, and I have all the time the mountain can give.
