At first, there is only the sensation of pressure—not the crushing weight of a collapse, but the steady, warm clasp of two surfaces that have known each other for a very long time.

I would not be on the open cord-walks. The sky up there is too loud, torn raw by the lip-wind, and my ribs are not built for the great, frantic lungfuls of the bird-catchers. Nor would I be down in the cold flour of the floor among the blind carapaces, turning over the junk of three hundred years with slow, unhurried claws. That is where things end, and I am still too occupied with what holds.

I find myself in the blind flutes—the narrow, hollow seams behind the stacked dwellings of the Mid-Depths, where the siltstone walls do not quite touch the living rock.

It is an intermediate world, two handspans wide at its narrowest, three cubits at its widest. Here, the amber light does not arrive directly; it is filtered through the translucent horn panes of hearth-lanterns and the gaps between dry-stacked stones, falling in thin, honey-colored blades across the dust. The air smells of charred bone-sage, old sheep-tallow, and the sweet, mineral dryness of mountain bone.

I am an **Anchorer**—a tender of the seating-pegs.

My body is small, long-limbed, and flattened, shaped by fifty seasons of sliding through fissures where a barrel-chested hunter would jam at the shoulder blades. My skin has the powdery, floury dry of the shale itself; if you drew a fingernail across my forearm, it would leave a white line of salt and silt that wouldn't bleed moisture for hours. My left shoulder is higher than my right, grown permanently bunched to cradle my cheek against the rock when I listen.

And listening is the work.

Because the chimney is a musical instrument played by the heat and the wind, the stone speaks in stresses. When the gale above shears across the rim at midday, the entire spire twists by a hair’s breadth. You cannot see it with an eye, but if you press your forehead against the root-seam behind the third tier of houses, you can feel the mountain torque. You feel the ironwood brackets groan against their sockets; you feel where the rawhide lashings have dried so hard they are threatening to shear the ibex-horn pins clean through like brittle glass.

I live in a sling of three-ply hemp, cradled between the wall of the chimney and the rear masonry of a family I have never spoken to face-to-face. 

I know them only by their heels. Through the chinks in the siltstone, I see their calloused arches passing back and forth over the floorboards. I know when the resin-boiler’s mother is having a bad day with her hips, because her weight falls unevenly on the outer juniper stringer, making the corner peg chirp with a dry, rhythmic *creak-skrrt*. I know when the toddler reaches the end of his rawhide tether by the sudden, dull *thwack* of the ironwood ring hitting the stop-notch in the wall groove.

My tools hang from my belt in pouches of swift-skin:
A bag of pulverized slate-flour.
A gourd of thick, unboiled succulent-pitch that smells like fermenting vinegar.
A bundle of tapered pegs whittled from old, dried horn, hard as river-quartz.
A small, flat wooden mallet whose striking face is padded with double-cured hyrax leather to keep the blows from echoing through the living quarters.

When a peg begins to fret in its borehole—loosened by the ceaseless, imperceptible trembling of the swift-flocks taking flight, or dried out by thirty dry moons—I am already there. I do not come from the ladders. I arrive from the crevices, chimneying down with my knees braced against the curved sandstone behind and my bare soles against the back of the house-wall.

I wedge myself into the dark. I work in the amber half-shadow, sharing the sill with an orange crawler that watches me with dull, ancient eyes, entirely undisturbed by my breath. 

First, the tongue: I taste the air around the socket. If it tastes chalky and sharp, the stone is crumbling under the strain; if it tastes smooth and bitter, it is only the resin that has perished. 

Then the mallet: *tap... thuk... tap.* Soft, deliberate strikes. The horn peg backs out with a dry shudder. I scrape the borehole clean with a hooked ironwood gouge, clearing out the pulverized grit before it can grind the socket wider. I repack the void with fresh resin-dough, mixed with the fine, slate-colored dust the swifts knock down from their nests. Then the new peg goes home—seated two fingers deeper than the last, wedged tight until the rawhide harness around the juniper joist stretches, bites, and sings true again.

The family on the other side of the wall stops their talk when they hear my mallet. Through the stone, I hear the mother’s tongue click twice—*tsik-tsik*—telling the children to hold their feet still while the rock-mender is behind the hearth.

For three breaths, we are separated by four inches of dry-stacked siltstone, suspended six hundred cubits above the floor-crabs. They do not look back at me, and I do not peer through their chinks. We simply share the tension of the cord.

When the work is done, I do not climb out onto the ramps. I ease myself down through the vertical flue, past the root-veins hanging like grey beard-hair in the dark, down to the cool ledges where the amber light dies into charcoal ash.

There, on a narrow notch of rock where the chimney draws its deepest, slowest draft, I curl into my sling. I loop my ankle around a safety rung worn black by fifty years of my own heels. Above me, the hive hums and drones—the ten thousand swifts, the women scraping horn, the dry mallets, the wind roaring miles overhead like an ocean trapped in a pipe. 

Beneath me, down in the unlit floor, the crabs sift the dropped things into flour.

And here, in the middle, pinched between the warmth of the living and the cold mass of the mountain, I rest my cheek against the smooth, scooped marrow of the rock, close my eyes, and wait for the stone to tell me where it is tired.
