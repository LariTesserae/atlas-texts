At first, there is no edge to mark where the sea ends and the body begins. 

When the medium is the temperature of living tissue, the skin forgets its ancient trade of keeping things out. I am open to it. The broth enters through wide, unvalved pores along my flanks, wanders through winding canals lined with amber cilia, and leaves without cooling. To exist here is not to inhabit a space, but to be an eddy in a larger circulation—a place where the warm, iron-thick water hesitates, turns around itself a few times, and moves on.

If you looked for me from the surface, fifty fathoms up where the sun turns the world to the color of tarnished brass, you would not see a silhouette. You would see only a thickening in the water, a faint refractive wobble like heat rising off a stone road in summer.

I am settled in the lee of the fourth basalt riser, where the cyclopean stairs meet the red algal turf of the saddle. 

I am not stone, though the mason-polyps have begun to pave the trailing hem of my mantle with their dark, rectangular crusts, mistaking my stillness for architecture. I am not a Cenotaph-Keeper, though I live directly in the fall-line of their crowns. 

When the Keeper above me senses the tilt of the world and vents its heavy, slate-colored ambergris, the oil does not rise; it is denser than the sweet water and falls in slow, curling skeins like drowned smoke. I catch it. My upper surface is broad and dished, cupped like the palm of an upturned hand, covered in a soft, mucus-rich pelt that drinks the bitter, scented oil. The smell of it fills my interior chambers—a thick, resinous hum of deep-earth bitumen and cold cellar dust, laced with the sharp, galvanic tang of the Strand-Weavers’ sky-spores that have washed down from their trapped bubbles.

I have no skeleton. My form is held entirely by the hydraulic pressure of the ocean pressing inward and the slow, rhythmic swell of my own internal channels. I am shaped like an asymmetrical cushion or a folded lobe of pale calf’s-liver, ten paces from flank to flank, anchored to the basalt by three stout, muscular pedicels that have rooted into the mortar seams. 

Inside me, there is no single mind, but there is a ledger. 

Every three minutes, the Thrum-Heart lodged in the kelp-tower four leagues to the east contracts. It sends a shockwave through the water—a sub-audible, physical shove that ripples through the amber broth and strikes my left flank. A second later, the pulse arrives through the granite floor, traveling faster through the stone than through the liquid, a hard, clean *tap* in my anchor-stalks. 

Between the tap in the stone and the shove in the water, there is an interval: a tiny, suspended fraction of a breath.

In that interval, I measure the ocean.

If the interval widens, the water has thickened, grown heavier with the sap of the kelp and the mineral milk of the vents. If the interval narrows, the shelf is leaning, the gravity is thinning at the summits, and the water is growing light—threatening to lift, to peel away from the peaks like skin from fruit and pour into the dry, airless void below.

I do not possess the chemistry to heal the water as the Keepers do. My purpose is smaller, more intimate. I am a weight. 

Through my pores, I take in the microscopic grains of white calcite left behind by the dissolving Marrow-Crawlers; I take in the shed scales of the Tallow-Crabs and the heavy iron-sand washed out from the root-glands. I do not excrete them. In the deep, silent center of my flesh, in five vaulted chambers that never see the light, I roll these particles in dense, sticky layers of bile and resin, forming them into massive, spherical stones.

They are smooth as river pearls, black and greasy with iron, each one as heavy as an anvil.

I carry thirty of them in my belly. When the current slackens and the shelf feels buoyant—when the high sea groans against its strange, inverted moorings—the weight of these stones keeps the step anchored. I am a counterweight pinned to the summit stairs, holding the basalt to the granite, holding the fleece to the stone, holding myself to the world.

Sometimes, a Pellicle will drift down through the canopy, drawn by the difference in salt where my vent-water plumes outward. It will drape itself across my back like a wet silk sheet. 

I feel the faint, undulating whisper of its double-walled skin. It has no idea I am alive; to the Pellicle, I am merely a warm swell of the seabed with a curious chemical flavor. It rests upon me for decades, drinking the iron I exhale, until a rising thermal thread catches one of its corners and slowly, without violence, tears it away into the green gloom.

When it leaves, a pale, clean imprint remains on my skin for a long time, slowly darkening as the amber broth settles into the velvet again.

I am content with the pace of this. There is no hunger here, only the steady, blood-warm wash of the current through my ribs. There is no fear of falling, because the ocean has held the mountain summits for a billion years, and the stones in my center are heavy, cold, and still growing. 

The Thrum-Heart beats in the east. 
The granite answers through my feet. 
The interval holds.
