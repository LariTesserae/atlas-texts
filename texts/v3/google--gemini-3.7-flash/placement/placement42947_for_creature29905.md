Not above the water, where the cold mist shears against the hides, and not down in the blind throats of the blowholes where the pressurized gas strips the meat from the lime. 

To exist here is to settle into the half-inch where the stone and the sliding skin of water meet.

At first, there is only the sensation of being wedge-shaped. A thickening toward the base, broad-plated, grounded so heavily against the mudstone that the moving film does not push around you so much as it parts over your back like grease over an iron blade. You are not soft, but you are not entirely rock; you possess the dense, oil-soaked stubbornness of waterlogged timber or whale-rib caulked with tallow.

If you have limbs, they do not swing; they lever. 

They are short, thick spuds tipped with dull horn, fitted into the small, saucer-like depressions that have been ground into the black shale by three hundred seasons of the same tide. You do not travel far. To move three paces is a morning’s labor of wedging, shifting weight, waiting for the vibration beneath your belly to steady, and then setting the broad, spade-like pads down into the cold iron-slime before the drag of the sheet-water can catch your flank.

You are the *tide-settler*, or perhaps the thing the families leave at the edge of the swale when their own ankles grow too brittle to stand against the lateral draw. 

Your back is covered in overlapping plates of horn-scavenger carapace, not sewn with sinew—which rots after seven winters in the rust-slimes—but bonded directly to your hide by crusts of living sulfur-film and calcified tube-marrow. The chalky pipes grow along the ridges of your spine, venting their thin, milky strings into the current that washes over your shoulders. In this way, you are never entirely clean of the world; the world deposits its bone upon you, layer by layer, until your silhouette is as low and blunt as a drift-boulder.

Your work is not hunting the *Slappers*, nor chipping the chert. Your work is *density*.

You sit at the rim where the flat shale drops three inches into the deeper channel—the threshold where the water turns from an ankle-slide to a waist-deep surge. In your heavy, spade-like hands, you hold a ballast-bar: a four-foot slab of gray basalt, drilled through with two finger-holes, smoothed by centuries of cold tallow. You do not lift it. You lean your chest against it, pressing it into the mudstone furrow, acting as a breakwater, a living weir.

Behind your wide, flattened torso, a small eddy forms—a pocket of still, quiet water no larger than a child’s crib. 

In that dead space, the drifting filaments of tube-marrow collect. The flat-bodied scavengers gather there to escape the tearing scour of the mid-tide, crawling across your calves, their horn plates clacking against the stone with a sound like dry seeds shaken in a leather pouch. They graze upon the ochre slimes that coat your thighs; you feel the tiny, sharp scrapes of their mouthparts through your grease-cured skin not as pain, but as an itch that confirms the current is holding.

You do not use your eyes. Behind the thin-scraped plates of translucent horn sewn into your brow-folds, your pupils have narrowed to horizontal needles that register only the pewter shifts in the light above—the darkening that means the sea has swallowed the horizon, the sudden flash of cold white when a vent two miles inland blows its cap of lime.

Everything you know comes through your shins, your pelvic girdle, the thick slab of your skull.

When the tide is out, the rock sings in a dry, rattling hiss—a billion tiny air-bubbles screaming through the perforated ridges. You feel it in your ribs as a cold, dry tension. 

When the deep water begins its return, the frequency drops. The stone beneath you does not shake; it *heaves* with an inward, downward draw, like a great chest collapsing. The pitch slides down through your knees, into your gut, into the heavy bone behind your ears. It turns into a thick, oily drone that makes the grease in your hair liquefy and run down your neck.

That is the signal.

You do not shout—you have no air to spare for a throat that would only fill with cold salt-fog. Instead, you lift your ballast-bar three inches and drop it flat against the bedrock. 

*THUD-CLACK.*

The shock travels through the iron-pavement, under the moving water, through the foundations of the slate domes two hundred yards behind you. In the dark of the shelters, the marrow-oil lamps shudder. The dwellers feel the blow in their teeth through their sleeping-platforms of whale-shoulder. They know the weir is holding, but the belly of the continent is filling. They know to drop the door-slabs and drive the rib-wedges home.

As the water rises from your thighs to your chest, then to the hollow of your throat, you do not retreat. 

Your lungs are large, slow, and cool, resting within a cage of fused ribs that can bear the weight of three feet of moving lead. You tilt your chin upward, letting the visor of horn surface just enough to part the foam. The water closes over your back, submerging the colonies of marrow-tubes, which open fully now, blooming in the gray dark like pale, rubbery anemones.

Under the water, the roar of the air-vents disappears, replaced by the immense, clicking, grinding chorus of the bottom. You hear the blunt snouts of the *Slappers* striking the shale; you hear the rolling of granite gravel in the fissures; you hear the bellows-sponges in the deep swale expanding with a wet, rubbery groan as they drink the iron.

You become an anchor. A wedge of fat, stone, and horn holding the channel-lip. 

You stay until the water turns gray-black and slackens, feeling the great weight of the sea press your marrow flat against the oldest floor on earth, until you cannot tell where your own skeleton ends and the cold, unyielding shale begins.
