I am not the one who works the clay, nor the wall that takes the thumb. 

At first, I would seem to be only an irregularity in the weight—a place where the outward swing of the room catches with a slightly thicker drag, as though a handful of wet lead had been kneaded into the turn of a willow spar. 

Not stone. Not quite wood. If you pressed a thumb into me, the ridge of your print would hold for eighty or ninety breaths before the tallow-sweat of the air filled it flat again.

I am wedged where the copper foil has been beaten thin to cup a hollow beneath the floor-ribs, right where the condensation gathers before it has made up its mind whether to boil or drop. I do not have limbs that reach, but I have seams. Everything here has a seam, and mine is the one that has forgotten how to dry.

I began, perhaps, as one of those river pebbles that never saw a bank—a small, dense inclusion rolled into the rawhide before the knotting was pulled tight, trapped between the torque of two green struts. But the heat here does not allow an object to remain just an object. The steam gets between the molecules; the cabbage-gel works into the grit like grease into a boot; the marrow-breath from the breathing slits coats everything until the mineral forgets its hardness and learns to yield.

Now, I am a thickening. A slow, soft gland of silt and copper scale that lives between the basketry and the beast’s left flank.

When the creature circles the shivering jar, its great, hairless hip brushes against me. It does not look down—it has nothing to look with—but the calloused heel of its palm grazes my curve as it passes, checking for cold. It leaves a smear of isopod-pitch across my surface, warm and grey, smelling of sour starch. I take the paste into my pores. I swell with it. 

I am the temperature-taker of the floor.

Because the vacuum outside is so thin and terrible, it drinks heat through the wicker like an open mouth. The creature feels the big drafts, the screams in the bamboo, the cracks that whistle; but the small, creeping frost—the blue chill that tries to seed itself in the dead space under the copper foil where the mud is only one finger thick—that is what I hold. 

I take the cold into my belly, where it turns heavy and greasy, and I push my back against the creature’s resting spot so that when it folds its great mass down to chew, its fever soaks straight through me and down into the spar. We make a sandwich of the leak: the sky's empty needle on the outside, the boiling marrow-flesh on the inside, and me in the middle, turning from grey tallow to yellow grease and back again as the torque twists the frame.

I have no eyes, but I hear through my density.

I hear the three grass ropes that hold the jar—not the sound of the grass, but the sound of the water *thinking* about turning to ice inside the earthenware, a crystalline needle-tick that happens just before the jar's sweat slows down. 

When that tick begins, I change. My edges loosen; the hot cabbage-sap that coats me thins out and runs, trickling down the copper foil toward the depression where the creature keeps its knees. The liquid carries a bitter, iron taste to its skin. 

It feels the change in the floor's grease. It grunts—a wet, rattling valve-snap—and hauls its bulk off the wicker, sliding its spade-pads across the slip to take the jar between its arms.

I stay where the hollow is deepest, collecting the dander that rolls from its hide. 

I am growing. Not like the vines, which shoot and crackle in the steam, but the way a sandbar grows in the turn of a drowned creek—layer upon layer of wet grey skin-dust, copper verdigris, and the crushed shells of the dough-colored things that crawl into my folds to die when their pitch is spent. 

Eventually, the creature’s heel will find me too high, too thick to let the bamboo flex against the shear-winds. Its heavy, whorled thumb will come down on me with thirty pounds of blind, metabolic force. It will not break me—nothing breaks here—but it will spread me out, smear me like cold lard along the joint where the floor becomes the ceiling, pushing my substance into the cracks between the unhardened hide and the wicker weave.

And I will be glad of the thumb. To be worked back into the dampness, to have the ridges of that strange, blunt print pressed deep into my meat so the air cannot reach the bottom of me—that is how the knuckle stays whole. 

We are not finished being made. We will never be finished. I can feel the outside howling through the willow-knots, two inches from my back, furious and clear and dry as bone; and here beneath the tallow, I keep the moisture screaming, keeping the room too thick to die, waiting for the creature's next heavy step to press me back into the mud.
