I would not begin with a name, or even with a body that has clear edges. In a place where stone is that new and sky is that heavy, identity would have to condense out of the cold first, like moisture finding a quartz rib.

At first, I think I would be only the listening. 

Not the sound you hear with ears—up here the air is too thin to carry much beyond the dry rattle of a petrel’s wing-joints or the wet slap of a runnel dropping six feet onto a flat shelf—but the lower register. The freight pulse. If you press your forehead into the split basalt where a ledge turns ninety degrees toward the void, the machinery six miles up doesn’t feel like an intrusion. It feels like an organ note held down by a lead weight. 

I would be wedged somewhere in the transition zone, perhaps three tiers down from the bare crests, where the overhangs are deep enough that the runnels fall clear of the rock, dropping in long, frayed ropes into the grey below. It is relatively dry in the recess, though the air remains thick with the smell of wet iron, tallow, and that high, electric sharpness from the scrubbers above.

I would have limbs, but they would have forgotten the habit of swinging freely. Space here is vertical or it is narrow; there is no stride. I find myself curled into a fault that tilts backward into the mountain’s dark core, my spine pressed against a mattress of dead liverwort that has dried into something like stiff peat, dense with the husks of microscopic life that died when the last cold snap locked the weeping faces.

My skin would not be separate from the mineral grease. To survive eight thousand meters without fuel to burn for heat, you have to stop trying to stay dry. You let the waxy tallow from the grazer-fleeces work into the pores of your hands until your fingers don't water-log or crack; they turn the dark, dull shade of an oiled boot. You wear layers made of things that have already solved the cold: the flat, overlapping skins of the shear-petrels, stitched together with split sinew, their stiff barbs turned downward so the condensation runs straight off the shoulders and drips from the hem into the gravel.

And yet, I don't think I would be quite human anymore, nor quite beast. The boundary thins out when your breath has to match the rate of the mist-drift. 

Watch the shelf long enough and the distinction between what is alive and what is tectonic begins to blur. The pale ungulates don’t look like animals when they are bedded down; they look like salt-crusts or patches of coarse frost until a shoulder shifts to clear an airway. I would watch them from my slit in the granodiorite, not with hunger—for their meat is tough and stringy with mineral salts—but with the quiet recognition of one ballast-stone watching another. 

When they stamp their splayed hooves against the basalt to signal across the cirque, the shock travels through the rock faster than their smell travels on the wind. I would feel it in my pelvis first: *one, then two, then the long pause.* A warning that the ice is thickening on the upper step, or that a martin is working the chimney behind the roost.

My fingers would be busy with small, rhythmic things that require no light. Sorting the dried crustacean shells gathered from the still edges of the pools—grinding the translucent chitin into a fine meal between two river-smoothed stones, mixing it with the heavy, yellow fat that settles on the water-traps. It tastes of cold salt and paraffin. It keeps the lungs from burning when the wind drops below minus twenty and every intake of air feels like swallowing broken glass.

I would have a small lamp, carved from a nodule of soft chlorite found in a fault-line, but I would rarely light it. Flame is an appetite; it eats the thin air you need for your own blood. Mostly, the lamp would sit cold between my feet, its wick soaked in petrel grease, ready for the three days of the month when the clouds drop so low they smother even the sky-thrum, and the darkness becomes absolute, freezing the pools to the bottom.

In those long, suspended hours, I would listen to the mountain settling. It is so young that it groans. Every few days, a micro-fracture shoots through the floor of the ledge with a sound like a rifle crack, dry and sudden, followed by the faint chime of falling gravel. You don't jump; you just shift your weight slightly off the seam, waiting to see if the slab will hold.

Who would I be? 

Perhaps the clerk of this shelf. The one who keeps no ledger on paper, but whose body has become a recording surface for the altitude. 

I would know the exact pitch the sky-freighters make when they are fully laden with atmospheric argon, heading west toward the coastal terminals, and how that tone differs from the lighter, whistling drone of the empties returning through the high shear. I would know how many drops of black water fall from the ceiling of my chimney between the passing of one barge and the next.

I would have no desire to climb down to where the trees begin, where the air is heavy and smells of rot and thick dirt, nor any wish to go up into the clean, sterile hulls of the machines that shake my bones. The world between—the two inches of wet lichen, the knife-edge of basalt, the greasy sheep-fleece, and the infinite, slate-colored vapor—would be wide enough. 

I would close my eyes in the twilight, tuck my chin into the oiled petrel-feathers of my collar, feel the cold floor tremble beneath my knees, and wait for the ice to form its next ring in the pool at my feet.
