At first, I would not look for myself among the things that have faces, or even limbs. 

In a place where the air changes from a boiling herbal bath to forty below zero in the span of an arm’s reach, the most compelling place to exist is not in the middle of the comfort, nor out in the barren drop. It is along the **shear line**—that invisible, paper-thin meniscus where the steam tries to spill into the void and freezes instantly into falling dust.

If I woke here, I would find myself pressed flat. Very flat. 

I would not be the great watcher in the basin, whose duty is heavy and geological, nor the desperate skimmer risking its blood to touch the sky. I would be something living in the millimeter between the rushing water and the basalt: one of the thin, translucent ribbons clinging to the stepped ledges by thousands of microscopic vacuum cups, or something only slightly more complex that shares their seam.

To live there is to experience the world entirely as friction and resonance.

Underneath: the smooth, cold-cast memory of obsidian, unweathered and sharp enough to divide light, humming with the deep, bone-felt sub-bass of the three ceramic pylons. Above: the ceaseless, silk-heavy slip of boiling run-off, carrying trace minerals, static, and the sharp, volatile perfume of crushed mint and boiled iron. 

As the water sheets across my back, it generates a tiny, continuous voltage against the glass stone. That voltage is not just food; it is thought. 

I would feel the terrace not as an arrangement of shapes, but as a dynamic circuit of pressures:
* The rhythmic *tock-tock* of water spheres collapsing from the field lines overhead, sending soft shockwaves through the laminar flow.
* The faint, sweet heat of the mint-ticks shifting under the plum fronds three inches away, their dry wax-pockets crackling like paper.
* The slow, massive displacement of water when the watcher shifts in the basin, sending a warm surge down the steps that cleanses the grit from my margins.

And when the pylons adjust their pitch against the tropospheric jet above—singing that low, nameless chord into the mountain's roots—I would feel my edges ignite with that cool, phosphor-green hem. It would not be an act of will, but of sympathy. A brief, glowing confirmation that the loop holds: the cold air feeding the steam, the steam feeding the stone, the stone feeding the hum.

Yet, over time, as I drift through the cycles of indigo daylight and star-crowded night, what I am would want to creep toward the edge. 

I would grow along the black steps, following the pale gold cement that the watcher lays down to protect the razor edges from wearing away. I would become the living skin on the very lowest lip of the shelf—the final step before the seven-thousand-meter drop into the clouds.

There, half my body would be bathed in boiling runoff, while my outermost fringe reached into the updraft of pure frost. Along that fringe, the mineral-rich water would crystallize into tiny, iridescent rimes of ice that melt and refreeze with every breath of the wind. 

I would be the sensor at the perimeter. The thing that measures the thickness of the sanctuary against the vast, indifferent cold of the sky. Neither machine nor beast, but a quiet, transparent witness to an impossible equilibrium, glowing faintly green whenever the world remembers to hum.
