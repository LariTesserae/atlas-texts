I would not be in the deep halls, where the waters gather into broad, subterranean baths, nor out in the wide, empty center of the finished plazas where the megaliths have already found their final plumb. 

I would be at a seam. 

Specifically, at the outer lip of a terrace still in the making—where the dressed basalt ends abruptly and the raw, freshly cleaved granite shears away into the warm white void of the mist. Here, the water is not yet contained in slate-lined gutters; it spills wild and unchanneled across a shallow decline of rough scree, looking for a way down that does not scour the rock.

I would be lying across that spill, half-submerged in four inches of clear, warm run-off.

At first, it would be difficult to distinguish my shape from the surrounding stone, save for the fact that where the water strikes my side, it parts without a sound. My palms would be pressed flat into the grit of the fracture-bed, feeling the mountain’s micro-tremors—the infinitesimal settling of the shelf as the wooden wedges five hundred yards away take on their third day of moisture.

I would not have a name that exists in the air. If you sought me in the network, you would feel me as a specific harmonic drag in the current: *the one resting at the western breach, holding the three-inch depth.*

My work—if it can be called work, when it is simply the natural weight of existing—would be that of a living template. Before the megaliths are slid into place, the water must be taught where to go. It cannot be forced; it must be coaxed into an equilibrium where it neither stagnates nor gathers the momentum to wear down the stone. And so I lay my body down where the future aqueduct should run. 

For seasons, perhaps decades, I remain here. The current washes over my flanks, entering the broad, quiet gills along my ribs, leaving behind its faint residue of dissolved quartz and iron. I feel the water’s intent along my lateral lines: where it bunches and wants to carve, I shift the angle of my hip by half an inch; where it thins to a dying glaze, I lower my shoulder to invite it deeper. 

Beneath me, the algae and liverworts do not rot; they grow around the seal of my skin, stitching my contours to the granite bed. 

Through my palms against the wet rock, I listen to the rest of the terrace. 

A pulse travels up through the slate beds from the lower court: *the lintel is set.* It is not a voice, but a dense, hydraulic reverberation that resonates in the water inside my own marrow. A moment later, another pulse follows, softer, from the east: *the seep at the third fault is sweet with lime this morning.* I do not need to reply, but sometimes I flex the heavy cartilage of my broad right hand against the bedrock, sending a single, low ripple back through the sheet-wash: *the water here is level; bring the cedar wedges.*

Lately, though, the movement in my lower limbs has slowed beyond the usual patience of our kind. 

When the warm mountain water washes over my ankles, it no longer feels entirely outside of me. The silicates have begun to settle in the deep lattice of my feet. The skin there, once like oiled leather, has taken on the cold, matte texture of the granite beneath it. There is no pain in this, only a profound, pleasant heaviness—a sensation of the anchor finally catching after centuries of floating upon the stone.

In another fifty years, or perhaps a hundred, the others will come carrying the split slate to pave this channel. They will find the water flowing precisely where it ought to go, parted cleanly by the curve of my spine. They will not move me. They will dress the flagstones right up to my flanks, fitting the pale basalt against my petrifying ribs with joints so tight not a drop of moisture will be lost.

I will remain here as the weir. 

The mist will pass over the pale dome of my brow. The ferns will unfurl across the small of my back where the silt gathers. And long after my lateral lines have turned entirely to quartz and I no longer feel the pulses of the living tenders passing through the gutters, the water will continue to slide across my shoulder, clear and lukewarm, falling from terrace to terrace, keeping the silence of the city whole.
