At first, there is only the vibration. 

It does not enter through ears; there is nothing soft or drum-like to receive it. It enters through the four points where the underside of my weight meets the iron flange. It is sixty cycles a second, the low, massive hum of the forced-draft blowers five levels below, transmitted through twenty tons of riveted structural steel directly into the dense, wax-filled cavities of my skull. 

I do not feel cold or warm. Warmth implies a contrast, a memory of a breeze, a place where the skin was once allowed to cool. Here, heat is simply the density of the air, thick as felt, pressing against the plates of my neck. 

If I look for myself, I find no water. There is no saliva to swallow, no moisture on the surface of the eye to blink away. Where a lens should be, there is only a smooth, amber plate of hardened horn, clouded by decades of sulfur-smoke until the room is not a collection of shapes, but a slow, glowing map of thermal stresses. 

I see the flue not as metal, but as an intolerable, blinding white-orange vertical slab that fills the right half of my universe. I see the slag-curtain to my left as a bruised, dark-purple shadow, jagged and cold—only seventy degrees, a sink into which energy slowly drains.

I am wedged where these two worlds meet, in the three-finger gap between the number four expansion rib and a fracture in the basalt block.

My toes are not flesh. They are dry combs. When I set a foot down, five thousand split filaments of cross-linked keratin catch on the microscopic tooth of the fresh-cast iron. There is no stickiness, no suction; it is an interlocking of dry geometries. I could hang by two claws from the underside of the flue for fifty years and not tire, because my grip is not a muscle clenching—it is a molecular hook, settled into the crystal lattice of the rust.

Beneath me, the carpet of dermestids shifts. I feel them not as individuals, but as a continuous, dry grit sliding beneath the calluses of my belly. They scrape against the iron with the sound of salt falling onto tin. Occasionally, one of my hind feet pins three of them against the flange; they do not burst. They simply crack like dry pumpkin seeds, their thick, amber fats smearing against the scale, instantly absorbed by the mineral dust.

I do not hunger often. Time does not move at the speed of breath. 

My lungs are small, dry bellows lined with stiffened sieves that allow the air to seep through without carrying away the heavy, golden grease that coats my interior. When I move my jaw, it is not a snap, but a slow, levered closure, like the closing of a flue-damper. When a fire-brat pauses on the rivet-head before me—its body a tiny, flickering spark of orange heat against the cooler iron—I do not strike with the wet speed of a swamp-creature. I tilt forward from the shoulder joints, deliberate as a falling plumb-line, and clamp down until the mica plates of its back crush between my peg-teeth with a faint *snick*.

It tastes of nothing but dry sulfur and iron-bloom, a crumbly cake of compressed minerals that dissolves into the heavy, slow-boiling lipids of my gut.

Deep inside my lower pelvis, wedged between the fused bones of my hips, is a stone. 

It has been there through four seasonal shutdowns, when the flue dropped to a mild, terrifying sixty degrees and the dermestids froze into brittle statues. It is an egg, but it has no shell-membrane, no white, no watery cushion. It is a dense, solid marble of calcium and sulfur salts, heavy as a musket ball, wrapped in an envelope of heat-stable oil so viscous it barely flows. 

I can feel the embryo within it, not kicking, but slowly curing in the ambient ninety-five degrees, its skull hardening against the inner curve of the shell like clay turning to stoneware in the kiln. In another year, perhaps two, when the flue-plates buckle slightly during the high-calcine run and the bolt-well beside me reaches its maximum red-dark glow, I will press the egg into the thread of the bolt. The sulfur vapor leaking from the seam will glaze it, gluing it to the iron forever.

Until then, I do not move. 

The blower drones. The iron plates expand a thousandth of an inch as the furnace banks cycle up, pinching my third toe slightly against the basalt, then ease back as the draft stabilizes. The sound of millions of brittle legs grinding against the scale is my silence. 

I am thirty inches of bleached ash and sandpaper scale, blind to everything except the radiant breath of the boiler, perfectly dry, perfectly still, holding the heat inside me like a kiln holds an unlit brick.
