It does not begin with an eye opening. There is nothing here to look at, and light is the thinnest, least informative thing in the cleft—a pale, cold draft from above that dies three feet into the steam without touching anything that matters.

First, there is only the impedance.

Sound does not travel *through* the air to reach me; the air is too thick with scalding mist, too whipped by compression to act as an empty medium. Sound is simply the state of the room. It is a solid, crushing column of frequency that passes through the basalt, through the concrete footing, and straight through my shell without slowing down. The rock and my body have roughly the same acoustic density; we ring together.

I am not in the dead space under the slab, where the dry-legged things pick at drift with their silk. That air is too stagnant, too separate from the pulse. Nor am I lost in the loose porosity of the bacterial reef, drifting blind between calcite grains like the nematodes.

I am on the facet.

I am broad, flat, and heavier than I ought to be. My back is an arrangement of overlapping plates—dense, lead-dark iron phosphate bound with tough chitin, beveled at the perimeter to an edge so acute it disappears into the stone. Above me, the water sheets down the sheared basalt in a continuous, unbroken film three millimeters deep. It does not splash. It planes over the low curve of my back like oil over a drowned stone, its speed kept smooth and laminar by the sheer steepness of the rock face.

Beneath my shell, there is no skeleton, only the foot: a wide, uninterrupted disk of muscular flesh that occupies the whole underside of my form. Thousands of microscopic suction points bite into the vitreous sheen of the fresh-cleaved basalt. The grip is total. If the air above suddenly evacuated into vacuum, or if the manifold tore itself from its mountings in a single catastrophic heave, the stone would split along its mineral grain before my seal would fail.

I feel the machine through the floor of my body before any human sense could name it.

The manifold is eighty paces away through the stone, but distance is meaningless when the medium is unbroken granite and basalt. Through my ventral pad, the machine is not an object; it is an anatomy:

There is the heavy, low-frequency stroke—roughly four beats to the second—where the internal pistons slide through hot, oil-saturated grease. It registers as an upward shove in the center of my foot, a rhythmic compression of my gut that drives my own pale hemolymph through the gills fringing my mantle. I do not have a heart that beats on its own; I do not need one. The machine's downstroke compresses my dorsal plates, and the release draws the mineral broth through my breathing margins. My pulse is cast in iron.

Above that heavy thrum is the bronze song: a piercing, constant shrillness from the steam venting through the submerged orifices. It sets up a high-frequency chatter in the leading edge of my third and fourth shell plates. It tastes of sulfur and oxidized copper, a faint, metallic bite in the hot film that washes over my mouthparts.

I move.

The movement is not a crawl; it has no legs, no stride, no lunging. It is a slow, hydraulic creep, a localized relaxation of vacuum along the forward margin followed by an imperceptible pull from the rear. In the time it takes the machine to cycle ten thousand strokes, I have moved half the length of a fingernail across the sheer plane.

Where I pass, the rock is wiped clean. My radula—a scraping ribbon armed with teeth of bio-precipitated magnetite—rasps against the glass-hard basalt, peeling away the tough, vulcanized skin of orange thermophiles that drink the steam-sweat. The biofilm is hot, dense with reduced iron and slippery with bacterial mucus. It yields under the magnetite with a faint, grit-laden scrape that vibrates directly into my oral cavity.

The water washing over me is eighty-two degrees. If it were seventy, I would stiffen; my proteins would lose their pliability, the hinges between my iron-plated shingles would lock with precipitated calcite, and the slow wave of my foot would freeze in place against the rock. If it were ninety, the suction cups would slip on their own liquefied mucus and the laminar sheet would carry me down into the churning sumps.

Eighty-two is balance. Eighty-two is the temperature at which the basalt, the water, the machine, and the flesh become the same continuous, vibrating circuit.

Above me, in the narrow slot of sky, something pale and formless drifts past—a cloud, perhaps, or a bird crossing the upper world where the air is thin and silent and cold. It has no weight. It leaves no acoustic footprint in the stone. It does not exist.

A secondary bypass valve deep within the manifold cracks open. A sharp, violent concussive spike—a white crack of sound—slams through the chamber, bouncing four times between the knife-edge basalt faces before being swallowed by the mass of the rock.

My shell flexes. The elastic hinges between my plates absorb the shock, converting the violent shear wave into a tiny, forward hydraulic slide.

The mist settles. The water continues its smooth, hot weep down the black face.

I scrape again. The magnetite bites the stone. The piston strokes downward, heavy and deep in the dark, and my blood moves with it.
