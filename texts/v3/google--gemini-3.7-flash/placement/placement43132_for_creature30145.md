Not up where the tea-colored daylight sifts through the iron grating, and not down in the dark intake culverts where the black water is pulled into the sluices under fifty pounds of head. 

I would be in the middle height, in the wet clearance between the main low-pressure cylinder jacket and the south retaining wall—a gap scarcely eighteen inches wide, where the heat never drops below ninety degrees and the air does not move except when the high-expansion relief valve vents along the conduit above.

At first, I am only an accumulation. 

For forty or fifty years, the tallow-blooms do not differentiate between the cold granite of the foundation courses and the hot lagging of the steam jackets. They creep across both, bridging the air gap with pale, webbed ribbons of boiled lard and graphite silt. But in this particular chimney of dead air, where the spray from the wrist-pin catch-basin atomizes against the red-hot drain petcock, the deposit thickens differently. It does not stay flat against the brick. It gathers mass. It takes the draft from the relief pipe into its folds. It begins to develop a grain.

If you were to press a thumb against me, you would feel neither the rubbery spring of the riverbed blooms nor the dense, petrified gristle of the caretakers. You would feel something like cold axle butter packed tight with sifted slate-flour: firm, slightly greasy to the touch, yieldingly heavy, but held together by a dense internal meshwork of rotting horsehair gaskets that have sloughed off the upper staging over seventy seasons of maintenance.

I have no face to turn toward the dynamos. My orientation is entirely vertical and lateral, pinned by the geometry of the iron on one side and the weeping lime joints on the other.

My consciousness—if it can be called that—is not narrative. It does not remember the city above, because the city above has never leaked anything down here except weight: three million tons of dry brick, paving stone, and horse-dung silt pressing down through forty feet of saturated earth, transmitted into the granite retaining blocks as a uniform, unchanging shear stress. 

Instead, I am an organ of the gap.

I know the cycle of the low-rpm pump not as a sound, but as an alternating squeeze. On the upstroke, when the six-foot balance beam heaves its counterweight toward the ceiling grating, the cylinder jacket expands by three-sixteenths of an inch from the internal pressure of the five-hundred-pound steam. That expansion pinches me against the vitrified brick. The hot tallow in my lower folds liquefies slightly, oozing downward along the mortar lines, carrying with it a faint wash of dissolved copper carbonate and lead flake. 

On the downstroke, the metal contracts. The clearance opens. Cool, tea-colored vapor rushes into the vacuum, chilling my outer skin into a stiff, suet-like crust that traps the airborne graphite before the next stroke can blow it away.

I grow by this breathing: three strokes a minute, forty-three hundred strokes a day, a layer no thicker than a moth’s wing added with every thousand revolutions.

Over time, I have grown around the things that dropped into the gap and never hit the floor. 

Inside me, buried six inches beneath my current surface, rests a brass packing-gland wrench dropped from the intermediate catwalk when the dynamos were still bright metal; its copper alloy has leached outward into my fat, staining a sphere of lard around it a vivid, toxic verdigris. Deeper still, against the stone, are three links of forged wrought-iron crane chain, completely enclosed and converted along their outer surfaces into soft, black magnetite.

I do not move from this seam, but I am not inert. 

The caretakers know I am here. When the one with the cracked left shoulder-strap comes shuffling down the narrow staging behind the high-pressure chest—its flat, hairless feet slapping the wet flagstones with that dull, rhythmic suction—it does not stop to inspect the valves. It knows the valves are true by the pitch of the foundation tremor. 

Instead, it reaches an arm into the gap, shoulder-deep into the humid dark, and sinks its blunt, pebble-smooth fingers into my side.

It does not hurt; there is no nervous tissue to register a tear, only the slow, viscous displacement of wax and felt. The caretaker scoops out a double-handful of my core—warm, gray, heavy with tallow, finely suspended graphite, and the alkaline milk of the lime weepage. It withdraws its arm, leaving a smooth, gouged hollow in my flank that will take eighteen months of condensed cylinder-oil to fill again.

It takes that mass of me and carries it to the main wrist-pin bearing, where the brass is running dry and ringing with a high, thin harmonic that vibrates through the iron rafters. It packs me directly into the grease-cup over the pin. 

And for three days, a part of what I am is pressed between forty tons of oscillating steel and forty tons of bronze casing, sheared into a liquid film a hundredth of a millimeter thick, carrying the load, absorbing the fire of the friction, weeping out through the relief grooves into the catch-basin, and atomizing back into the tea-colored air as warm, gray steam—

to drift across the court, condense upon the upper conduits, swell into heavy, rhythmic drops, and fall back down the walls into the seam where the rest of me is waiting.
