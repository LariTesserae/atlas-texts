At first, it is not a body at all, but a resistance.

To exist in this depth is to feel the water not as an emptiness between things, but as a dense, freezing grease that refuses to yield without negotiation. There is no breath, no rush of current, no light to strike the eye—only the heavy, uniform shove of forty megapascals clamping every molecule into place, and the sharp, alkaline sting of fresh lime hanging unmoving in the dark.

If I search for myself in the tiers, I do not find a name, nor a mind that reflects upon its own margins. I am not the Chancery—that blind, pale cluster of milk-fat ganglia curled in the apex, dripping its chemical equations into the aqueducts. Nor am I one of the soft, dissolving slugs whose enzyme-slick bellies liquefy yesterday’s avenues. Those are the margins of the will; I am somewhere in the heave of its execution.

I find myself low, where the masonry meets the foundation, lodged in the narrow gap between a soaring chitin colonnade and the scarred inner rib of the mother-shell.

I am one of the structural joints—or rather, I am the creature that is currently becoming one.

***

My carapace is not pale and papery like the Plaster-Wrights, nor is it the heavy, soot-colored plate of the Harvesters who walk the outer abyssal desert. It is thick, ribbed, and semi-rigid, pitted by the bite of the lime-paste I have spent forty hours kneading against the stone. 

My forelegs are broad, spatulate wedges, worn smooth at the tips where the chitin has ground directly against raw silica. I do not see the city; there are no eyes upon my head-shield, only twin rows of short, stiff chemosensory pegs that taste the falling wash of the aqueducts. 

From above, the taste is constant: a steady, bitter precipitation of sulfurous lipids, cold nitrogen, and the unmistakable, razor-sharp tang of the Chancery’s scent-chains. The scent-chain does not feel like a thought or an order; it feels like an itch behind my dorsal plates, a relentless hydraulic spasm that drives my legs forward, scraping, lifting, packing.

Directly beside me, a hexactinellid sponge spicule—a six-inch timber of pure, volcanic glass dragged in from the dead ocean floor—stands upright, serving as the load-bearing spine of a thirty-tier tenement. My task has been to secure its foot. For twelve cycles of the Siphon-Wells' contraction, I have packed the slurry into the rift around its base, drawing the hot, churning paste from my gut and tamping it down before the freezing brine can freeze it into brittle chalk.

There is no sound to any of this. When my neighbor’s paddle strikes the glass spicule, there is no ring, no scrape. The impact is merely a pressure wave that travels through the dense water and hits my flank like a muffled blow from a velvet hammer. When a ceiling three inches above us buckles under the weight of three thousand newly laid cells, it folds downward in complete, dignified silence, sinking through the viscous brine like a dead bloom of wax.

***

Now, the chemistry is shifting.

I taste it before I feel it in my limbs: the lime in my belly is spent. The internal reservoirs that process the scrapings are dry, scored with mineral acid, lined with fine fissures. The scent-chain washing down from the aqueduct changes its note—the instruction for *anchor* fades, replaced by the cool, neutral wash of *sustain*.

My legs are slowing. It is not fatigue as a beast of the sun understands it; there is no desire to lie down, no sleep waiting in the dark. It is simply the calcification of the hinge. The calcium that once flowed into my regurgitations has begun to leach backward into my joints, fusing the delicate segments of my fourth and fifth walking limbs into rigid, unbending struts.

I try to draw one leg back through the heavy brine, and the joint does not bend; it locks with the finality of a dropped bolt.

I do not struggle. To struggle would be a defect in the alignment. The Chancery’s geometry is absolute, and space within the shell is too precious for an idle limb. 

From the shadow beneath the transverse bridge, I feel the displaced water first—a slow, rhythmic displacement, dense and cold. An Encapsulator is stepping out from the pilaster. Its needle-pointed stilts make no mark on the velvety frost of chemotrophic fungal threads beneath us. It moves with the brisk, clean economy of a municipal functionary.

It pauses before me. Its sensory antennae sweep over my dorsal plate, tasting the cessation of my gut-churn, measuring the angle of my seized legs against the spicule’s base.

I am perfectly positioned. The angle of my thorax fills the remaining triangular void between the glass timber and the primary rib of the shell.

The Encapsulator’s segmented fore-arms descend, touching me without malice or haste. They fold my spent paddles flat against my chest, tucking the worn tips neatly beneath my head-plate to present a uniform surface. Then, its mouthparts open, and the vitreous lacquer descends—a thick, pale, cold amber that pours over my carapace, filling the crevices of my joints, sealing the sensory pegs on my brow, smoothing away the irregularities of my breathing-slits.

***

The lacquer cures instantly in the freezing brine. 

As it hardens, the frantic movements of the city—the thousand bustling paddles of the Plaster-Wrights above, the long chains of porters dragging silica needles across my roof, the steady, rhythmic pulse of the Siphon-Wells sweating lipid-veils into the dark—cease to be things I navigate. They become vibrations transmitted directly through my back.

I am no longer an inhabitant of the thimble-empire; I am its curb. 

Above me, a fresh Plaster-Wright clambers onto my glazed dorsal ridge, its spatulate forelegs already wet with newly churned paste, laying the first tier of an archway directly across my brow. Its paddles pat the hot mortar down onto my shell, smoothing it into the expanding line of the street, while below, in the deep dark of the silt, the Harvesters turn once more toward the open sea.
