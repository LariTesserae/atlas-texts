I would not be found upon the highest catwalks, where the plume-serpents turn their silver bellies to the glare, nor deep in the black, grease-choked sump where the glass-eels twist blindly around the piston rods. 

I would be at the hinge of the two—in the intermediate shallows, where the boiling sheet of brine is only three centimeters deep, sliding like liquid glass over the wide, terraced apron of a mid-level kiln tower.

***

At first, you might mistake me for a broken bollard or a slab of weathered shale left behind by a forgotten masonry gang. I sit low, my legs folded beneath a broad, heavy torso, the flat soles of my feet pressed flush against the submerged flagstones to catch the deep, subsonic pulse of the distant counterweights. 

My skin is not skin as softer worlds understand it; it is a thick, fissured rind of slate-grey callus, smeared with a stiff, protective unguent of sheep-tallow, sulfur, and ground gypsum. The sun does not burn me because I have made myself into stone before the sun could do it for me. Where the paste dries and flakes in the thin mountain wind, the mineral wash from the apron coats me anew, leaving a pale, sparkling crust along the ridges of my collarbones and the knuckles of my hands.

I have no need for ears. The air here is too thin to carry a shout, and steam does not roar; it only sighs through the ceramic mufflers with a long, dry breath. Instead, my entire body is an acoustic receiver for the ironwork. 

When I lay the flat of my palm against the lead lip of the sluice gate, I feel the health of the province:
* A low, dragging shudder means a siphon five leagues to the west is choking on an aragonite bloom.
* A rhythmic, three-stroke ringing means the skimmers have roosted too heavily on the upper condenser vanes, throwing the counterpoise out of true.
* A faint, high-pitched warble means the brine is running too rich in copper, scouring the joints faster than the sediment can seal them.

Around my shins, the lithophages drift in their slow, unhurried migrations. Their porcelain backs bump against my knees with the dry clatter of dropped saucers. I reach down—my fingers blunt, the nails thick as horn—and lift one from the film of water. Its thousands of tiny quartz-tipped cilia ripple against my palm, tasting the tallow and salt on my flesh. With the tip of my bronze-sheathed pick, I gently clear a knot of oxidized rust from its intake vents, then set it down facing a fresh ledge of lime that threatens to dam the overflow. It grinds forward obediently, its belly rasping a clean, turquoise channel through the crust.

I do not look at the sun directly; to do so would crack the vitreous humor of the eyes. My outer lids are heavy, folded hoods of scarred hide, but behind them sits the amber membrane—smooth, transparent, and naturally stained the color of dark beer. Through it, the basin loses its blinding, bleached cruelty. The world becomes a study in values of honey, bronze, and deep iron-black. The towering skimmers, stepping on their stilted needles across the distant pans, do not look like pale ghosts; they look like intricate lanterns of violet and gold, their great vascular sails glowing like stained glass as they radiate the fury of the boil into the sky.

I eat sparingly, because to introduce moisture to the belly is to invite the steam to rise within. Once every few cycles, when the sun dips behind the high volcanic rim and the basin turns the color of bruised plum, I pry a slab of rubbery, orange sulfur-mold from the throat of an exhaust duct. It is warm, salty, and tastes of vulcanized grease and bitter alkali. I chew it slowly, washing it down with a single draft of pure, scalding distillate caught directly from the weeping mouth of a condenser tube.

I do not dream of trees, or soil, or rain that falls cold from clouds. Such things seem porous, unstable, and prone to rot. In this basin, nothing is lost. The water that washes my thighs this morning was drawn from a fracture four miles beneath the earth; by noon it will be vapor in the gut of an air-plume kite; by twilight it will condense upon the high iron trusses and run down the aqueduct to wash my thighs again. It is a closed, perfect sentence that never needs to end.

Already, the lime is finding its way into me. 

When I rise to clear a jammed gate, my left knee does not bend with the fluid ease it had forty seasons ago; it grates with a dry, ceramic click—a tiny deposit of calcite forming in the capsule of the joint. I do not fear this. It is merely the architecture making its claim. 

In time, when the click becomes a lock, and the staff of bronze can no longer transmit the pulse of the machines through my arms, I will not need to be carried. I will walk down the terraced steps, past the clicking herds of grazers, into the deep, warm settling vats beneath the main manifold. I will sit among the submerged iron pillars, where the current is slow and heavy with dissolved stone. 

I will close both sets of lids. I will feel the vibration of the great pistons one last time in the base of my spine. And there, in the dark, silent warmth, I will let the turquoise brine build its bright, crystalline lattice around my ribs, until I am as solid, as quiet, and as permanent as the city itself.
