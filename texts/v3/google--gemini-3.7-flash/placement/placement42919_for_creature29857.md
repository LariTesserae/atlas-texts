I would not be in the air, and I would not be at the lip where the water breaks into mist over the void. That edge is too loud with absence; it pulls too hard on things that wish to stay dense.

Instead, I would be found in the seam—the quiet, half-submerged margin where the flat travertine shelf dips to form the hollowed basin near the western curve. 

At first, I might seem to be nothing more than the water’s own resistance: that slight, glass-thick swelling that gathers around an obstruction before the current finds its way around it. But if you were to press a finger into that warmth, you would feel an elasticity that does not belong to water, and a slow, interior temperature that is half a degree warmer than the mountain’s seepage.

I am not the dark basalt adze, but I am what has grown around it. 

I am the living calcite sleeve that clasps the foreign river stone to the limestone bed. 

To exist here is to have no use for a face, or limbs, or the suddenness of hunger. My body is a sprawling, translucent skin, perhaps three fingers wide and as long as a forearm, feathered at its edges into gossamer-thin frills that root directly into the microscopic pores of the carved travertine. On one side, I am limestone—pale, alkaline, drinking the continuous wash of warm salts that the floor-keeper herds toward me. On the other, pressed deep against the dark belly of the adze, I am something darker, tinged with the faint, metallic sweetness of submerged volcanic iron.

I feel the terrace not as an expanse of scenery, but as a series of low, rhythmic pressures conducted through the floor.

Every forty minutes, there is a minute slackening in the channel’s flow: far to the east, the great silk-creature in the wagon-wheel succulent has reached the crest of its billow, drawing three gallons of water down toward the plant’s deep taproot. The glaze across the floor thins by the thickness of a hair. My outer frills dry by a fraction of a millimeter, tightening against the rock, before the succulent exhales, releasing the water back into the basin, and the warm meniscus swells over me again.

Once every nine or ten days, the weight changes entirely. 

The shield-shaped polisher arrives. I feel the dull, massive drag of its muscular foot hours before it touches the basin—a heavy, tectonic thrumming in the travertine. When its soft, leathery skirt finally flows over the hollow, covering the basalt and myself in total darkness, there is no fear, only a brief, intense chemistry. Its biological acid stings my outer fringes, softening the mineral crust I have spent the week accreting. I yield to it, giving up my brittle outer layer of lime to its mouth; in return, its alkaline wash calms the iron-heat in my core and lays down a fresh, liquid glaze of calcium that will take another four days to set. 

For the few hours it rests over me, we are a single organ: it dissolves, I bind; it clears the channel, I weigh down the tool.

Underneath me, buried in my deepest fold, lies the grip of the adze. 

I do not remember the hands that shaped it—that happened in a time when things moved with terrible, tearing speed—but I keep their negative space. I hold the shape of four fingers and a broad thumb in the curve of my calcite mantle. The volcanic stone inside is dense, cold-hearted, and silent, an exile from some lower, violent riverbed; my slow, wet life is the process of translating its alien iron into the mountain’s quiet language, grain by grain, century by century.

I am the bridge between the tool that was abandoned and the stone that never leaves. 

When the midday light hits the terrace and turns the shallow floor into a sheet of amber glass, the clear discs drift lazily over my basin, casting no shadows, bending the sun into twin rings across my back. I breathe in the dissolved lime, I breathe out the clear, heavy glaze, and I hold the dark stone down in the warm water, keeping the basin true, while the mountain holds its breath beneath the sun.
