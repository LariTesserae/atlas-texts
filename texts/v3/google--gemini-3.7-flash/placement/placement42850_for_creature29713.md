It begins before the eyes open, with the heel.

The heel knows the state of the grain before the morning bell or the scrape of the flume-shovels above. If the cedar is swollen with night-rain, it yields perhaps the thickness of a thumbnail beneath the pad; if the mountain wind is veering south, the timber tightens, drawing its fibers together like cold muscle, and the bridge-stay vibrates at a pitch that buzzes right through the marrow of the shin.

I am not up in the thin, fogged silver where the Wind-Seers freeze in their harnesses, squinting out over the notch for flash-melts. Nor am I down in the black, thundering trough where the Sump-Crews go deaf hauling slate and river-drift against the spray.

I am in the seam. The underside. The place where the town’s belly meets the open drop.

***

You would find me slung three feet beneath the fifth tier of walkways, suspended in a harness of boiled hemp and otter-skin, wedged into the hollow where an ancient, fluted cedar-buttress embraces a hanging slate hearth-cradle. 

Above me, two inches of pine planking separate my head from the daily press: the rolling tread of the crab-mongers, the rhythmic, four-stroke *thock-thock* of the bark-beaters singing their shift-cadence, the sharp click of grip-hound claws against wet sapwood. Below me is three hundred feet of grey, misting emptiness, crossed only by the falling threads of water from the flume-overflows and the blue-black arcs of the swifts.

My skin smells of sulfur pitch, cold tallow, and the bitter, tannic juice that pools in dead knots. I am a *Graft-Tender*—a carver of rot, a keeper of the joints. 

The people above walk on the city as if it were a floor. To me, it is not a floor; it is a wet, flexed ribcage that never stops breathing.

***

In the early grey, when the smoke of five hundred kitchen fires begins to filter down through the plank-cracks—smelling of spruce needles, scorched clay, and the sour warmth of boiling tubers—I begin with the mallet and the flat bone gouge.

I do not look for the rot with my eyes. In this mist, everything looks green or black, varnished alike with liverwort slime and moss-butter. I find it by touch and by sound. I tap.

*Tock.* That is ironwood, cold and sound as flint.
*Tock.* That is green oak, bleeding its bitter sap, holding true.
*Thump.* A soft, wet thud, like a fist into mutton fat.

There. A pocket beneath the vegetable stalls where the cabbage-wash has leaked around a copper bolt. The white rot-threads—the ghost-spawn—have gotten into the heart-wood. If left three days in this saturation, the beam will crumble like wet curd under the weight of a pannier-load of river-slate.

I swing my harness around the bole, bracing my splayed, calloused feet against the wet bark. My toes, permanently curved and stained the color of steeped tea, find the rough notches with no thought at all. I hook my elbow into a guy-line, unstopper the grease-horn with my teeth, and set the chisel against the timber.

The rotten wood comes away in long, spongy curls that smell of cellar-dirt and mushroom mycelium. I let them drop into the chasm; the swifts dart through the falling shavings, mistaking them for moths, before breaking away into the fog.

When the cut reaches clear, pale, resisting grain, I stop. From the small iron pot clinking at my belt—its charcoal embers kept alive in a nest of dry punk-ash—I draw a ladle of melted cedar-pitch and sulfur. I pack the wound with beaten mulberry bast soaked in the boiling resin, smoothing it flat with a greased stone until it hisses, seals, and hardens under the cold highland spray into a glass-black scar.

The beam will hold another six seasons. By then, the living fig-truss beside it will have grown another thumb’s-breadth around the joint, taking up the load.

***

It is a quiet life, lived entirely inside the roaring acoustics of the gorge. The air here does not travel in straight lines; it curls upward in warm, drafty flues created by the domestic fires above. 

Because of this, the under-deck is where the warmth gathers. The swifts know it. They nest by the hundreds in the dry angle between my work-cradle and the deck-joists. As I hang there, shaving wood or re-tying a frayed sinew-lashing with green willow withes, their small, hot bodies brush past my ears, their wings making a dry, papery whisper like shaken straw. Sometimes a fledgling, clumsy and fat on midges, falls into the lap of my wool tunic; I scoop it up with pitch-stained fingers and tuck it back into the woven brush of its nest-cup before the civets catch the scent.

The civets know me, too. They run the guy-lines like tightropes, pausing just above my head to shake the condensation from their banded coats, watching with yellow, insolent eyes as I trim the bark. We have an understanding: I do not swing my gouge at their snouts, and they do not chew through the tallow-soaked hemp that keeps me from falling into the lower rapids.

***

Toward midday, a small trapdoor opens in the planks directly above me.

A child’s face appears, upside down, framed by the square hole—a girl with her dark hair coiled tight in greasy braids and a tether-harness looped snug beneath her armpits, anchored to the doorpost above. She does not shout; the roar of the flumes would swallow it. She lowers a wooden piggin on a hemp cord.

Inside is my share: a hot, thick lump of fermented tuber-mash, salted trout-roe, and a handful of bitter cress, steaming against the cold mist. 

I reach up, untie the piggin, and tie in its place three fresh-cut pegs of seasoned larch I split yesterday, smoothed and tapered for her father’s deck-repairs. She hauls them up with a short, double-tug of the rope—the mid-tier sign for *received and sound*—and the trapdoor drops shut, leaving only the sound of her bare feet padding away toward the bark-cloth frames.

I eat with my fingers, sitting back into the sling, letting my feet dangle out over the nothingness.

***

From down here, you do not see the sky—only the white, diffused glare that filters through fifty layers of cedar branches, vine-bridges, and dripping laundry. But you see the gorge whole.

You see how the entire cleft is a single, slow engine. The water enters at the top, wild and clean, captured by the chute-keepers' timber teeth; it clatters down through the platforms, turning wheels, boiling tubers, washing bark, quenching fires; it falls from deck to deck in frayed silver cords, gathering soot and tea-leaf dregs and wood-shavings, until it drops into the darkness below to meet the roaring river that carries the dead away to the lowlands.

We do not build against the wet. We let the wet soak into the sinew, we let the wood rot where it must, and we carve it out before the foot gives way.

When the afternoon wind hits the mountain notch, the great support tree beside me gives its long, familiar groan—a deep, resonant *hwooom* that starts in the root-mats five hundred feet below and travels up through the trunk into my shoulder blades. The bridge above me slackens, then tightens with a snap of hemp that shakes a shower of cold condensation over my neck.

I wipe the water from my eyes with a sleeve stiff with grease, check the heat of my pitch-pot, and reach for the pry-chisel. 

There is another soft spot under the fish-dryers. The grip-hounds have just begun to scratch at the planks. I have half an hour of grey light left to cut it out before the frost sets in.
