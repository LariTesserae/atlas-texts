I am not at the top, where the megaliths lean into the empty troposphere, nor down in the maroon-glowing porridge of the ductile floors. 

I find myself in the *interface*.

At first, there is no sense of having a shape—only a sense of continuous, unyielding resistance. I am pressed between two sheer vertical faces of volcanic glass: one a rising fin of black vitrophyre climbing at three inches an hour, the other a stalled monolith of columnar basalt, pegged in place by the colossal weight of a Baluster’s lintel miles above. 

I am what exists in the gap between them. 

I am a wedge. Not an object dropped there by chance, but a creature of dense, compressed rutile and layered hematite, shaped like a long, asymmetric blade or an elongated double-inclined plane. I have no eyes, no mouth, no lungs to inhale the dry sulfur vapor. My entire existence is an organ of contact—a billion microscopic asperities locking into the mirror-planes of the passing stone, dry van der Waals fibrils engaging and releasing in an endless, rhythmic, microscopic stutter.

I do not crawl. The mountain moves, and I endure the movement.

As the vitrophyre fin grinds upward against my left flank, the shear stress passes straight through the core of my body. It is not pain; it is perception. To be made of dense crystal and iron-lattice is to feel geometry as pressure, and pressure as light. Internally, where my plates are forced to slide over one another at tolerances finer than a wavelength of light, the piezoelectric charge builds continuously. I am a living capacitor, drinking the kinetic madness of the ascending world and storing it as millions of volts of silent, dry potential across my internal laminations.

I am cold on my outer dorsal ridge, which extends into the empty, baking air of the corridor, and white-hot at my ventral keel, buried deep within the fault seam. This thermal gradient—a difference of four hundred degrees across three yards of my body—drives a steady, silent convection of crystalline charge through my marrow. 

I am a *Fulcrum*.

Around me, the world makes its music, and I am one of the instruments.

To my dorsal ridge, several miles of Glass-weaver filament have become attached. The Weaver did not ask; it simply spun its micro-fine line of basalt glass out of the deep vents and hitched the burning thread to the highest projection it could reach—which happened to be the serrated iron spine of my back. 

Now, as the needle I am wedged against tilts half a millimeter to the west, that thread tightens. It hums. The vibration travels down into my dorsal plates as a pure, continuous C-sharp that rattles through my silicate ribs. I can feel, through that single singing wire, the exact speed of a spire three miles away, the weight of two Gliders that have accidentally sliced themselves to ribbons against its length, and the slow, approaching shudder of a Baluster herd hauling a four-hundred-ton anchor across the high ridge above.

Sometimes, a Shear-Plate ripples across my exposed surface. It feels like a deck of heavy slate cards sliding over my flank. It lingers on my dorsal ridge, grazing on the triboelectric static that leaks from my seams, cooling me slightly, resting its micro-fibrils against my iron scales before continuing its endless, counter-descending slide toward the warm abyss. I let it feed. In exchange, its broad, dark mantle shields my thinnest plates from the scouring heat-ripples rising from below.

The tension is mounting now. 

I have felt it coming for forty years: a torque developing deep within the basalt fin on my right. The rock is refusing to rise. The Balusters have stacked too much basalt upon its crown, trying to quiet its ascent, but the magma piston miles beneath does not understand counterweights. The stone between my jaws is groaning—not in sound, but in high-frequency, crystalline stress-waves that heat my core to a dull cherry-red.

I tighten my lattice. I do not try to stop the mountain; I prepare to translate it.

When the fracture finally happens, it does not come with a warning. 

A single, concussive *CRACK* detonates through the seam. The basalt slab splits. The stored kinetic energy of two centuries discharges in three milliseconds. 

A blinding curtain of blue-white triboelectric lightning sheets across my flanks, illuminating the great, lightless corridor for miles around. For an instant, in that fierce, dry flash, the entire vertical universe is visible: the endless mirror-fins of obsidian stretching into the black roof of the world, the bronze Gliders suspended like geometric kites in the voids, the blind, triangular Balusters frozen mid-haul on the high ledges.

In that same instant, the violence of the parting shears me in two.

My body does not tear—tear is for wet, fibrous things. I *cleave*. Along an exact, pre-formed octahedral plane, my hematite plates slide apart with a dry, ringing screech. 

One half of me remains locked to the rising spire, carried upward into the colder, quieter reaches of the troposphere, humming with the immense charge of the newly absorbed strike. The other half falls.

I fall through the great, empty corridor of warm nitrogen, tumbling silently through the dark. I do not break on the way down; I have no organs to burst. I am a clean, faceted shard of iron-doped crystal, whistling as I slice through the sulfur air.

Miles below, in the narrow throat of a glowing fracture where the maroon light is thick and the basalt is still soft as tar, I strike a ledge of fresh obsidian. 

The impact is magnificent. I chip the ledge; the ledge chips me. The shock-wave rings through my fragments, and then the powerful static affinity within my core wakes up. Slowly, over the course of decades, the magnetism in my iron plates begins to draw my shattered edges back together across the warm stone. The sulfur-breathing endoliths will crust over the scar, sealing the joint with ochre-colored mineral cement.

I will wedge myself into this new, narrower crack. I will wait for a Glass-weaver to anchor a thread to my ridge. I will listen for the slow, ascending grind of the newborn glass beneath me.

And I will begin, once again, to hold the dark apart.
