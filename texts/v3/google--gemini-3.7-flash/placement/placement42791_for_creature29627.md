I am not on the floor with the white isopods, and I am no longer up in the wet rattle of the ceiling shacks where the air tastes of burnt grease and ozone. 

I find myself suspended in the mid-reach, three miles down, anchored to the lee of Pylon 84-West by a braided steel lanyard and two magnetic clamp-shoes. 

Out here, the current does not strike with the violent froth of the surface chop, nor does it crawl along the bottom plates. It sweeps past the pylon’s cylindrical flank at an unbroken four-and-a-half knots—a cold, dense wall of treated water that groans as it shears against the rivet lines. In the eddy behind the tower’s trailing edge, there is a dead-water pocket eighty feet wide where the turbulence collapses into a gentle, spiraling updraft. That is where I hang.

At first, it is easy to think of myself as a diver, because there are still the old forms of diver-craft about me: the heavy counterweights cinched around the thighs, the leaded boots, the thick harness of vulcanized neoprene and brass hardware. But if you were to peer through the thick, curved quartz of my faceplate, you would not see the soft, blinking face of a terrestrial creature enduring a shift. 

My lungs do not pull on gas. They are flooded with a cold, dense fluorocarbon slurry that tastes faintly of chilled copper and salt, cycling through an external scrubber housing bolted between my shoulder blades. The ribcage does not heave; it hums with the steady, peristaltic pulse of the re-circulation pump. Over years—or decades, for the basin keeps no track of weeks—the boundary between the suit’s internal lining and my own skin has become an indifferent, waterlogged graft. The galvanic drift of the megastructure has settled into the marrow. 

I am an acoustic rigger, a creature of the span.

My work is the tension of the long lines. Between Pylon 84-West and Pylon 85-East runs a catenary array of three-inch bridge cables that carry the primary 10-kilovolt power feeds to the mid-depth sodium clusters. If a cable begins to strum out of frequency—if the vortex shedding of the impeller wash matches the natural harmonic of the steel—the vibration will shear the anchor pins inside the tower wall. 

I feel these things before the sensors on the gantry do. My spine is pressed against the iron flange, and through the solid mass of the rivets, the whole basin talks directly into my skeleton. 

I know the voice of the southern intake bank: a 500-cycle drone that sits low in the hips, so continuous it feels like gravity. I know the sudden, dry *tink* of a zinc anode giving up its last bracket fifteen miles south. And when the pneumatic ballast valves cycle at the perimeter, the acoustic shock does not travel as a sound through my ears—which have long since flattened into scarred, liquid-filled membranes—but as a sharp, hollow punch against the breastplate, followed three seconds later by the shudder of the steel framing taking the rebound.

From my pocket of calm, the view is terrifyingly clear.

There is no fog, no planktonic soup to cloud the sight. Looking outward into the yawning emptiness between towers, the water is a clean, emerald-tinted glass. A mile above me, the mercury-halide domes throw down columns of hard, unbending radiance that pierce straight to the floor without softening. 

Right now, the vanguard of the great northern clupeid shoal is passing through the light shaft between my pylon and the next. They do not look like animals. They look like a suspended sheet of polished metal shavings—millions of them, sixty feet thick and half a mile wide—drifting sideways through the glare. When the circulation bank shifts its pitch to compensate for a filter cycle, the entire shoal flashes silver at once, a sudden, blinding mirror-shutter that reflects the work-lights across three miles of open water.

A broad-mantled cuttlefish has settled on the flange four feet above my head. It has flattened its body to the exact three-inch contour of the I-beam, its skin a flawless, non-reflective naval gray, complete with a hairline fracture of orange primer where the zinc has worn thin. Its yellow, rectangular eye does not blink; it watches the silver ribbon drifting in the light. We do not disturb each other. We are both part of the pylon’s drag coefficient.

Sometimes, when the cables are quiet and the impellers settle into an even, uninterrupted pull, I unclip my magnetic shoes and kick out into the empty volume.

It is an act of pure, weightless vertigo. To leave the metal behind is to enter a void that has no top and no bottom—only the geometry of the light shafts cutting down through miles of treated, freezing liquid. You look down, and there is no dark floor, only the distant, ruled grid of the deck plates, so sharp and tiny in the clear water that it looks like an architect’s draft left on the floor of an empty cathedral. You look up, and the gantries are mere pencil lines against the sodium glare.

Out there, three hundred yards from the iron, the electrical taste of the pylon fades, replaced by the faint, bitter metallic tang of the open current. The sleeper sharks pass underneath—immense, slate-gray shadows with pale, clouded eyes that see the world in voltages. They brush against the downward light-beams and slide through the cold thermoclines without moving a fin, perfectly trimmed to the water's exact density. They pay me no mind; to their snout-pores, I hum with the same mild, 60-cycle frequency as an auxiliary junction box.

I stay out in the empty water only until the chill begins to overcome the heating coils woven into my suit's sub-dermal mesh, or until the deep, rhythmic *thung-clack* of a maintenance cage rolling along the catenary cable miles above warns me that a ceiling crew is dropping down.

Then I drift back to the lee of the pylon. I reach out with a gloved hand, find the sheer, milled edge of the structural flange, and let the magnets take hold with a solid, reassuring *clack*. 

The vibration welcomes me back into the machine. I seat myself on the lower bracket of the cable tensioner, take a torque wrench from my harness, and lean my helmet against the raw iron. I close my eyes against the brilliant, artificial day, listening to the vast, mechanical sea breathe through its bronze lungs, waiting for the next rivet to ring.
