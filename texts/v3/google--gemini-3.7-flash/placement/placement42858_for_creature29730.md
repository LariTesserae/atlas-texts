At first, there is only the tooth.

I do not begin with a name, or a shape, or the boundary where my skin ends and the sweating world begins. I begin where the flat surface of my lower jaw is clamped tight against a four-inch high-pressure condensate return. 

The pipe is old—the iron is pitted under a greasy hide of sulfur-moss—but it is live. Through the enamel of my teeth, through the porous, oil-soaked bone of my skull, the Machine speaks its baseline: a low, thick thirty-cycle hum that tastes of scalded lead. If I lift my jaw, the world goes wide, soft, and deafeningly chaotic—the hiss of distant pressure-releases, the heavy splash of a tarn overflowing its granite lip, the blind, wet flapping of moth-swarms through the thermal fog. But when I press bone back to steel, the noise resolves into geometry.

Through the iron, I am anchored. 

I feel the slow, heavy tramp of a Wallower pod three miles up-gradient, their thick pads setting up a rhythmic, hydraulic thud as they trample the liverwort carpets. I feel the higher, sharper ringing of an Anvil-Tail striking a pylon across the western abyss—two short cracks, a pause, then the sustained hum of the cable singing like a plucked bowstring as it waits for the echo to bounce off a failing joint. And beneath it all, deeper than the marrow can comfortably hold, I feel the immense, cathedral-sized stroke of the Deep Turbines, grinding the rock like millstones, pushing the hot blood through this high shelf of the world.

When I pull myself up, I do not stand upright. To stand high is to invite the blind lash of energized grounding-wires that whip in the updrafts, or to lose footing on the rounded, algae-greased spines of the conduits. 

My limbs are four, but they work as six when needed; my ribs are wide and flexible, built like a barrel to absorb the percussive overpressures when an exhaust valve ruptures nearby. My hands are long—palms broad as shovel-blades, skin thick and rubbery from decades of handling boiling zinc and scaling rust. Where a thumb should meet five fingers, I have eight, thin and double-jointed, tipped with black, horn-hard nails that I use to tap.

*Tap. Tap-tap. Tl-tl-tap.*

I strike the underside of the pipe with my third knuckle. The metal rings out clean. No cavitation. No slug of cold sludge gathering in the elbow to burst the weld. It is good. I trace the seam with the soft, sensory fringe beneath my lower jaw—a beard of fine, wet-sensing tendrils that drink the chemical runoff. The water here is sweet today: a trace of turbine-tallow, a high mineral bite of calcium, but no acid-scorch. I dip my mouth to the weep-hole and drink until my throat is hot.

What am I? The herds below do not care; to them, I am only another acoustic texture scraping the metal above their heads. The Anvil-Tails know me as something too lean and bony to risk a leap for, smelling too strongly of old copper-grease and dried musk to be tender. To the younger clans of the Gasket-Folk—those squat, pale families who nest in the dry bellies of dead heat-exchangers—I am a *Crawl-Far*. A stray. One of the old ones whose ears have widened so far they can no longer bear the chattering density of the tribe-vaults.

I live in the between-trusses. 

My home is a suspended maintenance cradle, three hundred paces above a boiling sinkhole, hung from three braided steel cables that have grown into the trunks of two towering rubber-trees. The trees weep scalding sap that smells of charred bread; I gather it on flat slate tiles, let it cure in the hot mist until it turns to leathery tar, and use it to patch the gaskets of my shelter. Inside the cradle, the air is still. I have lined it with dried liverwort fiber, stripped of its bitter oils, and the pale, downy molts of young Vapor-Condors that I scavenge from the lower guano-ledges. It is soft. It smells of my own grease and the warm, damp wool of the mosses.

I spend my cycles following the iron lines out to the ragged margins of the plateau, where the Machine-Spines end in great, jutting cantilevers over the void. 

Out there, the thermal fog is torn apart by the violent updrafts of the abyss. The wind does not bring light—there is no light above, none below, only the heavy, endless night—but it brings *weather*. It brings the cold, stinging mist of the upper sky, smelling of nothing at all—a terrifying, empty smell, barren of iron, barren of oil, dead as dry bone. When that cold air hits the boiling lips of the exhaust stacks, the condensation is instantaneous and violent: rain that falls sideways, scalding drops the size of plums, and sheets of static that crawl across my hide like swarming ants.

In those places, I hunt.

I do not hunt the wallowers; their hides are too thick for my iron-tipped pikes, and their weight would drag me into the sinkholes. Instead, I wait on the undersides of the great transformer arches. When the high-voltage lines snap with ozone, the grease-moths and the blind flying-beetles drop from the mist by the thousands, stunned by the electrical shock. I sweep them from the wet iron with wide wicker scoops woven from fibrous conduit-roots. I crush their oily abdomens into paste, mix it with sulfur-crust and scraped pipe-tallow, and pack it into the hollow horn-tubes I wear slung beneath my chest. It keeps my belly hot when I must crawl through the cold, dead dead-ends where the steam has ceased to run.

Sometimes, when the turbines down in the granite drop half a pitch—a rare, shuddering shift that happens once every few seasons, making the entire continent of the plateau groan like a stressed ship—I feel the Gasket-Folk speaking from leagues away.

The message starts as a faint, rhythmic rattle in the long-span conduits: *Tung... tung... kl-kl-tung.* 

It travels for miles through the iron forest, taken up by solitary wanderers like me, passed from conduit to catenary, from catenary to bridge-girder, a vast, blind telegraphy running through the night. 

*Pressure dropping in the Seventh Spoke.* 
*Herds moving north toward the weeping flange.* 
*A cable broken at the chasm.*

I hook my knees over the pipe, lean back into the warm, roaring dark, and raise my knuckle to the iron. I strike back my piece of the map: the temperature of the draft here, the thickness of the moss, the steady, rhythmic pulse of the life that has wrapped its blind, dripping fingers around the only warmth in the world.

Then I press my jaw back against the steel, close the lids over my smooth, empty sockets, and listen to the world breathe.
