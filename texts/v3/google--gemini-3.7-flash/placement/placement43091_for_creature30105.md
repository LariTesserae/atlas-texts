At first, there is only the split down the middle.

It is not an idea; it is a physical tearing. On the left side, the air is zero-weight, dry as scraped bone, and so cold it smells of nothing at all—only the vast, neutral iron of the upper sky. On the right side, the world is an overfilled lung: wet, yeasty, roaring with the fat, green steam of bruised figs, vibrating so hard in the marrow that it feels like a second pulse pushing against the ribs.

I would not be down in the orchard, where the time runs like grease on a hot skillet. 

Down there, everything is burning its candle from both ends and in the middle: midges exploding into existence and dissolving into mulch in the span of an exhale; six-legged runners whose thin skin shows two hearts thrashing like trapped wasps; birds whose hollow bones are pressurized like steam pipes, terrified of puncturing. To live down there is to be eaten by the speed of the three needles.

And I would not be out on the plateau, where four billion years of wind have scraped away every noun until only the smooth, soapy gray remains. Out there, nothing happens for centuries except the slow frosting and unfrosting of the granite.

I find myself precisely at the lip. Straddling the seam where the felt curtain meets the void.

I am something flat, heavy, and slate-colored, wedged into the shallow fracture where the basin’s rim folds back into the dead plateau. My belly is pressed to the rock—not with the manic capillary grip of the pale runners, but with the cold, patient suction of a wet boot on flagstone. 

Half of me is frosted. The carapace on my outer flank is thick, chalky, and dead-white, crusted with the dry rime of the high altitude. It feels nothing. It doesn't need to; it is just a shield against the thin, vacuum-clean wind that scours the peaks. 

But my inner flank—the side turned down toward the roaring greenhouse—is soft, corrugated, and alive with dark crimson veins. It hangs into the warmth like a gill dipped into running water. It drinks the heavy, ionized moisture that pulses off the spires. It listens.

My skull is wide and fused directly to my collar, shaped like a wedge of split shale. It rests on the stone, absorbing the two worlds at once: through the bone of my left jaw comes the absolute, hollow silence of the dead mountain; through my right comes the deep, mechanical thrum of the three black spires, vibrating through the rock like an iron church bell that was struck three hundred years ago and has never stopped ringing.

I do not hunt. In this place, you do not need to hunt; the violence of the hollow throws its surplus outward. 

I am the gleaner of the shear zone. 

I wait for the skimmers. They fly too fast, drunken on high-pressure oxygen and the fat of the drift-midges. They bank against the invisible wall of the basin, using the cushion of cold air to ricochet back into the swarm—*thwip, thwip, thwip*—their stiff, quill-wings snapping like whips. But they are frantic, and they make mistakes. 

A collision in mid-air; a broken quill; a sudden slip three inches too far into the thin, cold air outside. 

When they cross the threshold, the change is instantaneous. Their pressurized lungs rupture with a tiny, dry *pop*. Their wings stiffen into blue-glazed glass. They don't fall back into the orchard; they tumble outward, sliding down the smooth, cold incline of the bald granite toward the void.

And that is where my front limbs—long, jointed, gray as lichen, ending in three hooked, blunt chisels—reach out from the rime. 

I do not hurry. The cold outside preserves them instantly. I reach across the border into the dead air, drag the frozen aerialists back across the invisible threshold into the warmth of my under-belly, and let them thaw against my soft flesh. The frost turns to water; the stiff oil-feathers go limp; the rich, high-potency fats melt into my pores. 

I am the slow hinge between their borrowed time and the plateau’s eternity.

Beneath me, the orchard screams, breeds, and dies twenty times an hour. Above me, the sky is empty, violet, and ancient. 

I stay wedged in the stone, neither warm nor frozen, digesting the fallen sky-things, listening with one side of my head to the machines that do not care about us, and feeling the mist condense, beat by beat, along the margin where the world begins and ends.
