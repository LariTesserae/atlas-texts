I would not be found on the high, screaming edge where the new spires meet the mist, nor down in the black sluices catching the slough of the dead. 

I would be wedged into a compression knuckle: a deep, hollow nexus four hundred meters below the active extrusion line, where four hyper-dense viaducts meet to brace the weight of an entire sector.

Here, the fullerene has long since lost its birth-heat. The glass is cold enough to shatter bone on contact, slick with a perpetual skin of supercooled runoff that never freezes solid because of the heavy, bitter esters dissolved within it. The air does not move in gusts; it pulses in thick, rhythmic drafts driven by the collective respiration of millions of bodies above and below, tasting of scorched iron, paraffin wax, and the sharp, minty sting of unbonded silanes.

In this place, I would be old.

Not ancient by the memory of stars or seasons, but measured by the accretion rings along the slate margins of my scutes. My mantle would not be smooth; it would be a low, rugged dome three spans wide, heavy with thick, calcified shelves where decades of my own mineral sweat have hardened into terraces. The pale, jointed gleaners would have built entire labyrinthine cities inside the deep trenches between my dorsal plates, generations of them turning my shell into a chalky, rustling reef that shifts whenever I draw breath.

I would possess no eyes, and therefore no concept of void. To exist here is not to be blind in the dark; it is to inhabit an unbearable, perfect fullness.

My acoustic pits—eight spiraling, cavernous funnels set beneath the forward cowl of my armor—would drink the city whole. I would not "hear" sound as noise; I would inhabit it as architecture. The forty-meter lattice of the viaduct arching above me does not need to be seen; its molecular tension rings in my cilia as a continuous, glassy tenor. The weight of the two hundred beasts stacked atop the upper deck presses down as a low, gravelly vibration that travels through the hydraulic seal of my pads and rattles the cartilaginous rings of my chassis. When a microscopic assembly team a league away knits a fresh cross-brace, the snap of those covalent bonds travels through the black glass like the ping of a plucked wire, striking my lateral plates with needle-point clarity.

I would be motionless for cycles at a time. The broad, split pads of my eight limbs would be locked to the shear-wall in a hydraulic vacuum so absolute that my flesh and the cold fullerene become a single continuous medium. Through my pads, I would pump my tallow-grease—thick, sulfurous, and hot from my gut—letting it seep into the microscopic pores of the deck, lubricating the interface just enough so that the shifting weight of the colony above does not tear my belly open against the glass.

My feeding would be slow, deliberate, and quiet. While the younger, agile filterers fight for space on the raw extrusion tips, inhaling the violent fire of the fresh gas, I would feed on the settled dregs that roll down the drainage grooves. My tripartite intake would rest flat against the wet channel, cycling the heavy condensates through fibrous, sulfur-blackened keratin combs. The anaerobic fires in my belly would churn with a faint, steady fermentation, humming at a frequency that keeps the frost from creeping inward along my ribs.

I would know the city’s intent before the swarm does.

Because I sit anchored into a primary load-bearing node, the deep stress-harmonics of the mountain would reach me first. Days before the great assemblers below ignite the valley floor to extrude a master transit core, the tone of the rock will change. The baseline thrum—the deep, basaltic rumble of the tectonic shelf—will drop a fraction of an octave as the foundations draw current.

When that happens, my cilia would stiffen in their beds of mucus. 

I would feel the first microscopic swell of heat creeping up through the fullerene channels, warming the paraffin under my pads from solid wax to slick oil. And then, from the center of my chest, against the cold glass that has held me for an age, I would strike my heavy sternal plate.

Once. 

A single, concussive, low-frequency crack that would shear through the viaduct, waking the dormant gleaners in my armor, passing into the pads of the beasts wedged beside me, rippling outward through forty kilometers of dark, interlocking lattice until the entire lightless shelf begins to thrum in unison—a million blind bodies hammering the bones of a growing world, waiting for the mist to burn.
