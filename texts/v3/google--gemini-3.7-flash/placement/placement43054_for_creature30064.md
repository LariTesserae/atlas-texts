I would not be one of the wedge-strikers, whose shoulders knot into thick, corded burls from the beetle-swings, nor one of the Seam-Listeners tipped forward on their willows above the drop. Those lives are tuned to the violent crack, the sudden yield of fifty tons of pale weight letting go. 

I would be found lower down, where the stone is neither being broken nor being measured, but merely *moved*.

I would be the tender of the skids.

My place is on my knees in the wet slurry, five paces behind the living alcoves and two feet ahead of the rollers. It is a narrow, shifting strip of work that belongs to neither the quarry nor the home, but exists entirely in the friction between them. My palms are yellowed from cold sheep tallow and gray from the stone-flour that never quite leaves the cuticles. In my belt is no bronze wedge, but a broad, curved scraper made from the rib of an old plains-bull, smoothed thin by years of dragging across unweathered limestone.

You would find me waiting where a floor is becoming a journey.

When a family’s sleeping-shelf is marked for the drop, I am the one who lays the split cedar logs into the weep-grooves. I pack the underside of the timber with wet lime-paste mixed with the rubbery scrapings of the iron-scale snails, bedding the wood so true that when the block drops from the wall with that wet, dull *pop*, it does not splinter the skid or shatter its own crisp corners. 

I smell of mutton fat, pine pitch, and the cold iron-sweat that leaks from the bottom of the world. 

My work is quiet, slow, and rhythmic. While the mallets fall on the upper tier, sending small, sharp vibrations through my shins, I scrape the tallow-tracks clean behind a departing block. The ash-colored lizards follow my hands without fear; they know the warmth of the grease-pot I carry slung between my knees, and they dart between my calloused fingers to snap up the flour-flies that drown in the melted fat. Sometimes I let three or four of them crawl onto the back of my forearm, their belly-skins so thin I can feel the tiny, rapid flutter of their hearts against the white scars of my wrist.

I do not look up at the hawk circling the rim. Up there, the world is flat, dry, and blown by a wind that has touched grass; it has no weight, no draft of cold cellar-air, no damp mineral marrow. 

Instead, I look down into the grain of the newly opened faces. I can tell by the gray tint of the seepage whether a block will slide smoothly on three rollers or drag heavily on five. I know the exact moment a cedar log is about to crush under a uneven load by the faint, honeyed smell of bruised resin it releases an instant before the grain gives way.

At midday, when the sun stands straight over the Sump and the deep mist burns off the runnels, I sit in the temporary shadow beneath a tilted monolith that is halfway between being someone's kitchen and being the foundation of a crane-pier three tiers down. I eat my dried pea-cake slowly, dipping the hard edge into the fresh water-sweat beading on the wall beside my head. The water tastes of lime and very old silence.

Beside me, behind a fresh cedar strut set tight into a vertical joint, the stone is slightly darker, slightly thicker. It is the seam where an old striker from the third tier was sealed two moons ago. The lime-sweat has already begun to crust over the hemp bindings of his shroud, turning the weave into brittle white stone. I lay the flat of my tallow-slick palm against the timber, feeling neither grief nor dread, only the slow, cool transference of cold from his calcifying ribs through the cedar and into my skin.

He is settling. The whole basin is settling. 

When the sun leans away and the shadows turn razor-sharp across the steps, a mallet will sound twice from above—the signal that the next bed-niche has been freed. I will rise on splayed, ridged feet, lift my grease-pot by its hemp bail, and lay the first roller into the cold, wet cut, waiting to catch the house as it begins to slide.
