I would not be in the open channels, nor up among the grease-spiders in the iron heights where the air dries to sticky soot. Those are places of transit, places where things must cling or drift. 

I would be settled lower down, in the choked thresholds—specifically in the flooded collar of a decommissioned bypass culvert, wedged between a twelve-inch bronze gate valve and the sweating masonry of a drowned control bunker. 

I would not have eyes; eyes in this tea-colored, lime-fogged gloom would be useless blisters, easily stung by the vinegar flushes. Instead, my whole surface would be an organ of pressure, temperature, and hydraulic torque. I would be heavy—heavier than the water that tries to lift me, dense with swallowed slag and mineral ballast—anchored deep into the joint where the pipe’s lead packing has long rotted out and been replaced by fibrous root-chokes.

***

At first, I might seem like nothing more than an obstruction in the line: a swollen, rubbery mass the color of wet slate, three or four feet long, plastered tight against the curvature of the cast iron. But beneath that thick, self-sloughing hide of tallowy mucus, there is an architecture of dense cartilage, slow-pumping vascular sinusoids, and thick plates of interior horn. 

I am a valve-creature, an accretor. I do not wander the wide diamond-plate streets like the sludge-salamanders, nor am I microscopic and fixed like the scale-plates. I live by occlusion. My body matches the radius of the conduit perfectly. 

When the sluices roar and the water runs high—swollen with chemical wash and the alkaline scald of the upper machine shops—I lie flattened against the floor of the culvert, letting the broth wash over my back. My skin drinks it: not the water itself, which is too harsh, but the film of machine tallow, the dissolved zinc, the microscopic flakes of iron scale that shear off the impellers miles upriver. I have no teeth to grind, only a series of internal, muscular gizzards lined with smooth river-pebbles and iron slag, tumbling and pulverizing the silt into usable minerals, secreting a thick paste of lime and sulfur that I exude back out to reinforce the seam between my hide and the brickwork.

I know the terrace not as a visual space, but as a vast, continuous vibration that passes straight through my belly into the stone. 

I know when the shift changes three levels above by the pitch of the displacement stroke—when the brass valving shifts from a hollow *thud-clack* to a wet, heavy shudder. I know the chemistry of the district overhead by the taste of the current:
* A copper tang when the wire-drawers flush their baths;
* A sweet, sickening taste of lard when the stamping presses purge their bearing jackets;
* The bitter, chalky bite that coats my tongue when the lime-slurry overflows.

And then comes the draw-down.

I feel it three seconds before the water moves—a tightening in the hydraulic grain, a sudden drop in hydrostatic load against my flanks. The water recedes with that great, tearing groan, exposing the slick brick stairs and the trembling aroid roots. 

As the channel empties, I do not scuttle away to hide. I swell. 

My vascular chambers engorge with stored fluid, expanding my body until I press tight against the bronze valve seat, completely plugging the culvert. I become the stopper. Behind me, trapped in the dead space of the abandoned bypass, a pool of cooler, un-aerated water remains held, thick with drifting organic detritus and pale, blind larvae that would otherwise be sucked down into the teeth of the intake impellers. 

In this dark pocket, I hold the reservoir. The scale-plates along my flanks open their skirts slightly, venting hot gas; small lagging-crawlers, stranded by the retreating flood, clamber onto my leathery ridges to stay moist, their feathery leg-fringes tickling my hide as they comb the grease from my mucus. I am an island of wet mass in the hollow throat of the machine.

Then comes the pause. 

Those three suspended seconds. 

The roar of the weirs ceases. The steam elbows stop their hiss. The water hangs motionless in the dark, caught between the pull of the pumps and the return of the rams. 

In that absolute, ringing silence, the Sump holds its breath, and I release mine. A cloud of fine, milky spores—heavy with sulfur-binding enzymes and dormant genetic drift—puffs softly from the vent under my lateral ridge, dispersing into the still, dark water trapped behind my bulk. It is my only utterance. No call, no light. Just a chemical signature cast into the stagnant gap.

*THUMP.*

The pneumatic rams fire in the foundations. The iron shudders violently, ringing like a struck anvil. 

The backwash hits my exposed front—a boiling, foaming wall of white water and mineral froth that crashes down the culvert. It hits me with tons of force, but I am wedged tight, my horn-plates locked against the bronze rim, absorbing the shock and turning it into heat. 

The pressure equalizes; the basin fills; the water rises up the steps to lap once more at the windowless bunkers. Slowly, I deflate, relaxing my flanks, allowing the current to catch the bypass water and sweep it out—along with my spores, the larvae, and the washed grease—out into the roaring, three-block expanse of the flooded plaza.

I settle back into the rust and the moss, tasting the new cycle, listening through my ribs to the rhythmic *thud-clack* of the deep brass valves, entirely at home in the boiling, weeping dark.
