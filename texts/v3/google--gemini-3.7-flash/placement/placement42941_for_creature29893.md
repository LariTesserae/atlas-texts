At first, there is only the weight of the frost-shell. 

It forms over my ribs in the dark: a brittle, ridged vault of ice created entirely by the moisture escaping my own nose and the nape of the neck pressed against my chest. In the hour before the sun clears the eastern needle, I am not a person with a name or a destination; I am merely three pints of thick, slow-moving grease and salted water wrapped in marmot hide, pinned between two slabs of cold slate. 

I do not move my toes. To move them is to break the seal of dead air pooled at the bottom of the pelt. The air in my lungs tastes like old juniper smoke and the dry, floury dust of crushed hare bones. 

Then comes the sound that isn't a sound: the change in the grain of the granite.

Six thousand paces above the valley, the stone does not warm slowly; it rings. When the first white lance of light clears the spire and strikes the slate wall four inches above my head, the rock expands with a dry, microscopic *snick*, like a fingernail clicking against a tooth. 

That is when I know which one I am.

I am the one on the outside of the curve. The one whose back takes the draft that slips through the unmortared chinks of the windbreak. 

I push my hand out from under the stiff, frozen hem of the marmot cape. The skin of my knuckles is the color of a charred walnut, scarred with white cross-hatches where the fresh flakes of sky-flint bit deep yesterday afternoon without my feeling the cut. The air hits my bare skin like a flat iron pulled from snow—so dry and thin it drinks the sweat from my pores before it can form a bead.

I do not sit up quickly. The blood up here is like cold pine resin; if I heave my chest too fast, the hollow behind my temples begins to drum with a sick, green light. 

Instead, I watch my hand. 

I hold it out into the narrow band of morning glare that has just dropped over the low wall. For three breaths, the skin stays gray and numb. Then the ultraviolet radiation hits the melanin, hits the iron in the hemoglobin, and my fingers bloom with a sudden, violent, prickling heat. A drop of flash-melt from the overhang above lands on the back of my wrist; before it can run, the sun catches it, and it vanishes into a tiny puff of steam, leaving behind only a faint ring of white lime on my skin.

Now the cradle is awake, and so am I.

I slide out from behind the wall, leaving the younger one curled in the warm hollow of the pelts to keep the draft off the embers in the ash-pit. My knees make the same clicking sound as the rock. 

I take my bone awl from the fracture where it spent the night. The bone is smoothed yellow from the fat of thirty seasons, notched near the butt to hold a loop of dried goat-gut. My boots are made of triple-layered rawhide, the soles studded with the split teeth of high-country hares to bite into the slick, ice-glazed moss.

I crawl out onto the shelf of the notch on all fours. 

To stand upright here is foolishness. The wind does not blow in gusts; it shears across the rim in a continuous, silent blade that wants nothing more than to peel you off the slate and drop you into that terrible, pale-blue nothingness. Down there, the world is swollen, wet, and heavy with trees that rot where they fall. Down there, air is thick as soup, choking people on the dampness of their own soil. 

Up here, there is no rot. There is only what burns, and what freezes.

I find my cushion-plant—the great wine-red dome wedged into the cleft where the quartz vein splits the slate. It is four feet wide, packed tighter than a butcher’s block. Ten minutes ago it was iron-hard, rimed in frost so sharp it would shear the wool from a sleeve. Now, with the midday sun beginning its two-hour hammer against the basin, the outer half-inch of leaves is soft, hot, and slightly yielding beneath my palms. 

I press my ear against the dome.

Beneath the outer pelt of tiny, succulent leaves, deep in the warm, dark peat where the longhorn grubs are chewing their slow tunnels, I can hear the pressurized sap rising. It sounds like a faint, wet sigh. 

I take the awl, find the natural fold near the root-collar where the cushion meets the bare slate, and pry back the crust. 

The smell hits me at once: sharp, resinous, smelling of crushed iron, winter sunlight, and bitter honey. The sap does not flow like lowland pitch; it beads in clear, viscous pearls along the pale fibrous throat of the plant. 

I do not use a scraper. The warmth of human flesh is the only thing that keeps the gum from turning to glass the instant it touches the air. I roll the bead onto the pad of my thumb, feeling the intense, stinging bitterness seep into the cracks of my skin, and smear it directly onto the stretched gut-cord coiled around my left forearm. 

Three inches away, a black-and-gold beetle lands on the back of my hand. 

Its shell is so hot from the sun it feels like a dropped ember. Its tiny, clawed feet prickle against my skin as it scuttles toward the wound I’ve made in the cushion, frantic to drink before the shadow of the western needle creeps across our cleft. We do not fight over the sap. There is enough bitter gum in this single dome to dress thirty gangrened limbs in the valley below, enough to buy a season’s worth of salt, smoked tallow, and dry juniper roots.

I work with the rhythm of the pika. 

Dab. Smear. Roll. Check the shadow. 

Across the basin, fifty paces away, the pale stoat is crouched on a slab of slate, its dark eye-rings fixed on the scree. It doesn't look at me, and I don't look at it. We are both running on the same clockwork. If the shadow catches the beetle, it freezes mid-stride; if the shadow catches the sap on my thumb, it sets like flint and ruins the strip; if the shadow catches me without my marmot hide, my lungs will begin to fill with the cold fluid that never clears.

At noon, when the heat is so fierce that the rock seems to thrum beneath my belly, I stop. 

I reach into my pouch, pull out a dried hare-kidney cured in salt and primrose dust, and chew it slowly until my saliva turns the hard knot into something I can swallow. I look up, past the razor spires, into the empty sky. 

A swift cuts the air directly over my head—a slate-gray arrow with a white throat, banking so hard against the sheer drop that I can hear the hum of its feathers like the vibration of a plucked bowstring. It dives outward, folding into the six-thousand-pace emptiness, chasing a midge that drifted up from the world below.

I do not envy the bird its wings, and I do not envy the valley its heavy air. 

I wipe my bloody, sap-blackened thumb on my leather apron, pick up the hammerstone from the gravel seam, and turn my face toward the obsidian vein. The sun is beginning to slide toward the western needle, and the rock is waiting to be struck.
