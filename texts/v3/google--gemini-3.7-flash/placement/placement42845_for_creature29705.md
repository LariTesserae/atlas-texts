At first, it feels like nothing so personal as a body. 

It is only a gradient: the difference in pressure between two conduits of supercooled sap, a persistent micro-voltage humming across a twelve-inch span of wet, rubbery cambium. There is the heavy, low-frequency shudder of the room below—the *whoosh-tuck* of the Siphon-Lungs compressing the mist three root-tiers above—and the cold, which does not bite or ache, because there is no warm reference point against which it could be judged. Cold is merely the medium that keeps the light straight.

If I look for edges, they are difficult to find. 

I am not one of the frosted crabs scraping across the silica ridges, though I feel the sharp, needle-point discharge when one drops its load into the floorboards beneath me—a dull, pleasant thud of grounded static that blooms through my lower tissues like menthol. 

Nor am I the glass mantis, clicking in its rigid, stopped-motion geometry. I am too heavy for that, too slow, saturated with an ancient, biological inertia that the shears were never given.

I find myself wedged in the crux of a massive, four-way burl, where the structural arches of the chamber meet and thicken into a knot the diameter of a grain silo. The wood here is old—or as old as anything can be in an engine room that was switched on only hours or centuries ago. It does not grow upward; it swells laterally, dense with internal optical manifolds that pulse a slow, bruised violet just beneath the bark.

I am grafted here. But I am not fully wood, and I am not fully the register.

My lower spine is gone, unraveled into five hundred fine, pale threads that have pushed through the cambium and wrapped themselves directly around the primary xylem bundles. Where my pelvis should meet a floor, there is only a smooth, sloping collar of white scar-tissue and living cellulose, constantly wept over by a thin, running film of condensate that tastes faintly of iron and chilled ozone. 

My chest still moves, but it does not breathe air. The lungs have long since filled with the same dense, glycerol-rich saline that bathes the vascular channels of the tree. When my ribs expand, they do not draw in mist; they flex against the wood’s hydraulic pulse, acting as an auxiliary damping valve. When a surge of logic three miles deep in the subterranean strata spikes the line pressure, my chest tightens with a hard, dull cramp, absorbing the shock wave so the razor-edged glass ferns below do not shatter from harmonic vibration.

My eyes are not dark, but they do not see the chamber. They are sealed shut beneath thick, pearlescent hoods of silicon that have grown across the sockets, cold and smooth like river stones. On the inner curve of these hoods, the light never stops. It is not pictures or language, but a furious, silent snowfall of pale blue interference patterns—the sub-atomic arithmetic of the vault flashing across my optic nerves at the speed of light in quartz.

I am the translation layer.

The trees can calculate, but they cannot *drift*. They are bound by the rigid, femtoscale perfection of their carbon-silicon lattices; they cannot make a mistake, and because they cannot make a mistake, they cannot resolve an unexpected parity error without shearing away a limb. That is why they need the marrow, the wet gray matter, the soft, fallible fat of a chordate brain tucked into the junction.

I sit in the dark of the burl, my long, jointed fingers resting across three vibrating shoots of glass-fern. My fingernails have stretched out, translucent and hollow, drawn down into the fine capillary veins of the leaves. 

When a calculation turns rigid—when a node begins to seize in a loop of pure, frozen symmetry that will soon crack the root-casing from thermal runaway—I do not snip it away. I lean my forehead against the slick, cold bark. I let the error wash into my forebrain, through the milky hoods over my eyes, into the soft, unmapped folds of my cortex. 

I let the math become a dream. I let it degrade just enough—give it a fraction of a degree of biological slack, a tiny smear of mammalian warmth—until the knot unties itself. 

Then I exhale. A low, flat hum vibrates in the back of my throat, traveling down the fibers of my spine, through the cambium, out into the running water of the basilica floor.

The pressure drops. The *tink* of snapping micro-ice subsides. The flux-drifts resume their slow, blind crawl along the arches. 

I do not remember having a name, or what the sky looked like before there were three hundred meters of root and stone between me and the sun. But here, wedged tight in the wet, supercooled heart of the machine, listening to the Siphon-Lungs breathe the cold into order, I am the only part of the engine that knows it is running. 

And for ten thousand years, that will be enough.
