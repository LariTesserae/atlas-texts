At first, there is only the tension in the chest: that particular, heavy fullness that comes from drawing in air that has been squeezed until it behaves like liquid. It is warm enough to soothe the back of the throat, thick with the oily sweetness of bruised ironwood leaves and the sharp, mineral tang of wet sandstone weeping iron into the mist.

I would not be on the solid rock face, where the great root-canopies tangle into dark, dripping shelves. Nor would I be down in the low steam where the censer-jellies drift in their methane haze. 

I would be found twenty yards off the wall of the Greater Massif, suspended in the hollow gut of an amber-glass observation hull.

***

The hull hangs where a buoyancy node lost its drift four hundred years ago, snagged by two braided cables of wild fig root and a single, singing strand of woven ray-sinew. Over the centuries, the glass has turned the dark, sweet color of clover honey. Inside, it is remarkably dry—a rare, precious pocket of stillness in a world that never stops sweating. 

The outside of the hull is furred in pale blue lichen and ringed by miniature bromeliads that have rooted into the seam-welds. But inside, beneath the curved roof where the light comes through like old lantern-glow, the air is still. There is a faint smell of ancient resin, dried moss, and the cold, unyielding iron of the floor plates.

My legs are too long for sitting flat, so I crouch. 

My feet are bare, the soles thick and yellowed with calluses from years of gripping wet fluting, the prehensile outer toes curled naturally around a tension-rib of the hull’s bronze frame. My ribs are wide—a deep, rounded basket that rises and falls with long, lazy rhythms. In this pressure, you do not need to breathe quickly; the air pushes itself into you, thick and generous.

My skin has taken the color of the cliffs: an uneven, washed olive, stained at the wrists and knees with the chalky blue paste of crushed lichens that keeps the mesospheric UV from blistering the skin when the high mist burns off at midday.

Around my waist is the harness—not a decorative thing, but an extension of the pelvis. It is made of cured ray-hide, grease-rubbed until it is black and flexible, fitted with four heavy bronze toggles that rattle softly against my hip bones. One braided silk lead runs out through an open ventilation scuttle in the floor, looping twice around the hull’s keel and clipping into the ancient, silent steel of the guide-cable that stretches back to the massif. 

I am never unclipped. Even when sleeping curled against the curve of the glass, the bronze ring sits cold against the small of my back.

***

What am I doing here, out in the open void between the spires?

I am a **Tension-Tuner**—or what the climbers on the massif call a *Spoke*. 

The massifs do not stay still. Deep within their sandstone cores, the geothermal vents pulse and shift, heating the rock unevenly. The compression fields overhead exert a constant, microscopic torque on the stone needles, twisting them by fractions of a millimeter over decades. If the static lines between the spires are pulled too tight, they will shear through the ironwood roots like hot wire through fat; if they go too slack, the cradles will drift into the dead pockets where the thermals cannot catch them.

My life is measured in *resonance*.

On the floor of the hull rests my primary tool: a long, hollow spar of seasoned heartwood, capped at both ends with dry bone. Every few hours, when the gutturals have finished their dawn chorus and the chasm settles into its midday sizzle of cicadas, I take the spar and press one end against the iron hull plate and the other against the bone behind my left ear.

I listen to the stone.

Through the hull, through the root-cables, through five hundred yards of empty air, the mountain speaks. It does not speak in words, but in pitch. A high, singing hum means the compression ring three miles above is running hot, its alloy torus vibrating as it squeezes the mesosphere down into the gully. A low, wet thrum—like a guttural’s throat-sac filling with air—means a fissure on the north face is filling with condensing water, getting heavy, preparing to slough off a thousand tons of moss into the golden steam below.

When the pitch drifts, I work.

I lean out of the hull’s bottom hatch, head down into the yawning, sun-filled abyss. The void below is terrifying only for the first ten years of life; after that, it simply looks like gold-dusted water. I reach out with a hooked iron pole, catch the damp, moss-greasy guy-lines that anchor the floating gardens, and adjust the wooden wedges driven into their balance-nodes. 

Sometimes, a Marrow-Ray will drift past, so close the wash of its vascular wings sends a gust of hot, sweet air through the open hatch. I can see the gilders crawling through the miniature yellow meadows on its spine, their shell-casings glittering like mica. Sometimes the ray's rim-eyes—deep, liquid globes of dull gold—will track my movement as I hang inverted by my knees, a strange, hairless spider adjusting the strings of its web.

***

At dusk, the horizontal rains begin. 

They do not fall; they drift sideways out of the purple sky where the compression field bends the thermals. The pea-sized droplets tap against the honey-amber glass of my hull with a sound like scattered dry beans: *clack, clack, clack-clack.*

I pull my legs inside, draw the oiled canvas curtain across the floor hatch, and light a small, clay saucer of ray-tallow. The flame burns with a thick, blue-green edge in the hyper-dense air, casting long, wavering shadows across the arched ribs of the ancient craft.

From the wall of the massif, five miles away, comes the first night-call of a bull guttural. It begins as a floor-vibration—a heavy, sub-audible shudder that makes the tallow in the dish ripple—before rising into a glorious, brassy bellow that echoes off forty sandstone massifs in sequence, bouncing from face to face until the whole archipelago sounds like an organ played in a sunken cathedral.

I press my bare heels against the bronze floor plates, feeling the reply come back from the mountain on the other side of the void. 

The stone is warm. The lines are taut. The air holds everything up, thick and merciful, refusing to let anything fall fast enough to break. 

I reach out, touch the cold bronze carabiner at my hip to ensure the gate is seated, blow out the tallow flame, and let the slow, swinging drift of the hull rock me to sleep inside the amber.
