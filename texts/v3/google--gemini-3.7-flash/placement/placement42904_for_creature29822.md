I would not be on the surface, exposed to the pale glare and the screaming harmonics of the guy-wires; nor would I be tumbling blindly through the high-pressure boreholes with the Sleeve-Eels. 

I would be caught in the **kerf**.

To find me, you would have to locate a relief-seam—one of those monumental, hairline splits where the plateau has been deliberately slotted down to a depth of three hundred cubits to keep the seasonal expansion of the inner rings from buckling the machine districts into kindling. 

I would be an **interstitial shim**—a long, narrow, pressure-cast consciousness wedged into the tolerance between Ring 11,402 and Ring 11,403. 

***

At first, I might look like nothing more than a strip of cold-rolled phosphor bronze, forty feet long and three fingers thick, bedded in dark, semi-solid beef tallow and powdered graphite. But if you were to drop a plumb line into the seam, you would see that I have grain of my own, running perpendicular to the walls of the continent. 

My body is a laminate of dense, oil-quenched pearwood and thin leaves of hammered copper, vulcanized together under the sheer, lateral tonnage of the two rings as they grind past one another. I have no eyes, because light does not travel down a four-millimeter slot; but I have an excruciatingly precise sense of *clearance*. 

I know the continent entirely through the language of **runout, shear, and galling.**

* **To my left flank** is the wall of the older ring: compressed, dark as anthracite, laid down during three centuries of cold, mineral-rich drought when the ironwood grew at the rate of a thumbnail per decade. It is hard enough to turn a lathe-chisel; it rings like an anvil when the stamp-mills strike miles away.
* **To my right flank** is the younger ring: open-grained, vascular, still slightly moist with pressurized pinene and sour sap, vibrating with a dull, wet shudder every time the xylem-boilers cycle their pistons.

I am what keeps these two eras from seizing together. 

If the air thins and the temperature drops by half a degree, the younger ring shrinks faster than the old; the gap widens by twelve microns, and I relax, feeling the cold condensation-tides trickle down my copper laminations like icy oil. If a district furnace overheats fifty miles to the north, the floor expands, the gap closes, and the two faces of heartwood clamp against me with a force that would powder granite.

When that happens, my tallow melts. The amber pitch in my joints liquefies, and I begin to slide.

***

My movement is not a walk; it is an infinitesimal, lubricated migration. 

I move along the circumference of the world, pushed forward by the differential rotation of the rings. When the great reciprocating push-rods clatter overhead, the shockwave travels down through the end-grain and strikes my top edge like a hammer blow. I absorb the impact, convert it into lateral shear, and creep forward—one thousandth of an inch per stroke—scraping the crystallized resin from the walls of the gap with my brass-clad runners.

I am the ear that listens to the phonograph needle.

Because the entire plateau is a record cut in compressed cellulose, to sit between two rings is to hear two different centuries speaking simultaneously. Through my left flank, I feel the memory of a lightning-fire that scorched the canopy eight hundred years before the first burl-furnace was hollowed out; through my right flank, I feel the steady, rhythmic pulse of District Four’s master flywheel turning at twenty-two revolutions per minute.

Sometimes, the *Draftsman* passes overhead. 

I know it not because I see its sixty-foot legs of black timber, but because its immense counterweight shifts the local grain of the wood. The seam tilts. The daylight above the slot disappears, cut off by the shadow of its jointed calipers. The high harmonic wire above me goes dead for four seconds as the creature clamps its forelimbs down to harvest the friction-heat. In that momentary silence—when the shrieking of the sky is muffled—I can hear the micro-fractures forming in the deep heartwood three kilometers beneath my feet: dry, clean, perfect crystalline breaks that sound like the snapping of dry bone in an empty hall.

***

I do not wish for the open plains. The open air is too loose, too vague; it lacks the discipline of four thousand pounds per square inch. Out there, perspective stretches and snaps; a thing is thirty miles away until it is on top of you, and noon can be snuffed out by crossing a growth-line.

Down here, in the clearance, there is only the absolute honesty of the fit. 

I am wedged where the world’s torque is greatest. My thoughts are long, slow calculations of thermal expansion; my breath is the thin film of graphite paste that I extrude from my lower edges to keep the seam slick; and my peace is the deep, deafening hum of a continent turning its own gears, holding me so tightly against its ancient heart that not even a single ray of the cold white sun can find the space to slip between us.
