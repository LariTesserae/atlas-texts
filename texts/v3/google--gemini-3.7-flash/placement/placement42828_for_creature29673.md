I would not be in the open swells, where the Oilers graze the slime-films until their contours blur into the peat. Nor would I be suspended in the milky sky-pools, light as a vellum-skate, existing purely as an intake of dissolved spores. 

I would be found in the narrow, bruised-purple shadow where a root-arch thick as a ship’s belly has lifted a rounded boulder of grey granite three spans above the peat-floor.

I am one of the Silt-Born, but one who has grown very quiet, even for our kind. 

My back is wedged into the concave hollow where the living wood meets the dead stone. If you were to pass your hand over my shoulder, you would have trouble telling where the wet slate of my hide ends and the cool, dense mineral begins: both are slick with the same greasy film of liverwort, both are bathed in the continuous, tepid trickle of condensation dripping from the arch overhead. 

My legs are long and heavy, settled deep into the spongy cushion of decayed ferns below. They have not moved since the last long amber-shift of the season. Fiber has begun to throw tiny, hair-like anchor-threads across the tops of my wide feet, stitching me gently to the floor. I let it. It is comfortable, like being tucked into a warm, saturated bed that breathes with you.

In my lap lies a curved scrape-chisel fashioned from the thick, calcified radula of a timber-snail. It has taken me three years to wear down the cutting edge by half an inch, working with agonizing slowness to peel back the encroaching wood-rind where it tries to swallow the granite. 

The wood is always trying to swallow it. The root secretes its tea-colored resin, warm and sweet-smelling, creeping by fractions of a millimeter each week across the stone’s flank. The tree wants to digest this impossible thing—this cold, hard intruder that does not give, does not rot, and does not breathe. 

My purpose is simply to stay between them.

Every few days—or what would be days, measured by the slow brightening of the jade fog into amber and back into bruised violet—I lift the chisel. I pare away a curl of soft, pale cambium from the stone’s lip. The tree sighs a drop of sap onto my wrist, where it cools into a clear, sticky ring. My skin carries hundreds of these amber rings; they have fused with the blue symbiotic lichen tracing my forearms, making my flesh smolder with a muffled, greenish-gold lantern-glow when the twilight settles.

Why do I stay here? Because the world is too soft. 

Everywhere else, beneath the soles of your feet, the world yields. You press down, and it sinks. You speak, and the moss swallows your hum before it can travel three paces. You die, and the floor drinks your pulp in a month, lifting your ghost into the canopy as a knot of amber. Everything here is an endless, lukewarm circle of water becoming leaf, leaf becoming mire, mire becoming root. 

Only the stone refuses.

When I press the back of my skull against this boulder, my thoughts stop drifting into the mist. The stone has an unyielding, ancient chill that does not leave it, even in this muggy bath. It does not drink me. It does not ask me to soften. It is a single, stubborn *No* spoken against ten million years of rot.

Sometimes, when a distant peat-pocket collapses miles away and the floor shudders with that low, heavy thrum, I draw the air deep into my throat. I drop my chin toward my sternum and let loose a low chest-hum—a thick, resonant vibration that passes through my ribs and strikes the rock. 

The wood around me hums back, eager to vibrate, eager to pass the song along to the next root-arch. 

But the stone holds the sound. It absorbs the hum into its dense, silent core and does not echo it. It keeps the vibration safe inside its iron-heavy dark, where the rain cannot reach it, where the moss can never grow, and where the sky can never dissolve it into mist.

I will stay here until the sap finally overtakes my hands, gluing the chisel to the rock and my ribs to the bark. And even then, centuries from now, when the root has lifted both of us twenty fathoms into the amber-lit canopy, my bones will still be there—not bone at all, but slate and amber—keeping the stone company in the high, silent damp.
