I would not be in the high, airy lofts where the kelp-timbers swell toward the light, nor would I be out in the blinding sulfur-blind canyons of the plume-worms. 

I would be in the middle tiers, where the city forgets that it was made by hands and begins to believe it is a reef.

Here, forty spans above the jagged obsidian floor, the current barely turns over twice in a moon. The water has the density of warm broth, heavy with powdered quartz and dissolved iron. If I let go of the mooring with my feet, I do not sink; I simply hang, suspended by the gentle lift of my chest and the fat behind my ribs, tilting five degrees toward whatever chimney is breathing hardest beneath the terrace.

***

At first, you might not distinguish me from the wicker. 

My skin has taken the gray-green tint of the liverwort that coats the frame-timbers. Across the heels of my hands and along the underside of my forearms, the horn-pads have grown thick, grooved, and yellowed like boiled hoof—scored by twenty seasons of flint-edges and the sharp, razor-fractures of fresh volcanic glass. I am long in the spine, even for our kind, and my branchial slits do not snap shut with the sharp, quick appetite of the youth who harvest the siphonophore bells; they open and draw with a slow, accordion sigh, tasting the iron-content of the draft to know which seam of the mountain has cracked open in the dark below.

I am an *Anchor-Listener*.

My work is not the grand weaving of communal halls, nor the heavy goading of the pavement-skates across the lava shelves. My work is the tension. 

A city hung in water does not fall to ruin by collapsing downward; it tears itself apart upward, straining against its stones until a line parts, a basket-cage snaps its stays, and the whole dwelling drifts toward the dead, thin water of the upper sky where the gas-bladders burst from lack of pressure. Everything here wants to rise. The timber wants to float; the liverwort gas-bladders pull toward the sun; even our own sleeping bodies must be tucked under a lattice strap so we do not drift into the guy-lines while our minds wander.

To keep a thousand families suspended in the green dusk requires that the weight below precisely matches the yearning above.

***

My fingers—elongated, double-jointed, tipped with blunt, insensitive spade-nails—are hooked into a six-ply cable of cured sea-vine. 

I don't look at the rope. My eyes, large and lensed with clear amber gelatin, are half-shuttered against the gray silt-snow drifting down through the emerald gloom. Sight is a clumsy tool at twenty cubits anyway; it only shows you the green ghost of a wicker tower or the slow, rhythmic flash of a lantern-polyp swinging in a doorway.

Instead, I press my cheek against the damp, swell-tightened fiber.

I listen with the lateral line that runs from behind my gill-crests down to the small of my flank. Through the vine, the whole valley speaks in a language of pull and shear:

*Thrumm.* That is the main mooring of the Fourth Terrace, three hundred paces south. It is healthy. It carries the weight of twelve sleeping cages and three drying racks of skate-leather. The tone is a steady, low C-sharp, vibrating at the pitch of a resting skate’s heart.

*Tick... clack... tick.* That is the basalt ledge beneath the western chimney settling. The glass is cooling too fast where the cold seep meets the vent-water. A hairline fracture is creeping through the shelf where an anchor-stone sits. If it shears, fifty spans of braided vine will go slack in a single heartbeat, and the upper tiers will heel over like a torn net.

I reach into the hide pouch slung across my pelvis and draw out an adze of black volcanic glass, lashed to the jawbone of a skate with boiled sinew. I do not strike the rock; I tap it. *Tink. Tink-tink.*

The sound runs down through the water, sharp and bright. 

Two tiers below, an answer comes back—not sound, but a heavy, sub-audible pressure wave that thuds against the sacs in my throat. *Understood.* It is the young ones with the goads, already moving a slate-skate toward the fault to wedge it with ballast before the stone can split.

***

Around my shoulders, the Ash-Menders crawl with total indifference to my presence. 

They are cool and light—sixteen porcelain legs picking their way across my collarbone, their blind, white carapaces bumping gently against my jaw. One of them pauses at a frayed splice near my left wrist, its delicate mouthparts working over the bruised fibers. It tastes the gray ash clinging to the hemp, draws it in, and exudes behind itself that thick, pearlescent paste that smells faintly of lime and crushed shell. 

By tomorrow, the place where the rope was weak will be as hard as slate, welded forever by the mender’s digestion. Sometimes they mistake the thick horn-pads on my palms for dead fiber and try to cement my fingers to the lattice while I rest. I have to flex my hands every few hours, cracking their fresh mortar with a series of small, chalky snaps, just to remind them that I am among the living.

***

When the shift is done, I do not swim upward to the noisy communal cages where the children flicker their fingers in quick gossip and the lantern-polyps are fed with vent-slime. 

I descend ten spans, into the quiet space between the lowest floor-basket and the raw, black needles of the bottom. 

Here, the water is warmer—almost the temperature of a nursing mother's pouch. The mineral plumes rise around me like pillars of rippling crystal, distorting the green light into braids of gold and bronze. 

I find my resting knot: an old anchor-stone, round and heavy with iron, wrapped so deeply in creeping liverwort that the stone has vanished beneath a pelt of golden-brown velvet. Underneath that velvet lie the ribs of my grandfather's sister, bound here seventy winters ago to hold down the southern corner of the weaver-hall. 

The plants have made her stone; the stone has made her city.

I hook one splayed, callused foot into the guy-line that springs from her breastbone. I let my limbs loosen into the thick, warm broth, feeling the gentle convective drag pull my hair toward the far-off surface I will never see and have no name for. 

Inside my throat, my air-sacs tighten and release, sending a low, wordless hum into the root-mat—a single, sustained pulse that says: *The lines are holding. The tension is true. We are still anchored to the mountain.*

And from the deep, fractured obsidian beneath us, through twenty fathoms of warm, silent green, the stone ticks softly in reply.
