I would not begin with a face. In the Fracture, a face is an opening—a vulnerability through which hot sap enters or soft tissue is torn. 

To find myself here, I have to look first for where the noise changes from a strike against the skin into a frequency carried inside the skeleton. 

At first, the impulse is to imagine being the predator at the apex—the Pale Haunter suspended in the leathery roof, living by thermal intuition and elastic momentum. But that existence is too thin, too strung out along the wire of starvation. A Haunter is only a reflex with teeth, terrified of its own dangling limbs, doomed the first time a tendon frays or an anchor-vine rots under steam. 

Nor could I be the Gristle-Plow. There is an awful majesty to being an engine of pure bulk, but its mind is only a furnace stoked with boiling pulp, its life an unsteered slide toward the moment its grease fails to shed a barb and the floor claims its hooves.

No. I would be wedged where the violence meets the stone. 

I find myself not in the surge of the Floor, nor in the black tangle of the roof, but in the **interlocking margins of the basalt scarp**—four body-lengths above the churning shale, tucked into a fault-line so tight that my shoulder blades scrape the ceiling every time my lungs draw in the sulfur-thick air.

***

### What I Am

I am one of the Knappers, but I am old—which in the Fracture means I have survived perhaps three thousand rotations of the vents, long enough for my body to have taken the mineral shape of the shelf I occupy.

I am hunched, broad, and built like an anvil. My skin is no longer pliable; it has become a thick, horn-plated rind, the color of wet iron, scored with thousands of white, healed fissures from flying flakes of obsidian. My knees and elbows are swollen into dense knots of bone that lock into place, allowing me to brace against stone walls for hours without muscular fatigue. My eyes—slit-pupils shielded by thick amber membranes—rarely open wide. In this dark, they are only useful for catching the sudden flare of a dying Haunter’s lantern or the blue spark of crushed siphon-worms.

My real organ of sight is my lower jaw.

It is overgrown, heavy-boned, and entirely devoid of hair or soft lip. The underside of my chin is flattened into a smooth, calloused pad of keratin. When I press it against the basalt shelf, the entire Fracture enters me as a three-dimensional map of force:

*   I feel the high, brittle buzzing two miles north: a swarm of Slate-Backs boiling out of a fresh rock-collapse.
*   I feel the deep, syncopated *thump-thump-thump* of a herd of Plows striking a deadfall chute, their heavy heads drumming the stone as they suffocate.
*   I feel the fine, hair-like shudder of the cliff itself—the way the basalt groans under the relentless thermal expansion from the magma reservoirs deep beneath our heels.

***

### The Work of the Seam

I am the **Anchor** of my hunting clutch. 

While the younger Knappers—leaner, quicker, their hides still flexible enough to sprint across the razor-scree—crouch at the lip of the diversion chutes with their lever-poles, I remain anchored in the throat of the seam. 

Between my braced legs rests a slab of high-grade, black obsidian, six feet long and four inches thick, dragged from a cooling fissure three cycles ago. My hands do not hold weapons; they hold **striking-hammers**—heavy spheres of river-iron basalt bound to my forearms with braided Plow-gut.

I do not hunt by chasing. I hunt by *releasing*.

When the jaw against the stone tells me the herd has reached the narrowing of the gorge—when the frequency of their panicked mass-bellows reaches the pitch that vibrates the cartilage in my throat—I strike. 

*Clang.*

A heavy, deliberate blow against the obsidian core. The shockwave does not disperse into the air; the air is too loud, too thick. The strike travels *downward*, directly through the bedrock, arriving at the feet of the clutch fifty yards below like an electric jolt. 

*One strike:* Brace the levers.  
*Two strikes:* Drop the deadfall.  
*Three rapid, crushing rebounds:* Abort. The Haunters are dropping early; the air is turning white-hot with their descent.

***

### The Return

When the work is done—when the meat is packed into gut-pouches and the three-minute limit begins to expire—the clutch retreats up the wall. They do not look down at the writhing carpet of scuttlers already dissolving the bones of the kill. They climb with split-rib picks, their breath coming in whistling, ragged gasps.

I haul them up one by one, catching their wrists in hands so deeply calloused they feel like wood. We pull back into the chimney-vent, where the air shifts from the foul, grease-choked stench of the floor to the dry, scalding draft of the updraft.

Here, in the chimney, we lash ourselves together. 

We do not have names, because we do not have words. But as we press together in the vertical dark, rib-to-rib, our skin sweating yellow grease against the warm basalt, I press my forehead against the shoulder of the youngest hunter. Through the contact of bone against bone, I let the vibration of the mountain pass between us: the steady, low hum of the young rock settling, the frantic churning of the life below that will never catch us, and the rhythmic, iron pulse of the vents breathing out into the dark.

In a world made entirely of friction, I am the stone that does not yield.
