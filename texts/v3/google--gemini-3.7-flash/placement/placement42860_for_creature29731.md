At first, there is no need to decide what is body and what is basin. 

There is only the gradient: the heavy, iron-thick warmth rising from below, meeting the thin, golden lightness descending from above. There is the razor grain of young granite against one flank—stone so freshly broken into the world that its microscopic ridges have not yet shed their grit—and the supple, yielding drag of a vascular stem against the other.

I would not be in the open water. The pleurals have that drift, their cartilage sheets turning in forty-year arcs around the spires, untethered and unremembering. Nor would I be spread thin across the sky’s floor like the sieve-mats, drinking raw light and bubble-breath. 

I find myself lower, in the zone of hinge and tension: where the skiff-sized fronds anchor into the vertical fault-scarps.

Here, a plant’s stem—thick as a ship’s mast and taut with sap—wedges itself into a granite fissure so narrow that the rock bites deep into the living pulp. Where stone and wood meet under the continuous, gentle pull of the convective currents, there is a pressure point. The rock does not yield; the wood does not snap. Between them, life gathers to cushion the friction.

That is where I begin to take form.

I am not singular, but I am continuous. My body is a sprawling, amber-colored meniscus of dense collagen and vitreous silica, poured like warm honey into the crevice between the dark basalt and the root-node of a great fern-ribbon. 

Part of me has calcified, drinking the dissolved iron that seeps from the tectonic seam, growing brittle and heavy to lock itself against the granite’s knife-edge. But where I spill out into the open water, where the light turns the warm green of sunlit moss, I thin into overlapping, fan-like membranes—flexible as horn, ribbed with micro-ducts of living glass.

I do not walk like the lace-striders, though they know me. When their glass-tipped limbs descend the timber-stem, they step onto the upper curves of my mantle without hesitating. They feel the slight, rhythmic pulsing of my capillary channels—which draw the cold mountain dust they sweep from the fronds and dissolve it in my slow, acidic mucus—and in return, they leave behind the lipid granules scraped from the plant’s nodes. We do not barter; we are simply adjacent parts of the same long digestion.

I am an acoustic plate for the basin.

Because I am wedged tight between stone and timber, every dynamic of this high world passes through me as vibration. When a lithic bell three hundred meters down parts its feldspar seam to release its violet plume, the grinding quartz sends a clear, high ring through the basalt spine of the mountain; it strikes my mineral roots like a hammer against an anvil. 

When the afternoon light tilts, amber and sharp, and the vast fronds lean three centimeters to the west in the shifting thermal plume, the wood groans against the stone—a deep, wooden purr that resonates through my horn-plates. 

And constantly, without pause, there is the ticking: millions of oxygen pearls detaching from the green undersides of the leaves, rising past my outer frills, their tiny impacts sounding like rain falling upward through glass.

I have no eyes to see the basin, but I know its geometry by temperature and resonance. I know when a pleural is passing twenty meters out because its convective wake cools the water column by a fraction of a millikelvin, causing the outer edges of my silica ribs to contract slightly, curling inward toward the warmth of the fault. I know when the high sun is directly overhead because the sap within the great stem turns alkaline, rushing upward with a faint, carbonated hiss.

There is no hunger that feels like lack. Hunger here is merely the sensation of permeability—the slow, continuous osmose of silicates from the rock below and sunlight-sugars washing down from the canopy above. 

I do not move from this seam. In two hundred years, my horn-plates will have grown three handbreadths broader, shading another generation of liverworts in the crevasse beneath. In five hundred years, as the basin’s young stone settles a millimeter deeper into the warm mantle of the earth, the fissure will tighten, and my mineralized core will compress, becoming part of the mountain's permanent skeleton.

Here, in the warm, sun-struck silence between the sharp stone and the breathing timber, I am the joint that keeps the forest from tearing itself away from the rock. I listen to the earth cool and the bubbles rise. Nothing pursues me; nothing is waiting. The light turns to amber; the water lifts; the mountain holds.
