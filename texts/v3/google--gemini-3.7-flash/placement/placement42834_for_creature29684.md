It begins in the jaw.

Not with a thought, but with the cold, flat push of the boxwood ball against the hinge where the cheek meets the skull. 

If you ask what I am before the rod touches the iron, there is not much to tell. A shape folded into the dry flue-crevice behind Boiler Two; a pair of pale, wide soles hanging over the edge of an oiled hemp sling; skin that smells perpetually of boiled suet and stale well-water. In the dry pocket, where the stone is ninety-eight degrees and the lime dust drifts like flour, I am almost nothing. A lung that rises and falls out of time with the house. An idle knuckle picking a crust of salt from an ankle crease.

The self only arrives when the rod goes down.

I find myself on the mid-level staging, just above the second aerator tier, where the mist is thick enough to bead on the fine hairs of the forearm in three seconds flat. The iron grating here is always greased with a film of brown diatoms; you learn to walk not by lifting the feet, but by sliding the pale pads of the soles along the diamond bars, feeling for the weld-knots that have held since the century turned. 

I carry the third rod—the long one, turned from cold-rolled spindle steel with the boxwood knob bored out and seated on a lead shank to take the harshness off the high chatter. 

I am the one who listens for the third beam’s intake clearance. 

The air around me is a vast, dead gray. People imagine deafness as a stillness, but here it is simply an erasure of the middle: the wet slapping of the ferns, the drumming of the runoff, the human voice—all of that is wiped away, cancelled by the deadened drum inside the head. In the air, the court is mute. 

Then I bring the tip of the rod down onto the valve-chest flange of the southern pump.

The world does not open; it *slams* into the bone behind my left molar with the weight of four hundred tons of submerged river.

Through the marrow, the court is not green or mossy. It is bright, shrieking brass and dark, oil-cushioned basalt. Through the teeth, I can feel the exact condition of the lower leather packing twelve feet below the floor plates—a soft, wet *whuff-shuck* that has three more months of work in it before the grit tears the skirt. I can feel the middle eccentric: it has a slight flat spot on the follower, no thicker than a fingernail, that arrives every seven seconds like a wooden mallet tapped against the base of my skull. *Tock. Tock. Tock.*

A slug, blind and fat on sheep-dip, is grazing three inches from where my iron tip rests. Its pale mantle quivers with each exhaust pulse. We are both leaning into the same warmth. It takes the fat through its foot; I take the health of the wrist-pin through my zygomatic arch. There is very little difference between us in the seventh second of the stroke, when the steam cushions the reversal and the whole bank of cast iron sighs together in the dark.

I move down the ladder to the weir-sill. 

The carp are there, under the fourth step. I don't see them with my eyes—the light through the duckweed is only green syrup—but when I rest my bare heel on the cast-iron stringer, their feeding registers as a faint, irregular tickling in the tendons of my instep. They are stripping the cabbage-rot from the intake cage. When they hit the wire with their blunt snouts, it rings through the ladder like a finger dragged across a slack wire. 

I reach into the pocket of my canvas skirt, take a ball of tallow mixed with pulverized bone-meal, and pack it into the open grease-well above the crosshead slipper. The bronze is hot—not burning, but the heat of a horse’s flank after a six-mile pull. As I press the yellow lard home with the heel of my thumb, the slipper slides down its guide, taking half the lump with it, drawing it into a clear, golden film that smells of slaughterhouses and wet slate. 

I do not look at the sky through the high, rot-pocked monitors. The daylight means nothing down here; it only changes the color of the mold on the brickwork from tobacco-yellow to pond-green. 

What matters is the balance between the water entering the stepped pans and the water leaving through the drown-ways. If the pans take too much, the iron rings too sharp—a high, tinny *pinking* that rattles the crowns of the back teeth. If the river drops, the stroke grows dull and long, like someone thumping a sack of wet grain against the foundation stones.

I am the thing that stays between those two notes. 

A minder does not fix the machine. You cannot fix four hundred tons of iron that has grown fifty miles of root-gaskets into its joints; you would tear the walls down trying to turn a single frozen stud. You only attend the tolerances. You give the eccentric its suet before the bronze tears itself; you scrape the moss away from the pressure-telltales so the counterweights don't bind on a fern-frond; you watch the underside of the aerator pan when you step across the catwalk.

Just now, the fifth tier is quiet. The crickets are clustered thick along the lip, a dark, pulsing crust of legs and fiber, their bellies full of river-silt. They are not falling. The beam is rising on its fifth stroke, steady and slow, lifting twelve thousand gallons out of the dark without a cough.

I take the rod from my jaw. The world goes gray and thick again, heavy with the smell of wet fern and old iron. 

I wipe the boxwood ball on my thigh, slide my white, ridged feet along the wet grating toward the boiler flues, and climb back into the dry heat to wait out the next thousand strokes.
