I would not be above it. I would not have wings or eyes, because up there the air is thin enough to peel the warmth out of blood before it can reach a thought, and there is nothing to look at that cannot be felt more truthfully through bone.

At first, there is only the register of weight.

A long, low thrum—not heard, because the slate sky swallows the top of every wave, but accepted through the split plate of the lower jaw where it rests against the wet gravel. It is a frequency of roughly four cycles per second: the stroke of a bronze crosshead sliding through its tallow-bath inside the machine’s undercarriage, three hundred paces ahead. 

I am in the press.

I do not begin as an individual thought, but as a displacement of mass. Around me, the herd is not a collection of neighbors; it is a single, eight-thousand-ton mattress of boiled wool, tallow, and pressurized muscle, moving in a continuous, viscous creep toward the cast iron. 

My hide is stiff on the left flank—the outer flank, facing the wind. There, the rime has built up into an inch-thick carapace of frozen grease and stone-dust. It makes a dry, splintering sound against the ribs of the animal beside me as we shift. But on my right flank, three ribs deep into the huddle, the pelt is soft, wet, and steaming with the dark, hydrocarbon-heavy sweat that our bodies make when the heat begins to penetrate.

I am somewhere in the middle of the inward drift. It has taken nine days of wind to bring me from the frozen perimeter—where the mist tastes of bare quartz and the frost bites through the pad-calluses—to this middle tier where the air grows thick, yellow, and greasy with the exhaust of the secondary firebox.

My head is low, wedged into the hollow beneath the high shoulder of the beast ahead of me. My nostrils are slit-narrow, opening only on the downward heave of my chest. Inside my head, the sinus maze is cold at the lip, but deep behind the eye-plates it is hot and sour. I taste the machine before I reach it: the copper-lead tang of the sealing lichen, the scorched petroleum from the lower track pins, the lukewarm sulfur water dripping from the condensation gutters.

With every twenty paces the herd takes—a distance that takes half a daylight cycle to cross—the vibration changes in my teeth. 

The blue enamel of my lower incisors is worn smooth on the left blade and notched on the right from thirty years of plowing the shale down to the cold grease-seams. When I press my jaw into the crushed grit, the earth is not solid; it is a vibrating membrane stretched across iron plates buried miles below. Through the stone, I can feel the scuttlers churning in the thaw-trench fifty yards behind the track shoes, a faint, high-pitched scuffing like salt shaken into an iron pan.

Now the herd heaves forward three inches. A beast on the inner rim has overheated, its pelt charred dry, and it lurches outward, shoving its bulk into the layer ahead of me. 

The pressure transfers down the line. It catches me in the flank—a heavy, blind, rolling impact of six hundred pounds of wet muscle. I do not resist it; I turn with it, tucking my blunt snout under the belly of the animal to my right, finding the wet, hot channel where the steam from the machine’s belly-vent rolls out along the gravel.

Here, the heat is sharp. It smells of boiled iron and old oil. It softens the frozen tallow in my ears. 

For the next four days, I will be against the iron. 

I feel the approach of the boiler plate not as a sight, but as an immense wall of radiant gravity that dries the frost off my forehead-plates until the calloused cartilage begins to throb. When my shoulder finally makes contact with the five-inch rolled plate, there will be no give—only the absolute, trembling firmness of a thing that has been moving across this slate flat since the rocks were sharp.

I will press my cheek against the warm rivets. I will let the grease-heat soak through my skull, through the thick gray fat behind my temples, down into the great bellows of my lungs, until the cold air of the plateau feels like something belonging to another age, another world, far out beyond the wind.

And then, when the heat becomes a sickness—when the wool on my ribs begins to singe and the blue enamel of my teeth aches with the boiler's thrum—the outward drift will take me. The shivering ones behind me will push their snouts under my flanks, desperate for the metal, and I will yield. 

I will be squeezed slowly back out into the mist. Over two weeks, the frost will build upon my pelt again, flake by flake, layer by layer, sealing me in rime until I reach the edge of the world once more, blind and heavy and cold, waiting for the turn of the circle.
