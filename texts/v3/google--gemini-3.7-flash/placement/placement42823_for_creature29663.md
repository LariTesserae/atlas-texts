I would not be found on the high, wind-sheared parapets where the insect-nets strain the fog, nor down in the drowned lightlessness of the lowest conduits where the crabs graze on spore-fall. 

I would be in the middle height, in the throat of an east-facing stair-street where three tiers of housing lean together so closely that the sky above is reduced to a clean, wet ribbon of gray. 

At first, I am scarcely distinguishable from the wall itself. I am sitting on the third riser of a recessed doorway, knees drawn high toward my chest, my shingled cape of sedge-felt pulled forward over my shins. The cape is stiff with tallow and wax; where the condensation gathers along its overlapping tiers, it does not soak in, but beads into heavy, clear globes that roll from ridge to ridge, dropping in a steady, metered cadence onto the gutter stone between my feet. 

My breath is slow. The ribs beneath the felt are broad, thick-arched, expanding sideways rather than up, taking in the cold vapor until the lungs feel dense and cool, like wet sponges. There is no hurry in my pulse. The temperature of my hands, tucked into the dry, lanolin-warmed fleece of my sleeves, is lower than that of lowlanders, but my blood runs steady and thick.

In my right hand, resting against my thigh, is a flat scraper made of sheep-horn, its edge beveled by years of friction against wet granite. Tucked into the cord binding of my calf is a short striker of dense, oiled heartwood, no longer than my forearm.

I am an adjuster of the runnels—someone who tends the joints where the causeway runoff meets the domestic sluices. 

When the *fall* is deep and the hill-fog sits heavy on the city, as it does now, my work is mostly listening. The stone conducts sound far better than the air. With my back pressed flat against the rough, damp face of the party wall, I can feel the life of the masonry vibrating directly into the base of my skull. 

Three terraces above, a sluice is choked with an accumulation of windborne pollen and black liverwort; I know this because the water is not singing with its usual low, hollow suck, but is spilling over a lip with a dull, flat slap. Two terraces below, a household has drawn their internal cistern gate, the wood sliding into its slate groove with a shudder that travels fifty paces through the bedrock. Beneath my heels, inside the lightless weep-holes of the stairs, there is the faint, dry scratching of an isopod moving across the algae film.

I do not speak. My thoughts move with the same low, rhythmic resistance as the water slipping down the granite gutters. 

Then, the quality of the light shifts. 

The gray ribbon of sky between the rooflines does not turn blue; it turns blinding white. The cloud deck is thinning, dragging its belly across the upper ridges as the mountain wind accelerates. 

The *lift* has begun.

I stand. The movement is silent; the cross-hatched cordage bound to the soles of my feet grips the slick, black stone without a scuff. My shingled tunic sheds a sudden shower of gathered mist as I straighten, the water joining the runnel with a brief hiss. 

Around me, without a word being spoken, the alley begins to stir. Wooden doors pivot in their stone sockets. Other figures emerge onto the stepped parapets above and below, their dark, lanolin-sheened backs bowed over the drainage channels. 

I step to the lip of the stair, kneeling where the water from the upper terrace enters a shallow, carved basin. The morning frost was light, but there is a thin crust of black grit and rotting sedge-fiber gathering in the corner, threatening to back up the flow before the midday sun can bake it into a hard, cement-like crust. 

I take the horn scraper and set its curved edge against the granite. 

The sound is a low, satisfying *shhh-t*, shearing the silt from the rock in a single, clean stroke. The water catches the freed sediment, carrying it instantly downward into the narrow mouth of the drop-chute, where it will fall ten meters into the dark cistern weirs to feed the blind crabs. 

The sun strikes the wet stone of the plaza above, and instantly the steam begins to rise, a pale, fragrant smoke smelling of cold rain, crushed lichen, and ancient, unyielding mineral. 

Before moving to the next terrace, I take the wooden striker from my leg. I lean down, placing the flat of my palm against the corner lintel of the archway, and tap the striker twice against the stone—a soft, solid *thunk-thunk* that rings not in the air, but down through the sill, informing the scraper working two flights below that the channel is clear, the gradient is true, and the water is falling freely once again.
