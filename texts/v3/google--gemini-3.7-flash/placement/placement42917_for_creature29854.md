The first thing to understand is that there is no *elsewhere*. There is only the chamber, the weight of the thirty feet above, and the pitch of the current through the teeth of the intake grates.

At first, consciousness here does not feel like a body at all. It feels like a point of friction. 

It begins in the skull—or what serves as a skull—as a relentless, heavy vibration that never resolves into silence. It is the sixty-cycle thrum of the copper cables running in their iron troughs, felt not as sound, but as an itch deep in the jaw, a low-voltage simmer that keeps the blood thick and wakeful. To be here is to exist inside an instrument that is always being struck. When the distant turbine throttles up, the marrow registers the shift half a second before the water moves; when the counterweight drops in the wall, the impact travels up through the belly like a dull kick from an iron boot.

You would find me not in the open drift of the tea-colored water, where the pale minnows waste their strength fighting the standing waves, nor in the deep drifts of orange sludge along the railway ties where the crabs scrape their heavy meals. 

You would find me wedged into the throat of the secondary bypass—a cast-iron sleeve four feet across, bolted to the lower manifold forty inches above the floor plates. 

The valve gate inside this sleeve jammed forty years ago, stuck open at three-eighths of its travel. That frozen bronze wedge creates an eddy: a smooth, dead pocket of water in the lee of the high-velocity sluice. That is the bed. That is the territory.

My body has taken the shape of the void it fills. 

I am long, thick-shouldered, and sluggish with the heavy density of things that never touch the sun. My skin has long ceased to look like animal hide; it is tough as vulcanized rubber, slick with a thick, ropey grease that smells of stagnant silt and machine oil, sloughing off the abrasive bite of the suspended rust snow. Along my flanks, the lateral line is not a seam of scales but a deep, sensitive canal—an exposed nerve bed that drinks the architecture. 

Through that nerve, the room is entirely transparent. I do not need the jaundiced amber glow of the waterlogged luminaires; light is a blunt, clumsy medium that gets lost in the flocculent clouds. Instead, I feel the geometry in displacement. 

I feel the broad-backed carp drift past the overhead gantry sixty feet away—not by sight, but by the slow, heavy displacement of three barrels of water parting around its slate-colored flanks. I feel the crabs clambering over one another on the warm conduit casing, their jointed dactyls clicking against the iron like hail on a tin roof. I feel the exact moment the intake grates half a mile up-canal catch a mat of dead reeds: the draw alters by a fraction of an atmosphere, the pitch of the bypass hiss sharpens, and the current across my snout goes lean and sour.

My tail is curled into the dark spiral of the valve’s threaded stem, anchoring sixty pounds of cold, dense muscle against the draw. I do not fight the machine; I lean into its tolerances. When the five-minute surge comes tearing down from the southern intake, lifting the orange dust in lazy, billowing sheets and ringing the crane chain against the girder, I do not retreat into the pipe. I merely stiffen. I let the cold, heavy water cram itself down my gullet and wash out across the dark red comb of my gills, filtering the river’s spilled refuse—drowned beetles, shreds of water-lily root, the oily fat of dead dace—tasting the whole surface world as a diluted soup of debris.

I have swallowed so much iron over the years that my belly is weighted with it. My teeth are blunt pegs, stained the dark, metallic brown of old aggregate, suited for crushing the river snails that drift down the shaft and plaster themselves to the bronze flanges. 

There is no memory of open air. If there was ever a sky, it was only a brighter, thinner kind of water that could not support the weight of a body. 

Here, wedged between the rusted casting and the bronze seat, life is measured in the duty cycles of the pumps and the slow, geological shedding of the I-beams. The machine does not know I am inside its throat, steadying its pulse with my bulk, tasting its copper bleed, waiting in the cold, loud dark for the next pulse to ring the iron bell of the room.
