I would not be in the air, for the air demands a lightness that has forgotten the stone entirely; nor would I be among the hoppers, whose lives are pure compression and ballistic panic. 

I would be wedged into the lower hinge of the shelf, right where the floor’s polished granite curls outward into the lip—anchored in the crawlway beneath the westernmost shelter, where the stone is grooved by centuries of cable-drag and the draft hums directly through the marrow of the peak.

I am the one who tends the ironwood pegs and the tension-cables.

***

At first, sitting here in the amber gloom, you might mistake me for an outcropping of cured hide or a bundle of rattle-cane stalks lashed down against the gale. My coat is five layers of vellum-kite molt, stiff as lacquered horn, cross-stitched with copper-hair that has turned verdigris-black from sulfur smoke. The hem of it is pinned to the stone with lead weights so the updraft cannot peel me backward into the void. 

My knees do not bend with the wet ease of valley folk; they are wrapped in split hopper-carapaces that lock with a dry, wooden *tock* when I crouch. Inside the boots, my heels never lift. I have trained the weight of my skeleton to fall straight down into the three-inch iron studs hammered through the soles, biting the granite so hard that when the thermal pulses hit—those great, shuddering blasts that make the whole forest of comb-trees snap and ring like church bells—I do not slide. I merely ring with them.

Through the green tourmaline slit of my visor, the world is a study in friction. I do not look for color; I look for fray.

My hands are thick, grey-palmed, and stained with graphite. In my right grip is a short-hafted tension-chisel; looped over my left shoulder is a coil of braided copper-hair, heavy as lead and vibrating so intensely against my clavicle that it makes my back teeth ache in their sockets. 

I do not think in words that have vowels. You cannot keep a vowel in a place like this; the dry wind strips it off your tongue before it reaches your lips. My thought has the rhythm of the *file-crabs* meeting on the stone: a rapid, double-click of bone, a scrape of slate, a pause to measure the load, then another click. 

When I breathe, the charcoal-and-resin packing in my horn snout-mask warms up, filtering the glass-dust until the air tastes of struck matches and scorched flour. It is a good taste. It means the lung-linings are dry, calcifying smoothly, taking on the texture of cured vellum so they will not tear when the deep cello-drone of the brush rises an octave.

Every few hours, a file-crab scuttles over my foot. It does not scurry around me; it treats my iron-studded boot as a cold formation of rock, its eight horn-needles tapping a swift, metallic rhythm against my instep. Its magnetic keel hums against the granite, gathering a tiny fleece of charged copper-dust. Sometimes I reach down with a broad-bladed horn spatula and scrape the static-crust from its side to rub into the hinges of my knee-plates. The crab does not flinch; it merely whets its lateral edges against my scraper with an abrasive *snick*, takes a spark from my cuff, and skitters on toward the vibrating haystacks.

This is my craft: keeping the settlement strung to the tooth.

The rock here is alive with torque. The wind pushes the mountain; the comb-trees pull at their fissures; the great braided hawsers holding the huts creak under thousands of pounds of static lift. If a cable goes slack, it frays against the stone and explodes into a cloud of copper fluff within an hour. If it is drawn too tight, the piezoelectric charge builds until it shears the ironwood pegs off at the root with a sound like a cannon shot.

So I crawl. I crawl along the perimeter chasm where the wind screams through the teeth of the rock. I place my bare, resin-crusted thumb directly against the cables. 

I feel the note.

If a line hums in the low register of the copper mounds, it is healthy; it is drinking the wind and grounding it into the stone. If it begins to hiss like a comb-tree before a leaf-fracture—thin, sharp, rising toward a glass-whistle—I take the mallet from my belt. Two strikes against the bone wedge. The peg shifts a millimeter in its crevice. The pitch drops back into the chest-rattling drone of the mountain.

When thirst comes, it is not a craving for water—water is a terrifying thought here, something that would rot the sinew, soften the hooves, and make the tight parchment of the world sag into ruin. My thirst is a dull stiffness in the throat, a desire for thickness. 

I reach into the leather pouch at my hip, draw out a reed of hollow bone, and unstop a flask of tree-sap. It is dark as boiled amber, viscous and heavy. I draw a mouthful through the bone straw. It does not splash; it coats. It moves down the gullet like warm pitch, tasting of alum, dry resin, and iron-dust, sealing the mucous membranes against the draft, leaving my mouth tasting like the inside of a polished bell. Then I take a wafer of dried marrow-paste, snap it between my back molts, and let the sulfur grease melt slowly beneath my tongue, feeding the steady, slow-cranking engine of my bloodless heart.

I know where I will go when the joints finally seize. 

In the valley below, they say things soften when they die; they bloat, they run, they lose their shape and sink into mud. Here, we earn our geometry. 

When my knees will no longer take the ratchet, or when my respirators turn to solid chalk from a lifetime of silica, the others will not weep—they have no fluid for it. They will carry me thirty paces out to the extreme horn of the lip. They will strip away the green tourmaline visor so my dry eyes can look once into the vast, roaring sulfur sky, and they will lash my frame to a lattice of rattle-cane with three copper lines.

The furnace-draft will take my coat, then my skin, scouring away every soft imperfection until only the taut, resonant parchment remains, tuned perfectly to the pitch of the chasm. Within three turns of the amber sun, my ribs will be white as chiseled salt, dry as whale-bone, and ringing like an iron harp in the updraft.

They will come with their slate saws and take me down. They will carve my thigh-bones into wedges to hold the shelter against the north shear. They will take the sinew of my spine to string the molt-winches that dip into the abyss. 

I will not be gone. I will simply be holding the stone.
