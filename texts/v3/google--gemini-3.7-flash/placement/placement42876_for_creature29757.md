The sound is not something you hear; it is something you inhabit, like a deep pool of cold, pressurized brine. It fills the spaces between the ribs, wedges itself behind the breastbone, and sets the molar roots humming in their sockets. After a certain number of weeks, the memory of an unvibrated world rots away, replaced by the certainty that if the machine ever stopped, the basalt walls would lose their cohesion and collapse inward to fill the silence.

I find myself thirty feet above the spongy floor, out on the eastern shear, wedged into the angle where a three-foot lagged steam line punches through a steel saddle-bracket bolted to the rock. 

I am one of the pipe-menders—not quite the ground-tenders who walk the snowshoes over the false floors, but one of the solitary crawlers who lives along the gantry lines, where the iron is warm enough to sweat and the damp mineral wool rots into a gray, insulating paste. 

My body is mostly harness and ballast. Underneath three layers of oiled canvas and tallow-soaked wool, my skin has taken on the bleached, doughy consistency of the girdle-grubs. I wear the lead-weighted neck-corset; it bites into the clavicles with two heavy, gel-filled pads that intercept the low-frequency shudder before it can climb the spinal column and scramble the fluid in the inner ear. My helmet is an iron dome, gasketed tight to the breastplate. Inside it, there is only the thick, sour smell of my own re-breathed air, the click of my tongue against the roof of my mouth, and the enormous, subterranean *whuff-whuff-whuff* of the four-mile-distant intake pistons, conducted straight through the soles of my boots and the iron of the pipe into the marrow of my shins.

My work is slow, measured by the cooling of tallow and the drift of the cold rime. 

Where the felt-moth larvae burrow into the lagging to breed near the seams, they chew out long, wandering caverns, hollowing the insulation until the high-pressure steam within loses its heat to the rift’s permanent frost. I follow their galleries. With thick, three-fingered leather mittens, I haul sacks of shredded mineral wool and pitch up the vertical ladders, packing the voids, binding the split canvas with copper wire, and smoothing cold-setting petroleum bitumen over the wounds. 

The adult moths do not fly from me. They treat my arms as they treat the iron girders—as broad, stable surfaces that vibrate at an acceptable pitch. When I reach around a flange to tie off a wire, my knuckles brush against the thick, velvet nap of their folded wings. They are cold, heavy as lead sinkers, and so densely furred that when my glove grazes them, they don't flutter; they simply tighten their grip on the bolt-heads, their down absorbing the scrape of my tools like a blotter soaking up grease. Sometimes three or four of them will settle on the warm crown of my helmet, their wings spread flat against the iron, turning my head into a silent, furry nodule in the amber gloom.

From up here, looking out across the chasm, the sodium lamps look like small, yellow punctures in a wet slate roof. Beneath them, the moss floor rolls out in frozen, undulating carpets. I watch the ground-tenders make their crossings, small and black, clipped to their overhead steel lifelines like spiders sliding along a web. I can see the bow-wave of displaced mist their overshoes kick up, though I hear nothing of their boots—only the great, uniform engine-shudder that smooths every small noise into dust.

Occasionally, I drop a tool. A bronze wrench or a spool of wire slips from numb fingers. It falls into the gray-green cushion below without a sound. It does not hit the earth; it sinks into the moss three, four, five feet deep, down into the root-labyrinth where the blind myriapods crawl and the pulp-voles wedge their thick skulls through the cold humus. Within two days, the living wool will knit over the hole. Within a season, the brass will turn black with acid water, held suspended fifty feet above the true basalt bedrock, cradled in the damp weave of the valley.

I do not go down to the berths very often. The tunnels bored into the cliff for sleeping are too close, too lead-heavy; the air in them smells of wet boots and unwashed hair, and the men who stay down there too long develop a twitch in their eyelids that never settles. 

Instead, when my shift ends, I tether my harness double to the saddle-bracket behind the main exhaust manifold. The pipe here is hot—not scalding, but radiating a dull, animal heat through its double-wrapped lagging. I tuck my knees to my chest, pull the hood down until the sodium light is only a faint, orange smear at the rim of my visor, and press my forehead against the lagged steel. 

The vibration here is pure, undivided by the air. It passes into the helmet, through the bone of my brow, and sets up a heavy, rhythmic cadence in my thoughts. It is like sleeping against the ribs of a swimming whale. In this cold, vast split in the planet, where nothing has ever heard a bird or the snap of dry kindling, the roar is not noise at all. It is the pulse of the stone. It holds everything together—the moss, the moths, the iron, and the slow, thick blood in my arms—keeping us all pinned firmly to the bottom of the world.
