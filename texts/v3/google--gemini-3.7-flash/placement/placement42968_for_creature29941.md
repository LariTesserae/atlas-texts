At first, I would not be anything with a voice. 

I would be the point of contact where the horn scraper meets the shale shingle in the gray quarter-hour before the sun clears the massif. That particular friction—not stone on stone, but the softer, waxy bite of polished ram-horn shaving a skin of frost and mist into a bronze lip. The sound it makes is low and wet, a dull hum that travels down through the arm, through the heavy collar of the wool tunic, and seats itself directly in the collarbone.

If I look for myself in the wide light, I vanish into the geometry. The shelf is too old and too finished to need an observer; it does not ask to be witnessed. So I would find myself lower down, where the mass presses hardest against the mountain.

I am sitting on the third step of the alley that leads down to the central cistern, just where the shadow of an overhanging eave cuts the day in half. 

My back is against the dry-laid wall. Through three layers of ash-fulled wool and the thick grease of the felt, the chill of the megalith is steady—not sharp, but deep, the subterranean cold of a massif that has never held heat more than three inches into its skin. My knees are drawn up toward a barrel-wide chest that expands and falls with a rhythm so slow it would look like sleep to anyone from the valleys below. But the eyes are open, squinting through the tight fold of sun-creased lids, watching the shadow of the western parapet move across the pale flags like a sundial made of iron.

I would be the one whose hands know the bronze chisels when they are dull.

In the midday heat, when the air shivers over the empty streets and everyone has pulled the heavy leather across their doorways, I do not sleep. I sit in the threshold with a flat river-stone between my thighs, a stone brought up from the world below so many generations ago that its green silt-streaks are faded to slate. A skin of morning water sits beside me in a shallow bowl carved from dwarf pine. 

I do not speak to the stone, and I do not hurry the metal. It takes two hundred passes of the flat copper-alloy edge across the wet grit to take out the nick left by a stubborn grain of quartz. With each pull of the blade, a tiny ribbon of grey slurry collects at the stone’s heel. I scrape it away with a thumbnail that is thick, split at the corner, and perpetually stained with the dark tallow we use to seal the cistern baffles.

My tongue rests against the roof of my mouth, dry and still. If the person across the alley—the one who mends the carrying baskets from split sedge—looks up through the parting in their hide curtain, we do not nod. We do not exchange the small, unnecessary courtesies of crowded lowlands. I simply shift the chisel three inches to the left, showing the bright line of the newly dressed bevel in the glare. They lower their chin a fraction of an inch. The work is whole; the day is accounted for.

I have never seen the valley floor without its veil of blue distance. To me, the world below the four-thousand-pace drop is not a place where people walk, but a floor of purple fog from which the cold thermals rise at noon to lift the swifts. 

When dusk comes, my boots—rawhide stiffened with grease, their copper studs filed down to blunt nubs by fifty years of shale treads—make no sound as I stand. I take the hide cover, heavy and black with sheep-fat, and walk to the corner basin. The water inside is still as glass, reflecting only the pale horn of the mountain above us and the first cold star.

I drop the baffle. It lands with a soft, airtight *thud* on the stone rim.

I step inside, let the grease-rubbed curtain swing shut against the night, and sit down in the dark where the stone is quiet, dry, and entirely at rest.
