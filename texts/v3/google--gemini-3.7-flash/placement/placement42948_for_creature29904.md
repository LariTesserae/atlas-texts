At first, there is only the frequency.

Before there is a body, before there is a name or a shape to cast a shadow across the amber-colored grain, there is the ache in the teeth. It is the thirty-cycle thrum of twenty million shields striking fossilized cedar sixty leagues away, traveling through sixty miles of petrified vascular fiber to arrive beneath the soles of the feet as a continuous, boiling tremor.

If you stood flat-footed, the marrow in your shins would rattle loose in a season. So you learn not to stand. You learn to hang.

***

I find myself not down on the pale, sun-bleached marrow-floor where the blind grazers drag their chisel-heads, nor up in the highest arches where the Loom-Striders pull their vertical harps taut against the cobalt sky.

I am suspended in the middle air, in the long shadow of the Third Great Rib—a bridge of fossilized heartwood so wide that the noon sun takes two hours to cross its underside.

I live inside a teardrop of dried bident-vine, twelve paces across, tethered fifty yards above the floor by three braided cords of scarlet moss. My skin has the color and dry texture of cured sumac; my eyes are narrow slits beneath double lids, the inner membrane clouded like frosted amber to cut the violent, white-gold glare bouncing off the plains below.

I am a *Grain-Tuner*. 

My people do not write, and our voices are useless things—thin whistles eaten by the whine of the siphon-flies before they can clear the lip of the pod. But we have hands. My knuckles are capped with ground hollow-beetle horn, polished with pine pitch until they click like dry river stones.

When the wood groans under the midday heat, I do not look out. I press my forehead against the central suspension line—the thickest cord, braided forty summers ago from the red moss that drank my grandfather’s ribs dry—and I listen.

Through the dead man’s fiber, the health of the continent speaks:
*   A high, wet sizzle means a pressurized sap-vein three miles deep has sprung a fissure; the dusk-sweat will be heavy and hot, rich in turpentine.
*   A dull, iron-hard *thud-crack* that repeats every sixty breaths means a Stilt-Grazer has died on its feet, its jaw locked into the cambium, its seventy-foot bone-stilts ringing like struck anvils under the drum-beetle roar.
*   A sudden dropping of the pitch—a silence that opens up like a sinkhole in the roaring air—means the shelf is holding its breath. That is when we check the knots.

***

My days are measured by the drift of the shadows and the thickening of the resin in the crucibles.

In the morning, when the air is still cold enough to smell of old iron and frozen cellulose, I lean out from the hatch of the cradle with a long-arm scoop of boiled horn. I reach down toward the nearest burl-well, scraping up the crust of dry, wind-blown wood flour that the drum-beetles have beaten out of the micro-fissures. It is gray, powdery, and tastes of alkaline dust; mixed with a drop of copper-water from yesterday’s dusk-catch, it makes a bitter paste that coats the belly and stops the vibration from churning the stomach into sickness.

At midday, I work the chitin. 

I take the serrated jaw of a burrowing beetle, clamp it between my bare feet, and hone its teeth with a rasp of petrified grain. You cannot rush this. If the friction gets too hot, the chitin scorches and loses its temper, turning brittle as glass. You must spit on the edge—spit that tastes permanently of pine oil and metal—and draw the stone slowly, keeping time with the beetles below. *Stroke on the drum. Stroke on the rest. Stroke on the drum.*

Around me, the pod sways on its three red ropes. The air smells of sun-cured hay, hot sap, and the sweet, dizzying musk of millions of singing carapaces drifting on the thermals.

***

When twilight comes, the sky does not bruise softly; it turns a hard, deep indigo, like a kiln door shutting.

The roar of the beetles shifts key. The hammers slow, their synchronized rhythm breaking apart into a stuttering, rolling thunder as the heat drains from the surface wood. 

Then comes the sigh. 

It is a sound like a drowned city surfacing—a colossal, wet suction that runs along the floor from horizon to horizon. The vascular walls contract under the cooling air, and the bark fissures weep.

I descend.

I do not touch the wood with my heels. I drop down a knotted whip-tendril, my toes finding the smooth, slick ridges of the lower knothole where the basin has formed. The sap is already boiling into the hollow, thick and steaming, smelling of bruised juniper and burnt copper.

A pulse-eel’s blunt head breaches the surface—armored in plates of pale, translucent xylem, its blind mouth pulsing in the steam. It looks like a knot of living wood carved by a careless knife. It does not care about me; it turns once in the scalding pool, casts its cloud of milky seed into the amber fluid, and slides back into the dark hydraulic throat of the shelf before the fissure can close.

I dip my pitch-glass bowl into the cooling rim of the basin, taking only the top-water, the thin layer where the condensation has separated from the heavy resin. 

It is lukewarm. It burns the back of the throat with the sting of raw tannin. It tastes like the blood of something that has been dying for ten million years without ever growing cold.

***

Back in the cradle, as the sky goes completely black and the siphon-flies rise toward the high arches like glowing sparks of ruby dust, I tap the knuckle-horns against the family rope.

*Three quick strikes: The water is caught.*
*Two slow rolls: The line holds true.*
*One long pause: The shelf still breathes.*

From five miles to the north, suspended beneath the curve of the same great rib, comes the faint, dry *tock-tock-tock* of another pod answering through the wood. 

I pull the lichen-cloth across the hatch, lie down in the curved basket of the pod, and feel the slow, swinging pendulum of the air. Below, the floor vibrates on, sixty miles of solid ancient grain singing its perpetual, ungrounded song into the dark. 

I close my inner eyelids. The dead hold the ropes. The world groans in its sleep, and does not fall.
