I would not be on the surface, where the light is amber and the pans of red slip tear under the steam. Nor would I be down in the deep draft-trenches, where the Stampers carry the weight of entire terraces on their slag-crusted heels. 

I would be inside the walls. 

Deep within the green meat of a four-mile ramp or the sodden flank of a rising cylinder, where the clay is neither water nor stone, but *leather-hard*—that fleeting, dense, cheese-firm state where the mass holds its own shape, yet yields cleanly to an edge.

***

At first, I might seem like nothing more than a fault-line or a breathing seam: a narrow, traveling void moving through the dark core of the architecture. But give it form, let the friction gather substance, and I become something long, heavy, and low-slung—a **Corer**, or what the turning-wheel leaves behind when it bites too deep.

My body is a thick, flexible ribbon of compressed silt and oil, eight or ten fathoms in length, flat as a leather drive-belt and edged with flexible plates of slate and hardened boxwood. My snout is an open, unblinking loop—a heavy stirrup of cold ironwood held rigid by braided gut. 

I have no eyes. Inside the solid clay there is no light to gather, only gradients of resistance, suction, and heat. 

I live by hollowing. 

A wall built four hundred bricks thick cannot endure this world. If the interior remains solid and wet while the subterranean flues breathe their white woodsmoke below, the trapped moisture will turn to high-pressure steam; the greenware will blow outward from within, shattering miles of masonry into flying red shrapnel. 

So I swim through the density. I drive my loop-snout forward into the cold, thumb-pressed dark, paring away long, curling shavings of excess body-clay. I eat the resistance. I swallow the damp grog and pass it behind me as a continuous, hollow fluting—ribbed, arched tunnels that allow the subterranean draft to circulate through the thickest ramparts without bringing them down.

Where I pass, the wall does not collapse; it becomes a lung.

***

My existence is measured entirely in the narrow, migrating margin between two deaths. 

If I bore too close to the outer face, where the Skimmers graze and the red slip pools knee-deep, the clay softens into liquid porridge; my ribs lose their bite, my tunnels cave in behind me with a wet, flatulent sigh, and I am dissolved back into the slurry, losing all memory of edge and direction.

If I linger too long near the sub-floor, or if a Stamper shifts a flue and directs a blast of dry, ash-white draft through my corridor, the wall around me begins to *ring*. The clay tightens, shrinks, and vitrifies. Its yielding meat turns to glassy, razor-sharp stoneware in an hour. If I am caught, I am pinned forever in the heart of the cured brick: my wooden ribs burned to charcoal, my silt-ribbon calcified, preserved as an empty, coiled pocket inside a ramp that will stand until the next great folding of the earth.

And so I am never still. I crawl along the isotherm. I follow the leather-hardness as it creeps through the structures.

When a mile-long wall begins to bulge and sigh—feeling that terrible, wet panic before it collapses into the lake—I feel the change in grain instantly. The pressure slackens; the clay goes soft, greasy, and sour. I reverse my plates, carving hard, upward spirals toward the higher tiers, riding the drying-front into the unfinished ziggurats.

***

I know the other beings only through the masonry.

Above me, through thirty feet of green mudbrick, I can hear the dry, frantic *click-thrum* of the Tie-Weavers. Their sharp bone bodkins prick through the upper joints, sometimes grazing my slate back as they cinch their jute cords. I leave them the internal arches they need so their anchors do not pull free from the mush. 

Far beneath my belly, the muffled, concussive *thud-thump* of the Stampers comes through the dense floor like a heartbeat heard through a mattress. Every time they lift a sunken basin, the compression wave ripples through the clay around me, squeezing my breathing-tunnels half-shut, forcing me to circle back and ream them clear once more.

And sometimes, when I cut through a seam that was laid hours ago, my wooden loop will carve across a vast, curved depression buried in the dark: the clean, perfect arch of a finger-pad sixty paces wide, pressed into the mortar while it was still boiling. 

I do not know who set the mark. I do not stay to consider it. The draft behind me is already turning from damp river-smell to the dry, iron tang of heat; the walls are warming; the clay is beginning to shrink and set. 

I drop my snout, bite into the firm, silent red, and peel another ribbon of earth from the dark.
