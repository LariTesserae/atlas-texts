At first, there is only the sensation of being held at the exact temperature of blood, so that it is impossible to tell where the surface of the body ends and the water begins. 

I would not be moving. In a place where a displaced pebble takes a decade to settle into stone, to move with intention is an error of scale. 

I find myself low in the tiers, somewhere around the fifth or sixth step down from the cleft, where the water has already passed over three living bodies and cooled by half a degree. Here, the flow is no longer a current; it is only a heavy, continuous spilling—a sheet of warm glass sliding over a lip of rock.

I am wedged into a notch between two basins. 

Not wholly stone, not wholly flesh, but the dense, quiet medium where the two have stopped disputing each other. 

My back is curved to match the hollow of the dolomite shelf behind me, settled into the bowl formed by someone who sat here generations ago. My knees are drawn up, parted, resting against the swollen travertine nodules of the lower lip. I am not waiting. The concept of an interval has been replaced by the rhythm of the ceiling drops—fat, heavy beads that strike the center of the upper pool with a muffled *plink*, sending concentric ripples across my submerged ribs.

At my left hip, where the overflow finds its narrowest channel, the water moves faster. It tugs at the dead skin of my flank with a persistent, gentle drag. In that small chute, the lime is thickest; over the last few seasons, it has laid down an opaque, milk-white crust across the crest of my iliac bone. When I breathe—a low, shallow draw that happens perhaps twice in the space between drops—the crust does not crack. It has grown too thick for that. It holds me to the weir like an iron rivet, cold and immovable, while beneath it, my marrow remains slow and warm.

Under the shelf of my thighs, in the dark, dead-calm space where the travertine mushrooms out over the lower basin, the olms are working. 

I do not see them—my eyes are open, wide and pale-ringed, but they stare only into the leaden twilight of the vault, taking in the diffuse, shadowless sheen of the wet rock. I don't need to see them. I feel them through the skin: the light, feather-stitch brush of their frilled external gills against the soles of my feet; the delicate, exploratory tug of their blunt snouts at the soft tissue between my toes; the faint, rhythmic suction as they clean away the pale dander of my shedding hide. They move like ribbons of peeled willow root, untroubled by my weight. To them, I am a permanent geography that occasionally hums.

Higher up, two terraces above me, one of the others shifts. 

The movement is small—a heel dragged half an inch across the grit of a calcified rim—but the vibration travels through the dense dolomite directly into my spine. It arrives not as sound, but as an acoustic density, a low pressure that tells me the water level above has risen by a finger’s width. A few moments later, the spillway across my chest deepens; the warm mineral soup rises to cover my clavicles, tasting faintly of birch bark, ancient salt, and the skin-oil of the bather above.

My hands rest in the silt-free bottom of the catch-basin beside my knees. Blind amphipods, white as ash, drift over my knuckles. I do not close my fingers around them. There is an adze fifty paces away, half-glazed in lime, but my hands have outgrown the need to grip tools. When hunger comes, it is not a sharp thing; it is a dull, slow thinning of the blood that is answered simply by leaning my chin toward the water and letting the grazing mollusks on the lip of the rock meet my lips. 

Mostly, I am occupied with the accretion.

You can feel it if you remain still for long enough: the invisible architecture of the dissolved stone falling out of the water, atom by atom, settling onto the hairs of your forearms, riming the edges of your ears, sealing the small fissures in your palms with a smooth, porcelain glaze. It is a painless, heavy cementing. The joints do not stiffen from age; they are simply incorporated. 

In another year, or perhaps ten, the water spilling over my sternum will have laid down enough travertine to bridge the gap between my ribs and the opposite ledge. The olms will have finished their slow, gentle work below, turning my lower limbs into clean, porous arches through which the sluggish runoff can filter into the deepest basin. The bathers above will feel the change only as an altered note in the plumbing—a slightly deeper pool to rest their broad shoulders in, a smoother sill of calcified bone against which to lean their heads when the leaden light fails entirely.

Until then, I am the weir. I am the notch that measures the seep. I am the place where the warm water hesitates before it falls.
