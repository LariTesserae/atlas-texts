At first, the sense of place is not an eye opening onto a landscape, but an adjustment of resistance. 

To be here is, first of all, to be hot from underneath.

It is easy to imagine being one of the great hanging veils, strung between the plum-colored spans, catching the amber grease of the updraft until the shudder comes. Or one of the pipers, anchored in the throat of a boiling flume, feeling the *tock* of the siphon pass straight through a body made of horn and silicate. But both of those are too fixed; they belong entirely to the apparatus, having surrendered the boundary between being a component and being an occupant.

Instead, I find myself not anchored, but not entirely loose either. 

I am in the middle air, in the cavernous, hollow bays where the skeletal framework meets the condensation line—about forty cubits above the scalded flagstones, where the updraft from the floor collides with the downdraft of heavy, mineral-choked droplets falling from the highest trusses. 

Here, the mist is neither rising nor falling; it shears.

What am I?

Not metal, though metal is the ground of all thought here. Not quite stone, though stone is what everything becomes if it stays still long enough.

I am an accretion. A composite creature formed in the interstitial slip between two expansion joints—a creature of the seams.

My body has the mass and density of wet slate, shaped like a long, segmented wedge, roughly three spans in length, tapered like an adze at the forward edge and broadening into a flattened, multi-layered keel. My underside is soft, ribbed with dense rows of pale, fleshy cilia that secrete a cold, oily lipid—the only cold thing in fifty square leagues—which allows me to slide across the superheated iron without bonding to it, surfing on a thin vapor-cushion of my own boiling secretions. 

My back, however, is not biological. It is an encrustation: years of iron-oxide flakes, heavy sulfate druses, and the crushed, discarded carapaces of juvenile suture-backs that have drifted down from the upper tiers, cemented together by a calcifying paste I extrude from between my dorsal plates. To the world above, I look like nothing more than a fallen piece of girder-scale, a curved slab of plum-and-ochre rust slowly traveling along a horizontal flange.

I do not eat the bacterial velvet directly, nor do I drink the raw condensate. I am an animal of the *vibrations*.

When the siphon strikes—*tock*—the entire lattice of the city rings. The sound does not travel primarily through the thin mountain air, which is too loose and vaporous to carry it well; it travels through the iron. It rings through the rivets, through the webbed trusses, through the long, hollow viaducts.

My nervous system is an array of fine, metallic filaments embedded along the keel, resting directly against the plum-colored iron. When the pulse hits, I read the tension of the city:

Where the pitch is sharp and high-strung, the iron is dry and under tensile stress, pulled tight by a cooling draft or an overburdened sluice. 
Where the pitch is dull, muddy, and thick, the rust has bitten deep, or a dead veil has accumulated too much salt, or a slab-mover has dissolved away too much of the curb, altering the flow of the boiling sheets below.

I move toward the imbalances. 

I am neither a repairer nor a consumer; I am a dampener. Where a truss begins to vibrate with a dangerous, self-amplifying harmonic—where the rising thermal wind sets an arch singing until the rivets strain in their seats—I slide into the junction, wedge my heavy, slate-dense bulk into the angle between the gusset plate and the beam, and expand. 

I press my cold, lipid-slick belly hard against the hot iron; I flare my heavy crust of gypsum and iron-scale against the opposite face. The vibration enters me, rattles through the chalky layers of my shell, is broken up by the million tiny fractures within the dead carapaces glued to my back, and dies away as heat. 

I absorb the city’s scream and turn it back into lukewarm water.

In the long intervals between harmonics, I move with an unhurried, lateral crawl along the under-flanges of the avenues. The scalding rain drips past me in heavy, glassy ropes. Below, the pale, blind trowel-heads scrape their patient lines through the orange film, never knowing I am above them; above, the veils swell and sigh against the dark sky, tossing their pale crystal showers down through the mist, some of which settle on my back to be incorporated into my armor.

I have no eyes, because light here has nothing to say; the sun is only a bruised spot, a rumor beyond the sulfur ceiling. But I know the shape of the entire empire of open space by its resonance: forty miles of iron singing at four cycles a second, suspended over an abyss of boiling stone, slowly turning itself into a reef of salt and bone.

And there I stay—wedged in an angle of plum-colored iron, wet, heavy, listening to the iron sigh, waiting for the next pulse to travel up from the deep foundations.
