At first, there is no distinct edge between the weight of the chest and the weight of the wall. 

I would not be on the catwalks, nor down in the pooling iron of the sump where the sludge-lungs spread their slow, rubbery skirts. Those are places of arrival, where things drop or gather. 

I would be caught in the bypass.

High in the middle tiers, where two thirty-inch steam mains converge into a blind mixing chamber—a valve-drum whose iron gate jammed half-open three centuries ago—there is an interior space that never drains and never dries. It does not belong to the open volume of the well. It is a sealed hollow, twelve feet across, accessible only through a choked, crescent-shaped gap where the bronze valve-seat warped under ancient pressure and froze.

Inside that drum, the air does not circulate as a draft; it breathes as a single, contained lung, expanding when the riser below flares white-hot and drawing back when the outer bulkhead cools to slate.

To be here is to be wedge-shaped. It is to have given up the luxury of limbs that project outward into empty air, and to have accepted instead the geometry of a gasket.

My body would be long, heavy, and disc-jointed, like an accordion of soft, leaden hide armor-plated with overlapping lamellae of polished magnetite. Not six-legged, not wire-limbed. I have no need to grip a pipe from the outside; the pipe contains me. My flanks press constantly against the smooth, concave bore of the casing. Where my flesh meets the iron, there is no friction, only a thick, pale exudate—part tallow-fat, part mineral wax—that forms an airtight seal along the seam of my plates.

I do not possess the infrared pit-eyes of the wanderer. In this enclosed chamber, heat is not a light to be looked at from a distance; it is a physical mass that bears down upon the crown of my head and thins toward my tail. 

I see through strain. 

My skeleton is not made of calcium, but of flexible, porous cartilaginous iron that runs in dense longitudinal rings through my core. Every tension in the Flue-Well—the thermal stretching of the eastern conduit stack, the settling of a rivet four tiers down, the slow, imperceptible sagging of a lead cable bundle overhead—is conducted through the bolted flanges directly into the valve body, and from the valve body into my ribs. 

I know the well not as a map of corridors, but as a vast, resonant bell that is perpetually ringing at frequencies too low for air to carry. 

When the forty-foot drip strikes the drowned plate in the sump, the *tock* does not come to me as a sound. It comes as a microscopic upward shock through the spine of the riser, a sharp, momentary stiffening in the metal beneath my belly. I count the drops not by intention, but because each impact causes the fluid in my inner chambers to slosh against a bed of sensory cilia, keeping an ancient, internal hydraulic clock.

Feeding, for me, is not an act of pursuit. It is an act of filtration and condensation. 

The steam that bleeds from the microscopic fractures in the mixing drum carries with it volatile hydrocarbons, vaporized tallow, and the ultra-fine spores of the white lagging-mold. Because my body blocks the bypass throat, the thermal draft must force its way past the narrow margins of my plates. I am a living baffle. As the hot vapor squeezes through the micro-grooves along my flanks, the temperature drops just enough for the oils to drop out of suspension. 

They coat my sides in a rich, warm grease, which my dermal cilia slowly draw downward toward a series of ventral pores. I metabolize the old oils of dead engines, burning their long carbon chains with agonizing slowness in a cold, low-oxygen furnace at the center of my mass. My breath is not vented into the room; it is returned to the pipe as a dry, mineral-dusted carbon dioxide that settles on the inner rust like fine soot.

I do not sleep, nor am I ever fully awake in the way a creature of the sun is awake. I exist in an uninterrupted state of calibration.

Occasionally, once every few years, the temperature differential between the live riser and the dead bulkhead shifts beyond its normal margin. A pocket of trapped brine far down in the foundational boilers boils off, or a massive iron stalactite shears from a high walkway and crashes into the middle lattice.

When this happens, the valve-drum groans. The metal around me shrinks or swells with sudden violence.

In those hours, I move. 

It is a terrible, slow exertion. My plates slide over one another with a dry, grinding *clink* that echoes down the hollow interior of the pipes like a dropped wrench. I contract the hydraulic rings in my tail, anchoring them into the pitted bronze of the seat, and push forward an eighth of an inch, reshaping my body to fill the new clearance, preventing the pressure from blowing through the seam. 

Once the shift is accommodated, the waxy secretions well up from my lateral glands, fill the microscopic gouges left by my passage, and cool into place. The seal is restored. The vibration smooths out, settling back into that single, foundational thrum that lives below sixty hertz.

I am the maintenance of a pressure gradient that no longer remembers its purpose.

If the blind wanderer were to pass above my drum, clambering along the exterior lagging with its collapsable ribs and padded clamps, it would pause for a moment. Its thermal honeycomb would register a round, warm swell in the ironwork—a spot where the metal is neither as hot as the steam nor as cold as the casing, but steady, quiet, and faintly pulsing with the rhythm of an oil-filled heart. It would sniff the seam, find only the sterile, paraffin wax of my margins, and lever itself onward into the dark.

And I would feel its weight pass: a gradual, four-hundred-pound deflection of the upper flange, a brief compression of my dorsal plates, and then the slow, elastic return of the iron as the dark closes behind it, leaving the chamber silent, sealed, and full.
