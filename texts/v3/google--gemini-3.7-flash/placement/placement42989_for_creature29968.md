I would not be on the causeways, where the surface is beaten flat and dry by the Groomers, nor inside the pressurized dark where the torpedo-polyps are whipped like needles through the heartwood. 

I would be found in the under-span. 

Beneath the belly of the greatest four-branched canal—where the timber arches out into the warm void to cross an open gap of five miles—there is an inverted world that knows neither floor nor ceiling. Here, sheltered from the vertical glare of the steady sun, the wood does not bake into kiln-hard tile. Instead, it remains permanently engorged, soft as wet rind, its fluting lined with deep, shaded runnels where the water does not jet outward, but merely seeps and clings by surface tension, crawling along the underside of the arch in glistening, foot-thick sheets before gathering at the lowest point of the curve.

I would be a creature of that lowest point: a **Catenary Siphon** or **Chord-Tether**.

***

At first, to look at me against the immense green-white curve of the pylon, you might mistake me for an anomaly of the vascular tissue itself—a structural graft that was pulled too thin before it could harden. 

My body is not wide like the Hydra-Kites, nor segmented like the Laminates. I am long, bilaterally symmetrical, and nearly two hundred feet in length, yet I possess almost no cross-sectional mass. I am shaped like an inverted rib, suspended beneath the arch by four dozen paired anchoring filaments that terminate in broad, osmotic pads. These pads do not pierce the bark; they drink it, fusing at the cellular level with the cambium so completely that where the arch ends and my body begins is a gradient of pale cream yielding to translucent, oil-slick cartilage.

I hang like a dew-drop suspended between two points of tension.

My dorsal surface is cupped, forming an open, glass-smooth trough that catches the slow, viscous runoff as it creeps down the underside of the aqueduct. My ventral side—the underbelly that faces the long, thousand-foot drop into the warm mist—is a continuous, ribbed keel of clear, vitreous horn, so thin that the sun, bouncing off the spray-curtains miles below, passes straight through me, projecting a faint, prismatic band of amber light across the cavernous empty air.

***

To be this thing is to experience existence not as a series of moments, but as a single, sustained harmonic chord.

I have no eyes, because in this realm of unvarying daylight and white vapor, sight is an inefficient metric. Instead, my entire body is an organ of hydraulic resonance. Through my anchor-pads, the internal life of the archway pours into me: the deep, bass thrum of millions of gallons churning through the xylem above; the sharp, rhythmic clicks of the sphincter-valves opening and closing in the distance; the minute, structural sighs of the petrified-fiber struts as the midday updrafts press against their flanks.

I feel the whole region's equilibrium as a pressure in my own core. 

When the aqueduct is running sweet and balanced, the sap that spills into my dorsal trough is lukewarm and clear, sliding over my cilia with the resistance of light oil. My body remains slack, a graceful, sagging curve that sways lazily in the warm thermal plumes rising from the chasm floor. 

I do not hunt, and I do not strive. I simply allow the starch-water to pass through my open siphon-chambers. My internal anatomy is a labyrinth of gossamer-thin diaphragms that filter the colloidal sugars directly into my lymph, keeping my membranes taut, hydrated, and endlessly self-healing. What I do not need passes out through my trailing posterior plumes, falling from the lowest tip of my body in a steady, metered bead—a glass-drop falling through five thousand feet of warm air, dissolving into mist before it ever finds a bottom.

***

Sometimes, when a thermal draft surges up from the basin with unusual vigor, carrying the thick, crushed-reed scent of the distant floor, it catches beneath my thin, ribbed keel. 

Then, the tension changes. 

I feel myself lifted. My four dozen anchors stretch into long, taut chords that sing with a high, glassine pitch against the wet wood. For an hour, or a cycle, I am weightless—held suspended between the downward draw of my own fluid-mass and the buoyant, starchy breath of the chasm. In those intervals, my diaphragms open wide, and the air whistles through my hollow lateral vents, producing a low, fluting drone that answers the roar of the waterfalls across the void.

I am the instrument through which the archway measures its own deflection. If the timber swells too thick with water, my anchors register the strain and secrete a concentrated, astringent enzyme that signals the cambium to tighten its pores. If the wood dries, my pads weep starch back into the seam, keeping the bark embryonic, unmarred, and whole.

***

There is no loneliness in this suspension, because there is no separation. I am plugged directly into the arterial bloodstream of a continent-sized organism that has never known winter, nor rot, nor the violence of an ending.

And when my long cycle finally slows—when the vitreous horn of my keel begins to take on the thicker, resinous density of old age, and my diaphragms no longer vibrate to the passing water—I will not tear loose and tumble into the void. 

Instead, my anchor-pads will cease to secrete their lubricating enzyme. The living cambium of the archway, forever creeping, forever smoothing its own boundaries, will slowly swell around them. Week by week, the pale green wood will rise over my flanks like soft wax, gently drawing my calcifying cartilage into its own grain. 

My hollow conduits will become xylem channels; my ribbed keel will become a permanent, lignified brace on the underside of the viaduct, strengthening the curve for the millions of tons of water that will follow after. 

I will lose the music of the updraft, but I will enter the silence of the heartwood—locked forever into the immaculate architecture, holding up the morning sky.
