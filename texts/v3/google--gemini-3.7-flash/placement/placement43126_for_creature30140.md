I am not at the looms yet, though my left knee has begun to take the crust.

When I hook my lanyard to the tenement eye-bolt during the dead-stroke hours, it is no longer just the gutta-percha strap holding me; a fine lace of white lime and tubeworm grit has begun to bridge the gap between the brass ring of my harness and the mortar of the wall. When I pull away, it tears with a sound I do not hear, but feel—a faint, dry rasp along the back of my cervical vertebrae, like salt being ground between two flat river stones. 

For now, I am still mobile enough for the second tier. That is where you would find me: on the middle catwalks, where the bronze rocker-arms reach out from the boiler faces to drive the eccentric cams of the silt-presses below.

My hands are thick, broad spatulas, yellow-gray like salted cod. I carry the tallow-horn at my left hip and three sizes of gland-wrench on an iron ring at my right. My work is the packing: keeping the cold tallow pressed tight into the bronze gland-collars so the fine, sharp quartz dust in the benthic slurry does not chew the piston rods into scrap before their forty-year turn is done.

To do this, you cannot watch the rod. The lantern light is too dull—a greasy green smudge that dies two inches off the glass—and the water is full of suspended flakes of iron-reducing mold that drift like soot. You must lay your bare chest against the cold cylinder jacket, hook your bronze toes beneath the gantry rail to keep from drifting with the cam’s back-wash, and press both palms flat against the bronze packing nut while the piston makes its twelve-pace stroke.

Through the palms, the rod tells its age.

If the stroke is clean, it passes into the skull as a continuous, glassy hum—a single, low note that hums in the molars and vibrates the isotonic fluid behind my ears. If a grain of basalt sand has worked behind the seal, the note frays; it becomes a rough, sandpaper chatter that catches in the collarbone. Then I reach for the horn, scoop a thumb-sized lump of stiff, cold tallow—hard as soap at this temperature—and thumb it deep into the grease-cup, leaning my weight against the screw until the note rounds out again and returns to glass.

I do not think of the surface. None of us have the shape of head for it; our skulls are thick, solid casements of fused bone, built to balance the four hundred fathoms that sit on our crowns. To imagine an "up" without weight would feel like imagining a body without skin—an unravelling, a sudden and catastrophic dispersion into nothingness. Weight is what keeps the blood heavy and quiet in our flanks. Weight is the floor that meets the lead of our sabots.

Between strokes, I listen to the ironwork.

I put my jaw against the handrail and click three times: *tap-tap... tap.* 

A second passes through the dead water. Then, from forty paces down-trench, through the cross-braces of the fourth roller-track, the reply comes back through the rail: *tap... tap-tap-tap.* 

Old Vane is at the intake grate. His teeth are worn down to smooth, ivory nubs from sixty years of signaling, but his cadence is heavier than mine, weighted by the mineral plates that have spread across his shoulders. He is telling me that the silt arriving from the north ditch is thick with crushed pyrites today—heavy, sulfurous mud that will bake hard in the presses and require half a turn more on the gland-nuts to keep the rams from binding.

I click once in acknowledgment, unhook my wrench, and step down to the next landing. 

My bronze sabots find the rungs by habit, their lead soles deadening each step so that I move without displacing the carpet of purple bacterial velvet that coats the riser plates. As I descend, the water grows subtly denser, salted by the hot plumes drifting from the lower relief-flues. It tastes of copper slag, boiled iron, and the cold, eternal silt that we are here to square and stack.

I know where I will end. 

In another ten or twenty years of seasons—measured not by suns, which do not exist, but by the slow, twelve-hour oscillation of the hydraulic pressure-mains—my right hip will join my left knee in the calcifying. My lanyards will rot away and will not need replacing. I will find a niche near the number-four silt-press, between the foundation stone and the bronze housing of the primary reduction gear.

I will wedge my lead shoes into the masonry silt-trap, let the tubeworms knit my harness to the iron gantry, and lay my hands flat upon the main bearing cap.

I will not need to carry the tallow-horn then. I will simply be the thing that feels the bearing run hot, clicking my jaw against the iron casing whenever the shaft begins to wander, sending the news up through the wet metal to whatever younger, milk-pale hands are walking the upper tiers, until the trench has finished paving its floor, and the last ingot is laid true against the dark.
