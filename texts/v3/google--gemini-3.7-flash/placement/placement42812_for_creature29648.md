At first, there is only the pulse through the heels: two hard thuds of walrus-hide calluses against the hollow flute of a mastodon rib, shivering up through the shins, into the pelvic bowl, into the skull. A warning of a high surge from the fourth tier.

I would not be in the air, where the diver-birds drop like lead plumb-lines, nor deep in the dark fissures where the blind ribbons spawn in the earth’s black heat. 

I would be on the eighth tier, wedged in the wet crook between a rounded sod-dome and an overhanging diorite lip, where the sluice runs chest-deep twice a day.

My eyes are amber slits. The glare of the cirque strikes the thick, oily veil of my inner lids and scatters into a bearable, yellow haze—a furnace that does not burn, only illuminates the violent, hair-thin seams in the world. Through this film, the sun is a quiet white hole in the ink of the sky, and every sheet of water sliding over the terraces is a skin of liquid mica, sliding, parting, knotting around my thighs.

My hands do not stop. They cannot.

They are broad, gray-skinned, smelling of cold tallow and crushed shale. Between the second and third fingers of my left hand, I hold a short horn-rasp; with my right, I pull lengths of soaked sinew tight around the knuckle-joint of a whale-spar weir. The sinew is greasy, cold as a split river-stone, biting into the salt-cracked rifts of my palms. If I drop the rasp, it will be gone in half a heartbeat, washed over the lip of the eighth tier down to the sixth, swallowed by the churn of blind white ribbons. If I pause to warm my fingers in my armpits, the brine will crust over the bone-joint in five breaths, forming a brittle collar of vitreous salt that will shatter under the next surge and take the dome behind me with it.

So the rhythm continues: *pull, wrap, bite, tie, heel-strike.*

To my left, three scrapers cling to the sod-line, their ten iron-calcite legs shivering against the wet peat. They do not care that my elbow grazes them every time I draw the cord. They are waiting for the lichen I scrape loose with the horn; their mouthparts twitch in tiny, metallic spasms, vibrating against the stone with a drone I feel in the teeth of my lower jaw. We share the shelf. If the brine surges high enough to float me, I will hook my broad toes into the gaps between their shells, using their diamond-tipped anchor-points to keep from sliding into the drop.

There is no thought of "tomorrow" or "yesterday." There is only the five-minute trance taken crouched on my hams, chin pressed hard into the greasy hollow of my breastbone until my lungs fill with the heavy, tallowy warmth of my own body, and then the violent, convulsive waking—the salt-water splashing the mouth, the pick back in the grip before the eyes have fully parted their amber shields.

Yet something shifts in the work over the years. 

You feel it first in the knuckles, then the ankles. The salt does not merely wash over the skin; it sinks into the micro-fissures of the bone. The movement of the blood slows, replaced by a dense, mineral drag. The lungs, which once drew the thin blue air with frantic, bellows-like elasticity, begin to hold the weight of the mountain within their lining. The skin thickens, gray as diorite, losing its copper-scent to the clean, iron-cold neutral of the rock.

And that is where I would truly find myself—not at the frantic beginning of a diver’s life, nor yet fixed into the masonry, but in the slow, conscious turning from *builder* to *foundation*.

When the fingers finally become too stiff to tie the fine sinew of the bird-nets, when the heel-stamps can no longer strike the marrow-flutes with enough force to warn the children on the tier below, there is no sorrow. Sorrow requires a pause, and the cirque does not have two seconds to spare for it. 

Instead, I would slide myself down into the central sluice of the eighth tier, where the flow is heaviest and the ribbons churn thickest, their cool, soft notochords whipping against my ribs like living water-grass. 

I would wedge my shoulders into the breach where the spring-thaw has cracked the basalt curb. I would take the drift of the supercooled brine full against my chest, closing the horizontal amber lids for the last time against the diamond glare of the sky. 

The cold would not strike like pain; it would strike like *settling*. The mineral water would rush through the hide wraps, leaching the last soft oils from the tissue, carrying in its freight of dissolved iron, lime, and vitreous salt. Hour by hour, as the divers plunge and the scrapers rattle their shields overhead, the water would do its work: filling the porous spongy bone, crystal by crystal, turning the marrow to quartz-hard spar, smoothing the brow until the features melt into the curve of the weir.

A generation later, a child with walrus-hide soles and splayed toes would balance on the shelf of my collarbone, driving an antler pick into the gap behind my neck to seat a new mastodon rib against the surge. 

She would stamp her heel twice upon my petrified shoulder to tell the fourth tier that the water is rising. And deep within the dense, iron-veined stone that I had become, the vibration would carry true—clean, muffled, and permanent—holding the terrace against the fall.
