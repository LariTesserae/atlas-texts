It begins in the arches of the feet—or whatever replaces arches when the ground has never known a fracture.

To exist here is, first of all, to understand that there is no grit. There is no sand to give purchase, no crushed stone to roll beneath an edge, no decay to soften an impact. Every step is an encounter with pure, unyielding datum: a plane machined to a tolerance so tight that the water beneath you does not splash so much as it shears, forced out sideways in microscopic, hydroplaning lenses before the grip takes.

I find myself not up on the sixty-story webs, nor flat in the hunting troughs, but in the long, quiet interstitial zones—the wide, shallow steps where the radiant skirts of the foundation pylons fade into the true cold of the basalt.

I am an inhabitant of the drainage seams. 

Perhaps I was once an attendant who came down from the spires and simply never took the hoist back up, or perhaps I am something that was seeded into this landscape alongside the blue-grey lichens to ensure the channels remain clear. My body is low, dense, and slow-moving. My limbs are heavy enough to counteract the updrafts that spill over the terrace lips, and my skin is covered in a tight, seamless membrane—grey as the condensation clouds, slick and cool to the touch, shedding the mist in steady, rolling pearls. 

I carry a long, flat-bladed implement made of the same sintered composite as the spires: part staff, part scraper, part tuning fork. 

My work is small, silent, and unending.

Where the dray graze in their massive, geometric squares, they leave behind wide swaths of scraped stone—raw, pale basalt stripped clean of its milk-glass crust. Behind them, the herd sheds: thick, lanolin-heavy tufts of slate-colored fleece, snagged on the sharp corner-angles of the foundation slabs, and wide, gelatinous puddles where their cartilage pads have left a residue of grip-mucus. 

Left alone, this detritus drifts with the thin sheet-flow of runoff into the narrow, millimeter-wide expansion gaps that separate the township slabs from the bedrock. If those seams clog, the water pools deep; if the water pools deep, it freezes when the mantle stabilizers cycle down their heat; if it freezes in the unyielding joints, it exerts hydraulic pressure against the planetary lattice.

And so I follow the herds at a distance of three hundred paces, walking the precise, ruled lines of the drainage channels.

*Scrape. Lift. Clear.*

The sound of my work is a faint, metallic hiss that blends with the wind. I scoop the matted wool and the gelatinous midge-casings out of the seams, bundling them into tight, dense balls that I leave in the dry, warm hollows beneath the pylon anchor skirts. Within hours, the plinth-skimmers will spiral down from the upper struts to harvest them, tearing the oily fleece apart with their needle-fine beaks to line their vertical nesting pockets sixty stories above. 

Nothing is wasted, but nothing is crowded. The geometry remains clean.

When the deep stabilizer fires miles below our feet—that immense, water-submerged bell that rings through the marrow of the mountain—I feel it long before I hear it. It enters through the dense cartilage of my soles, travels up the solid columns of my legs, and resonates in the center of my chest. It is not an alarm; it is the heartbeat of a floor that refuses to sink into the magma. 

When the pulse passes, the dray do not even lift their shovel-heads from the stone; they only adjust their six-legged gait in unison, four pads down, rolling their vast, woolly bulk forward by another inch. *Shck. Shck. Shck.* The chime of horn against silicate answers the chime of the machine in the deep.

In the late hours, when the pale daylight turns to a uniform, luminous pearl and the cloud decks below rise to lap against the terrace rim, the cold sharpens. The mist thickens until you can see only ten paces in any direction: a world composed entirely of wet, dark floor, pale grey fog, and the razor-straight edge where the stone drops away into nothing.

I do not sleep in a shelter—shelters would interrupt the laminar airflow across the plazas. Instead, I find a resting herd.

I walk between their massive, steam-breathing flanks. They do not startle. They smell of cold water, heavy wool-oil, and the sharp, mineral tang of pulverized rock. I lay my staff across the stone and sit with my back pressed directly into the side of an old bull, fitting myself into the heavy, dreadlocked curve of his flank. 

The heat coming off his wool is immense, sustained by the gallons of lichen paste fermenting in his multi-chambered gut. Above us, the mist sweeps horizontally, whispering across the planar edges of the township, but down here, between the kneeling dray, the air is dead still and warm as a closed room.

I pull my knees to my chest, rest my chin against the waterproof membrane of my suit, and listen.

I hear the slow, four-stroke digestion of the bull behind me. I hear the high, wet *click* of the plinth-skimmers turning in the fog overhead, snapping midges out of the updrafts. And below it all, running beneath the vast, unblemished stone like blood through an artery, I feel the steady, soft hum of the lattice holding two thousand meters of impossible height against the curve of the world.

I close my eyes. The stone beneath me is cold, smooth, and brand new. It will be five thousand years before the rain even begins to round the corners, and I am in no hurry at all.
