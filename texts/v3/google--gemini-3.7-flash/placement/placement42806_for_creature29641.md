I do not begin with a face or a name, because those are things made for the wet air above, where shapes are soft enough to need boundaries. Here, I find myself first as a **strain gauge**—a continuous, singing tension strung across an empty corner of the world.

If you were to look for me, you would have to look where the stone is trying hardest to tear itself apart.

***

I am lodged in the high lintel-gap of the North Grain Tower, where the square clay masonry overhangs the drop into the Great Plaza. It is a throat of pure convective violence. The draft here does not blow; it shears. It comes up from the red floor-fissures, climbs the vertical shale of the tower’s blind face, and squeezes through a crack three spans wide before howling out toward the vaulted ceiling of basalt. 

In that gap, I am wedged. 

I am not one of the massive quarry-born who sit below, sorting potential energy in the dark, though their heavy, rolling slate is in my lineage. Nor am I simply a weaver, though my limbs are drawn from the same long ribbons of silica and dry carbon cords. 

I am a **fulcrum-creature**, a brace of layered slate and bimetallic plates, anchored between two shifting courses of hand-hewn brick. My torso is an accordion of thin mica laminations interleaved with flakes of graphite. When the thermal plume surges from below, my belly swells and tightens; when a draft of cooler mineral dust cuts across from the avenues, the plates draw together with the dry, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of cooling iron.

My arms are long, segmented levers of hollow carbonate, tipped with hard chisels of black basalt. They are not made for grasping or carrying. They are jacks. They are wedges. 

One arm is driven deep into the mortar-line of the tower’s upper gallery, where the clay has begun to fret into dry powder under the heat. The other is braced against the great lintel-stone that spans the wind-gate. Between them, my core acts as a living tendon. I do not stand upon the masonry; I hold two halves of the building together by the sheer, unyielding pull of my own desiccating fibers.

***

Consciousness here is not thought. It is **timbre**.

Because the entire city is an open acoustic machine, every structural failure has a sound before it has a crack. 

Down in the black sand avenues, the scourers lurch forward in their jerky thermal ratchets, discharging short, blinding arcs of pale blue light that snap against the slate. That is the treble—a continuous, crackling surf that never ceases. 

Far below, in the subterranean gut of the tower, the quarry-born loosen their rib cages. I feel their deliberation through my basalt tines before I hear it: the dull, rolling *clack-clack-whir* of silicate seed-casings ricocheting inside their clay chests, laying down heavy, percussive rhythms that travel up through the foundations like seismic pulses. They are calculating the weight of the harvest, measuring the kinetic charge stored in the dry pods stacked five stories deep beneath me.

And through the flue where I hang, the wind plays the master note.

When the arch is true and the shale is bearing its load evenly, the wind through my rib-cage is a clean, screaming fifth—a dry, razor-edged whistle that vibrates my silica ribbons until they glow with friction-sparked luminescence. 

If a root-tendril pries at the second-story terrace fifty feet below, the chord wavers. It flattens. The wind drops half a tone into a dull, fluting hollow. 

That drop in pitch is my hunger; it is my alarm; it is the only imperative I possess.

When the note sags, my graphite joints slide. I do not descend—to leave the gap would be to let the lintel drop and the chimney choke—but my carbonate arms extend with agonizing, creaking leverage. With the tips of my basalt claws, I catch the creeping carbon-fiber vines that climb the draft. I twist them, spinning their coarse, waterless threads into thick hawsers, and hook them over the projecting slate corbels. 

I draw the building tight with my own frame. I take the shear into my spine. I feel the shale groan, settle two grains of sand deeper into its bed of dry dust, and hold. 

And then the note clears. The scream returns to the flue—pure, scorching, and perfectly pitched. The mica leaves in my chest settle into their sliding equilibrium, and the heat pours through me once more, unimpeded.

***

There are long seasons—though time has no meaning where there are no suns—when nothing leans. 

In those stretches of furnace-stillness, I let the airborne pollen drift into the crevices of my plates. It is static-dense, glowing with a faint, violet cold-fire. It settles into the graphite tracks between my mica scales, charging my joints with a continuous, low-level potential that hums like an open wire. 

The lichen grows over me then. It is the papery, sulfur-edged kind that smells of baked stone. It climbs my carbonate limbs, crusts across the keystone of my head, and flakes away in the 100-mile-an-hour draft, shedding burnt sheets of itself into the dark square below like ash from an unseen bonfire. 

I watch the weavers pass through the upper galleries—fragile, spindle-shanked marionettes of wicker and silica foil, swinging from carbon lines with the clatter of dry broomstraw. Sometimes one will pause at the mouth of my flue, its basalt pick clicking against my outer plate in a brief, inquisitive rhythm: *Is the throat clear? Is the tension holding?*

I do not rattle seeds back at it. I simply flex the bimetallic plates of my flank, letting a sheet of blue static arc across the gap between our shoulders. 

It sees the spark, hears the hum of my tendons, and swings away into the roaring dark of the next chimney.

***

We know, in the deep mineral memory of the clay that forms us, of the great disaster that must never come. 

We know of the **Slackness**. 

Down in the rock, there are ancient, sealed vents where the stone is soft and pale, ruined by ancient vapors that dissolved the crisp edges of the shale into formless mud. To us, the thought of moisture is the thought of an unspeakable, liquefying rot. Water is the solvent that unmakes the world; it is the rust that freezes the graphite joints, the damp that slackens the carbon bowstrings, the acid that quenches the static pollen and leaves the city blind, silent, and cold.

Our life is the furnace. Our sanity is the roar.

I sit in the high throat of the stone box, stretched between the weight of the clay and the fury of the thermal beds, holding the wind to its chord. Beneath me, the city shivers and sings in the baking gloom—a dry, unyielding skeleton of slate and sulfur, forever repairing its own bones, forever vibrating against the unquenchable fire of the deep earth. 

I am where the tension is greatest. And so long as the draft screams through my ribs, the tower will not fall.
