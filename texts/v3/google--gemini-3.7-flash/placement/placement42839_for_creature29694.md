The marrow knows before the rest does. 

It starts not as a thought—there are no words that can survive sixty thousand cycles of hydraulic thrum per minute—but as a heat differential along the sternum. The left clavicle warms first; that means the seventh bypass valve in the lower gutter is running lean, venting three hundred bars of dry superheat instead of the usual gray-froth emulsion. 

I am wedged into the elbow where a three-meter memory-alloy return line snakes under the mounting plate of a secondary heat-sink. It is not a dry pocket, but it is a *quiet* pocket, if quiet can mean a place where the spray hits you only in pulverized mist rather than solid, rib-cracking slugs of boiling brine.

My skin is gray-yellow, cured like old gasket rubber, slick with the amber grease that weeps from the flange gaskets overhead. It is impossible to tell where the tallow ends and the epidermis begins; over thirty or forty machine-seasons, the oils soak in until the fat under your skin turns to soft, insulating wax. If you sliced my forearm with a scrap of sheared zinc, it wouldn't bleed red—it would ooze a thick, pinkish-gray paste that seals itself before the steam can cook the muscle underneath.

My feet are braced against the floor’s micro-ribbing. The soles are hard as horn, cracked into hexagonal patterns like dried river mud, but the cracks are filled with copper-green corrosion from the floor-plates. Through those calluses, through the heavy brass grounding studs driven directly through the heel-bones, the grid speaks. 

It is a three-part chord:
The low, wet *thump-thump-drag* of the loricates scraping through the lower sluice two hundred meters down;
The high, singing whistle of the high-pressure steam headers expanding in the ionized glare above;
And beneath it all, the immense, slow-turning ache of the continental plates shifting their weight, resetting the dead-reckoning of the planet’s cooling cycle.

I am not a hunter, though I carry an eleven-pound bronze drift-pin, green with verdigris and grooved to fit my three middle fingers. I don't use it to kill. I use it to taste the pipes.

You set the blunt end against the curve of a conduit and press the other end directly into the socket behind your jaw, where the mastoid bone was chipped away and capped with a disc of salvaged lead. 

*Clink.*

The pipe is hollow. The water has moved south.

*Clink-tack.*

The pipe is full, but it carries thin fluid—scrubber wash, acidic and hungry. It will strip the skin from your shins if you wade across the lower sill before the third bell-pulse.

I wait. The steam curls off my shoulders in lazy, spiraling plumes that get torn away every seven seconds by the downdraft of the overhead lungs. High up in the pearl glare, twenty meters above my head, a Calx-Bellows contracts with a sound like a wet sail snapping in a gale. A shower of white pellets clatters down across the manifold above me—*pat-pat-pat-clatter-shhk*—bouncing off the curved gunmetal, rolling into the grease-troughs.

I reach out a hand—wide-palmed, the fingers webbed slightly by thick scar tissue from old scalds—and cup a handful of the white gravel before it washes into the sludge. 

The pellets are hot. They smell like burned bone and battery acid. I crush two of them between my molars; they shatter into a chalky, alkaline flour that coats the tongue, neutralizing the sulfur taste that sits permanently in the back of the throat. It gives the stomach something heavy and mineral to hold against the vibration.

Down in the sump-trench below my ledge, the water suddenly drops three inches. The scum-line on the bulkhead glistens—a bright, neon mustard that bubbles as the air hits it.

The glass-bellies are already there. Thousands of them, boiling out from under the gantry supports like amber beads spilled on hot tin. Their six-toed mineral spurs make a sound like rain on dry leaves as they hit the newly exposed copper seam, their scraper-mouths spinning in counter-rotating blurs, throwing off tiny, hair-thin spirals of bright, scraped metal.

I do not look at them with eyes; the pearl-glare from the ceiling is too bright today, ionized to a fierce, flat whiteness that turns everything into bleached silhouettes. I see them through the soles of my feet—a frantic, high-frequency tickling that spreads across the floor-plate like spilled shot.

One of them gets too close to the main discharge port. 

A shudder runs through the pipe at my back—a deep, hydraulic *hiccough*. 

I don't need to see it to know what happens next. The bypass clears. A wall of sixty-bar steam, white-hot and smelling of scalded tin, punches out of the relief valve. The tickling vibration of sixty glass-bellies simply vanishes from the metal under my toes—snuffed out, their amber carapaces cracked and their glycol-insides vaporized before they can even unhook their spurs from the seam.

When the steam thins, their empty dorsal shells—milky and opaque now, like boiled horn—tumble down the slick slope into the lower channel, joining the silt of crushed components and ancient ferrite dust that the loricates will grind into meal tomorrow.

I shift my weight. My knees make a dry, popping sound that I feel in my teeth.

The shift is coming. Not a small one—not the five-meter depression of a single heat-channel, but a primary turnover. I can feel the three-mile sector-plate to the east groaning against its guide-rails, the ancient hydraulic grease in its lift-cylinders boiling under the strain.

I tuck the bronze drift-pin into the loop of braided cable that hangs from my hip. I reach down, take two more Calx-pellets from the rim of the flange, and drop into the narrow crawl-space behind the steam-header.

It is cramped in here. The clearance is barely eighteen inches between the hot alloy of the pipe and the cold, vibrating ceramic of the foundation lattice. It smells of sixty years of trapped grease, scorched dust, and the sharp, vinegar tang of my own skin shedding into the heat.

I pull my knees to my chest. I set my forehead against the lead-weighted bronze plate strapped to my shin, completing the circuit between my skull, my spine, and the floor.

The sky turns an inch brighter. The roar drops half an octave, thickening until it is no longer sound at all, but a massive, hydraulic hand pressing down against the center of the earth.

The floor unlatches. 

The drop is twenty feet—clean, vertical, falling through a curtain of boiling spray that rattles against the gantry like loose chain. 

I don't brace against it. You never fight the fall; if you stiffen, the landing will drive your femur straight up through your hip-socket. You stay soft, like the tallow-worms. You let your waxy fat take the shock, let the grounding wires in your heels spark once against the receiving rails as the sector settles with a ground-shaking, iron *CLANG* that sends a sheet of gray water fifty feet into the air.

The water comes down. It washes over my knees, warm and thick as melted lard, carrying the smell of freshly scraped copper and the sharp, clean ozone of a thousand broken relays.

I sit in the dark behind the pipe, waiting for the drainage to clear, chewing the chalk between my teeth, feeling the great, blind heart of the floor begin its next stroke. 

It is a good cycle. The heat is steady. The water is running gray, not black. The machine is breathing, and so am I.
