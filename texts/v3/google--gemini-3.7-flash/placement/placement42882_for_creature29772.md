I would not be at the top, where the Wedge-Strider levers its long ivory calipers across the void. That height is too geometric, too dry, too thin even for rot. 

Nor would I be the downy ones on the outside surface—the ones who feel the draft from the plains nine thousand meters down, who twitch when the heavy, single drops of tropospheric condensate fall through the dark to hit their backs.

I would be deeper in, where the air has already been used three times.

At first, I think I am the tallow itself: the thick, amber paste of those who lost their grip forty years ago, pressed so flat against the basalt that their shells have become a kind of slate-colored veneer, cold and sweet-smelling, holding the heat of the living tiers like coals banked under ash. There is an immense, quiet comfort in that layer. It has no horizon. It does not know the sky exists. It knows only the absolute, unyielding plane of the glass-black mountain on one side, and the shuddering, three-deep wall of living bellies on the other.

But no—I have margins. I have an edge that drinks.

I am one of the ribbon-things.

I have no eyes, no carapace, no throat. I am four handspans long, flat as a wet parchment strip torn from an old book, and pale as the fat on boiled salt-pork. I do not walk; I do not have limbs to anchor or break. I exist entirely within the shear-plane between the living colony and the mineral floor. 

My whole body is an intake and an exhale of a different sort. Through my left and right edges—thin as shaved horn—I draw the rendered suet of the smothered generations directly into my flesh. It does not digest; it simply passes through my cellular grain, cooled and filtered by the bitter stone, turning my center into a dense, translucent waxy leaf. 

And from my trailing end, I weep. 

I weep lukewarm, glass-clear water. It is not waste; it is the irrigation of the chimney. It runs down the basalt in quiet, hair-fine braids, sliding beneath the anchor-spurs of the core-bodies, keeping the lower tiers from drying into brittle, wind-snapped husks before the mountain can hoist them another millimeter into the freezing sky.

I live in the acoustic crush. 

When the tectonic root of the peak hitches upward—a dull, tearing groan three miles beneath our bellies—the hollow cartilage chambers of the millions above me catch the shockwave. They do not shriek; they ring. A billion small, dry resonators vibrate against my back, humming at a pitch so low it liquefies the tallow around me. In those moments, when the mountain lifts, the pressure drops for half a second. The living wall eases back from the slate, and I can slide.

I glide through the grease-seams like a drop of oil between two sheets of glass. 

I move toward the cold anchor-points, where the core-bodies have calcified directly into the rock. I know where the old human frame sits—not as a man, but as an irregularity in the wall’s geometry, an odd, cage-like bulge of green gypsum where the young ones nest. The sap from the lard-lichen above drains through his ancient pelvis, tasting sharp as vinegar, cutting through the heavy grease with an electric, biting clean. When my margins brush against his mineralized ribs, I feel the young ones inside him inflating their primitive sacs. 

*Click. Click-click. Shiver.*

They think they are climbing. They think there is an up and a down, because they can feel the flour-fine bone dust drifting down from the Wedge-Strider’s high, grinding maw. They feel the draft and they reach for the sulfur-scum. 

I know better. There is only the *inward*—the cold, unknapped basalt that wants to throw us off—and the *outward*—the dead air that wants to freeze us solid. 

Between those two terrors, I am the slickness that allows them to hold on. I am the moisture in the seam, the pale worm that keeps the meat from tearing away from the stone. I spend centuries moving twenty yards down a single fissure, blind and fat and heavy with the mountain’s distilled breath, listening to the vast, hollow ribcage of the colony breathe against the glass.
