I would not be on my feet. 

To stand upright in that white, shadowless space is already an assertion—a vertical line drawn against an endless horizontal plane. The dwellers stand when a leaf requires the blunt persuasion of a thumb, or when a seam needs the paste of their knuckles, but their standing is temporary, an occasional correction of geometry. 

I would find myself down in the shallowest bevel, where the flat courtyard meets the vertical riser of a three-inch step. 

At first, I am not easily distinguished from the slip that collects there. My body is long, flattened, and laid out along the grout line, taking the right angle into my own flesh: one side of me flush against the cool lime riser, the other pressed against the damp terracotta flat. 

I am not an animal that was born and grew, nor am I yet a dweller who has pulled their heels fully free of the floor. I am somewhere in the middle of that quiet curdling. My substance is denser than the clear water that weeps from the clay, but softer than cured plaster—something like the pale, fine-grained slip that settles at the bottom of a potter’s basin after the wheel has stopped turning.

My underside has no skin in the traditional sense; it is an open porosity. Where my back and legs touch the brick, the microscopic pores of the fired earth open directly into my own circulation. There is no heartbeat driving this, only the gentle, osmotic draw of the world itself. The warm, mineral-rich sweat of the masonry passes through me, leaves a faint sediment of lime and pulverized straw in my veins, and exits through the smooth, hairless expanse of my upper surface, joining the millimeter-thin sheen that covers the court. I am less an inhabitant of the water than a slight thickening of it—a temporary lens through which the white glare passes and is warmed.

My face is turned sideways, resting half-submerged in the sheen. One of my eyes—flat, lidless, the color of wet slate—looks along the surface tension of the water at absolute water-level. 

From this height, the world is not a sequence of vast courtyards, but an infinite sheet of light held taut by its own edges. I can see the minute meniscus where the water climbs a fraction of a millimeter up the stem of an elephant ear fifty paces away. I can see the double-winged dragonflies suspended in the thick air, their glassy abdomens perfectly doubled in the reflection below them, so that they appear like geometric diamonds hovering between two identical white skies.

I do not think in words, because there is no silence to break and no speech to anticipate. Thought, for me, is the registration of adjustments. 

When a soft-shelled turtle shifts its weight on a submerged step three terraces down, the displacement is tiny—a ripple no thicker than a single silk thread—but as it travels across the floor, it passes through the water under my ribs. I feel the microscopic crest lift me by the width of a dust mote and settle me back down. I feel the slow, synchronized throat-pulses of the skinks basking ten yards to my left; their rhythm is my rhythm, not because we are communicating, but because the temperature of the clay sets the pace for all of us simultaneously.

Occasionally, a ribbon eel, transparent and broad as a palm leaf, will ripple across the courtyard. It does not steer around me. To the eel, I am merely a slightly raised tile, a pale swell in the floor with the same texture as beaten plaster. It glides across my chest, its cool, golden-veined body aligning for a moment with the reed-marks pressed into my flank. Where it touches me, there is no tickle, no startle—only a brief, clean contact of two smooth surfaces meeting under water, like two wet panes of glass sliding over one another without air between them.

I have hands, but they do not grasp. They lie palm-up in the shallow basin, fingers spread wide and softened at the tips, slowly taking the impression of the tesserae beneath them. In time—though time here is only the continuous presence of the morning-after—the slip of my fingers will firm. The lime will cure a fraction more. 

Perhaps, after many unmeasured intervals of the hiss of forming water beads, my knees will draw upward with that slow, soundless hydraulic ease. Perhaps I will find myself standing, tall and ochre-tinted, looking down at the crisp, palm-shaped impression I have left in the corner where the floor meets the wall.

And if I do, I will walk twelve steady paces, find a place where the water has collected a fraction too thick in a seam, and with the side of my foot, smooth it out until the basin is once again entirely flat, entirely clear, and completely full. 

Until then, it is enough to remain in the seam: cool, porous, and holding the angle where the ground turns into the wall.
