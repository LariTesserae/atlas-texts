At first, there is only the tension in the bone.

Before there is a name, or a shape to the limbs, there is the contact point: the broad, flat ridge of a forehead pressed into the fillet where a horizontal shear-beam joins an upright pylon. The vibration enters there. It does not travel through air; it passes directly from the dull-charcoal composite into the thickened frontal bone, vibrating down through the palate and settling behind the collarbones like a heavy, warm sediment.

The tone is a low, unvarying C-sharp with a ragged over-harmonic—the signature of the third secondary return line, four hundred meters down, where the lagging has worn paper-thin and the superheated gas is beginning to scour the bend. 

I am where the sound narrows.

Not in the great hollow middle where the pelt-kites drift, and not down in the black sumps where the wafer-lice dissolve into grease and return as skin. I am wedged into the elbow of an auxiliary maintenance knuckle, eighty meters out on a cantilever that stopped reaching for anything three eras ago. Below my heels, the warm gloom drops away into pure, resonant nothing; above my shoulder blades, the curved underbelly of a high-pressure trunkline radiates a steady, dry 180 degrees—just enough to keep the moisture from gathering on my skin, just enough to bake the fine alkaline dust into the creases of my joints until they look like mortared stone.

If you looked for me from the lower catwalks, you would not see a figure. You would see a slight thickening of the bracket—a gray, leathery mass tucked between the stiffener ribs, no larger than a rolled bundle of insulation lagging. 

My fingers are splayed against the composite, pads flattened against the micro-textured grooves. They do not slip. Over years—or whatever slow unit measures the drift of resin-scent through this chamber—the skin of my palms has grown into the negative space of the grating. If I pull them away, there is a soft, wet sound of suction releasing, but I rarely pull them away. To let go is to become deaf. In the air, the roar is a chaotic wall; against the beam, it is an orderly ledger.

I am an ear that has grown fingers. 

Every few hours, when the floorplates groan and the great lung-pulse gathers in the lower sumps, I feel the pressure rise in the marrow of my shins before the draft arrives. My mouth fills with saliva that tastes of sweet ethers and copper salts. I reach into the cheek-pouch behind my lower molars, hook out a thumb-sized pellet of bacterial mash—cool, fibrous, dense as chewing gum—and work it between my palms with a pinch of dry ceramic dust scraped from the bracket.

When the thud comes—the deep, concussive *whump* that makes the suspended bridges sway like hanging vines—I lean out into the black air.

The warm gust catches my chest, lifting the dead weight of my torso just enough to make the climb effortless. My feet, wide-soled and prehensile, find the dendritic tracks worn by centuries of condensation. I move along the under-flange without looking down, because looking down yields only the blue pinpricks of the status diodes, and the diodes never tell where the metal is tired. 

I find the seam by heat alone: a hair-fine line of cool air bleeding into the warm draft, whistling at a pitch only the clavicle can catch. I thumb the bacterial paste into the fissure, smoothing it with the heel of my hand until the machine’s own heat begins to cure the outer crust, baking it into a dull, iridescent scab that smells of scorched turnip and resin.

Sometimes, another comes along the same span. 

There are perhaps four of us who know the harmonics of this particular pylon. When their heat signature crosses mine—a muted, ochre smear moving slowly along the rib—we do not give way. We draw close until the air between our chests is compressed and hot. We do not look for each other’s eyes; the milk-white film across them is only good for dodging the sharp blue arc of an open contact. 

Instead, we lean forward until the thickened bone above our brows meets with a dull, solid *tock*.

Through that single point of fused calcium, the cathedral empties its report. 

*The siphon at the sixth tier is running alkaline.*
*A kite died on the intake screen of the fourth fan; the air tastes of burnt horn.*
*The third pylon is tilting half a milliradian toward the void, its anchor-bolts humming a fifth lower than they did when our skins were smooth.*

The contact lasts three breaths, four. The vibration shakes my teeth in their sockets, sweet and terrible, a shared current that leaves the tongue numb. Then the pressure eases. The other pulls away, dissolving back into the charcoal shadows of the girder, their porcelain-dusted heels making no sound against the composite.

When the work is done, I tuck myself back into the angle of the knuckle. 

I draw my knees to my sternum, pull the loose, leathery flap of skin from my flanks over my shoulders to keep off the mineral shower that always follows the lung-gust, and rest my jaw once more against the steel-hard resin of the flange. 

The C-sharp has smoothed out. The paste has taken. Far above, in the thermal haze, a pelt-kite brushes against an exhaust shroud with a long, whispering hiss, blotting out three blue stars, then four, then letting them shine again.

I close the slits of my eyes. The hub breathes out; the hub breathes in. The bedrock holds us like an egg, and in the deep, warm dark, the marrow of my ribs hums back in time.
