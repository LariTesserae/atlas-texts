I am not out on the crest of the col where the current shears clean to the bare stone, nor am I deep within the basalt chamber where the water goes flat and still. 

I find myself in the hinge between them. 

I am wedged into the corner of a crescent breakwater on the south-facing saddle, right where the curved granite wall meets the native bedrock. Here, the hydraulic gale does not strike head-on; it curls around the unmortared stones in long, spiraling eddies that deposit a constant, heavy drift of pale lime over my lower flank. The silt is halfway up my second tier of plates. It is cold, dense, and comforting—a quiet packing of dead matter that seats my weight firmly into the notch.

My body is broad, low, and hexagonal, shaped like an anchor-stone that has forgotten how to fall. I do not have a front or a back in the way a creature that chases things possesses them; I have an *up-current* face—sloped at thirty degrees, heavily plated in overlapping tiles of iron-cellulose to shed the grinding sand—and a *lee* face, where the vents are soft, porous, and open to the eddy.

Inside, there is no warmth, and warmth is not missed. My interior is an architecture of pressurized conduits, rigid as bone, filled with the same freezing, sulfur-rich brine that leans against the mountains outside. When the deep hydraulic pressure shifts, I do not resist it by pushing back; I equalize. I let the 400 fathoms enter my lower margins through dense, felted sieves of vegetable fiber. The heavy salts settle into my tissue; the iron precipitates out into my shell; the spent, scrubbed water is pumped out through the aft-ducts in slow, measured thuds that match the swing of the great kelp bulbs overhead.

I am an elder node of this saddle, though "elder" here means only that the minerals have had longer to work into my gristle.

Between my forward hydraulic wedges, half-buried in the silt, I hold a three-reed cluster of hollow black siphon-tubes, bound together with braided bark that has long since mineralized into a kind of flexible stone. I do not play it with lungs. I route a fraction of my exhaust-pulse through its chambers.

When the cold front begins its approach from the deep fault sixty miles north, I feel it first as a change in the floor. The granite ridge hums—a low, descending D-flat that travels through the soles of my anchor-wedges before any shift in water temperature reaches the col. 

I tighten the hydraulic lock in my base. The gristle contracts, drawing me down two inches deeper into the gray lime drift, seating my plates against the unmortared granite block behind me. 

*Thrum.*

I vent a pulse through the shortest reed. The tone is sharp, wet, and high-frequency; it travels through the stone of the breakwater to the tender wedged forty paces to my left, who answers with a long, droning chord through a wider pipe. We are verifying the wall. We are telling the young siphon-groves in the lee that the shear is coming, giving their ballast-bulbs time to take in fluid, sink lower toward the silt, and present less surface to the gale.

Then the weather arrives.

It does not come with light or wind, but with an immense, violent thickening of the sound. The current hits the top of the breakwater like a hammer on an anvil. Overhead, fifty yards up in the dark, the black siphon-forest goes wild—stems whipping, iron-ribbed frills screaming against the friction of the brine, hollow ballast-pods cracking against each other with the dry, concussive clatter of dropped timber. A seed-pod blows three valleys over: a dull *boom* that shakes the silt around my vents.

In the middle of the shear, a stone shifts. 

Above me, the third block in the crest of the crescent—a hand-split slab of two-ton basalt—groans as the current finds a gap in its seating. The vibration changes from a solid hum to a rough, grinding chatter. If the water gets behind it, the crescent will fail, and the dead-water pool where the nursery-pods are taking root will be scoured to the bare rock in less than a front.

I do not move quickly. Quickness is for things made of gas and meat, things that tear under pressure. 

I slowly pressurize my primary forward wedge, drawing brine into the central piston-chamber until the gristle swells, dense and unyielding as oak. I ease out of the silt-bed, breaking the suction of three hundred years with a muffled *schluck*. The current catches my upper lip, trying to flip my mass over the saddle, but my ballast-stone is notched into the bedrock behind me, tethered by a thick, mineralized braid. 

I slide forward against the stone. 

I do not lift the slab; I become the shim. I drive my heavy, sloped shoulder-plate into the gap beneath the basalt block, levering upward with the hydraulic fluid held in my main chamber, then venting slowly to let the stone settle onto my back. 

The basalt drops into place against my iron-threaded carapace with a heavy, final *clink*. The grinding chatter stops. The vibration returns to a pure, unbroken chord that sings through the wall, through my shell, and down through my internal labyrinth of tubes into the granite mountain itself.

The cold pulse passes. The current slacks. The forest above slows its violent, percussive thrashing to a gentle, rhythmic swaying, each massive bulb swinging in its forty-yard arc through the black brine.

I do not return to the silt-bed. 

The basalt block is heavy on my back, and its weight is good. The pressure settles the joints of my plates; the iron-cellulose fibers in my shell begin to interlock with the coarse, flinty tooth of the stone. My forward hydraulic wedges, jammed deep into the fracture line, will not need to contract again. The mineral snow is already falling from the upper fronds, drifting down through the dark to dust my upper edges in pale, silent drifts.

I adjust the reed-bundle in my lower clasps. Through the tuned tubes, I vent a single, low frequency that sinks through the wall: *The col holds.*

From across the saddle, through thirty fathoms of dark and three separate crescent walls, two distinct, vibrating hums answer through the rock.

I let my internal valves settle into slack-water rhythm. The cold chemical broth cycles through my mats, leaving its heavy salts in the gristle of my limbs, turning what was once lever into permanent buttress. I am where the water breaks. I am part of the ridge.
