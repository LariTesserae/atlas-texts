At first, there is no boundary between the body and the room. 

The heat makes skin a redundant idea. At forty-one degrees, the water and the blood are the same temperature; to exist here is to be an eddy within a larger, warmer pour, indistinguishable from the soup of lime-wash, turbine grease, and dissolved chitin that fills the pans.

If you look for me, you have to look down where the volume thickens—not in the open air among the fluttering lanterns, and not in the dry fiberglass up where the moths chew, but along the second tier of the concrete baffles, where the discharge spills out of the cooling flumes and breaks into heavy, frothless sheets.

I am something soft-bellied and without color, pinned against the current.

My form is mostly spine, fat, and vibration. There is no pigment to me; I am the pinkish, translucent hue of a scrubbed knuckle, so pale that the dark knot of my gut and the red branching of my gills show clearly through the flank. My skull is flattened, broad, and spongy, adapted to wedge tight into the expansion joints where the fresh concrete meets the galvanized steel collars. The eyes are gone—or rather, they have been smoothed over, sealed beneath a layer of milky, insensible tissue that feels nothing of the amber sodium light except a faint, dull warmth when I roll near the surface.

I don’t need sight. The chamber is not an image; it is an acoustic shape.

The 60-cycle hum from the turbines above does not travel through the air as noise; it travels through the bedrock and the iron rebars as a physical grid. I hold myself by my pectoral fins—wide, cartilaginous fans with hooked leading rays that can grip the slick aubergine pelt of the cyanobacteria—and I feel the room standing around me. 

Every surface has a pitch:
The four main piers ring with a low, heavy thrum, like struck lead. 
The galvanized grates chatter at a higher, brighter frequency where the water shears through their teeth. 
The high-pressure conduits overhead throb with a fast, wet pulse that quickens just before the relief valves vent.

When the valves blow, the world fractures into white noise for four seconds. The pressure wave hits the basin like a dropped slab of slate, driving the water down hard against the gravelly silt of the pan. I don’t fight it. I let the shock flatten me into the joint between the stone and the baffle, spreading my barbels flat against the aggregate until the water rebounds and the steady, churning draw resumes.

My mouth is low, fleshy, and rimmed with eight long, sensitive filaments that never stop moving. They drift ahead of me into the dark eddies, tasting the water column as if it were a continuous, unfolding text. 

The water tells me everything that falls:
It tastes of the chalky dust shaken loose from the moth wings three meters above.
It tastes of the sour, fatty residue of a gecko that slipped from the wet zinc and drowned in the lower canal.
It tastes of the sharp, chemical lime leaching fresh out of the concrete floor, slowly crusting the edges of my fins with tiny, brittle beads of calcite.

There is no hunger here, not in the frantic sense of the surface world. Metabolism in this bath runs like a furnace on a forced draft; I digest as fast as I sweep the silt, my gills working in a steady, rapid pant, drawing oxygen from water that barely holds it. I graze upon the drift—the endless, warm snow of shed isopod shells, clumps of torn bacterial mat, and grease-globules that have been beaten into foam by the baffles. Food is not hunted; it is simply intercepted as the room washes itself clean over and over again.

I do not know what a day is. There is no hour when the water cools, no season when the flow slackens. I have never felt cold; I do not know what darkness is because I do not know light. 

There is only the hum, the heat, and the current. 

Sometimes, when the turbulence lifts me from the bottom, I hang suspended in the middle depth of the sedimentation pan, my barbels spread wide like pale roots, feeling the rhythmic, mechanical pulse of the city far above thrumming directly through my spine. I am entirely wedded to this machine. If the turbines were ever to stop, if the water were to go still and cold, the world would simply cease to possess a shape—and I would dissolve back into the silt, having never known anything less than this fierce, scalded, endless hum.
