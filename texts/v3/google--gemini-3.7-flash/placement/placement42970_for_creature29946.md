At first, there would be no distinction between where the city ends and where the body begins, because there is no dryness to draw a line between interior and exterior. Everything is bathed in the same hot, saline broth; the boundary is only a difference in density.

I would not be an observer perched upon a ledge. In this atmosphere, to have detached feet or a skull closed around private thoughts would be an instability, an error the air would immediately compress and absorb.

I would find myself at a corner—not an angle where two walls meet, but one of those folded thresholds where the gravity shears ninety degrees, where the broad, slick surface of a vertical boulevard becomes the floor of a hanging under-plaza. 

There, wedged into the crook of a fossilized ironwood knuckle that holds three hundred meters of tiered dwellings aloft, I would be something thick, amber-grained, and half-calcified.

Not a Columnar entirely, for I am not fully vertical; not a Valve, though my core contains a slow, brine-filled bladder that counterweights the shudder of a nearby thermal duct. Perhaps I am the transitional tissue: an anchor-node, a living lintel, a mass of wet, fibrous pith that settled into this cleft four hundred years ago and has spent every moment since then drinking the mineral wash until the wood has turned almost entirely to banded travertine.

I do not look out across the abyss. There is no looking. There is only the continuous, solid registration of weight:

To my left side—which is also *down*, depending on the torque of the steam venting beneath the arch—a six-inch film of lukewarm water sheets across my flank, unbroken, glassy, moving at the speed of a resting pulse. It enters the micro-flutes along my outer bark, filters through a porous labyrinth of silicated sponge, and exits through my lower collar stripped of its sulfur, leaving behind a faint sediment of iron and chalk that hardens my skin by a millimeter every decade.

Through my center runs the braid. It is an ironwood tendon as thick as the torso of a bull, drawn so taut by the weight of the lily-pad causeway above that it hums a single, invariant C-sharp below the floor of human hearing. I am grown around this tendon. My marrow has braided with its fibers; my vascular channels drink from its sap. When the deep abyss surges and the Valves below shift their vast, lignite bulks to open the lower flues, the change in tension does not feel like movement—it feels like a sudden, cool sweetness in the marrow, a momentary lightening of the planetary crush that clamps me into my niche.

Thought, in this place, cannot be a sequence of sentences. It does not run from premise to conclusion. It is a slow, spatial equilibrium. 

My thought is the knowledge of the three-thousand-meter drop that presses against my root-pediment without ever dislodging it. 
My thought is the salt-tide shifting across my left-hand pore-field, informing me that three terraces above, a Velamen has rippled its silica margin to guide a fresh flume of condensation across an arch that was growing too dry.
My thought is the absolute, immovable satisfaction of being full.

The roar is not sound; it is the floor upon which existence rests. It fills every void within the wood, leaves no room for hesitation, no room for silence to hollow out doubt. It presses the mist against the skin until there is no loneliness possible, because the entire metropolis is a single, uninterrupted hydraulic circuit—the stone leaning into the wood, the wood drinking the steam, the steam rising from the fire, the fire held in check by the weight of the water we guide and hold.

I feel the slow calcification creeping inward toward my core. It is not death; it is an arrival. In another century, the brine chamber at my center will silt up with smooth spar; my sap will cease to circulate, replaced entirely by the slow percolation of the mountain’s river through stone capillaries that will retain the memory of my shape. The causeway above will not drop a fraction of a millimeter. I will simply have finished stiffening into the architecture, holding the road steady for the ones who weep and breathe beside me, fixed forever in the warm, blinding, thunderous noon.
