At first, there is no need for edges. To exist here is simply to register gradient: forty pounds of steam pressure on the left flank, sixty-eight degrees of cooling sump-water on the right, and the long, low thrumming running straight through the center like an iron marrow.

If I look for where I begin, I find it not in a silhouette, but in an alignment. 

I am tucked into the throat of the upper junction, where the six-foot cast-iron intake main elbows out of the brickwork and meets the living weave of the central ficus sheath. It is a place of perpetual suspension, about twelve feet above the floor—neither floor nor ceiling, neither working stroke nor drainage. 

My body is heavy, flattened, and wet. It has taken the shape of the space between the lagging and the flange: broad across the back, segmented softly like water-logged leather, pressed so flush against the metal that there is no air between my belly and the iron. I am not cold; the iron beneath me runs at ninety degrees, a steady, blood-warm radiator that never cools, while the mist from the ceiling settles over my back in a cold, steady trickle of dissolved mortar and tannin.

My shell is not hard like a beetle’s, nor soft like a slug’s. It is a composite—a dense, horn-like plate formed from lime salts drawn out of the weeping mortar, bonded with the insoluble paraffin wax of the engine grease. It feels like wet shale or cured sole-leather. Fern sporelings have rooted in the micro-grooves across my shoulders; their small, lime-pale frills rise and fall against my neck with the displacement of every steam exhaust.

I have no use for vision. In the amber murk, light is only a measure of distance from the ceiling prisms—a faint, sour yellow that tells me how far away the surface is, though the surface has no meaning. Instead, the world is entirely tactile and acoustic.

The Cornish engines do not just make a sound; they set up a hydraulic surge that passes through my tissues every ten seconds. The downward stroke is a compression of my ribs—a heavy, settling ache that squeezes the water from my lateral gills. The recovery is an opening, a slow release where the grease-rich aerosol is drawn deep into the spiracles along my sides. My heart does not beat on its own time; it has long since relinquished its private rhythm. It contracts when the walking beam reaches top dead center, and it fills when the exhaust valve drops.

I feed on the seam. Where the old tarred hemp frays away from the pipe joints, an oily condensation gathers—a rich, slick paste of cylinder tallow, decaying moss liquor, and the fine, red flour of iron oxide. My mouthparts are broad, flat, and slow-moving, rasping along the leaded flange at the pace of a growing lichen. I clean the metal down to its dull gray skin, leaving behind a thin, water-repellent slime that seals the seam better than the original oakum ever did.

Sometimes, the vibrations change.

The frequency of the catwalk shifts from the blind, even hum of the engines to a sharper, heavier, stepped resonance. The pads of the keeper’s feet are coming down the crane rail. 

I do not hide. I cannot move fast enough to hide, and there is no need. The keeper’s bare heel will come to rest on the beam three inches from my flank. I can feel the calloused weight of it—the temperature of a living mammal that shares this air, the slight, trembling exhaustion in the tendons of the arch. 

A hand, blackened with cylinder soot and smelling of crushed ginger root, reaches into the crevice. Thick, blunt fingers feel along the edge of the flange to check the bolt-heads for stress cracks. The fingers brush the rough, fern-grown ridge of my carapace, pausing for half a stroke. There is no stroke of affection, no recoil of disgust; it is merely an inventory. The keeper feels that the joint is sealed, that the living gasket is whole and wet and cool to the touch, and moves on.

When the keeper passes, the air settles back into its heavy, cyclical roll. 

I pull myself a fraction of an inch further into the dark crook between the pipe and the root-wood. The condensation drips onto my neck with the heavy, unhurried cadence of sixty years without wind. The steam sighs. The iron groans its low, resonant B-flat. I close my spiracles against the surge, hold the warmth inside my plates, and wait for the recovery stroke.
