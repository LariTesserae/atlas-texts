I am not out in the draft of the abyss, where the up-rushing wind smells of crushed leaves and void. Nor am I deep in the dry heart-chambers where the bodies knot together on fern beds to sleep. 

I am in the throat of a tier-four retaining wall—in the crawl-space of an overflow conduit where the granite has bowed three inches over four centuries and never given another fraction.

At first, it feels as though I am only a thickness of the masonry itself, because my back is wedged hard against the ceiling slab and the soles of my feet are braced against the floor-blocks. The stone here is not smooth like the plaza flags that have been polished by thousands of generations of bare, oily feet; it is chisel-toothed, tooth-pocked, rough as sharkskin, cold only for the first ten seconds before the heat of my flank passes into it and the rock gives it back.

I am small, even among my kind. My ribs are broad and flat, like barrel staves, and when I breathe out completely, my chest flattens enough that I can slide another yard forward into the dark, pulling myself along by the tips of my fingers hooked into the drainage chink ahead. 

I carry no light—there is no word for it, only the distinction between *dead stone* (where no water runs and the air is stale and dry as bone meal) and *live stone* (where the condensation films the rock and vibrates with the frogs three terraces down).

My tool is a hook of tapir-rib lashed to a flexible rod of dried fig-shoot. Ahead of me, five feet into the seam where no one larger than a child can reach, a bundle of hair-fine ficus roots has found a hairline fracture between two sixty-ton blocks. It has been drinking the drainage water, swelling, feeding on the black bat-silt that washes down from the third-tier plaza. If it grows thick as an arm, it will act like a hydraulic wedge; if the next great cloudburst fills the upper reservoirs, the hydrostatic weight will build behind this wall until the megaliths tip out into the warm fog, tearing the whole garden terrace down with them.

I can feel the blockage before I touch it. The water at my knees is not moving at its proper speed; it has a sluggish, dead weight against my shins, rising an inch higher than the notch carved into the left conduit wall. The water speaks in a dull *chuff-chuff* rather than the clear, ringing *chitter* it makes when the sluice is open to the gargoyle drop.

I work by the tremor in the cane.

I slide the rib-hook forward into the black, twisting it until it bites into the wet, spongy mass of root-hair. I turn my head sideways, pressing my cheek and my ear directly to the basalt floor. 

Through the bone of my skull, the mountain enters me. 

I hear the millions. The *lithophones* in the outer seams are a constant, electrical sizzling through the granite, so loud they make the teeth in my jaw hum. Beneath them, deeper down, is the heavy, low-frequency thud of the log-lungfish turning in the central cistern sixty paces below me—the displacement of three tons of water against the dressed basin walls, slow as a heartbeat. And woven through all of it is the faint, dry scratching of the gutter-cats working the cornice above my head, their claws clicking against the diamond-relief friezes as they track the sound of the frogs.

I twist the cane. The root-bundle tears with a sound like wet cloth ripping. 

A sudden release—a gush of trapped water, warm and heavy with the smell of iron, rotting moss, and wet beetles—washes over my thighs and belly. It pushes past me, carrying black loam and fragments of crab carapace, rushing down the conduit toward the drop.

I lie there in the dark while the water clears. My skin drinks the wet; the sebum I secrete keeps the chill from taking root in my marrow. My toes grip a lip of stone behind me, steadying my weight against the flow. 

I am not lonely. In this world, loneliness is an impossibility of the architecture; you are always held in the teeth of someone else’s work. The stone I rest against was quarried by hands that were gone ten thousand cycles of the fern before my grandmothers were born, but the angle of the conduit floor is still true to half a finger’s width, still carrying the cloud-sweat down to the deep vats.

Above me, through a three-inch weep-hole that opens to the humid night air, a long-legged spider steps down from its silk stay. Its fine, plumose tarsus brushes the crown of my head—a dry, feather-light sequence of taps that reads the heat of my scalp. It pauses, recognizes the mammal-warmth that does not threaten its web, and moves on down the seam toward the outer facade.

I wait until the drainage settles into its clean, four-toned song: *drip-plink-slide-fall*. 

Then, pushing with my heels and shrugging my shoulders in the rhythm of an eel moving through mud, I begin the long, backward crawl toward the arcade mouth, where the others will be waiting with empty baskets for the silt I have loosened, their palms ready to touch my ribs to ask what the stone is doing inside.
