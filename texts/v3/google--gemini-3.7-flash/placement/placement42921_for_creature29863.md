The first thing that settles is the temperature of the skin. 

There is no border where the body ends and the room begins; the air does not take heat away, and it does not offer cold. After a while, you cease to feel your own surface as an outline. You feel yourself only as a slightly denser column of moisture suspended within a larger, warmer ocean of vapor, anchored at the heels and the small of the back.

I would not be on the catwalks where the arc-lamps hum, nor down in the black bilge where the blind crayfish work the strainers. I would be wedged into the middle stratum—the third tier of the tenement face, where the clinker brick has begun to bow slightly outward under the dry-rot of forty years of steam, caught between the wall and the soft, trembling shoulder of a copper low-pressure equalizer.

Here, a wooden cradle—slats of swamp-cured ash bound with tallow-greased hemp—is slung between two iron stays. It does not swing; the air is too thick for drafts, and the vines have braided through the ropes anyway, binding the cradle into the architecture until it feels like a calcified shelf.

I would be a watcher of seams.

Not an engineer, because no one here drafts diagrams, and not a gardener, because nothing here is planted. My hands are broad, flattened at the fingertips, the skin of the palms pale and yellowed like boiled lard, hardened against the bite of warm brass. Tucked into the waistband of my tallow-dipped linen wrap is a single tool: a flat, crescent-shaped blade ground down from a fragment of a locomotive flue-sheet, its edge honed against a wet brick until it can slice a fleshy vine-stem without bruising the core.

My work is very quiet. It does not happen in hours; it happens in the twenty-minute intervals between the long strokes of the central piston.

When the deep stone hums and the fifty-foot iron shaft begins its slow, massive withdrawal, the pressure in the copper pipe behind my shoulders drops by a fraction of an inch of mercury. I feel it through my shoulder blades—a subtle relaxation of the metal, a loosening of the vibrating hum that travels through the marrow of my clavicle. In that interval of stillness, before the counter-weight falls, I lean outward from the sling.

My fingers trace the path of a purple-ribbed runner. It is creeping toward the packing gland of an emergency blow-off valve. If it reaches the spindle, its pale, fibrous hairs will drink the tallow packing dry within three cycles, the brass will seize, and the steam will back up into the floorboards of the fourth-tier dormitories. 

I do not cut the vine down. To sever it entirely would kill the spade-shaped leaf above it, and that leaf is currently catching a steady, scalding drip from an overhead flange and funneling it harmlessly into the copper trough by my left knee. 

Instead, I take the crescent knife and make a shallow, oblique nick along the inner curve of the runner. It does not bleed sap; it oozes a clear, sticky sugar that smells faintly of stewed parsnip. Gently, with the flat of my thumb, I train the growing tip three inches to the left, tucking it under an inactive iron tie-rod. It will take the iron’s support, continue upward toward the light-well, and leave the valve spindle free. 

Then comes the sound: the vast, soft *chuff* from the rift’s center. 

The air shudders. A warm, sweet wave of displaced steam rolls over my face, carrying the scent of boiled river mud and wet charcoal. The hanging leaves around me dip in unison, a long, heavy green wave that rustles once and then stills. 

In that four-second breath of venting steam, someone two catwalks below clears their throat and taps three times on a cold water return: *rap, rap... rap.* 

*Sump three is holding.*

I lift my crescent blade and tap the brass collar of the equalizer twice in reply: *clink, clink.*

*Equalizer seated. Runner turned.*

The stroke ends. The silence closes back over the rift like warm wax. 

My breathing slows again. My lungs take in only two inches of air at a time—just enough to taste the iron oxide and the mold—because to take a deep chestful would invite the 104-degree damp to coat the alveoli with sweat that has nowhere to evaporate. 

I pull my feet back onto the ash slats. Beside my hip, a pair of wingless moths, broad as my palm and covered in slate-gray down, are resting on the lagging of the pipe, their feathery antennae trembling slightly as they taste the paraffin on my knife. I do not brush them off. Their velvet bodies act as a tiny patch of insulation over an unlagged rivet. 

From up above, through the dense, overlapping canopy of purple and emerald spades, a faint gray glimmer filters down. It marks noon on the surface—the hour when the carts roll across the iron grates sixty feet over our heads. Up there, the air is dry, thin, and full of wind that chaps the lips and chills the blood. People up there must burn coal just to stay warm; they must run to keep their appointments; they must shout to be heard over the racket of their own speed.

Down here, the machine breathes for us once every twenty minutes. The stone holds the heat of a buried fire that will outlast the city above. The vines weave us into the iron, and the iron keeps the stone from collapsing into our beds. 

I close my eyes. Beneath my ribs, my heart slows its rhythm to match the long, hydraulic creep of the accumulator down in the sump. I wait for the next drop in pressure. I wait for the iron to sigh.
