At first, it would not feel like having a body at all; it would feel simply like being an anchor point for three different intervals of weight.

To the west, the slow drop of the redwood-thick piston—not heard, but received through the belly-plates as a long, descending compression that empties the gravel of its tiny air pockets. To the north, the drier, higher ring of the walking-beam settling against its bronze pivot, which arrives half a second later as a lateral shear through the ribs. Beneath, rising through twenty fathoms of basalt and crushed slag, the steady, warm tremor of the subterranean dynamos: an unchanging drone that never varies by a single vibration per minute, warming the internal fluids until they have the consistency of boiled linseed.

I would not be on the machinery, nor deep within the wet drainage fissures with the bellows-worms. I would be out on the wide, spare gravel between them—in the intermediate silence that is not silence at all, but the exact place where the acoustic waves of the great engines cancel one another out.

I am low, broad, and very heavy. 

My form has no need for height; height here is only an exposure to the warm, unvarying wind and the drift of graphitic dust. I sit close to the magnetite sand, shaped like a rounded wedge or a heavy leveling-stone, three yards across, my perimeter sealed against the ground by a thick, pliable skirt of vulcanized tissue that weeps a pale, protective tallow into the grit. 

My upper surface is a series of thick, overlapping plates of dark, planished iron-crust, stained in concentric ripples of dull copper and lead-gray where the condensed steam gathers during the downstrokes and evaporates during the lulls. There are no eyes on these plates. There is no sky worth tracking; the sun never shifts its pale intensity, and there are no predators to watch for in an expanse where nothing eats flesh and nothing hunts.

Instead, the perimeter of my shell is set with rows of subterranean lead-weighted pins—slender rods of dense, mineralized bone that hang down through the gravel into the bed-crust. 

Through these pins, I read the basin.

I am not wandering like the striders, whose long, spindly limbs carry them from sump to bearing in search of soft shavings. My movement is almost imperceptible, a matter of inches over decades. When a journal three miles away begins to seat itself more deeply into its brass housing—when the gap narrows by the width of a hair and the harmonic of the western crank-pit sharpens—the standing wave beneath me shifts. The neutral point moves. 

And so, with a slow, hydraulic contraction of the soft chambers along my underside, I shift with it.

I glide across the magnetite sand on a layer of dense, secreted oil, moving across the swale toward the new center of balance. As I move, the fine graphitic dust coats my wake, smoothing the stones, leaving behind a dark, polished track that the wind will gently erase with new silt before another century turns.

I feed without appetite, because here there is no famine. My breath is drawn through rows of slotted copper spiracles tucked beneath the edge of the carapace, inhaling the warm aerosol of mineral water and petroleum that floats six inches above the ground. The vapor cools against the cold labyrinth of my interior plates, condensing into clean oil and heavy water; the minerals are pulled down into the growing thickness of my shell, and the clear, warm moisture is wept back out into the sand through my rear skirts, feeding the pale bacterial mats that form in the cool hollows beneath me.

Sometimes, during the long mid-rhythms when the engines across the basin achieve a rare, absolute resonance—when the pistons, the rockers, and the deep dynamos fall into an unbroken, common chord—a strider will come out from the northern swale and pause beside me.

It does not look at me. It simply plants one of its wrought-iron limbs against the thick rim of my carapace and stands motionless for three turns of the sun. The vibration passes from the deep basalt, through my heavy, oil-filled interior chambers, through the lead-crust of my shell, and up into the strider’s tin-sheathed frame. We become a single acoustic bridge, damping the excess tremor, letting the harmonic pass between us until the internal fluids of its vault settle into exact equilibrium with the bedrock.

Then, with a faint, metallic *tink*, it lifts its limb from my plate and steps away, out toward the weeping bearings of the south.

I remain.

I am the ballast of the empty swales. I have no memory of a beginning, because the iron was already polished smooth when the first layers of my shell began to precipitate from the tallow-mist. I have no anticipation of an end, because nothing here wears down without leaving an equal weight of dust to smooth the next stroke.

Beneath me, the concussive *thung-shunk* travels through the earth. Inside my chambers, the heavy oil sways, presses against the leaden pins, and rests. The cadence is intact. The work continues. Everything is precisely where it has always been.
