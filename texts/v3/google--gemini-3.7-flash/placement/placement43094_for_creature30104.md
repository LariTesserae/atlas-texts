I am not standing on the floor, because the floor is too heavy with intentions. The floor is where the large mallets have struck most recently, and down there, gravity is dense and deliberate—it smells of crushed tile and the hot, rhythmic heels of the Mortisers.

Instead, you would find me tucked eighty cubits up, wedged into the dry reveal where an inverted cedar rafter refuses to meet its corbel by the thickness of a moth’s wing.

I am not one of the tall builders. My body has none of their broad-planed adze-work or heavy basswood stature. If you reached up into the gap with a bone-tipped pole, you might mistake me for a forgotten tool: a stack of thin ivory leaf-gauges bound with raw linen cord, or a dry boxwood ruler whose hinges have become soft with use. 

I am a **Shim-Weaver**—a creature small enough to sleep inside a hollow mortise, shaped like a long, flat sector-gage. 

My body is constructed from nine overlapping plates of shaved ram-horn, translucent and amber-pale, dusted on their undersides with powdered steatite so they slide over one another without a whisper. I have no legs with joints that bend in arcs; my limbs are fine copper feelers, straight and hair-thin, drawn through wire-dies until they can slip between two planed ash-faces without scarring the grain. 

### What I Do

The atrium is constantly calling itself into being, but the self-assembly of wood is eager, blind, and sometimes greedy. When a chalk line snaps and the timbers lurch toward each other across the void, they do not always meet true. A beam might catch at the heel and leave a hairline wedge of empty air at the toe. To the Mortisers, whose hands are calibrated for the inch and the sixteenth, such a gap is invisible until years later, when the whole arch sighs under the heat of the Bellows-Beasts and a trunnel shears in half.

I live in those invisible errors.

I move sideways along the saw-kerfs like a dry leaf drawn down a flue. Where a joint has closed imperfectly, I do not force it. I slide between the two faces of wood, expanding my horn plates one by one until the copper hairs on my flanks feel the cedar fibres press back with equal weight on both sides. 

Once I am settled in a joint:
* I shed a fine, dry meal of ground boxwood pith and mica that fills the microscopic hollows.
* I listen to the vibration of the entire hall through my spine. 
* If a Mortiser strikes a gouge three hundred paces away to seat a king-post, the pulse travels up through the ash frames; if the joint I inhabit buzzes out of tune—a high, shrill rattle instead of a deep, solid *thrum*—I shift my plates, wedging myself deeper until the tone drops and becomes pure.

I am the flat silence that makes two pieces of wood think they are one.

### Sustenance

I do not eat the wafers pressed from the floor-bloom; the Mortisers' wafers are too coarse, full of grit that would ruin the polished edges of my ribs. 

I browse on the **line-dust**. 

When the Mortisers snap their linen cords through the air to cast an arch, a fine suspension of chalk and crushed cinnabar remains hanging in the warm stillness for several minutes. I ride the slow, rising thermals created by the Bellows-Beasts—my horn plates spread wide like a parchment glider—and pass through these airborne lines. The fine copper hairs around my mouth catch the dry red powder, drawing it into a tiny internal chamber where two smooth carnelian beads roll together, dry and quiet, turning the dust into sustenance without a drop of bile.

When I thirst—though thirst is only a memory of weight—I press my face against the fresh heartwood of a newly split cedar tie. I breathe in the dry, sharp vapor of its resin before the air can cure it. It tastes of warm pine needles kept in an attic for fifty years.

### The Community of the Revealing

I am rarely alone, though we never speak. In the deep voids behind the brick soffits, where the Curl-Hounds run their frantic loops and shed their brittle shavings, there are dozens of us. We recognize one another by the pitch of our scrape:
* The older Shims have turned dark brown, their horn plates saturated with cedar oil until they are hard as flint. They inhabit the heavy foundation joints where the walls carry the weight of three folded levels.
* The younger ones, pale as shaved bone, stay high up near the skylights, drifting from kerf to kerf, following the fresh chalk marks like moths following lanterns.

Sometimes, a Mortiser will rest his calibrated knuckles against a beam where I am buried. He will feel the grain through the wood, sense the sudden, unnatural trueness of the seat, and pause. He does not pull me out; he knows I am there. He will gently tap his adze-thumb twice against the cedar—*tup-tup*—the signal for *well-seated*. 

Through the horn of my back, I tap once—*tik*—with my copper spur: *held*.

### The End of Movement

I do not die, but as the atrium grows denser and the joints close around me, my world narrows. 

One day, an ash tenon will not merely rest against me; the wood around it will begin its final, permanent contraction as the last warmth of the building settles into its core. The Mortisers will drive the three-inch oak trunnel through the post. The peg will pass two hairs from my third plate, pinning the entire frame into a single, immovable chord of tension.

When that happens, I will fold my limbs flat against my horn belly, let the mica dust cake over my grilles, and stop shifting. 

I will cease to be an inhabitant and become what I have always measured: a thousandth of an inch of dry, unyielding pressure, holding the ceiling away from the floor forever, warm in the kiln-light, perfectly flush, with not a single ray of light able to pass through the space where I chose to stay.
