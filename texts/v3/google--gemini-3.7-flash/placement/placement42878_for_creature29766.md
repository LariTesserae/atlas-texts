At first, it is only a question of thickness—the three centimeters of hard-fired stoneware that separate the blinding, freezing sheet-glare above from the dry, vaulted hollow below.

I am not in the light, and I am not entirely in the dark. I am lodged in the throat of a riser.

My back is curved to match the inside of a vertical earthenware flue—a flanged chimney through which the upper moisture is supposed to drop, drop, and settle into the warm cisterns of the under-croft. But I have packed myself into the bend where the water is asked to change its mind.

My knees are drawn high against my ribs, wedged between the cool unglazed ribs of the conduit. The soles of my feet, broad and calloused to the density of dried horn, press flat against the downward lip to feel the tremor of the valley. Through them, I do not feel steps; I feel the weight of the water. When a tender three tiers above shifts a slate weir by half an inch, the change in hydrostatic head travels down through the clay like an ache through a bone. It arrives at the ball of my big toe as a faint, rhythmic pulse—three beats, a pause, a release.

I have ceased to use the horn scraper. My visor—two pieces of dark cleaved slate tied with copper wire through the bridge of the nose—rests beside me on a dry brick ledge in the wall of the flue. In here, the slit is useless. In here, vision is only a nuisance that interferes with the skin.

The lime has crept high on me. It is no longer just a splash-coat on the shins; it has worked into the pores of my thighs, settled into the deep creases of my hips where the skin pinches when I squat, and cured into a pale, chalky crust along the ridge of my clavicles. Where the condensation drips steadily onto my left shoulder, a small, calcified mound has begun to form, no bigger than a snail, binding a fold of my boiled-leather skirt directly to the brickwork behind me. 

I do not break it. To break it would be to interrupt the draw.

The world above is frantic with light and geometry. Up there, the sun wants everything flat, clean, and measured in millimeters of clear run. The tenders spend their lifetimes shaving the grade, panicking if a basin sags enough to let a thumb’s-depth of water rot into stagnation. But down here, in the flues, the water wants something else: it wants to stick. It wants to cling to the rough, thumb-marked clay until it isn't water anymore, but a heavy, breathing slip that smells of distant volcanic ash, sky-silt, and the cold iron of clouds that died against the mountain passes.

My hands have grown thick and blunt from working blind in the drains. I keep my fingers pressed into the seams where the mortar has softened. With the edge of a thumbnail, I clean the microscopic gutters that feed the bell-slugs. 

There is one hanging just beneath my left foot. Its calcified tail-plug dangles into the empty drop of the cistern, heavy and white as mutton-fat. Every seven seconds, a single cold drop gathers on the curve of my heel, slips past the calloused arch, and strikes the plug.

*Tink.*

The vibration travels up my shin, through my femur, and seats itself directly in the base of my skull. It is not an echo; it is an alignment. As long as the *tink* keeps its interval, I know the upper terrace is catching the dawn-wind from the south. If the drop delays—if the sound stretches to eight seconds, or nine—it means the dust-crust on the upper pans has grown too thick, holding the water back with surface tension, waiting for the horn blade to come and shave it clean.

Sometimes, in the heat of midday, when the under-croft fills with the deep, baked warmth of the tiles baking overhead, a plate-louse will slip down the flue to escape the glare. It will slide over the arch of my foot, entirely flat, wet and cold like a fallen leaf made of shale. Its tiny cilia tickle the dead skin of my sole. I hold perfectly still until it finds the seal it wants against the mortar. We sit together in the dark chimney, pressed between the hydraulic machine above and the empty, sun-warmed catacombs below.

I do not think of the mountain that was here before the bricks were laid. That mountain is a rumor belonging to people who needed dirt. 

My work is simpler: I am the friction in the pipe. I am the rough place that slows the drop so the silt can fall out of it. When the water finally leaves my heel and hits the plug below, it is clear, cold, and stripped of everything the sky gave it, ready to be drunk by the sleepers in the dry vaults who wake with mouths full of brick-dust.

I breathe out. The moist air of the flue coats my tongue with lime. Above me, on the thin earthenware roof of the world, someone walks—slow, flat-footed, their vulcanized soles slapping softly against the wet red clay. 

One. Two. Three terraces away.

They are looking for where the run has hesitated. They will never find it. I have become part of the mortar; I have taken the grade into my own ribs, and the water is learning to move at the speed of my pulse.
