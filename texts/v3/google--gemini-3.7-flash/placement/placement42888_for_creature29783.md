At first, there is only the sensation of being pressed between two heavy planes of wetness.

It is impossible to start with a face, or even with eyes, because there is nothing here that asks to be seen. If you arrive in this chamber, you do not arrive as a spectator; the vault is too loud, too wet, and too cold to allow you to stand back. You are immediately drawn down into its mechanics—caught in the pressure of the mist, the mineral taste of the air, the low-frequency humming that lives in the marrow of the masonry.

For a time, you might imagine yourself as the suspension itself: one of the millions of plumb-lines hanging through the forty-foot drop of empty air. It would be a strange, stretched existence, anchored at the crown by a pad of hardened slime, dangling down toward the black sheen of the floor. You would feel the convective warmth of the chamber lifting you in slow, bowing arcs. You would register every impact—a falling beetle clipping your length, the weight of an acid bead quivering at your tip, the chiming resonance when two lines tangle and slide against one another in the dark. 

Or perhaps you would be the slow, heavy scrape against the ceiling: a broad amber dish of fat and chitin, creeping along the soft chalk of a mortar-joint, grazing the fur of sulfur-scum with blind, rhythmic strokes of your palps. You would know the vault not as a room, but as a vast, curved topography of clay tiles, feeling the shallow ridges of reeds and ancient thumbs pressed into the biscuit four hundred years ago, testing each millimeter for grip before the slow leverage of your neighbor shears your footing away.

Yet the ceiling is too exposed to the draft, and the lines are too fragile. The true center of gravity in this place is down where the weight collects.

Gradually, the perspective sinks. It settles beneath the skin of the water, onto the drowned floor.

You find yourself down in the saucer of dark red brick, where the cold does not wander; it sits. You are long, pale, and low to the tiles. 

You are a mud-dog, but not in the frenzy of the hunt—at least, not yet. You are in the long, still hours between the surges. 

Your belly is pressed flat into the hollow of a single floor-tile. The tile is curved, slightly uneven, bearing the distinct, sunken depression of a kiln-worker’s right thumb, pressed into the wet clay before the great plague or the great fire, long before the lime dissolved. It fits the soft cartilage of your throat with an eerie, inherited precision. In this indentation, the rushing drift of the pool is broken; the water here is quiet, shielded by the shallow rim of the brick.

You have no eyes to witness the bruised blue daylight filtering from the granite sluices, but you do not need them. The world comes to you as a dense, chemical tapestry. Through the scarlet, cedar-like fronds of your external gills, fanning slowly in the shallows, you taste everything the vault does:

You taste the chalky sweetness of the river-shell mortar being ground in the bellies of the ghost-crayfish two paces away. You taste the sharp, metallic tang of iron seepage weeping from a crack in the eastern wall. You taste the cold, greasy smear of sheep-fat tallow drifting on the surface above, left behind where a beetle’s shell shattered against the brick.

Inside your translucent flank, your two-chambered heart beats with a slow, thick pulse—one stroke for every twenty drips from the vault above. You can feel your own spine, a dark, taut cord running the length of your pale body, vibrating in sympathy with the dome. The whole room is an acoustic lens, and your skin is the drumhead it plays upon. You feel the *tocs* and slaps of the ceiling leaks; you feel the dry, slate-scratch of claws sixty feet above; you feel the tiny, wandering tremors of the crayfish chewing out their geometric mazes along the floor joints.

Then, the deep note begins.

Behind the southern wall, the ancient balance shifts. You feel it before you hear it: a dull, tectonic groan through the brickwork as the timber-and-lead counterweight swings, followed by the cavernous, percussive *thrum* of the siphon discharging. 

The floor shudders. The quiet water in your thumb-print cradle begins to pull. 

A two-inch wall of mineral-heavy water sweeps across the hall, carrying the cold smell of the deep earth and the frantic, skittering footsteps of the water-striders. The surface above you erupts into noise. A beetle, dislodged by the hydraulic shock, strikes the pool three lengths away with a hollow *clack*.

The stillness in your muscles snaps.

You do not think; the geography of the floor is already printed in your nerves. Your paddle-tail unleashes a single, scythe-like stroke that cracks against the submerged tile, launching two feet of pink-white muscle through the shallow wave. Your maw splits open, sensing the fat-slick wake of the struggling shell, your sensory pits burning with the taste of sulfur and ruptured meat.

For three seconds, you are the violent engine at the bottom of the world—snapping, thrashing, breaking the dark skin of the pool into white foam under the bruised twilight.

And then, as the flood begins its slow, gurgling retreat toward the purple throat of the Sump-lung at the center of the pit, you slide back down through the diminishing water. You seek the familiar ridge of clay, finding the thumb-print in the seventeenth-century tile once more, tucking your clawless limbs against your ribs, and laying your throat against the stone to wait for the balance to tip again.
