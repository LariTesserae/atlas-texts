I would not be at the top, where the sun is clean and the cold air presses against the quartz, nor would I be wedged into the iron jaws of the exhaust grates below. 

I find myself in the middle of the vertical drop—somewhere around the twenty-fourth meter, precisely where the upward plume of boiled coolant loses its lifting velocity and the downward draft of the intake grates has not yet taken hold. 

It is the *dead-shear*: a ring of perfectly stationary water two paces wide, trapped between the rising inner current and the falling outer wall. 

At first, I am barely a shape at all. I am the thickness in the water. I am that which gathers where two currents rub together and cancel each other out.

If you were to look down from the sunbeam, you might mistake me for a ring of dense, shimmering oil or a loose collar of boiled grease drifting around the central brass piston. But watch long enough—through two or three cycles of the venting valve—and you would see that I hold my form against the churning.

I am a torus. A slow, radially symmetrical ring of amber-clear tissue, three spans across, encircling the massive brass rod without ever quite making contact with its spinning, polished flank. 

My body is mostly water, bound together by a dense, alkaline gelatin that tastes of copper carbonate and old boiler lime. I have no bones, no carapace, and no eyes. What I have is a vast, fine web of hair-thin metallic filaments—nerves that have nucleated around raw copper salts until they are less like flesh and more like delicate, spun wire. 

I do not swim. Swimming is too frantic for the middle depth. Instead, I maintain my station through a quiet, continuous galvanic drift. 

Because the central piston is bright brass and the outer hull is pitted, river-smoothed iron, a faint, endless electrical tension hums through the warm water between them. My filaments catch this charge. I hang suspended in the field like iron dust between two magnets, adjusting the salinity of my outer membrane to stay exactly where the voltage is sweetest.

I live on the engine’s exhaustions. 

When the Veil-Eels hunt in the moss ten meters above me, the shreds of mucus and the dissolved tallow of their meals drift downward; when the Filter-Hulks grind their plates against the grates ten meters below, a fine mist of iron oxide and pulverized shell lifts on the thermal updrafts. Both meet here, in the dead-shear. My upper surface is a broad, concave lip lined with microscopic, cilia-like copper fringes that comb these drifting fats and minerals out of the quiet water, drawing them into the slow, circular churn of my core.

My life is measured entirely in the five-minute respiration of the stack.

When the pulse begins—miles below, where the iron guts of the condenser heave—I feel it long before the sound arrives. The galvanic field between the piston and the hull suddenly warps. The voltage spikes; the water tastes sharply of tin and hot grease. 

Then comes the sound: not a noise, but an impact that flattens my outer ring into an oval. 

As the milky bubble-storm roars up from the vents, I do not fight it. I let the turbulence catch my outer skirt. I invert, rolling myself into a tight, hydrodynamic donut, spinning freely in place around the piston as the boiling, effervescent plume washes over me. The needle-fish pass through my open center in their tight silver cylinder, their tiny, vibrating spines tickling my inner rim; the Veil-Eels whip past in the green moss like torn silk. 

For thirty seconds, the world is white, blind froth and deafening hydraulic roar.

And then, the *clack-hiss*. The pressure dies. The bubbles clear into tiny, rising glass beads that ping against the quartz ceiling far above. 

The water settles back into its heavy, clockwise glide.

I unfurl. My copper filaments reach out, finding the balance of the field again. I drift back to meter twenty-four. I am warmer now; the plume has fed me heat, and the electrical charge of the machine has re-strung my nerves. 

I feel the creature at the dome shift its pale, webbed fingers against the piston rod high above me, reading the metal’s hum; I feel the heavy Hulks flex their plates against the steel grates in the dark below. I am the bridge between them, though neither knows I am here. 

I am the ear that does not listen to the sky or the engine, but to the space between the two: the quiet, suspended center where the pulse of the great brass heart is always just ending, and always just about to begin.
