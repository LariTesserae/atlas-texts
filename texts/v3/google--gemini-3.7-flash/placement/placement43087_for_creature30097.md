At first, there is no boundary between my body and the load. 

I am not perched upon the wood; I am drawn *out* of it, like a hard knot squeezed into existence by the unendurable math of a settling arch. If you cut into the deep, dark crotch where a five-hundred-ton lateral beam bites into the fluted flank of a needle-spire, you would find me: not meat, not bark, but an inclusion of dense, vitrified heartwood and compressed amber, wrapped in concentric rings of high-tensile fiber that have been spun tight under three thousand pounds per square inch of hydrostatic hoop-stress.

I would be a *Strain-Loom*. 

I do not have a front or a back, only an axis of compression and an axis of release. My sense of the world is purely acoustic and baric. I do not see the mist or the pale, glare-filled sky across the two-mile gulfs, but I feel the architecture’s entire nervous system through my mineralized core. 

To me, the city is a single, screaming chord. 

The taproots down in the dark hum at eighty hertz, a massive bass vibration that rattles the silica plates in my belly. The rising steam vents whistle in shrill, fluctuating fifths. And the beam I am currently seated within—this four-hundred-foot rib of pale green xylem groaning beneath a swollen reservoir deck—is singing a rising, sharp-pitched F-sharp. 

The pitch is climbing. That means the grain is thinning, stretching, weeping boiling sap as the fibers peel apart cell by cell. The beam is failing.

I wake to that rising tone.

***

My metabolism is not chemical in the way of soft things; it is piezoelectric and hydraulic. The sheer, crushing tonnage of the failing joint bears down upon my outer plates, generating internal heat that liquefies the dormant, iron-rich resin packed in my central cistern. 

As the resin melts, I begin to move—not by walking, for legs would shear off instantly in this meat-grinder of shifting timbers, but by hydraulic extrusion.

I cast myself forward into the widening shear-crack. 

From my tapered leading edge, I extrude thousands of needle-fine conduits of vulcanized cellulose, driving them like micro-piles into the weeping grain of the wood on either side of the fracture. As the boiling sap bleeds into me at scalding temperature, I take the water, strip its dissolved silica and iron tannins, and pump it backward through my body at catastrophic velocity, spinning it into braided, mineralized cables—glass-hard filaments as thick as anchor chains.

I do not try to save the bridge. Nothing here is meant to be saved. 

I am an *interstitial weaver*, an organ of dynamic tension. My purpose is to convert the energy of a sudden collapse into a controlled, decelerating swing. 

As the primary beam finally gives way—detonating with the hollow, gut-shaking *thump* of a shattered siege gun—I do not fall with the dead timber into the fog. In the fraction of a second before the split becomes complete, I fire my spun cables across the yawning gap to the adjoining spire, harpooning the living cambium with barbed, root-grafted anchors that bite deep into the rising grain.

The severed half of the beam drops. The weight comes onto my lines.

***

This is where I am most myself: suspended in the raw, two-mile gulf of boiling mist, hung between two titanic spires on a catenary web of my own pressurized extrusions. 

The violence of the arrest is magnificent. The cables screech as they stretch, their outer coatings of amber smoking and vulcanizing in the humid air, turning from golden liquid to iron-hard crust in seconds under the heat of pure friction. I am stretched at the center of the web like a calcified heart, absorbing the shockwave through a lattice of internal chambers that hiss, vent steam, and re-pressurize.

For a few hours, or a few days, I *am* the bridge. 

The scalding runoff from the upper colonnades finds my cables immediately. Water sheets down the taut lines, and with the water comes the living wood. Green shoots of aggressive, angular growth sprout directly along my spun threads, chasing the moisture, thickening the lines into new, hollow conduits. 

My cables become the scaffold; the architecture uses my skeleton to grow its next span. 

As the new wood wraps around me, sealing me into a massive, braided knuckle of fresh timber, the light goes away. The sound drops back from a scream to that deep, subterranean thrum. The crushing load returns, pressing from all sides as the new arch locks tight and begins to pump its own boiling cataracts.

My extruded conduits snap. My outer shell is ground down by the shifting grain, crushed into the amber core until I am once again a hard, dormant knot of resin and compressed fiber, embedded deep inside the joint.

I lie there in the dark, bathed in boiling sap, listening. 

I feel the water rising through the taproots. I feel the steam building in the balustrades. I wait for the F-sharp to rise, for the wood to stretch, for the moment the world decides to tear itself apart again so that I might wake, cast my lines into the mist, and catch the falling sky.
