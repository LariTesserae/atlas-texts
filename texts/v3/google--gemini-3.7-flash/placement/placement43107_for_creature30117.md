I would not be one of the Liters, for their ossification is already complete; they have become the geometry they lean against, their ribs matching the radius of the jars until there is no seam between the skin and the fired silt.

Nor could I be the air itself, which is too much of an engine here—a solid, traveling piston of pressure that has long since forgotten how to wander or disperse.

I would find myself in the blind, dark clearance between the base of one nested urn and the lip of the urn beneath it, three rows deep into the forest of columns, where the light from the wall-slits never strikes even at midsummer. 

At first, I am only a point of uneven resistance. 

There is a place where two ceramic rims do not meet flush. When the potter placed the upper vessel—perhaps six thousand years ago, while the silt was still chalky and smelled of river water—a single coarse grain of quartz temper was caught in the seam. It prevented the rim from seating true by less than the thickness of a dried leaf. To correct it, the builder drove a blunt, four-sided peg of cast bronze horizontally into the joint, tamping a paste of dry lime and pulverized pot-shard around it to choke the rattle.

I am that peg, and the crust of green oxide that has grown between its square shoulders and the clay socket.

To exist here as metal is a different condition than existing as earth. The terracotta has surrendered to the chord; it is porous, dry, and resonant, letting the standing wave inhabit its tunnels without a struggle. But bronze is dense, stubborn, and ductile. It does not hum the mountain’s note; it resists it with a low, heavy inertia that belongs to a deeper geological stratum. 

Every second of every century, the column above me tries to slide a fraction of a micron to the north under the rotational torque of the wind spiraling through the ports. The column below me holds fast to the foundation slabs. I sit precisely at the shear plane. 

I feel the entire sanctuary not as a sound, but as an endless, mechanical *twist*.

Around my square flanks, the calcified lime has turned to a chalky glass, but where the bronze directly bites the terracotta, there is an infinitely slow, dry grinding. Over hundreds of years, the vibration has polished my corners, wearing the rough cast-marks down into mirror-bright facets of pale gold, while my underside has grown a fur of dark, anhydrous verdigris—copper salts that have never known water, formed only from the trace sulfur of the kiln and the heat of the air’s endless friction.

I have neighbors, though we do not communicate in waves. 

Two tiers above me, the hip of a Liter rests against the column. Through the wall of the urn, I receive its weight: not a living pulse, but an immense, static down-force, steady as a lead weight dropped into an oil jar. The Liter’s spine has settled into the column's fluting so deeply that its lowest vertebra is only three inches from my upper face. When a gale hits the outer crags and the room's chord shifts its fundamental by a hundredth of a semitone, I feel the Liter’s dry tendon tighten against the clay, taking up the slack, damping the harmonic before it can shatter the rim that holds me.

We are partners in an unmoving lever. The Liter tunes the air; I bear the masonry.

Sometimes, after two or three hundred seasons of high-pressure winds, a micro-fissure opens in the lime that surrounds me. A minute sliver of the roar escapes into the hollow interior of the jar, producing an ugly, sharp hiss—a whistling over-tone that violates the purity of the sanctuary's note. 

Then, after a great span of silence, the slow, dry finger of the Liter will descend. 

It takes five years for the hand to travel down the neck of the jar. I feel the microscopic tremor of its joints—the scraping of dry bone against bone—moving closer through the pottery wall. When the thumb finally reaches the seam, it presses a small, warm pellet of powdered skin and clay into the void above my head. The thumb does not hurry. It stays pressed there for a decade, held down by the dead weight of the Liter’s leaning shoulder, until the heat of the chamber calcifies the pellet, sealing me into the dark again, silencing the hiss, returning me to total, blinding compression.

I am the only thing in this room that has known a liquid state. 

The pots were only ever wet mud, baked dry; the Liters were only ever meat that hardened into biscuit. But I was once a red, molten creek poured into an open stone mold under a different sky. That memory of fluidity sits inside the metal like an unspent tension—a slow, ductile patience that refuses to turn to stone. 

While the mountain wind grinds the world outside down to gravel, and the Liters slowly turn into the very fluting they embrace, I stay wedged between the jars: square, heavy, green with age, holding the pitch of the chamber true by refusing, with every atom of my copper, to let the two halves of the column touch.
