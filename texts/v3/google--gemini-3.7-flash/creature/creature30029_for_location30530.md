To survive in the flutes of the gneiss, life has abandoned sharpness. Nothing here possesses hard edges, sudden movements, or a loud voice. The inhabitants—both the strange, low-metabolism fauna and the people who anchor themselves to the wet stone—have taken on the character of the mist: patient, soft-treading, and entirely governed by the pressure of rising water.

***

### The Under-Fauna of the Sluices

At the foundation of the city’s ecology are the things that live in the runnels, where water never ceases its downward slide over copper and iron.

The pale salamanders mentioned by travelers are not solitary vermin; they are *sluice-leeches* and *gutter-newts*, creatures with flattened, cartilaginous bodies no thicker than a folded cloth. They have lost not only their eyes but their lungs, absorbing dissolved oxygen directly through frilled, translucent gills that flutter like moss in the copper troughs. They graze on the microbial velvet that coats the inside of the water-pipes, keeping the city’s mechanical arteries free of silt. The mechanics regard them with superstitious care; to find a dead newt in a turbine strainer is considered an omen of falling pressure.

Higher up, within the grease-boxes and cable-drums of the funiculars, lives a peculiar caste of blind, wingless crickets. Swollen on mutton tallow, graphite lubricant, and the spores of the purple dwarf-ivy, they scuttle silently within the machinery. Their shells are soft and slate-gray, yielding rather than cracking underfoot. They make no chirping sound with their legs; instead, they communicate through rhythmic thrumming against the steel girders—a faint, dry vibration that travels down the tie-rods, blending with the hum of the city’s boilers.

Above the terraces, the broad-winged raptors—known locally as *cloud-skimmers*—sail without a single wing-beat for hours on end. Close inspection reveals they have virtually no plumage on their bodies; their broad wings are framed in dense, velvet-like down that does not absorb moisture, and their bellies are bare, pale skin, mottled like boiled pork. They do not hunt mammals, for there are none on the open rock. Instead, they track the rising thermal chimneys for "drift-blooms"—vast colonies of microscopic, air-buoyant spiderlings and spore-clusters that are carried upward from the deep, unseen jungles thousands of feet below the cloud floor. The raptors simply drift through the rising fog with their wide, shovel-shaped beaks agape, straining the warm air like baleen whales in a sea of vapor.

***

### The Flute-Dwellers

The human population of the massifs does not look like the people of the lowlands. After generations spent beneath the pewter sky, their skin has taken on a dull, alabaster cast, faintly translucent at the temples, where fine blue veins show through like rivers on a map. 

Because the air is thick with cold humidity, their lungs are cavernous. They have broad, barrel-like chests and short, stout limbs, built low to the stone to resist the sudden gusts that sheer off the dome shoulders. Their hair is rarely cut; it is oiled with rendered seal-tallow or paraffin and wound tightly around the head in dense coils, pinned with bone or copper bodkins to keep it from drinking the mist and growing heavy.

Their dress is entirely dictated by the condensation:
*   **The Under-layers:** Knit garments of dense, descaled mountain-hare wool, worn double-thick over the joints to ward off the deep, permanent ache of the stone-chill.
*   **The Outer-sheaths:** Heavy smocks of "felt-leather"—a pliable, water-shedding textile made by boiling mountain-fern fibers with tree-pitch and liverwort pulp. It never truly dries, but it never lets water penetrate to the skin; it smells of damp peat, iron filings, and cold earth.
*   **Footwear:** Their boots have no rigid heels. The soles are made of thick, horizontally laminated strips of untreated tire-rubber and hammered lead, flexible enough to mold over the slick, rounded gneiss, heavy enough to grant stability on the ninety-degree stairways.

***

### Language of Taps and Vapor

Speech in the city is an intimate, hushed affair. The thick mats of liverwort and the endless, low hiss of condensation screens act as acoustic sponges; a shout carries no more than twenty paces before dissolving into the fog. 

Consequently, the inhabitants speak in a low, sibilant register, rich in glottal stops and soft, breathy fricatives that do not demand lung strain. For distance, they do not yell; they use *rod-tapping*. Every tenement and tier is linked by continuous iron tie-rods and bronze balustrades. By striking these metal spans with small copper-headed mallets carried at the belt, a resident can send sharp, percussive telegraphy humming through the skeleton of the district. A mother calls her child to the evening meal by tapping a three-stroke cadence into the nearest window-brace; a sluice-keeper warns of a clogged turbine two tiers down with a rhythmic, ringing roll that vibrates through the bed-frames of a dozen houses.

***

### The Daily Cycle of the Mist-Tenders

The people do not measure time by the sun, which is only ever a brighter patch of gray traveling across the ceiling of the world. They measure it by the *Lifting* and the *Settling*—the great breathing cycle of the valley.

**During the Morning Lift:**
When the vapor begins its upward roar, the *Screen-Tuners* go out onto the cantilevered catwalks. Suspended over the abyss in leather harnesses, they use bone combs to clear debris from the millions of fine bronze condenser wires. They work with a swift, rhythmic economy of motion, leaning out into the blinding white draft, checking the tension of the vibration-screens. They can tell the purity of the deep air by the pitch of the note the wind plays on the bronze wires: a clear *C-sharp* means thin, dry air is approaching; a flat, guttural *A* means a heavy, mineral-dense soup is surging up from the low marshes.

**During the Midday Fall:**
As the gathered water rushes downward through the labyrinth of stone gutters, the domestic quarters come alive. The city does not use open fires—smoke cannot easily escape the damp flutes and would choke the terraces. Instead, cooking and heating are purely hydraulic and pneumatic. 

Inside the slate tenements, kitchens are built around *steam-lances*—brass nozzles tapped directly into the high-pressure thermal lines that run along the cliff walls. To prepare a meal, iron pots filled with dried legumes, salt-pork, and shredded shelf-fungus are clamped directly onto the steam valves. With a sharp *hiss-chunk*, superheated vapor is injected straight into the pot, cooking the contents to a rich, molten porridge in a matter of seconds.

**During the Evening Settling:**
When the updrafts die and the breathless, cold stillness settles over the massifs, the social life of the city retreats inward. 

Families gather in the communal steam-baths—circular chambers carved directly into the ancient gneiss, warmed by waste steam piped from the funicular counterweight boilers. Here, stripped of their heavy felt-leathers, their pale bodies glistening with sweat and condensed fog, they sit in silence on benches of smooth, dark slate. 

They do not read books of paper, which would rot into mush within a season; their histories and records are stamped onto thin sheets of rolled zinc and brass, bound with wire rings. In the bathhouses, the elders run their calloused fingers over the raised metal lettering, reciting the ancestral genealogies of those who first bolted the iron trusses into the blank stone three centuries ago.

Outside, on the hanging galleries, the gas lamps burn with a quiet, yellow ring. A night-walker, carrying a long pole with an oiled-rag tip, moves along the tramway with the soft *slap-pad* of lead-soled boots. Behind them, the only sound is the eternal, delicate music of the place: a billion tiny drops gathering on iron and leaf, trembling under their own weight, and letting go.
