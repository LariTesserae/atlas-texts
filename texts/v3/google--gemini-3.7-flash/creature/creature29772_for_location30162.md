Underneath the shivering crust of the downy climbers, the chasm is not a flat surface of stone, but an entire, self-generating soil composed purely of flesh, tallow, and shed chitin. 

If one peels back a handspan of the living carpet, the basalt is rarely exposed. Instead, there is the *stratum of the smothered*—generations of climbers who lost their purchase or were grown over by their own offspring, crushed against the rock and preserved in the bitter, oxygen-starved cold. Their fat does not turn rancid; it cures into a waxy, translucent amber grease that glues the colony to the mountain. In this dense, suet-like sub-layer, where no air circulates, lives the wall’s true digestive system: long, ribbon-flat creatures with skins like boiled pork and no mouths at all. These are gutless scavengers, thin as parchment, that slide between the pressed bodies like bookmarks in a closed ledger. They absorb the tallow directly through their damp, permeable margins, excreting pure, glass-clear water that trickles downward in endless, lukewarm threads to keep the lower tiers from desiccating.

Between the breathing bodies and the rock, the pressure is vast and dynamic. The climbers themselves are not entirely animal in the terrestrial sense. Their triple-hinged ribs do not protect lungs; they frame hollow, chambered resonators made of thin, horn-like cartilage. When the mountain shifts—a micro-fracture yawning or a sheet of basalt groaning under tectonic lift—the stone hums, and these internal chambers catch the frequency, amplifying it into the rhythmic, subsonic heave that fills the vault. They are living bellows, drawing the mineral-scented updrafts from the sulfur fissures below and compressing them against the cold slate, wringing the sulfur flakes from the drafts like butter from milk.

They do not reproduce by pairs. At the center of every square yard of wall sits a *core-body*—a climber that has long ceased to move. Its limbs have fused directly into the stone, its shell cracked open and calcified like an oyster’s hinge, turned into a static brood-crater. Around these anchors, smaller, hyper-mobile males—pale, shell-less, little more than knotty bundles of muscle and greasy feather-down—scurry frantically across the backs of the resting masses, bearing drop-sized spheres of biological wax from crater to crater, working in total, sightless panic before their brief, three-day lives are spent.

Higher up, where the chimney narrows so severely that the obsidian walls are barely a man’s wingspan apart, the air is thin enough to pop blood vessels. Here lives the sole predator of the rift: the **Wedge-Strider**. 

The Strider has no eyes and no tail; it is a creature of pure lever-physics. It consists of four extraordinarily long, jointed limbs of dense, ivory-like bone, spanning eight paces across, joined at a central, disc-shaped torso no thicker than a butcher’s block. The Strider does not climb by gripping the wall; it bridges the gap between the opposing basalt sheets, wedging its spiked knee-joints into the acute corners of the chasm. It moves with a slow, mechanical gait—one deliberate, bone-crack sound every few minutes—walking *between* the walls rather than upon them.

Beneath its flat chassis hangs an array of long, barbed feelers that drag lightly through the fur of the climber colonies below. It does not eat the creatures whole. Instead, its feelers seek out the sick, the dying, or the brood-craters that have begun to rot. With horrifying, surgical gentleness, a prehensile limb will dart downward, its tip split into three needle-sharp blades, lance a single, grease-heavy body from the shivering pavement, and draw it upward into a central crushing-orifice that grinds bone, feather, and shell into a dry, gray paste. The Strider’s dung is pure mineral dust; it drops back down the chimney in a steady, flour-like drift, dusting the climbers below and providing the abrasive grit they need to scrape the frost-scum from the glass-smooth rock.

And then there are the ones who tried to stay.

On the high ledge where the rib-bone poles lie, the skeleton of the harvester is not bone anymore; it has become mineral architecture. The mountain’s sulfur steam and the acidic breath of the climbers have soaked into the human frame, replacing the marrow with green-black gypsum. The ribcage has become a preferred roost for the youngest climbers—they crowd inside the thoracic cavity, their needle-spurs hooked into the dead man’s spine, their collective air-sacs inflating and deflating in the pitch-dark so that the ancient chest appears to be breathing again, slowly and with terrible labor.

The lichen has grown up the harvester’s legs, encasing the dried leather leggings in a thick, blubber-white rind that drips its vinegar sap down over the edge of the shelf. If one were to strike a light in this chimney—though nothing here has ever known fire—the entire vertical world would flare in a wet, greasy sheen of black basalt, pale lard-leaf, and the rippling, rhythmic twitch of millions of down-covered bodies, all inhaling as one, holding the mountain’s new, raw wounds tight against the troposphere.
