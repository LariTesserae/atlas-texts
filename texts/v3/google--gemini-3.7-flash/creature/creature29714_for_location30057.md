They are creatures made almost entirely of tendon, pale fat, and callous. 

To look at their skin in the yellow glare of an eel-lamp is to see the texture of a peeled turnip—dense, bloodless near the surface, and cross-hatched by the fine, permanent puckering of flesh that has never dried. Because true hair would mat with the mineral-heavy drizzle and rot against the scalp, they are completely hairless, their scalps polished smooth and rubbed with the same heavy, gray grease they render from the blind eels. This grease is their second skin; it causes the falling mist to bead into pearls and roll down their shoulders before the cold can bite into the muscle underneath, leaving trails of dull, gleaming track-lines across their gray-white backs.

Their feet are disproportionately broad, with thick, prehensile toes that have grown spatulate from generations of bracing against wet ironstone. The skin of their soles is not soft like the rest of them, but has the yellow, horn-like toughness of an ox’s hoof, deeply grooved to match the carved treads of the bone ladders. They do not walk so much as they wedge themselves into the geology; a person resting on a sheer slope looks less like a climber and more like a limp, pale growth clamped into a joint of shale.

Because the canyon is an inescapable kettle of roaring sound, they have ceased to use vowels. 

Speech here has been whittled down to a percussive code of high, clicking consonants, sharp tongue-slaps, and a deep, chest-resonant hum that can vibrate through the marrow of a companion’s hand when pressed palm-to-palm across the narrow gap between their thresholds. Over longer distances—from one dripping tier to the terrace forty spans below—they communicate with *clackers*: small discs of dense rib-bone worn strapped to the thumb and forefinger. A message travels down the fissure as a flurry of dry, wooden-sounding snaps, cutting clean through the low, hydraulic roar of the falling water.

Their eyes are wide, flat, and watery, with large, unblinking pupils that drink the meager light of the fat-lamps. In the dark, when the lamps burn low, a pale, reflective layer behind their retinas catches the milky phosphorescence of the bacterial sills, glowing back with a cold, greenish fire like the eyes of deep-sea predators. 

They live not on agriculture, for nothing sprouts, but on the tireless harvest of the seeps and sumps:

*   **The Midge-Catchers:** On the lower terraces, where the silt gathers in grey, quivering banks, clouds of blind, translucent midges hatch in regular pulses from the mineral ooze. The catchers sweep broad, greasy paddles of woven reed through the air, gathering the insects into a thick, fatty smear that is scraped off with bone knives and pressed into dense, grey cakes. It tastes of sulfur, wet lime, and cold oil.
*   **The Trap-Tenders:** Deep below the lowest inhabited tiers, where the fissure narrows into black pools of standing water, they hang long wicker cylinders woven from blackened reeds. These are baited with crushed midge-cakes to catch the blind eels—muscular, white beasts as thick as a man’s thigh, with skin like wet rubber and no fins to speak of. The eels provide everything: their white flesh is salted and cured into hard, rubbery strips; their fat is rendered in shallow stone troughs for the lamps; their thick skins are dried into waterproof aprons and harness straps.
*   **The Scum-Scrapers:** On the flowstone balconies where the pale-green anaerobic bacteria bleed down the wet limestone, elders and young children work with spoons of smoothed antler, peeling the bioluminescent curd from the stone. It is eaten raw—a sour, effervescent paste that numbs the tongue and supplies the vitamins that the dark denies them.

Domestic life is vertical, quiet, and compressed. 

Inside their carved cells, there is no furniture. To sleep, a dweller slides into a narrow trough cut into the floor of the cell, pulling a heavy, eel-skin quilt over their chest and resting their chin on a carved stone ledge that keeps the mouth and nostrils clear of the thin film of seepage that continuously washes through the doorway. 

Children are born into harnesses of braided sinew. Before an infant can stand, it is clipped by a bone peg to a safety line anchored deep into the ironstone wall. A child’s first toy is a smooth river-cobble; their first lesson is the *tock-tock* rhythm of chipping shale to widen their family's niche by a finger’s width each month. 

Death is not an interruption of this rhythm, but a material contribution to it. 

In a world without timber or metal veins, the dead are the only source of structural timber. When a dweller dies, the body is stripped of its fat for the lamps, and the meat is returned to the eel traps. The bones are cleaned, boiled in mineral brine until they turn as hard as porcelain, and handed over to the joiners. The long femurs and shins become the rungs of ladders; the wide pelvic plates are notched to make gutters and spouts; the ribs are split to make the stays for eel-traps. The city is literally held together by the skeletons of the thousands who came before, their calcified remains mortared into the rock with the same grey silt that builds the terraces.

Alongside them live the animals that have found a purchase in the damp machinery of the crack:

Small, hairless, flat-bodied salamanders, as pale as pork fat, cling to the door-lintels. The dwellers do not hunt them; they treat them with a passive, utilitarian familiarity. The salamanders are cold-blooded barometers; when the water above is thickening with toxic mineral silt from some deep-earth shift, their translucent skin turns mottled and bruised, warning the families to stop drinking from the stone spouts and turn to their cisterns.

In the ropes that bridge the void, colonies of tiny, blind pseudoscorpions live within the braids of sinew, hunting the mites that would otherwise rot the fibers. The dwellers encourage them, occasionally rubbing grease into the bridge-lines to feed the micro-ecology that keeps their footpaths from snapping.

There is no memory among these people of a sky, nor any myth of an open world. To them, the universe consists of two infinite, vertical planes of black stone, separated by three paces of roaring mist, stretching upward into cold, crushing dark, and downward into the deep, black water where the eels breed. 

They do not dream of rising or falling. They dream of the stone yielding easily to the copper chisel; of an eel trap pulled up heavy and thrashing; and of the steady, motionless point of yellow fire on the wall that proves, despite the thunder and the dripping cold, that they are still clamped in their place.
