### The Pelagic Air: The Drifters and the Spore-Cloud

The void between the spires is not empty space; it is a pelagic zone turned vertical, a buoyant ocean of warm, rushing gas charged with organic matter. 

The base of this open-air food chain is the *scud-plankton*—a dense, invisible drift of spore-capsules, oil-slicked fern dust, airborne cyanobacteria, and microscopic aerial worms that reproduce entirely within the suspended moisture droplets of the updrafts. Because the air never cools to freezing and never stills, these micro-organisms do not fall; they breed in the boiling updrafts, forming sweet-smelling, invisible pastures miles wide.

Feeding upon this soup are the **Drifters**, or *Cete-veils*. 

Seen from a distance, a Drifter looks like a torn scrap of silk or a discarded ship’s sail, thirty to fifty meters across, undulating with slow, hypnotic laziness against the blue drop. Close at hand, its architecture is a marvel of biological tension. It has no true skeleton, only an array of honeycombed, cartilage-like ribs filled with warm, lipid-rich gasses that keep it lighter than the dense, moisture-laden gale. Its skin is thick, semi-translucent, and patterned like the grain of old cedar, completely hairless and sloughed constantly by a fine layer of antimicrobial grease that keeps water droplets from clinging to its surface and weighting it down.

A Drifter has no eyes; in a world where wind moves at seventy knots, an eyeball would dry, pit, and blind in hours. Instead, its leading edge is lined with deep, pit-like ampullae that detect infinitesimal shifts in temperature and barometric pressure. It reads the sky as a topography of thermal ridges and acoustic pressure waves, riding the colossal bass notes that boom out of the *Coriath* as if they were solid walls, bouncing off the pressure shields created by the spires without ever striking the stone.

Underneath its broad, diamond-shaped body hangs a curtain of sticky, mucus-dripping filaments—delicate as spider-silk, yet strong as gut. As the Drifter wheels in the updrafts, these lines comb the scud-plankton from the air. Periodically, the creature ripples its body in a coordinated convulsion, drawing the filaments through a central, toothless, sphincter-like mouth that scrapes away the nutrient-rich paste. 

They never land. To touch stone is death; their ribs would shatter under their own weight in the absence of the wind's support. They mate on the wing, drifting in locked pairs for days through the cloud banks, and their young are born live, small as handkerchiefs, already falling and catching the updraft before they hit the lower dark.

Clinging to the ventral folds of the Drifters are their commensals: **Kite-fleas**. These are flat, dinner-plate-sized isopods with iridescent, paper-thin carapace extensions that act as air-brakes. They scuttle across the Drifter’s oily skin on needle-sharp pincers, feeding on parasites and necrotic mucus. When a Drifter passes near the green-cloaked flank of a spire, a Kite-flea will sometimes let go, gliding across the void on its chitinous vanes to colonize a bromeliad pool on the rock face, or to lay its eggs in the moss before dying in the unfamiliar friction of the stone.

### The Wall-Flesh: The Bromeliad-Tanks and the Flute-Dwellers

Where the air strikes rock and shears away into eddying, leeward pockets, the stone is not merely covered in vegetation; it is encased in a thick, spongy pelt of living muscle and moisture.

Here, the primary architects are the **Vane-Bromeliads**. These are not delicate flowers, but titanic, fibrous organisms whose outer husks are as tough as cured ox-hide. Rooted ten meters deep into the iron-stained gneiss by cables that secrete an acid to bite into the rock, their leaves cup together to form hanging reservoirs holding hundreds of gallons of warm, rust-colored condensation.

These hanging tanks are the freshwater ponds of the high troposphere. Within their swashing depths lives an entire aquatic world suspended thousands of meters above the unseen ground:

*   **The Needle-Newts (The Pale Six-Legs):** These are the blind, translucent salamanders that creep through the moss carpets. They are nearly boneless, their bodies filled with a dense, glycerin-rich gel that absorbs the brutal acoustic shockwaves rattling through the stone. Their six legs end in broad, circular pads covered in microscopic, vacuum-sealing cilia; when a sudden gust punches into a crevice, a newt can lock itself to wet granite with such force that its body will tear before its grip fails. They spend their larval stage in the bromeliad tanks, hunting microscopic water-fleas, before emerging onto the vertical moss-plains to graze on lichen-pulp and the dead Kite-fleas that tumble from the sky.
*   **The Harp-Gnats:** Tiny, wingless dipterans that live in colonies on the leeward edges of the boreholes. They do not fly; instead, they spin single strands of silken, resinous line out into the open air, anchoring the other end across narrow rock chasms. As the wind screams through the natural flutes of the spire, these thousands of tight lines vibrate at deafening pitches, shredding airborne micro-insects that get blown into the web-field. The gnats run along the humming strings, gathering the pulverized matter.
*   **Siphon-Snails:** Massive, foot-wide gastropods whose shells are flattened like river-stones to minimize drag. Their shells are not spiral, but parabolic, curved so that the rushing wind actually pushes them down, sealing their suction against the rock face rather than prying them loose. They graze the green felt, leaving behind trails of thick, rubbery slime that hardens into translucent roadways—paths that other creatures, including humans, use for purchase.

Deep within the dark, wind-scoured boreholes—where the air rushes so fast it can peel the skin from a man’s hand—live the **Chasm-Vultures** or *Whistle-Bats*. They are leathery, blind, and possess wings made not of skin over bone, but of thick, interlocking quills that can be locked rigid like wooden planks. They do not flap; they wedge themselves into the narrow throats of the whistling stone tubes, facing the gale with wide, gaping mouths lined with fibrous baleen. They are stationary filter-feeders that use the wind's own tremendous velocity to ram air-plankton, bugs, and lost birds down their gullets. When they must move to a new flute, they unhook their iron-hard talons, shoot backwards out of the cavern on a jet of wind, and lock their quills to slice across the current in a blinding, instantaneous dive before slamming into a new fissure.

### The Hollow-Breathers: The Remnant

Humanity here has not died, but it has been refined down to an austere, desperate essence. They are the **Hollow-Breathers**, or the *Cori-folk*, and there are likely fewer than three hundred of them scattered across forty square leagues of rock.

They do not live in villages. A "home" is a **Node**—a dead-zone deep within the labyrinthine interior of a spire where the geometry of intersecting tunnels causes the sound waves to cancel each other out. In these eerie, silent pockets, where the air is still warm and damp but the roar drops to a tolerable, heavy thrum, small family groups cling to existence.

#### Anatomy and Appearance
Generations in the high, furious air have shaped their bodies:
*   **Stature:** They are short, broad, and extremely low to the ground, with disproportionately wide ribcages and massive lung capacity to draw oxygen from the thin, violently moving troposphere.
*   **Limbs:** Their fingers and toes are long, thick, and hyper-flexible, with calloused pads almost like leather. Their fingernails are never cut; they are deliberately worn down into thick, spatulate chisels used to dig into rock fissures.
*   **Skin:** Their skin is permanently stained deep brick-red from rubbing themselves with ochre-clay mixed with Drifter-fat—a grease barrier that protects them from the agonizing wind-burn and prevents the perpetual mist from softening their flesh into rot.
*   **Eyes:** They have a prominent, thickened second eyelid—a nictitating membrane—cloudy and semi-transparent, which they can draw across their eyes when leaning out into the wind, allowing them to see shapes in the blinding gale without their eyeballs drying out.

#### Language of the Stone
Because speech is utterly useless outside the deep nodes—the wind instantly rips words from the mouth, and the rock's roar obliterates consonants—the Cori-folk are almost entirely mute by habit. 
*   **Tactile and Visual Signing:** Within the dark tunnels, they communicate through a complex language of finger-taps delivered directly onto each other's collarbones or forearms, or via rapid, one-handed gestures that mimic the flexing of vines.
*   **Bone-Chiming:** For long-distance communication through the rock, they carry small, dense gneiss hammers. By striking specific nodes of the cavern wall, they send high-frequency acoustic pings through the dense iron-veins of the spire. A mother can feel the vibration of her child’s hammer through the soles of her bare feet three hundred meters away through solid rock.

#### Technology and Survival
Their material culture is based entirely on the three things the environment provides: *gneiss, vine-gut, and drifter-bone.*

*   **Tether-Culture:** A Cori-folk never steps onto a ledge without two *drop-cables* made from the braided core of the high-tensile succulent vines, soaked in snail-slime to prevent fraying. They move using three points of bone-hook contact at all times. Falling is not a fear; it is the fundamental law of the world, like gravity or breath.
*   **Drifter-Hunting:** Once or twice a year, when a great Drifter rides a low-pressure wave that brings it within fifty meters of a cavern mouth, the hunters engage in *Leap-Hooking*. They fire weighted harpoons made of fire-hardened hardwood pegs lashed to long vine-ropes into the creature’s leathery trailing edge. They do not pull the leviathan in—that would rip the platform from the stone. Instead, they ride the rope out into the void, hanging beneath the creature in the whistling blue, rapidly slicing strips of lipid-rich blubber and skin with flint scrapers before rappelling down the trailing tether back to their rock-perch as the dying beast sweeps past the spire.
*   **Water and Foraging:** They drink from the hanging bromeliad pools, scooping out the sweet, amber water with hollowed gourd-bladders. They harvest the fat, oily grubs of the Harp-Gnats, dry the felt-moss into flat, edible cakes, and preserve the gelatinous meat of the Needle-Newts in stone jars sealed with snail-grease.

#### The Spirit of the Spires
The Cori-folk do not worship gods; they worship the **Air-Currents** and the **Flutes**. 

They believe that the spires are the ribcage of an ancient, petrified mother whose breath is the updraft, and that the world below—the unseen floor of the abyss—is the place where breath goes to die. To fall is not merely to perish; it is to be swallowed by the cold silence at the bottom of the world.

When an elder grows too weak to hold their weight on the ladder-pegs, or when their joints swell so badly from the damp that they can no longer climb, they perform their last rite: the *Un-Tethering*. They do not jump. They climb out to the most exposed, wind-ward parabolic prow of their spire, strip the ochre-grease from their skin so the air may claim them, and lean forward into the seventy-knot gale. 

For several seconds, the furious, warm updraft holds them suspended against the sky, hovering weightless above the kilometers of blue mist, before the wind shifts, the updraft tears, and the void takes them down into the humming dark.
