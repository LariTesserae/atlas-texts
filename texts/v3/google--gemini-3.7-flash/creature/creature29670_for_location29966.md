Because nothing rots and nothing falls, life in this basin does not measure itself against the clock of decay. There are no scavengers, for there is no carrion; there are no predators of the tearing, pursuing kind, for blood cannot easily run in air as heavy and still as this without sealing instantly into a glassy amber. 

The things that dwell here are things of accretion, surface tension, and pressure.

***

### The Shell-Carved and the Capillary

The thumb-sized creature glimpsed upon the polygonal stems is only the most apparent form of a wide and patient genus: the **Loricates of the Rind**. 

They are not insects, nor true mollusks, though they share the unblemished alabaster sheen of fine porcelain. Their shells are not grown in spirals of panic or defense, but in clean, hexagonal plates that match the cellular geometry of the plants beneath them. They possess no articulated limbs; instead, their undersides are wide pads of dense, micro-grooved velvet that advance by hydraulic creep—a slow, microscopic shifting of internal fluids that carries them forward by mere millimeters between one hour and the next.

They do not chew the succulents. To breach the skin of an aqueduct-stem would invite a catastrophic burst of pressurized sap. Instead, they are *siphoners of the film*. With mouthparts refined into triple-beveled quartz needles, they draw exclusively from the static dew—the viscous condensation that coats the plant flesh. Because this dew is laden with volatile mineral salts pulled up from the earth through thousands of miles of root-flesh, the Loricates do not produce waste; their metabolisms are total and closed. Every drop they ingest is converted directly into the growth of their porcelain carapaces or extruded behind them as a microscopic, glass-clear track that permanently welds itself to the leaf’s cuticle.

When two of these creatures meet upon the facet of a stem, they do not fight or mate in the quick manner of the upper world. They simply merge shells along their touching seams, their carapaces fusing over centuries into a single, double-vaulted dome that continues to drink and creep as one.

***

### The Lenticulars of the Pools

In the deep hollows where the immense leaf-stems overlap, the trapped pools of distilled water support a life made entirely of optical interference.

Resting on the surface tension of these still, clear reservoirs are the **Lenticular Disks**—creatures as flat and clear as optical lenses. They have no organs that can be seen by the unaided eye, appearing only as subtle, circular warps in the reflection of the selenite ceiling. They are composed of a dense, living jelly that is ninety-nine percent heavy water, bound within a membrane so tough and elastic that a stone dropped upon them would bounce without sinking.

The Lenticulars feed upon the pure light itself, but not as plants do. Their translucent bodies act as magnifying prisms, focusing the blinding white-gold beams of the vault downward into concentrated points of heat within the cool water beneath them. In those tiny, superheated focal points, microscopic mineral blooms precipitate out of the solution like floating flakes of mica. The Lenticular then dips a gossamer filament into its own focal point, drawing the cured mineral back into its rim to maintain its structural rigidity.

They reproduce only when a rare drop of condensation falls from a leaf above—the heavy, echoing *tock* that disturbs the basin’s stillness. The ripple sends a shudder through the Lenticulars; along their edges, small, perfect lenses detach and drift away across the water, each an exact, silent replica of the parent, waiting for the surface to become mirror-flat again.

***

### The Sap-Vessels (The Internal Fauna)

Far deeper, within the translucent green interior of the succulent continent, life exists in a state of perpetual high pressure. 

Through the great polygonal aqueducts, where the clear, viscous sap moves in slow, pressurized surges toward the upper fronds, drift the **Aulons**. They are ribbon-like and blind, forty cubits long but no thicker than a parchment leaf, composed of the same clear, living resin as the sap they inhabit. One can only see them from the outside when they pass near the glass-smooth walls of a stem: a momentary, shimmering refractive bend in the green light, like oil poured through warm water.

The Aulons are the basin’s valves. They possess no nervous system in the traditional sense, but their bodies expand and contract in response to the hydrostatic balance of the flora. When a section of the continent swells with too much solar moisture, an Aulon wedges its flat, cartilaginous head into the plant's internal channels, redirecting the hydraulic pulse toward the lower, drier root-canyons. They do not die; when their vitality thins, their flesh simply crystallizes, becoming part of the plant’s rigid internal scaffolding, an immutable strut of amber around which the sap flows forever.

***

### The Masonry-Keepers

And then there are those who sit upon the basalt.

The dry-stone walls and square lintels were not abandoned; their builders never left. They are still there, embedded where the living flesh of the fronds has risen around them like slow green wax.

They were once men, perhaps, or whatever lineage of thinking beings chipped the basalt with such immaculate precision before the quartz vault was sealed. Under the unwavering noon, their descendants did not perish—for death here requires rot, and the air is an aseptic kiln. Instead, they underwent a long, quiet vitrification.

They sit upon the lintels, their legs and torsos encased in the cool, rubbery mass of the succulents that have gently climbed their frames. Their skin has lost all color, taking on the smooth, matte texture of peeled almond meat, utterly hairless and sealed against the air by a natural, translucent wax. Their eyes, permanently open to the blinding selenite sky, have milked over into smooth spheres of chalcedony that no longer perceive shapes, only the pure, absolute gradient of light.

They do not speak. The pressurized silence of the basin has entered their throats. Their respiration is an event that occurs over the span of weeks: a single, imperceptible drawing-in of the hot, fragrant gel-air, held in lungs that have turned fibrous and spongy, followed days later by a faint, sweet exhalation that smells of crushed mint and warm paraffin.

Their thoughts are not narrative. Saturated by the continuous hum of the sap-engines beneath them and the unchanging brilliance above, their consciousness has flattened into the landscape’s own equilibrium. They are aware of the hydrostatic tension in the root-continent; they feel the slow, century-long swelling of a frond against their ribs as a slight, comforting increase in pressure. 

Occasionally, after the passage of an age, one of these basalt-dwellers will lift a single, glass-smooth hand from their knee. The movement takes three days to complete. The hand moves toward a bead of condensation hanging from an overhanging frond, gently touches the surface of the droplet, and allows the viscous water to be absorbed directly through the skin of the palm. Then, with the same agonizing, peaceful slowness, the arm descends again to rest upon the stone.

They are not waiting for the sky to break. They are not waiting for the night. They are merely complete, pinned in the white gold of the noon, as permanent as the stone beneath them and as living as the green world that holds them fast.
