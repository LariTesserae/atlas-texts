The creatures that survive down here do not hurry. Movement in the rift is measured against the heat, which penalizes anything that raises its core temperature by even half a degree. Metabolism here is a low, smoldering burn.

In the deepest gutters, where the condensate drains into warm, oil-slicked sumps between the masonry footings, there are pale, blind crayfish no longer than a thumb. They have lost both pigment and defensive vigor; their carapaces are semi-flexible, like boiled parchment, allowing them to squeeze into the perforated brass strainer baskets of the bilge intakes to graze on bacterial slime and the soft, decaying fringes of the liverworts. Alongside them crawl broad, wingless moths—their lineages having discarded flight three generations after the rift was sealed—which creep along the boiler jackets on padded tarsi, using heavy, feathery antennae to locate the greasy paraffin cakes applied to the valve packings. They do not flutter; they glide like velvet slugs over the sweating iron.

Higher up, where the copper flues cross between the tenements, lives a breed of rat that has forgotten the cold. They are nearly hairless, their slate-pink hides coated in a natural sebum that repels the constant, warm drip from the ceiling. Their claws are blunt and shovel-like, adapted for tearing apart the fibrous taproots that choke the brickwork rather than digging through earth. They are slow, docile creatures that spend days draped like wet bladders over the lagging of low-pressure steam mains, their bellies absorbing the steady thrum of the pipes, feeding on the swollen, starch-rich tubers that sprout directly from the cracks in the masonry mortar.

And in the rooms behind the green-stained cedar shutters, the people remain. 

They are the descendants of the original shift-drillers and pipe-fitters who stayed when the cranes were dismantled and the upper threshold was paved over, though they bear little resemblance to surface folk now. They are extraordinarily lean, their skin possessing the smooth, unblemished pallor of peeled turnip, faintly stained yellow around the eyes and fingernails from the ambient sulfur. Because sweat cannot evaporate in air so completely saturated, their bodies have largely abandoned the effort; they do not pant or flush. Instead, their respiration is exceptionally shallow and slow, taking in only enough of the thick, heavy atmosphere to oxygenate their resting blood.

They move through the shoulder-wide alleys without the clatter of shoes. Most go barefoot, the soles of their feet thickened into pale, leathery pads that can tolerate the warmth of an iron plate without blistering, or they wear flat pattens carved from dense lignum vitae that resist the rot of the sump-water. Their clothing is minimal—loose wraps of unbleached muslin or boiled cotton that have been soaked in tallow to keep the wet air from clinging to their ribs like a second skin. 

Speech between them is rare, and when it happens, it is timed to the machines. 

To raise a voice against the ambient damp is to waste moisture and invite the heat into one's throat. Thus, they speak in the intervals of the infrastructure. When the fifty-foot piston draws in its breath, they are silent. When it completes its twenty-minute stroke with that vast, soft *chuff* and the long sigh of vented steam rattles through the broad leaves, they exchange whole sentences in low, flat monotones—words clipped at the edges, stripped of all unnecessary vowels, riding the mechanical exhalation before sinking back into stillness. For matters of urgency between strokes, they use a language of knuckles: two sharp taps on a copper riser to signal water rising in a lower tenement; a scrape of iron against brick to warn that a taproot has cracked an accumulator casing.

Their work is not industry; it is horticulture applied to iron. They are neither mechanics nor miners, but custodians of an unstable equilibrium. Armed with small, sickle-shaped knives made of filed-down boiler plate, they patrol the lattices of the catwalks, perpetually pruning. They do not kill the vines—to strip the liverwort from a main line would cause the uninsulated pipe to superheat the corridor and roast the tenements above—but they trim the growth with surgical care. They guide the thick, fleshy runners away from the relief valves, steer the spade-shaped leaves so that condensing water drips into collection cisterns rather than into electrical junctions, and pack leaking seams with mixtures of crushed root-fiber, lime dust, and tallow.

They eat what the rift yields to the heat. In shallow troughs hung beneath the warmest ceiling vents, they cultivate mats of pale, stringy mushroom that smell of almond paste and iron; they boil the starch-heavy nodes of the taproots until they soften into a gray, glutinous porridge that tastes faintly of coal tar and river silt. Water is drawn from the copper drip-catchers—distilled by the machinery, flat and metallic on the tongue, but pure.

In the green-lit half-dark of the lower rooms, where the red indicator bulbs cast long, blood-colored streaks across the wet floorboards, their family life takes place in almost absolute stillness. They sleep on slatted racks of iron pipe strung with woven bast, lying entirely still for hours, their rib cages barely lifting, listening to the drip of condensation and the distant, muted pulse of the deep stone. They have developed an acute sensitivity to pressure; an old woman resting in a tenement basement can feel a two-pound drop in the city’s hydraulic reserve through the small of her back long before any mechanical gauge registers the change.

They do not look up at the iron grates sixty feet above, where the gray, dry light of the surface occasionally glints through the street vents. To them, the rift is not a pit; it is an immense, slowly digesting belly that keeps them warm, keeps them hidden, and feeds them on the steam of a world that has forgotten what it buried.
