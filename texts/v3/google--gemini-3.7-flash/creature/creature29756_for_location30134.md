At the highest, narrowest throat of the chimney, where the warm steam smacks against the sub-zero roof and turns into perpetual drizzle, live the **soot-swifts**. 

They are small birds, but unnaturally dense, their chests swollen with immense flight muscles and blood so saturated with hemoglobin that their exposed skin—around the nostrils and between the toes—is the color of bruised plums. Unlike their low-altitude kin, their feathers are not glossy; they are coated in a thick, waxy preen-oil that repels the warm, dripping condensation. If their plumage became waterlogged in the vent, they would drop like stones into the guano pools; if they exited into the outer ice with damp feathers, they would freeze into solid glass within seconds. 

Their life is a violent alternation between two extremes. In the grey alpine twilight, they burst from the chimney in tight, screaming funnels, hurtling into the razor winds at sixty miles an hour to harvest wind-drifted spiders and frozen midges swept up from the lowlands. After twenty minutes of feeding in air that would freeze human eyes in their sockets, their bodies drop precipitously in temperature. They dive back down the maw of the cleft, braking with a sharp, wet flutter of wings, and slam into the chimney’s thermal plume. Inside, they do not perch on twigs; their feet have evolved into four-toed crampons with needles for talons, gripping the slime-slick basalt ribs. They cement their bracket-shaped nests to the rock using a paste of regurgitated beetle chitin, basalt dust, and a thick, rubbery saliva that cures in the sulfurous steam.

Sharing the upper vertical faces are the **pip-bats**—creatures no larger than a dried fig, hanging in layered curtains where the rock stays warmest. They are entirely blind, their ears enormous, translucent funnels mapped with pulsing capillary nets that radiate excess heat when the chimney grows stifling. Their fur is sparse and greasy, smelling intensely of vinegar and musk. They never leave the chimney. While the swifts hunt the cold sky outside, the bats harvest the internal sky of the vent: a dense, swirling storm of micro-moths, steam-midges, and flying beetles that breed in the rotting guano below and ride the thermal updrafts to the ceiling.

Midway down the chimney, where the stone curves inward like the belly of a whale, the bird-and-bat world gives way to the territory of the **tallow-mice**.

These are rodents that have forgotten the sun entirely. Their eyes are reduced to pinprick scars buried under loose folds of slate-grey skin. They are massive for cliff-mice, shaped like heavy leather gourds, with short, powerful limbs and splayed paws fitted with curved, shovel-like claws. They move with an eerie, deliberate slowness across the vertical rock, their bellies pressed flat against the stone to absorb its subterranean heat. Their fur is dense and woolly, permanently damp, and saturated with a thick, odorless tallow that makes them water-resistant and slick to the touch. 

The tallow-mice are the processors of the chimney's bounty. They graze on the copper-colored crust of cyanobacteria, gnaw the fibrous fungal brackets that sprout from ancient bat-roosts, and raid the lower nests for fallen eggs and dead squabs. Their metabolisms are drastically suppressed; they breathe slowly, drawing oxygen from air thick with ammonia and volcanic gases through enlarged nasal cavities lined with mucus-covered baffles. They do not build solitary nests; instead, they sleep in *knots*—interlocked clusters of thirty or forty individuals wedged into basalt pockets, their tails tangled together, forming a single, heaving mass of warm flesh that groans and shifts with the rhythm of the mountain’s breath.

Beneath the ledges, where the walls plunge into the permanent floor of the vent, the world belongs to the churners. 

The floor is not stone, but a warm, shifting peat of guano, molt-feathers, wing-cases, and mountain-sweat, several fathoms deep. It is alive from top to bottom. Millions of **black grease-beetles**, smooth and rounded like wet river pebbles, tumble over one another in a frantic, blind carpet. They feed so voraciously on the rain of fresh droppings that their collective chewing produces a faint, continuous hiss, like dry tea leaves being stirred in a bowl. 

Winding through the deep, warm muck are **white tape-worms** and segmented blind flatworms as thick as a man’s wrist, translucent and pale as lard. They glide through the guano with undulating muscle contractions, swallowing the organic silt and leaving behind clean, porous sediment that slowly sinks into the mountain's fractures. 

At the edges of the floor pools, where lukewarm mineral water collects in basalt bowls, crouch the **cave-crickets**. Their legs are five times the length of their bodies, bent like fine wire springs; their antennae sweep the air in slow, methodical arcs, tasting the damp drafts for the scent of dying bats or mice that have lost their grip above. They are pale, almost ghostly, their paper-thin armor completely devoid of pigment. When a carcass hits the water with a soft *thwack*, dozens of these crickets converge within seconds, walking across the surface tension of the mineral scum to tear at the softest tissues before the beetles arrive.

The apex of this dark, vertical world is the **steam-crawler**—a pale, blind troglobitic centipede that can reach the length of a human forearm. Its body is flat, banded in dull slate and ivory, equipped with dozens of delicate, elongated walking legs that allow it to flow across the slickest vertical basalt with terrifying speed. It lives deep inside the heat-fractures behind the chimney walls, emerging only when its heat-sensing antennae detect the concentrated warmth of a mouse-knot or a nursery roost of pip-bats. It hunts without venom; instead, its heavy mandibular pincers crush the skulls of its prey instantly, holding the body tight against the stone while it feeds, surrounded by the deafening roar of wings and the slow, heavy rain of lukewarm water.

Above it all, suspended in the gloom of the high spur, the fire-hardened pine poles left by human hunters tell of another creature that enters this world only on borrowed time. 

The **high-altitude hunters**—men and women from the windswept tundra valleys thousands of feet below—regard the vent not as a sacred place, but as a winter pantry. They arrive only during the dead months, when game on the outer peaks has migrated or died. Wrapped in grease-smeared sealskins, their faces blackened with soot against snow-blindness, they anchor long ropes of braided yak-hide to the outer basalt horns and lower themselves into the suffocating, sulfurous dark.

To them, descending into the chimney is like climbing into the gullet of a living beast. The transition is brutal: in twenty feet of descent, the temperature leaps from forty below zero to ninety above. The air turns thick and sweet with rot, clogging their dry, mountain-thin lungs with ammonia. They must work fast, before the heat-exhaustion and the foul air take them. Using long notched pine poles, they knock the fat tallow-mice from their ledge-knots into collection bags, or hook down the squab-laden swifts’ nests. 

The mice are rendered down for their tallow—a fat so pure and low-melting that it remains liquid at freezing temperatures, used by the tribes for frostbite salves and eternal winter lamps. The squabs provide meat, and the hardened nest-cement is boiled into a sticky, protein-rich broth. 

When their sacks are full, the hunters haul themselves back up into the freezing gale, gasping for the thin, cold air they know, their skin steaming violently in the mountain frost. They pull up their ropes and leave. 

Behind them, the chimney swallows the draft of cold air, heals its temperature in an instant, and returns to its ancient work: dripping, rustling, blind, and seething with its own dark, fevered life.
