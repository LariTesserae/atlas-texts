In a place where earth has been replaced by kiln-work, life has surrendered the soft logic of soil and rot, taking on the hardness, stillness, and patience of the tiles themselves.

The most immediate inhabitants are the tenders of the grade—the people whose thumbs and palms pressed the damp clay before it met the fire. 

They are sparse across the breadth of the plateau, so low-slung and quiet in their movements that from a few terraces away they are easily mistaken for mounds of dark slate or stacked roofing tiles. They live entirely in the wet. Their feet, bare and broad, have soles like heavy vulcanized bark, cured by generations of lime-water and rough earthenware until the razor-sharp vitrified edges cannot bite them. The skin of their legs is permanently stained a pale, chalky gray up to the mid-calf, coated in a fine slip of river-lime that has dried and re-wetted ten thousand times. Above the water line, their bodies are lean, hairless, and dark as scorched brick, burnt by the raw mountain ultraviolet that reflects off the millions of mirror-pans around them.

They do not wear woven fabric, which would rot or drag heavy with condensation; instead, they wear stiffened aprons and skirts pieced together from thin, overlapping scales of shaved sheep-horn and boiled leather, held with copper wire. Over their eyes they tie narrow visors carved from dark roofing slate, pierced only by horizontal slits no thicker than a fingernail. Through these, they read the world not as landscapes, but as gradients of light and liquid tension.

Their existence is entirely hydraulic. They speak little—their voices are low, dry rasps that rarely rise above the *tink* of the water pans—because their communication is inscribed directly into the terrain. A tender three terraces up communicates with one below by altering the flow: dropping a flat ceramic wedge into a sluice to choke a stream, or lifting a slate gate to send a deliberate pulse of three rhythmic ripples across a neighboring basin.

They eat what the water catches. In a world without topsoil, the food falls from the sky. The thin, violent winds of the high atmosphere carry dust from distant lowland steppes—spores, dried pollen, pulverized organic matter, and volcanic silts. The immense catchment of the ceramic valley is designed to harvest this atmospheric fallout. As the condensation beads and creeps down the terraces, it carries the wind-dust into the quiet, dead-end pans where the water is calibrated to sit motionless. 

There, an extremely fine, gelatinous film forms at the surface: a pale, cold-loving bacteriological skin that feeds on the lime and the sky-silt. The tenders skim this film at dawn using long, flexible blades of planed horn. They dry the skim on the hot black tiles of the higher terraces, scraping it up as paper-thin, mineral-rich crisps that taste of cold river stone and iron.

Beneath the working surfaces, life retreats to stay dry. The landscape is not solid; beneath the overlapping field of tiles lies a vast, pitch-black under-croft—a labyrinth of low, dry brick vaults that support the catchments above. It is here, in the warm, radiant dark heated by the sun-baked ceramics overhead, that the people sleep. They crawl down through narrow, flanged chimneys disguised as drain-risers. Down in the hollow foundations, the air is dry, smelling richly of baked earth and curing mortar. They sleep directly on the smooth, warm undersides of the terrace floors, their ears pressed to the clay, listening to the millimeter-by-millimeter creep of the water above them to judge the health of the system.

Other, non-human things have found their way into this engineered ecology, though they barely resemble the fauna of the valleys below:

In the mid-level basins, where the water is no deeper than a knuckle and the current is slowest, there are the **plate-lice**—creatures no larger than a child’s palm, perfectly flat, with carapaces made of overlapping calcified scales that precisely mimic the tone and curvature of the earthenware pans. They are entirely blind, possessing no limbs, moving instead on an undulating underside of microscopic, mucus-slick cilia. They glide across the glazed tiles, grazing on the thinnest layers of calcium and trapped wind-pollen. When the high sun is at its fiercest, they align their flat bodies with the shadows cast by the lip of the retaining ridges, becoming entirely invisible. If a tender wades too close, they do not flee; they simply tighten their seal against the tile through capillary suction, becoming as immovably part of the floor as a mortared brick.

Deeper in the narrow flues and downspouts live the **bell-slugs**. Pallid, bone-white, and semi-translucent, they hang suspended inside the vertical drainage holes where the air is cool and choked with spray. They have evolved heavy, calcified plugs at their tails—dense porcelain-like knobs that dangle down into the open cisterns. As water drops fall onto these plugs from above, they vibrate, producing the faint, metallic *tink* that echoes throughout the valley. The sound is not mechanical; it is the slugs breathing, their bodies pulsing with each water impact, filtering the high-altitude moisture through hollow, gill-like tubes before it touches the reservoir below.

Even the architecture itself possesses a strange, slow mimicry of life. At the fringes, where the terraces halt against the mountain shale, the tenders continue to lay new beds of brick. They work without timber or metal scaffolding, using their own bodies as the counterweights, mortar-kneaders, and fire-tenders. The expansion is silent, creeping outward terrace by terrace, ridge by ridge, swallowing the mountain under a brilliant, sterile mantle of wet red clay—a civilization that has reduced the world to a single, immaculate machine for turning the cold sky into drinkable glass.
