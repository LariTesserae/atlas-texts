At the bottom of the mass, where the cast-iron plate is ground to a high, frictionless mirror, live the *graters*. 

They are flattened, spade-headed isopods no thicker than a playing card, the color of wet lead, though they have never touched water. Their undersides do not have soft bellies; instead, their sternal plates are ridged like coarse steel files. They move with an unswerving, hydraulic steadiness, using their heavy front legs to pull their bodies forward while their bellies rasp microscopic shavings of zinc, iron oxide, and graphite from the floor. They do not drink. Their internal chemistry is a dense, gelid paste sealed behind tightly overlapping intersegmental membranes, so perfectly gasketed that not a single molecule of moisture escapes into the bone-dry air. When a grater dies, it does not collapse or shrivel; its legs simply seize mid-stride, turning it into another small, skidding ramp over which its companions climb, their rasping undersides shaving its dorsal plates down to powder within three turns of the overhead shaft.

Above them, occupying the middle stratum of the moving drift, are the *box-weevils* and the *long-winders*.

The box-weevils are the size of sheep-shears, blocky and six-sided, their carapaces formed of fused, interlocking plates that look like stamped boiler iron. They are the miners of the exuviae. Their snouts are short, blunt hydraulic jacks equipped with counter-rotating, diamond-hard serrations. They do not hunt the living; they plow through the lower drift, scooping up the discarded molt-casings of the larger crawlers, crushing them into dry wafers, and excreting a pale, talc-fine dust that the graters consume. Their shells are so thick that when an ironclad crushes one, the sound does not echo like a shell breaking, but rings with the heavy, ringing *clink* of a dropped sledgehammer.

Woven between them like lengths of live wire are the millipedes—the *tinsel-trains*. They are four to six feet long, no thicker than a man’s finger, composed of hundreds of narrow rings of an alloy-like chitin that has taken up zinc from the dust until it gleams like galvanized wire. They move neither fast nor slow, but with the steady, indifferent torque of an auger bit. Their legs, clustered in thousands, are stiff and needle-sharp, striking the iron plate in long, traveling waves that produce a high, singing whistle beneath the general roar of the room. They feed solely on the graphite lubrication weeping from dormant bearing blocks and the dry fungal crust—thin as a coat of lacquer—that coats the iron rafters. 

Then come the hunters: the things that have evolved to harvest this cold, fast-moving pasture.

The dominant predators are the *mandibuloids*, or *chisel-jaws*. They are eight-legged, blind, and squat, shaped like low-profile turret tanks, up to eighteen inches across the beam. Their eyes have vanished entirely, replaced by two forward-facing sensory horns fringed with stiff, zinc-encrusted bristles that register the microscopic shockwaves traveling through the floor plates. A chisel-jaw does not track scent—in this zero-humidity air, scent has nowhere to float. It tracks the *frequency* of footfalls. When it senses a thick concentration of box-weevils, it charges on short, massive thighs, its heavy weight driving its belly-skids against the floor with a screech of dry metal. Its mandibles are not scissors; they are twin pickaxes that operate on a toggle-joint. With a single, sharp contraction of its thoracic musculature, the picks slam together with enough force to shear through the seam of a dead switchbox. It eats fast, swallowing dry chunks of chitin and marrow-paste without chewing, its internal gizzards lined with quartz-hard grinding stones that reduce the armor to meal in minutes.

Clinging to the higher zones—the lead conduits, the wire lamp-cages, and the flanged lips of the reduction-gear cases—are the *hangers*. 

These are long-limbed, spider-like forms whose carapaces are translucent and pale as paraffin wax. Their limbs are impossibly thin, double-jointed, and tipped with recurved hooks that fit perfectly into the diamond-mesh of the light guards. They live in the direct, blinding glare of the bulkhead bulbs, soaking up the hard white light through dorsal panels that are slightly phosphorescent. They are nearly motionless, hanging head-down over the rushing floor like dry gargoyles. They do not hunt with webs—silk cannot set in this moistureless chill. Instead, they drop. When a soft, newly-molted mantis-crawler passes beneath, a hanger releases its grip, falls like a dropped coat hanger, impales the prey through its neck-membrane with two long, needle-like styluses, and immediately begins drawing out the animal's thick, oil-based body fluid through capillary action. Within sixty seconds, the prey is an empty, brittle husk, and the hanger is climbing back up the conduit by its forelegs alone, leaving the shell to be pulverized by the crowd below.

Deepest in the machine housings, inside the dead hollows of the reduction gearboxes where the cast iron is four inches thick, dwell the *stills*. 

They are the oldest living things in the chamber. A still has lost all its legs; it has no eyes, no antennae, and no segments. It is a single, swollen, slate-grey blister of living leather, three feet across, that has adhered directly to the iron wall over the course of centuries. It is plugged directly into the seam where the dormant bronze bushings meet the steel shafts. It does not move, it does not hunt. It lives on the infinitesimal vibrations of the building’s dead weight and the trace minerals drawn up through the metal itself by galvanic action. Its breath is an imperceptible, once-a-day heave that draws in the graphite-laden dust and slowly, over years, deposits a thick, calcified shell around its base until it looks less like an animal and more like a slag-weld on the machine's casing.

Above them all, governing the rhythm of the entire pocket, is the countershaft.

The shaft is not an animal, but to the swarm, it is the sole seasonal pulse of the universe. Every four and a half seconds, as the bronze bushing groans, the shaft drops a microscopic fraction of a millimeter and rises again. The vibration travels down the structural H-beams into the floor plates. 

At the exact moment of the groan, the entire roar changes pitch. The millions of running legs hitch in unison; the chisel-jaws freeze their picks; the tinsel-trains tighten their coils against the seams of the iron plates to absorb the shock. For a quarter of a second, the dry clatter thins, replaced by the deep, hollow singing of five hundred feet of cast iron under stress.

Then the shaft completes its cycle, the brass shrieks its ungreased sigh, and the living gravel resumes its tumble—scuttling, shearing, and grinding itself into dust beneath the unblinking white suns of the ceiling, without a pause, without an evening, and without an end.
