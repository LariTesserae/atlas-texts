In a sea that does not fall, life does not bother with the frantic mechanics of escape or pursuit. Where the water is as warm as the interior of a mammal, the border between an organism’s flesh and its surrounding medium becomes porous, lazy, and continuous.

### The Pulse-Glands and the Iron-Braid
The great kelp-trees do not live alone; their tower-thick trunks are less like single plants and more like vertical cities of cooperative tissue. Wedged into the deep spiraling clefts of their fibrous bark are the **Thrum-Hearts**—vast, liver-colored masses of pure involuntary muscle, some as large as grain silos, that have grown directly into the vascular bundles of the timber. 

They possess neither bones nor brains, only a rhythmic, myogenic twitch that contracts every few minutes, squeezing the tree’s internal channels and driving the sap downward into the granite roots. In exchange, the wood feeds them directly through millions of subterranean rootlets that tap into their soft, rubbery hides. To touch one is to touch a warm, slick wall of vibrating tallow. They are entirely blind, but around their perimeter they sprout long, pale fringes of sensory moss that taste the iron content of passing currents, shivering when the thermal vents below shift their yield.

Deeper inside the porous trunks live the **Marrow-Crawlers**: pale, armless things like segmented sausages of translucent cartilage. They spend their centuries-long lives within the dark sap-tubes, grazing on internal fungal crusts, secreting a heavy, antiseptic resin that keeps the tree from rotting from the inside out. They have no eyes, no anus, and no lungs; they absorb the tree’s refined sugar through their skin and, when they die, dissolve entirely into the current, leaving only a pinch of chalky white gravel behind.

### The Ribbon-Skins and the Drifting Guts
The pale things that wander the bronze-green midwaters—the shapes like dropped ribbons—are not true fish, nor worms. They are known, if they are known at all, as **Pellicles**.

A Pellicle is a creature composed almost entirely of surface area. Thinner than wet parchment and three or four fathoms long, it has no internal organs, no front, and no back. Its body is a double sheet of undulating, gelatinous cells that absorbs nourishment directly from the amber broth of the sea. It moves by passing an almost imperceptible wave down its length, steering not toward light or food, but toward differences in salinity.

When a Pellicle encounters a shear-line—where a bitter, dense stratum of cold brine meets a rising thread of sweet geothermal water—it spreads itself flat along the invisible boundary like oil on glass. There it rests for weeks, its top half feeding on one chemical soup and its lower half drinking another, acting as a living membrane between two non-mixing worlds. 

Occasionally, three or four Pellicles will drift into one another. They do not fight or mate; they simply fuse at the edges, sharing nutrients across their combined skin until a shift in current gently tears them apart into new, randomly sized individuals. They are infinitely divisible and entirely without memory.

### The Sediment-Lords of the High Saddles
Down on the velvety swells of the submerged granite summits, the bryozoan mats are not merely a pasture; they are the upper crust of an ecosystem that moves at the speed of stone.

Half-buried in the thick olive-brown fleece are the **Barrow-Meats**. These are immense, domed creatures—often thirty paces across—that look like nothing so much as smooth, glistening boulders of purple suet. They have no limbs. Their underbellies are lined with thousands of microscopic, mucus-secreting cilia that slowly, atom by atom, dissolve the calcified red algae beneath them. 

A Barrow-Meat may take three human generations to cross a single granite saddle. Behind it, it leaves a trail of clean, white, polished stone that gleams like bone in the marshy twilight before the bryozoans slowly reclaim it. 

Living atop the backs of these slow leviathans are forests in miniature:
* **Glass-Anemones**, clear as blown crystal, whose hollow tentacles do not sting, but instead collect the tiny pearls of oxygen rising from the kelp-roots and hold them, glowing like chandeliers in the dim water.
* **Tallow-Crabs**, soft-bodied scavengers the size of shields, whose shells never harden in the warm, mineral-rich soup. They have long, hair-like legs with which they comb the Barrow-Meat’s back, grooming away dead skin and fungal rot with a methodical, mindless devotion. They have never known a predator; if lifted from the shell, they simply float in place, gently flexing their legs until the current brings them down onto another living surface.

### The Inhabitants of the Pinned Pockets
Under the rare stone overhangs, where compressed globes of ancient, unbreathable air have hung trapped since the world was molten, lives a distinct, gossamer fauna that exists entirely upside-down.

These are the **Strand-Weavers**. Resembling long-legged spindlers of milk-white glass, they walk on the *underside* of the water’s surface tension inside the air-bubble itself. Their feet never pierce the dry gas; they hang suspended from the liquid ceiling, spinning intricate, three-dimensional webs of dry, waterproof silk down into the stagnant air of the bubble. 

These webs do not catch insects—there are none. Instead, they catch the microscopic airborne spores of ancient, dry-rot molds that somehow continue to cycle within the sealed atmosphere of the pocket. The Strand-Weavers eat these dry spores, convert them into rich, greasy droplets of oil, and feed the oil back into the water through long, dangling siphon-tubes, creating tiny, shimmering slicks that scent the surrounding sea with the smell of old cellar dust and lightning.

### The Liturgists of the Monoliths
Near the cyclopean basalt stairs and the rounded stone pillars, the character of life changes. It becomes more structured, almost architectural.

Clinging to the basalt are colonies of **Mason-Polyps**—creatures that do not build natural, chaotic reefs, but instead deposit their black, iron-hard calcifications strictly along the straight lines and right angles of the ancient structures. Over millions of years, they have not buried the stairs; they have *maintained* them, replacing weathered basalt grain for grain with their own dark, mineralized shells, preserving the sharp corners of the lintels and the crisp depth of the long-eroded steps out of sheer, unthinking biological habit.

And resting on the highest platforms of these stairs, where the sweet upward currents rise like liquid heat-ripples, sit the **Cenotaph-Keepers**.

They are the closest thing the Shelf has to sentience, though it is a consciousness so slow that an empire could rise and turn to silt before a Keeper finishes an impulse. They are shaped like folded bells of heavy, slate-gray leather, anchored to the basalt by thick, petrified stalks. Inside their thick mantles, they do not possess a brain, but a massive, multi-chambered organ of balance—a labyrinth of calcite rings and magnetic iron-stones that drift in chambers of clear oil.

The Keepers feel the planetary tilt. They feel the slow, imperceptible wobble of the mountain summits as the mantle shifts beneath them, and they feel the tension of the sea’s high edge where the water meets the void. When the Shelf’s localized gravity fluctuates—when the ocean threatens, by some fraction of a millimeter, to slip its moorings and spill down the escarpments into the dry world below—the Keepers respond.

They do not move, but from the vents in their crowns they release dense, oily plumes of heavy, dark ambergris that alter the surface tension of the water above them, thickening it, knitting the liquid fabric back together, weighting the sea to the stone with chemistry.

They have sat on the stairs since the water was gathered. They do not know who cut the stone they rest upon; they only know the weight of the sea above them, the warmth of the blood-temperature broth in their gills, and the long, eternal thrum of the kelp-forest beating like a single, distant drum against the granite floor.
