In this basin, the boundary between what is plant and what is beast has never been sharpened by the struggle to escape being eaten. Where there are no jaws, there is no need for panic; where there is infinite warmth and sunlight, there is no hunger that requires chasing. What lives here does not hunt; it absorbs, it settles, and it waits.

The translucent drifters are the most visible of these dwellers, though to call them animals feels like an overstatement of their intent. They are known, in the quiet logic of the basin, as *pleurals*. Formed from double-layered sheets of clear, horn-textured cartilage, they possess neither eyes nor brains, but a decentralized lattice of nerves that registers temperature gradients and the faint, magnetic pull of the basin’s basalt ribs. A pleural moves by a mechanism so slow it can only be detected by watching the alignment of its amber veins against the background rock: it draws warm water into the margin of its mantle through microscopic pores, warms it further within internal capillary channels, and releases it through a trailing slit, drifting forward on a breath of convective thrust. 

They do not graze with teeth. When a pleural encounters a sunlit face of basalt furred with golden diatomaceous slime, it folds itself directly against the stone, creating a soft seal. Its lower skin secretes a weak, enzyme-rich dew that liquefies the algae, which is then drawn directly through the skin by osmotic pressure. When the film is spent, the creature relaxes its seal, curls slightly at the edges like a drying leaf, and allows the ascending thermal plumes to lift it away toward the next spire. They can spend forty years circling a single basalt needle, descending in the evening when the upper water cools, and rising with the gold morning light to ride the sunlit thermals.

Beneath them, inhabiting the tangled mid-canopy of the skiff-sized leaves, live the *lace-striders*. These are creatures composed almost entirely of articulated, hollow needles made of biogenic silica—living glass. No thicker than grass stems, their limbs span nearly two meters, ending in pad-like claws coated in an oily resin that repels water. They do not swim; they walk upon the tension of the broad leaves, stepping with a delicate, mechanical deliberation from one floating raft of vegetation to the next.

The lace-striders are the basin’s tenders. They feed upon the fine, gray sediment that drifts down from the mountain air—pollen blown from distant valleys, dust shaken from the new-thrust peaks. With feathery palps around their ventral mouths, they sweep the surface of the great leaves clear, preventing silt from choking the plants' photosynthetic skin. In return, the plants secrete dense droplets of lipid-rich wax from their leaf-nodes, which the striders harvest with patient, rhythmic scrapes of their forelegs. A single lace-strider may live three centuries upon three or four adjacent stems, never knowing the floor of the basin, never seeing the open sky, stepping across the emerald green of the canopy like a living constellation of glass rods.

Farther down, where the light shifts from gold to deep, bruised indigo and the granite walls close in, the life becomes heavier and more mineral.

Here, in the cold-shadowed canyons where the liver-colored mosses choke the rock, live the *lithic bells*. These are massive, sessile molluscoids that have abandoned shells entirely in favor of an outer mantle made of dense, mineralized feldspar crust. They resemble fractured segments of the rock itself—triangular, sharp-cornered, dark as slate. Only when the warm convective current wells up from the crust below do they reveal themselves: a seam along their apex parts with the sound of grinding quartz, and a plume of deep-violet, feather-like gills unfolds into the warm flow.

The lithic bells do not filter the water for food in the traditional sense. Their tissues are home to billions of chemosynthetic sulfur-archaea that thrive on the heat and dissolved iron weeping from the tectonic faults. The bells are the anchors of the deep; their root-structures—cables of braided calcium carbonate as thick as a man's thigh—penetrate several meters into the razor-sharp cracks of the rock, holding the shattered basalt blocks together, effectively stitching the young geology into stability with their own living stone.

Above them all, at the meniscus where the elevated sea meets the thin mountain atmosphere, exists something that is neither solitary nor mobile: the *sieve-mats*.

These are colonial organisms that look like floating sheets of beaten gold foil, spanning tens of meters across the calm between the leaf-tips. They are only three cells thick, utterly transparent save for the amber granules of their reproductive cores. They float upon the absolute surface, one side exposed to the raw, unfiltered ultraviolet light of the high-altitude sun, the other trailing millions of hair-like filaments into the oxygen-rich water. 

The sieve-mats drink the pearl-bubbles that rise continuously from the forest below. When an oxygen bubble touches the underside of a mat, it is trapped, held within a hexagonal mesh of mucus and cellulose. The mat feeds on the sheer energy of the gas exchange, its tissues glowing with a faint, warm iridescence as it processes the breath of the lower jungle.

There are no voices here. There are no calls of mating or warning, no thrum of fins beating in terror. The only communication is chemical—long, complex ribbons of organic esters released by the plants when a shadow falls across them, drifting through the forest until a pleural tastes it and tilts its horn-plate, or a lace-strider feels the water thicken and pauses its endless sweeping. 

Life here does not fear ending, because it has never been interrupted. It has only the warmth of the earth beneath, the fierce clarity of the sky above, and the vast, unhurried time of a world that was born high enough to be left alone.
