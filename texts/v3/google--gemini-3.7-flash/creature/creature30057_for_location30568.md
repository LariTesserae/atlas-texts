At first, there is only what was brought in on the soles of steel-toed boots during installation, or sucked through the upper intake louvers by the screaming draw of the turbines: the invisible biological detritus of the upper atmosphere. 

Two thousand meters up, the mountain air is thick with aerial plankton—dormant fungal spores, dessicated tardigrades, the shattered hulls of ballooning high-altitude spiders, and pollen grains sealed in waxy shells. Pulled into the tower’s intake at eighty miles per hour, most of this material is pulverized against the intake cowlings or swept straight down into the sump. But within the dead-air vortices behind the structural H-beams and under the flanges of the galvanized catwalks, the air slows. Here, in the micro-shelters where the steel creates tiny lee pockets out of the gale, the fallout gathers.

The earliest true residents do not look like animals; they look like mechanical stains. 

Along the vertical seams where the zinc galvanizing meets the blue weld-slag, the iron-oxidizing bacteria have already organized. Because the vibration is continuous, they do not grow in typical circular colonies. Instead, they align themselves into tight, linear ridges mapped precisely to the Chladni patterns—the standing acoustic waves that the turbine roar vibrates into the metal plates. Where the steel shivers most violently, the steel is bare; where the vibrations cancel out into narrow lines of relative stillness, a rust-colored, mucus-like felt takes root. This felt is tough, elastic, and rubbery, glued to the zinc by a secretion of acidic polysaccharides that etch microscopic anchors directly into the fresh metal.

Living inside this orange felt are the pioneers of the wash: minute, wheel-shaped bdelloid rotifers and nematodes that arrived as dust and revived within seconds of touching the hyper-saturated mist. They do not swim; swimming would cast them into the continuous sheet-flow that peels down the walls and drops into the sump. Instead, they are anchor-feeders. They wedge their transparent, segmented bodies into the micro-grooves left by the grinding wheels that smoothed the structural welds. With their heads extended into the boundary layer—the thin, millimeter-thick cushion of air that clings to the steel face despite the draft—they graze on the bacterial slime, their cilia beating in rhythm with the structural hum of the catwalks.

Higher up, where the amber bulkhead lights keep the moisture warm and cast their localized cones of orange glare, the green algae has formed a dense, fibrous mat over the prismatic glass. The glass is never dry, and the warmth of the 100-watt sealed lamps creates a localized micro-climate: a narrow, three-inch zone where the water is not near-freezing, but tepid. 

Here, tiny, wingless collembola—snow springtails, swept in from the mountain ridges—have found a foothold. Anywhere else in the shaft, their leaping mechanism would be suicide, flinging them into the down-rush of wind and water. Here, they have already ceased to jump. They crawl flat against the glass, their bodies coated in a hydrophobic wax that sheds the condensing fog like oil. They have pale, unpigmented bodies, almost ghostly in the amber light, and they feed on the algal fringe around the rubber seals of the lamp housings. When the pneumatic purge triggers and the air pressure drops with a concussive *thwip*, they hunker flat, locking their six clawed tarsi into the porous rubber gasket until the shockwave passes.

Beneath the grating, in the dripping darkness where the amber light cannot reach, something larger has begun to gather the fallout.

They are the nymphs of stoneflies and midges that were laid in the high mountain tarns near the pylon bases, sucked into the lower auxiliary bleed lines as eggs, and blown into the vertical void. They have survived the transition because they belong to cold, violent water; the cascading sheet-flow of the corrugated core wall is, to them, simply a vertical mountain torrent. 

These nymphs do not have the delicate gills of their stream-born siblings. They are flattened like copper coins, their legs splayed wide and tipped with double grappling-claws that hook directly into the spangles of the zinc coating. They move sideways across the corrugated alloy, ignoring the sixty-meter drop below them, feeding on the bacterial scum and the sodden, drowned carcasses of moths that get caught on the bolt threads. Their carapaces are dull slate-gray, matching the mill scale of the structural beams, and they move with an eerie, mechanical deliberation, timing their scurrying movements between the percussive thumps of the turbine exhaust.

And periodically, moving through this vertical chasm, there is the technician.

She does not belong to the ecology, but she is shaped by it. Suspended in a full-body harness from an overhead trolley line, she descends the void twice a week like an arachnid in yellow neoprene. Over her ears are dual-chamber acoustic muffs that press hard into her skull, yet the turbine noise still rattles through her teeth and jawbone, an inescapable bodily hum. Her visor is constantly wiped by a small, manual squeegee mounted on her thumb. 

She carries an inspection wand, its tip glowing with ultra-violet light, checking the welds for stress-fractures under the sheer hydraulic weight of the run-off. When her beam passes over the steel, the hidden life flares into startling visibility: the bacterial Chladni lines glow a vivid, fluorescent crimson against the purple-black steel; the flattened stonefly nymphs scatter like wet sparks; and the algae on the lamp rims burns a deep, photosynthetic violet.

She does not clean them away. She knows that on a structure this massive, isolated in the cold sky, life is not an infestation but a diagnostic. Where the bacterial felt grows thick, she knows the vibration is dampening; where the stonefly nymphs cluster, she knows a seal is leaking aerated mountain runoff rather than closed-loop industrial glycol. 

She hangs there in the roaring mist, four meters between the roaring core and the freezing hull, suspended above the dark throat of the sump pumps, sharing the vibrating steel with a billion clinging, grazing things that know nothing of the ground below or the sky above—things that know only the cold zinc, the amber lamps, and the endless, vertical river.
