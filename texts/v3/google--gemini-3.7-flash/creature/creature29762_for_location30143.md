To understand what lives on the plateau, one must first discard the distinction between the animal and the ground it rests upon. Over spans of time long enough to rub down mountain ranges, the flesh of these creatures has turned toward the mineral, and the mineral has taken on the warm, slow pulse of flesh.

### The Grinding Herds: *The Pachyderma of the Basin*

The vast majority of the living mass belongs to the *plinth-beasts* and the *slate-backed crawlers*. They are not ungulates in any familiar sense; they have no hooves, no slender ankles, and no need for speed. 

Their bodies are low, flat vaults—some eight or ten feet long, others no larger than a bread loaf—set upon six to eight short, columnar limbs that terminate in wide, fleshy suction pads. These pads are surfaced with a dense micro-velvet that grips the slick ironstone so securely that not even the strongest westerly gale can budge them. Their hides are thick, folded sheets of living leather impregnated with silicate granules and oxidized iron, giving them the exact color and hardness of the surrounding bedrock. 

Along their spines, the hide thickens into heavy, interlocking plates of mineralized bone, resembling ancient dry-laid tiles. In the older individuals, these carapaces are grooved by parallel runnels that match the natural slope of their posture. When the mid-afternoon mist rolls in, condensation collects along these dorsal gutters, running forward by gravity alone toward a pair of sponge-lined drinking clefts flanking the lower jaw. 

They do not graze on grasses, for there are none. Their heads are broad and wedge-shaped, devoid of external ears and possessing only small, milky eyes protected by thick corneal calluses. Beneath the snout lies a wide, muscular aperture containing an oscillating belt of horn-like rasps. All day, and through the warm, damp nights, they press their muzzles against the granite, scouring away the invisible film of cyanobacteria, the microscopic lichen-spores, and the mineral salts left behind by evaporated dew. 

Their breathing is visible only as a gentle, coordinated heave of their flanks. Instead of nostrils, they possess paired spiracles along their sides—thick-lipped slits lined with moist, pale cilia that filter the humid air. Every few seconds, across miles of packed bodies, these vents part with a faint, wet *shuck*, expelling a plume of warm, tallow-scented vapor before sealing shut again to conserve moisture.

Because there are no predators on the plateau, they have forgotten how to flee. Their defense is simply mass, stone-hardness, and patience. When one dies, it does not rot in the open; its heavy shell protects the soft interior while generations of blind, scavenging mites and tiny, soft-bodied grubs hollow it out from beneath. Eventually, the shell settles into the granite, its empty dome becoming another shallow basin for the mist to fill.

### The Commensals and the Crevice-Dwellers

Between the flanks of the plinth-beasts, in the warm, dark channels where living bodies press together, an entire secondary ecology thrives.

*   **The Lichen-Eaters (Chalk-Fleas and Bristle-Crabs):** Tiny, six-legged arthropods with bodies made of translucent, chalky calcium scurry across the carapaces. They do not feed on the beasts themselves, but graze upon the orange crustose lichens that grow on their backs, keeping the breathing vents clear of fungal overgrowth. In return, they shelter in the deep skin-folds where the heat is most constant.
*   **The Tongue-Leeches:** In the shallow rock pools, between the visits of the larger crawlers, live small, ribbon-like creatures the color of wet slate. They are completely flat, lying flush against the floor of the basins to avoid the dry wind. When a plinth-beast dips its broad jaw into the water to drink, these ribbons slide onto the tongue, anchoring themselves with microscopic hooks to siphon a fraction of the gathered mineral broth before detaching into the next pool.
*   **The Slate-Weavers:** In the hair-thin fissures beneath the herds, where the grey sedge takes root, blind, whip-tailed creatures no thicker than knitting needles spend their multi-century lives. They spin webs not of silk, but of a calcifying mucus that glues crumbling shale together, stabilizing the narrow ledges and creating micro-aquifers that hold water long after the surface has dried.

### The Mid-Air Tier: *The Drift-Fauna*

Suspended above the living stone, inhabiting the two or three meters of warm air sustained by the rock’s radiant heat, drifts an aerial population that never touches the ground voluntarily.

*   **Pneumatophores (The Mist-Bells):** These are buoyant, umbrella-shaped organisms made of paper-thin, semi-transparent membranes. They maintain their lift through internal cavities filled with warm, low-density metabolic gases—chiefly methane and water vapor—produced by symbiotic bacterial cultures within their central cores. They have no eyes or wings; they steer by subtly contracting their bell margins, riding the gentle thermal currents that rise from the dark ironstone. 
    From their rims trail dozens of gossamer filaments, fine as cobwebs and several feet long. As the bells drift through the afternoon fog, these filaments comb the mist for airborne organic dust, shed scales, and drifting spores. Once saturated, the filaments are slowly drawn up through a central oral pore, stripped clean, and released again.
*   **Sky-Worms (Ribbon-Flits):** Slender, flattened ribbons of dry, iridescent chitin, ranging from a hand’s span to several yards in length. They undulate through the air with a motion identical to eels swimming through deep water. Their skins are completely dry, rustling like dry corn husks when two individuals brush past one another in the haze. They are the grazers of the air, feeding directly upon the Mist-Bells by nibbling away the exhausted margins of their sails, a process that stimulates the bells to regenerate fresh, nutrient-rich tissue.

### The Architects: *The Slate-Keepers*

The low stone alignments—the long, snake-like walls and circular basins that divide the plateau—are not remnants of an extinct human culture, nor are they natural geological joints. They are the continuous work of the plateau's oldest lineage: the *Tenders*.

Scattered thinly among the dense herds are creatures that stand somewhat apart from the blind plinth-beasts. They possess a similar low-slung, quadrupedal anatomy, but their forelimbs are elongated and end in broad, three-fingered claspers tipped with thick, blunt spatulas of horn. Their hides are smoother, slate-grey, and devoid of lichen.

The Tenders possess a slow, methodical intelligence entirely devoted to the management of stone and water. They have no language, no fire, and no tools other than their own limbs, yet their lives are spent in perpetual, quiet masonry:

*   **Paving and Channeling:** With deliberate, heavy motions, a Tender will wedge its spatulate claws beneath a loose slab of shale, lift it, and carry it against its broad chest to a nearby wall. They lay these stones without mortar, fitting them by feel until the alignment directs the creeping afternoon mist toward the deepest communal basins.
*   **Rubbing and Polishing:** It is the Tenders who have worn the rub-posts to a mirror sheen. By coating their broad chins with an oily, waxy secretion from their throat-glands and working it into the porous granite through decades of rhythmic friction, they waterproof the stone corrals, ensuring that not a drop of dew is absorbed into the dry substrate beneath.
*   **The Herding of the Crawlers:** The low walls they build are precisely knee-high—high enough to gently turn the blind plinth-beasts away from the deepest water reservoirs, keeping the cleanest pools uncontaminated by heavy footfall, yet low enough for the floaters to drift across unimpeded.

The Tenders live for centuries. When two meet along a wall, they do not fight or display; they touch the tips of their blunt forelimbs together, share a long, slow exhalation of warm mist through their chest-vents, and together turn a heavy stone until its grain aligns with the prevailing wind.

### The Rhythm of the Plateau

As the sun lowers, the coppery light strikes the mist, and the entire high plain enters its waking phase. 

The floaters sink lower, settling until their long filaments drape like fine lace across the backs of the resting herds. The plinth-beasts, warmed by the stored heat of the dark granite beneath their bellies and the condensation coating their hides, begin their collective scraping. Millions of horn tongues rasp against the rock; millions of spiracles pulse in a vast, subterranean cadence. 

There is no hunger here in the violent sense, no chase, no terror, and no flight. There is only the slow accretion of mineral into flesh, the turning of mist into blood, and the quiet, continuous work of living things holding a mountain-top in a state of warm, immortal balance.
