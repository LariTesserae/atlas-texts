To live here is to understand that gravity is not a floor that catches you, but an appetite that must be constantly refused. Everything that breathes, creeps, or thinks within the bays is shaped by this refusal.

### The Knot-Dwellers and the Suture-Maw

Deep within the weeping swellings—where three or four colossal beams were joined in forgotten centuries by hemp and bone—the wood has swollen into spongy, vascular knots the size of barns. In the crevices of these saturated burls live the *knot-dwellers*, or *leech-mice*. They are small, broad-skulled animals, pale as peeled turnip, with skin that feels like oiled suede. Having no eyes to speak of, they possess instead long, sensitive whiskers that sprout from their snouts, their shoulder humps, and along their flanks. They do not walk so much as compress themselves into the grain, moving through the deep splits in the cedar like water through a sponge.

They drink the amber sweat of the timber, filtering the bitter tannins through complex, multi-chambered throats. Because they never touch dry air, their skin is constantly colonized by the same pale liverwort that carpets the bridges; a sleeping knot-dweller looks indistinguishable from a damp lump of moss.

Around the margins of these reservoirs lurks the *suture-maw*, a blind, ribbon-like predator four or five paces in length. It possesses no skeleton of calcium, but rather a flexible lattice of tough, cartilaginous hoops. It anchors its tail into the deep hemp-lashings and allows its body to hang slack into the open air beneath a crossing, trailing fine, hair-like sensory lures coated in sweet, sticky mucilage. When a drifting organism or a careless climber touches a lure, the suture-maw retracts in an explosive, coiled spasm, drawing its prey into the wet dark beneath the span, where it digests it slowly, never releasing its clamped grip on the load-bearing fiber.

### The Drift and the Vane-Gilled

In the great leagues of empty air between the spans, the ecosystem belongs to creatures that have abandoned the wood entirely.

The long-bodied rib-fins mentioned by travelers are the *drifts*. They are neither warm-blooded nor truly aquatic, though their motion has the undulating grace of an eel in deep water. Their ribs extend far beyond their bodies, spanned by tissue so thin it is nearly iridescent, like the skin of an oiled onion. These membranes do not flap; they are sails, perpetually trimmed to catch the rising columns of warm, tea-scented vapor that billow from the lower, decaying tiers.

The drifts spend entire lifespans without ever landing. They possess no limbs for gripping, only delicate, fringed barbels around their mouths with which they comb the air for drifting fern spores and the buoyant, gossamer egg-sacs of canopy spiders. They communicate through low, subsonic hums that vibrate the water droplets suspended in the air, creating faint, shimmering ripples in the mist around them. When a drift dies, it does not plummet immediately; its tissues are buoyant with light gases produced by internal fermentation, and it slowly tumbles through the gray-green void for days, unspooling like a ribbon of wet linen until it catches on an arch far below, where the moss immediately begins to digest it.

### The Tendon-Folk (The Splicers)

Humanity—or what remains of the lineage that first planted the bines—survives in small, widely spaced bands known to themselves simply as *the Span-Keepers* or *the Tendon-Folk*.

They are physically altered by their home. Their bodies are long, narrow, and nearly hairless, their skin a pale, weathered lichen-gray, permanently stained at the fingers and palms with the black-green dye of walnut oil and cedar gum. Their toes have lengthened into grasping digits with thick, hooked nails, and their chests are broad and shallow, adapted to drawing nourishment from the thick, moist air without strain.

They do not wear woven cloth, which would rot into slime within a season; instead, they wear wraps of beaten bark-cloth oiled with animal fat, cured until it has the suppleness of kidskin. 

#### Their Habitations
The Tendon-Folk do not build huts or shelters on top of the bridges, where wind and rot are most violent. They live *inside* the geometry of the suspension. They weave slung cradles—*nocks*—from boiled reed-gut and living, grafted willow shoots, hanging them in the dead-weight zones beneath the great arches where the air is stillest. These nocks look like colossal, teardrop-shaped cocoons swinging gently in the perpetual draft. A village is not a cluster of houses, but a chain of three or four of these woven pods suspended along a single twenty-mile length of load-bearing bine.

#### Language and Senses
They speak little, and when they do, it is in a sibilant, click-heavy register that mimics the wet *tick-tick* of expanding wood and the dripping of mist. Over long distances, they do not shout; shouting is lost in the damp void. Instead, they carry polished bone hammers no larger than a knuckle, with which they strike rhythmic taps against the tightest tension cables. 

By pressing their foreheads or jawbones directly against the ironwood beams, they can "listen" to the bridge. A skilled listener can read the state of the world through the grain:
* A dull, thrumming hum means a heavy drift-herd is passing through the upper bays.
* A high, glass-clear singing tone warns that a gale is tightening a cable twelve miles to the windward.
* A wet, tearing thud—the most dreaded sound—means a spliced truss has sheared somewhere below, dropping a whole bay into the bottomless gloom and throwing sudden, deadly loads onto the neighboring spans.

#### The Work of Holding
The primary occupation of the Tendon-Folk is not hunting or gathering, but *tending the craft*. They are gardeners of tension.

Every child is taught to carry a gourd of split-wood trunnels and a coil of wet hemp-gut. When they walk the paths, they do not look at the horizon; they look at the joins. They drive fresh wedges into loosening scarfs; they pare away dead, spongy timber before rot can infect a living spar; and most importantly, they *lead*. 

To lead is to find a young, green bine—perhaps no thicker than a thumb—that has sprouted from a living cedar arch, and braid it across an open gap, lashing it to a distant anchor with oiled cord. The bine will take twenty years to reach the far side and another fifty to grow thick enough to bear a man's weight, but it is done without thought, an automatic gesture of continuity. 

#### The Cycle of Death
When a Span-Keeper dies, there is no earth to receive them. To throw a body into the void is considered an act of desecration—not because of cruelty, but because it is a waste of material. 

Instead, the dead are *laid into the scarf*. The flesh is cleaned away with bone scrapers, and the long bones of the thighs, arms, and ribs are carved into trunnels—the wooden and bone pins that pin together the great splices. The sinew is dried and beaten into binding thread, and the hair is braided into cordage. 

In this way, every archway, every span, every bridge that trembles softly in the warm mist is held together by the physical bodies of those who walked it centuries before. When an elder presses their ear to the wood to hear the weather, they are listening through the bones of their ancestors, waiting for the bridge to tell them where the world is holding, and where it is about to give way.
