At first, it seems there is only the water, and that whatever might have lived here has long since dissolved into the boiling mist or been washed away through the sky-draining cisterns. But if you sit long enough on one of the tallow-slick basalt curbs—where the heat presses against the chest like a sodden wool blanket—you begin to notice that the water does not always sheet over the stone with uniform smoothness. In certain gutters, the boiling film breaks. It humps upward, parting around low, dense obstructions that do not have the angular grain of masonry.

They are there, pressed flat into the angles where wall meets street, or lodged deep within the vertical fluting of the obelisks where the water climbs.

They have no common name, for there is no language spoken in the city, but their presence is physical, massive, and slow. They are neither statues nor beasts, but something closer to living ballast—beings whose flesh has taken on the specific gravity, texture, and gray-black mineral density of the waterlogged tufa. 

Their bodies are long, broad, and unsegmented, like great slabs of soaked leather or wet slate that have been softened by centuries of steam. They possess no eyes; in a place where light has never touched a surface, the skull has smoothed over into a continuous, heavy brow of dense, porous bone, ridged with fine, vascular grooves that drink the ambient moisture directly from the air. Where a face might be expected, there is only a wide, horizontal cleft—a thermal mouth lined with thick, spongy pads of iron-stained tissue that filter the boiling silt as it washes past.

They do not crawl so much as they are displaced. Their undersides are broad and pale, textured like the foot of an enormous abalone, covered in microscopic, muscular fibrils that grip the microscopic vesicles in the basalt. When they move, they do not lift themselves from the stone; they flow along its plane, moving a single millimeter at a time, their transit leaving a faint, chemically cleaned path where the leathery lichen and lime-crust have been stripped bare and ingested.

Watch one long enough through the shifting density of the vapor, and its anatomy reveals a terrible, quiet specialization. They are entirely hydraulic organisms. They have no lungs; the rhythmic, suffocating pressure shifts of the city act as an external diaphragm for them. When the thermal cycle dips and the air condenses, their bodies swell, absorbing hundreds of gallons of hot, mineral-saturated water through lateral pores that line their flanks like the weeping weep-holes of a retaining wall. When the heat surges back and the architecture begins to bleed, their tissues contract with immense force, expelling jets of pressurized brine from small, volcanic siphons along their spines. 

This expelled water does not splash. It leaves them in silent, glass-clear laminations that join the horizontal sheets skimming across the plazas, feeding the reverse waterfalls that climb the megaliths. They are not merely inhabitants of the system; they are its living valves.

Sometimes, in the vast empty gulfs of warm mud between the tenement clusters, you will find them gathered in rings. They lie half-submerged in the boiling silt, their blunt heads oriented toward the center of the void where no building stands. In these spaces, they remain motionless for what must be decades, their leathery backs accumulating layers of iron biofilm until they are indistinguishable from the surrounding mudbanks. Yet beneath the mud, their pale, muscular undersides are linked, pressed sole-to-sole in a continuous, subterranean ring, transmitting low-frequency hydrostatic pulses through the slurry—a slow, geological conversation measured in degrees of heat and variations of mineral salt.

There are smaller things, too, that live not on the stone, but within the seams of the mortar itself. 

In the deep tenements, where the masonry has warped into undulating, melted forms, the joints between the basalt blocks are wide and soft. Inhabiting these gaps are slender, pale ribbon-creatures, no thicker than a thumb but many fathoms long. They are nearly transparent, their internal organs visible as faint, rust-red threads running through clear, gelatinous muscle. They never leave the mortar. They thread themselves through the porous interior of the walls, moving through the saturated stone like worms through wet earth, sustained entirely by the slow, chemical breakdown of the ancient lime. 

When you place a hand against the warm, weeping face of a tenement wall, you can feel them: a faint, continuous squirming deep inside the rock, a subtle hydraulic vibration as thousands of them pump water through the interior labyrinth of the masonry to keep it from drying out, even for an instant. If a stone were ever to become dry, it would crack; these creatures are the reason the city remains unbroken, holding the megaliths together with their own mucus and the perpetual moisture they cycle through the stone’s core.

And higher up, where the architecture ascends into the absolute blackness of the vaulted ceiling, there are the Drifters.

They do not touch the ground. They are vast, disc-shaped sheets of translucent tissue—thin as parchment and broad as the floor of a courtyard—that ride the upward-falling rain. They lift off from the summits of the highest obelisks, caught in the thermal updrafts and the strange, inverted gravity of the moisture sheets, ascending slowly toward the ceiling of the depression. Their edges ripple in languid, hypnotic waves, tasting the heavy vapors, filtering the rising steam for suspended iron particles and airborne spores of lichen. 

When they reach the cold threshold at the very top of the dark—where the air finally fails to hold its heat—they fold their bodies inward like collapsing umbrellas, their mass densifying in seconds, and drop like heavy, wet sails back through the pitch, falling silently through the steam to settle once more upon the steaming mud, only to begin the slow crawl toward the nearest rising pillar to begin the climb again.

None of these things have minds that would be recognizable as conscious. They do not hunt, they do not build, and they do not die in ways that leave bones. When one of the great ballast-beings reaches the end of its centuries, its muscular foot simply loses its grip upon the tufa. The horizontal sheets of boiling water catch its edges, lift its immense, softened bulk, and carry it smoothly, silently along the causeway, sliding it down the drowned streets until it plunges into the subterranean siphons. There, in the silent rush beneath the shale arches, the boiling current gently tears it apart, distributing its minerals back into the water that will climb the obelisks tomorrow, ensuring that the city never runs dry.
