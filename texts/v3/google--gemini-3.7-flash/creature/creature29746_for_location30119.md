At the lowest tier of this ecology are the things that never touch the wind at all: the deep-bore fauna that spend their entire life cycles embedded within the hydraulic conduits of the wood. Where sap surges through the interior channels under pressures capable of shearing stone, there live pale, arm-length isopods whose carapaces are curved to fit the inner curvature of the vessels. They have no eyes; their heads terminate in flat, rasping plates of horn that scour the nutrient-thick slimes from the conduit linings. They do not swim against the flood so much as wedge their serrated legs into the porous cell walls, letting the warm, pressurized sap scour over them. When a lateral shoot snaps out into the cold void with an artillery-crack, it ruptures these veins, spilling thousands of these creatures out into the freezing air where they instantly flash-freeze, raining into the lower dark like translucent, hard-shelled fruit.

Scavengers wait for these ruptures. 

The under-striders—those furred, low-slung quadrupeds clinging to the inverted bellies of the boles—are not true mammals, though their pelts are thick, oily, and reek intensely of wintergreen and fat. Their skeletons are largely cartilaginous, designed to deform under the massive compressive buffeting of crosswinds without splintering. Their four limbs do not end in paws, but in splayed, three-hinged radials tipped with black, recurved talons that fit precisely into the microscopic cleavage planes of the glassy jade bark. They walk upside down with a sickening, mechanical fluidness, their spines undulating like centipedes. 

Because the air is thick with wind-scream, these creatures are entirely deaf to airborne sound; instead, their lower jaws are broad, flat, and padded with dense acoustic fat that they press continuously against the wood. Through this contact, they read the geography of the limb: the frantic, scratching footsteps of rival packs three miles distant; the thrumming approach of a sap-surge; or the deep, groaning shear of a limb about to tear away into the abyss. They feed on whatever is trapped in the wind-lee—freezing sap-crusts, hair-moss grubs, and each other. Their young are born blind and jaw-fastened, burrowed directly into the mother’s thick ventral grease until their talons harden into dark, vitreous needles.

Above them, in the open howling emptiness of the gulfs, the air belongs to the sail-gliders. These are long-bodied, keel-breasted predators whose skin-patagia are not feathered, but ribbed with flexible horn struts that can reef and unfurl like the sails of a ship to manage the violent, shearing thermals. Their bones are pneumatic, filled with air that they superheat in internal lung-sacs warmed by dense, fast-beating metabolic cores. 

These gliders rarely land; to land on the glassy bark is to risk being smashed by the gale or gripped by the under-striders. Instead, they sleep on the wing, locking their wing-spar tendons and riding the massive thermal updrafts that boil out of the warm, rotting depths far below the fog line. When they hunt, they drop like lead weights through miles of black air, their wings folded tight against their flanks, homing in on the phosphorescent bruise-glow of leaking resin vents. Around these glowing vents, needle-beaked scavengers cluster like moths; the glider punches through the flock at terminal velocity, snatching prey with long, barbed tail-claspers before snapping its wings open with a sound like a cracked whip to soar back into the high twilight.

Human life here is narrow, dense, and radically modified by the terror of the drop.

The people who inhabit the seams are known to one another simply by their grip: they are short-statured, with broad, barrel-shaped ribcages built to extract oxygen from the thin, iron-cold draft, and their forearms are disproportionately thick, corded with tendon like braided hawser. Their skin is perpetually stained an inky, resinous charcoal from the soot of their grease fires and the bitter, tannin-heavy dust they scrape from the bark to dry their palms.

They do not walk as surface-dwellers walk. Their gait is a crouched, rhythmic shuffle, their centers of gravity kept inches from the jade plates, always moving with two points of iron-pinned contact to the notched tracks. They dress in multiple layers of cured membrane stripped from the sail-gliders, greased heavily on the outside to shed freezing rime, with inner linings of felted hair-moss that itch furiously but keep the flesh from deadening against the frost-burn of the wood.

Their camps are not built *upon* the world, but *into* its wounds. When the wood twists under its own growth-pressure and tears a longitudinal split thirty cubits deep, the people swarm into the chasm before the tree can heal it shut. They drive heavy wedges of petrified heartwood into the lips of the wound to keep the living flesh of the tree from closing and crushing them. Inside these narrow, vertical canyons, where the wind is reduced to a thin, whistling draft, they sling their hammocks of knotted sinew between bone struts.

A split-dwelling is a vertical village. At the lowest, narrowest point of the crack, where the wood bleeds pale, sticky sap, sit the fuel-collectors, tending the grooved bone gutters that direct the resin into stone boiling pots. The heat from these fires rises through the crack, warming the hanging sleeping-cocoons above, before venting out into the storm.

Language among them has largely abandoned vowel resonance, which is obliterated by the low-frequency thrum of the wood. They communicate in a rapid, percussive code of clicks, tongue-snaps, and the rhythmic striking of flint-tipped adzes against the bark. A hunter miles away on a knife-edge ridge can press his forehead against the guide-line and feel the rhythmic tap-tap-drag of his clan’s signal vibrating up from the deep anchors.

Their relationship with the wood is neither worshipful nor gentle; it is a state of perpetual, defensive siege. The wood grows so fast that an anchor pin driven into a seam will be swallowed and warped within two seasons, threatening to tear dwellings apart as the grain twists. Their elders are not keepers of written histories, but *listeners*—men and women whose ears are permanently scarred from pressing against the heartwood. By the pitch of the groan, a listener can tell if a branch is swelling with fresh sap, if it is rotting hollow five miles down-run, or if the entire continental rib is entering a "snap-cycle"—the terrifying moment when internal tension overcomes the fiber strength, and an entire arch whips sideways through the void, obliterating everything lashed to its skin.

When a person dies, they are not cast into the abyss—the void is empty enough, and the living cannot spare the fiber it takes to lower a corpse. Instead, the dead are wedged deep into the fresh, narrow growth-fissures of the prows. Within weeks, the violently surging wood flows around them, engulfing bone, leather, and hair, incorporating their bodies into the dense, jade-black grain. The people believe this strengthens the branch, giving the blind, roaring world a small grain of human stubbornness to keep it from snapping in the gale.
