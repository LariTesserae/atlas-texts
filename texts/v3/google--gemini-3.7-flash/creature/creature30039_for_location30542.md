The people who inhabit the fracture have the pale, flat-planed look of stones turned over in a streambed. Their skin rarely sees a direct strike of sun, and over years in the mist it takes on the cast of the basin’s siltstone: a dull, tallowy grey, mottled along the knuckles, ears, and cheekbones with the rust-red of the iron seeps.

They are not tall. Long limbs are a liability where a slip on wet slate can break a collarbone against the wall of the alley opposite, so they are thick-torsoed and low-slung, with broad, spade-like feet whose toes have spread from lifetimes of gripping wet rock without shoes. The soles of their feet are their most remarkable feature: thick, pale, water-bleached rinds of keratin, yellowed like horn at the heels and cross-hatched with fine scars where fresh shale has sliced them cleanly without bleeding.

They grease themselves against the mineral water. From the rendered fat of the blind, oily mud-eels they trap in the deep cisterns, they make a lard mixed with dried soot, rubbing it into their hair, their armpits, and the creases of their groins to prevent the lime-water from eating their skin raw. The smell of this grease—fishy, rancid, and mineral—hangs over the alleys as heavily as the vapor from the weeping walls.

Their society is strictly divided by the drop. 

The uppermost tiers, within fifty feet of the basin’s rim, are occupied by the *Tappers* and the *Timber-Wrights*. These are the dry people, though "dry" in the basin is relative. They wear stiffened mantles of twined bark lined with the pale algae felt, and their ears are attuned to the tension in the basin’s edge. Every morning, before the mist lifts enough to reveal the upper slate roofs, the Tappers walk the narrow ledges with long, fire-hardened poles of ash, striking the freshly cleaved rock face above. By the pitch of the thud—whether hollow, bright, or wet—they determine which slabs have had their clay beds washed out by the night's seepage and are ready to drop. When a slab must come down, they pry it loose systematically with antler levers, dropping it into controlled shoots to be caught, split, and dressed below.

Below the Tappers live the mass of the population: the *Keepers of the Joints*. Their entire domestic life is an unending, rhythmic battle against petrification. 

Because the water deposits lime so aggressively, nothing with a hinge or a seam can remain untended for more than forty-eight hours. The Keepers carry small, rounded river-cobbles drilled through the center and slung on wrist-cords, or chisels made from the leg bones of red deer. As they move from house to house, greeting one another or carrying wash-water, their hands are never idle. They chip. They chip the crusts forming over the wooden pivot-pins of their doors; they chip the calcite collars that choke the log gutters; they scrape the white, tooth-like crystals that grow across the stone drain-slits in their floors. The sound of this perpetual chipped tapping is the true heartbeat of the town, blending with the hollow clunk of the balance-scoops into a dry, frantic clockwork.

Their children are lean, silent, and water-slicked as otters. They run errands along the top of the gutter-stringers, barefoot on logs no wider than a man’s forearm, carrying bundles of bone chisels or pots of fat. Because their bodies are small and their chests flexible, the older children are sent into the *Flues*—the horizontal drainage seams that run under the lowest stack of dwellings—to clear blockages. They crawl on their bellies through inches of icy, calcite-choked water, pulling out rafts of trapped liverwort and the carcasses of blind cave-rats, emerging into the gray daylight coated from head to toe in the fine blue-grey clay, their lips blue with chill.

The dead of the basin are not buried; there is no soil to take them, and the rock floor is too hard to dig. Instead, they are incorporated directly into the architecture through a process they call *The Setting*. 

When an elder dies, the body is stripped, washed in the sharpest mineral-run, and bound with soaked leather thongs into a crouched, structural posture—knees to chest, arms folded tight around the shins. The body is then wedged into a chosen cavity in the foundation walls or set beneath a sagging lintel where the water seeps most heavily. Within three seasons, the calcite rind covers the skin; within ten, the body has become a pale, lumpy pillar of stone, its human form smoothed into the generalized curves of a stalagmite, bearing the weight of three rooms above it. In this way, the town is literally held up by the bones of its builders, and an old room can be identified by the number of white, calcified shoulders protruding from its corners.

Diet in the basin is narrow and strange. They have no fields, so their carbohydrates come entirely from the *felts*—the thick, fibrous algae harvested from the lowest sumps with rakes of split horn. The algae is rinsed repeatedly in the softer seeps near the rim, pressed into heavy disks between flat shale slabs, and dried over low, smokeless charcoal braziers fed on the hard knots of fossil wood pulled from the shale strata. The dried cakes are tough, salty, and taste intensely of iron; they are eaten boiled into a thick, black porridge thickened with crushed water-beetles, whose shells provide a gritty, calcium-rich flour.

Protein comes from the deep cracks. At the base of the western wall, where the dropped bedrock tore away from a subterranean watercourse, they have built long wicker traps set into the rock vents. Every few days, the rising pressure deep within the earth purges a rush of subterranean life: pale, sightless mud-eels up to three feet long, fat white amphipods with glass-clear shells, and soft-bodied cave crickets as broad as a man’s hand. 

The crickets are prized above all else; their pale, fat-rich bellies are fried directly on hot slate flags until they pop, producing a sweet, lardaceous paste that is given to infants and the sick.

There is almost no vocal culture. Sound travels dangerously across the tight stone amphitheater; a raised voice bounces off the wet slate and echoes through fifty homes, blurring into the hiss of the water. As a result, the basin’s people have developed a language of clicks, knuckle-raps on stone, and a wide vocabulary of two-handed gestures that can be read through the aqueous gloom from three tiers away. When they do speak, it is in a low, wet murmur, dropping their voices below the octave of the balance-scoops, their words short and heavy with consonants, like the sound of gravel sliding into an empty trough.

They live with a peculiar, urgent fatalism. They know the basin is not finished settling. Every month or two, a deep, shuddering *thung* vibrates up through the wet floor-flags—the bedrock fifty feet down shifting along its fresh fault. When this happens, the balance-scoops pause, the chippers hold their stones suspended in the air, and every inhabitant stands stock-still, listening to the water. If the seepage changes note—if a dry seam suddenly begins to gush, or a steady gutter runs dry—they move instantly, without a word, hauling logs, bone wedges, and heavy clay to meet the new flow before it washes the mortar from beneath their rooms or drowns the lower tiers in petrifying mud.

They do not dream of leaving the sink. Above the rim is the wind, the dry air that cracks their greased skin, and the terrifying, unbuttressed vastness of a sky with no rock above it. They are creaturely, architectural things now; their blood has taken in the lime, their joints are stiffening to match the slate, and they belong entirely to this cool, damp cradle of stone that is slowly, tirelessly trying to turn them into itself.
