To look into the quartz port is to witness not a colony in the conventional sense, but a single, multi-jointed tissue that has taken the exact negative impression of the machine. The life here has no outside referent; it does not know earth, leaf, or open sky. It knows only the tolerances of the mill, the chemistry of the vapor, and the geometry of its own crowding.

At the base of the stratum, pressed flat against the razor-fine grooves of the lathe work, are the **Plenars**. These are the pale, brush-limbed crawlers, scarcely thicker than a sheet of heavy parchment. Their carapaces are not hardened with calcium, but are soft, flexible, and matte, like wet vellum. They exist entirely in the low, cool film of condensed oil and run-off water that coats the floor of the troughs. Because space is at an absolute premium, their legs do not move independently through empty air; instead, the hundreds of micro-bristles along their flanks engage directly with the bristles of their neighbors. When a Plenar moves, it does not walk over the metal—it meshes. A wave of lateral twitching passes through an unbroken carpet of thousands, sliding them across the brass ribs like a living sheet of low-friction ball bearings. They are the hydraulic cushion of the territory, preventing the heavier shells above from wearing themselves raw against the raw steel floor.

Resting directly on the backs of the Plenars are the **Amber Carapaces**, the dominant physical mass of the chamber. These are thick-bodied, domed, and segmented into seven distinct, overlapping plates of rich, semi-translucent horn. They are the size of a thumb-knuckle, heavy and sluggish, built to withstand the relentless micro-vibration of the turbine’s core. Their dorsal plates are not smooth; they are micro-fluted with faint, longitudinal ridges that mimic the pitch of the machine’s internal threading. 

The Carapaces are the primary harvesters of the tallow mist. Along the anterior edge of their head-shields runs a row of microscopic, comb-like cilia that vibrate at a frequency resonant with the engine’s hum. When the aerosolized animal fat settles out of the warm air, it collects along these combs. The creature draws them through its mouthparts with a slow, mechanical regularity, cleaning one side, then the other, every forty seconds. 

Among the Carapaces, the **Brood-Dames** are stationary landmarks. They are three times the volume of the common foragers, their abdomens swollen into pale, oily globes that stretch the chitin until the internal vascular network—pulsing with clear, amber hemolymph—is fully visible beneath the quartz light. A Dame does not shift her station once she takes it. She wedges the trailing edge of her heavy thorax into the recess of a blue-tempered bolt head, using the hexagonal socket as an anchor against the machine's drift. Beneath her belly, cradled in the warm, stagnant grease between brass ribs, she carries hundreds of lentil-sized eggs, each coated in a stiff, water-repellent wax that smells of rancid suet. The younger, unburdened Plenars constantly circulate beneath her, grooming the clutch with their palps, clearing away any stray filaments of the white screw-head velvet that might choke the embryo shells.

Suspended above the crawling floor, clinging to the vertical bronze fluting where the heat is sharpest, are the **Siphoners**. Slender, almost skeletal, they possess elongated rear legs tipped with curved, needle-sharp dactyls that can grip the microscopic chatter-marks left by the lathe. They face perpetually upward toward the quartz glass. 

The Siphoners live by the steam pulse. Their fore-limbs are not for walking, but are modified into coiled, spring-tempered whips fringed with hydro-absorbent down. The instant the micro-valve vents its breath, the Siphoners snap these whips outward into the descending vapor. The movement is so fast it produces a faint, dry *thwack* against the casing wall. When the whips retract, saturated with condensed distilled water, the Siphoners strip them through a central siphon bore with an audible sucking click. They do not retain all they drink; they are the pressure-regulators of the colony. Through specialized glands beneath their wing-covers, they excrete a fine, clean fluid that trickles downward, washing the debris of the Carapaces into the reach of the floor-dwellers.

No creature here is permitted to decline. The cycle of recycling is carried out by the **Chisel-Jaws**—heavy-headed, blind morphs whose mandibles are darkened to the color of soot, hardened with concentrated iron and copper scraped from the machine itself. The Chisel-Jaws have no digestive tracts to speak of; they are living shearing engines. When an Amber Carapace ceases its rhythmic cilia-grooming, or when a Siphoner’s leg loses its spring tension, the nearest Chisel-Jaw moves in without hesitation. 

There is no struggle. The dying organism relaxes its segments, and the Chisel-Jaw clips through the connective tissue with tiny, precise snaps, reducing the shell into uniform hexagonal flakes. These flakes are not consumed for metabolic fuel; they are gathered immediately by the surrounding Plenars, who masticate them into a pale, greasy pulp and feed it to the brooding females. The shell of an individual that died under the light of one three-minute sweep is often incorporated into the newly hardened dorsal plates of a hatchling before the linkage arm has completed its tenth rotation.

Their collective behavior is locked entirely to the clockwork overhead. They possess no eyes for form, but their carapaces are uniformly photosensitive. When the long shadow of the brass linkage sweep blots out the light from the quartz disc, a profound, systemic reflex occurs. The entire living floor—the thousands of interlocking plates, the brush-limbed under-strata, the hanging siphoners—snaps its antennae flat against its shells in one instantaneous, dry rustle. For four seconds of darkness, the swarm holds its breath, perfectly rigid, perfectly silent, locked to the cold steel. 

Then, as the brass arm sweeps past and the hot, pure light strikes the floor once more, the mass shivers. Thousands of antennae spring upright in a single, upward flick, tracing the receding shadow with an urgent, clicking tremor, and the frantic, grease-slick metabolism resumes its work.
