### The Surface-Bound: Lathe-Ticks and Spindle-Climbers

To exist on the vertical shears, life must never break contact with the stone, lest it be plucked away by the sheer downdrafts or tumble five miles through the gloom. 

The brass-shelled crawlers—the *lathe-ticks*—are not insects in any true sense, but heavy, flattened capsules of segmented iron-chitin, broad as dinner plates and thick as flagstones. They do not have soft underbellies. Where legs would be, they possess sets of counter-rotating radial discs lined with micro-serrated carbide teeth. By grinding these discs against the basalt, they anchor themselves with continuous vacuum-friction, leaving dual tracks of mirror-polished rock in their wake. 

Their feeding is entirely lithotrophic and galvanic. As their mandibles spin—cutting away the superficial crust of iron blooms and heavy moss—they draw the low, ambient current running through the stone directly into a central, dry-cell organ: a dense coil of layered bone and metallic foil that takes the place of a heart. They do not bleed; when cracked open, their interiors reveal a pale, amber grease of extreme viscosity, an organic lubricant that smells faintly of sulfur and cold petroleum. 

Mating between them is a purely structural event. Two ticks lock their outer carapaces together along interlocking dental margins, their radial feet grinding in synchronized counter-phase to keep both anchored during the transfer of thick, ceramic-jacketed egg-capsules. Once deposited directly into the micro-fissures of the stone, these capsules remain dormant for decades, insulated against the absolute freeze, ticking faintly as their internal springs of coiled tendon slowly wind tight under the influence of atmospheric electrostatic charges.

Higher on the columns, where the moss thins into black, frozen veneer, move the *Spindle-Climbers*. These are long, tripod-limbed organisms built like surveyor’s transits. Their limbs are composed of hollow, pressurized bone-struts that telescope out with soft pneumatic clicks. They move without bending; instead, each leg extends and retracts through hydraulic fluid pumped from a central toroid lung. They graze upon the conductive fern frills, using heat-snouted proboscises to bore into the iron-pyrite seams, drinking the hot, electrified sap that circulates through the plant’s piston-joints. When a climber dies, its valves lock open; its calcified tripod remains anchored to the cliff-face for centuries, an empty tripod gallows upon which the moss slowly weaves its rubbery wool.

### The Reservoir Fauna: Agitator-Worms and Valve-Crabs

The hanging cistern-bladders beneath the basalt shelves are not merely storage organs of the flora; they are closed, self-regulating biomes suspended in the void. Inside these locomotive-sized sacs of translucent, ribbed skin, the brine is black, bitter, and saturated with zinc and magnesium salts.

Suspended at the core of each bladder are the *Agitators*: blind, worm-like annelids several meters long, whose bodies are lined with alternating bands of dense muscle and magnetite plates. They swim in continuous, unbroken figure-eights, never resting. This endless churning is not for locomotion—the bladder is their entire universe—but to generate internal friction. The heat of their heavy, muscular labor keeps the mineral brine just above its freezing point at -20°C. Their skin is smooth, gray, and completely cold to the touch, shedding flakes of nutrient-rich dead cuticle that feed the floor of the sac.

Patrolling the inner and outer walls of the bladders are the *Valve-Crabs*. Shaped like heavy iron washers, these creatures possess no sensory eyes, only pressure-sensitive diaphragms along their rims. They serve as living gaskets. Wherever the fibrous wall of a cistern begins to tear under its own titanic weight, a valve-crab crawls over the rupture, drives its barbed outer perimeter deep into the surrounding tissue, and pulls itself taut, sealing the leak with its own calcified shell. Over months, the host plant secretes vulcanizing resin over the crab, cementing it permanently into the wall. A very old cistern is a mosaic of dozens of dead and living crabs, its structural integrity sustained entirely by these embedded organic plates.

### The Voids: Flywheel-Chains and Siphon-Tethers

In the gulfs between the spires, where there is neither rock to grip nor shelter from the thin, sub-zero draft, the *Flywheel-Chains* drift in horizontal arcs that span miles of empty air. 

These siphonophores are colonial organisms composed of thousands of specialized, pale-white units linked end-to-end like heavy, braided cables. At the head and tail of each colony sits a large, bulbous bell that houses a rotating balance-wheel of dense osteo-calcite. Suspended within an oily, fluid-filled biological gimbals-socket, this bone wheel is kept spinning at several thousand revolutions per minute through rhythmic, undulating muscular pulses along the socket wall. The gyroscopic resistance created by these spinning stones keeps the colossal living cable straight, preventing it from twisting, kinking, or snapping in the unpredictable high-altitude shear winds.

The individual zooids that make up the cable have specific, mechanical duties:
* **The Intake Nodes:** Spaced every ten meters, these bell-shaped mouths suck in the thin, moisture-beaded air, compressing it within internal bladders to strip away supercooled water droplets and microscopic drifting spores.
* **The Arc-Nodes:** Metallic-sheathed nodules that trail long, micro-fine filaments of silver-laced silk into the void. These filaments act as drifting ground-wires, catching the static charge of the clouds and funneling it down the length of the colony to power the muscular engines of the gyroscopes.
* **The Valve-Mouths:** Rhythmic exhaust vents that release bursts of dried, un-ionized air in regulated pulses, nudging the entire mile-long organism through the black sky with the precision of a steerable vessel.

Occasionally, two Flywheel-Chains cross paths in the dark. They do not prey on one another. Instead, their trailing ground-wires tangle, causing a blinding, sustained discharge of amber electricity between them that fuses their outer skins at the point of contact. Over days, they share fluids and genetic information through this molten bridge before the joint deliberately calcifies and snaps, leaving both colonies to drift onward with new ballast.

### The Void-Hunters: Lung-Divers and Gantry-Gargoyles

Predation in this vertical world is dictated by the energy cost of falling and the impossibility of true flight in the thin atmosphere.

The large, fan-folded gliders—the *Lung-Divers*—do not nest; they roost by driving heavy, pick-like tail-anchors directly into the basalt of the highest pinnacles, hanging head-downward like folded umbrellas of black, greased hide. Their bodies are built around a massive, hollow chest-cavity lined with high-pressure lung-bladders.

When a Lung-Diver detects the low-frequency electrical hum of a drifting Flywheel-Chain miles below, it does not launch itself forward; it simply releases its tail-clamp. The fall is terrifyingly pure: a dead, vertical drop through three thousand meters of darkness, during which the creature remains completely motionless, its wings tucked tight to reduce drag, its speed approaching terminal velocity.

As it plunges through the cloud-strata, the rush of dense, supercooled moisture is forced down its gaping dorsal intake-ducts, ramming into its compression lungs. The air is chemically stripped of its oxygen, which is instantly combined with a store of volatile bodily hydrocarbons in an enclosed, thick-walled metabolic furnace at the base of its spine. 

Seconds before impact, the creature opens its lateral venting ports. The expanding gas from the reaction vents outward with a muffled, hollow hiss, creating an instantaneous lateral thrust that snaps the creature out of its vertical dive into a razor-sharp horizontal glide. It passes through the drifting siphonophore cable like a cleaver, its rotary, circular jaw—lined with three concentric rings of self-sharpening horn-teeth—spinning at blinding speed to carve out a massive cylindrical core of living tissue.

The Lung-Diver does not try to climb back up. The air is too thin to flap against. Instead, having fed, it deploys immense, ribbed parachutes of semi-permeable parchment from its flank-slots. It sinks slowly into the thick, boiling cloud-seas of the unreachable bottom-floor miles below, where the dense, warm atmosphere allows it to feed on the abyssal carrion and slowly swell its internal gas-chambers with warm, light methane over the course of years, until—bloated and buoyant—it ascends once more through the cloud deck like an unhurried, rising mine, returning to the freezing spires of its youth to anchor, starve, and dive again.

### The Apex: The Arc-Baskers

At the absolute summits of the tallest needles, where the stone narrows to a needle-sharp point and the sky is black even at noon, live the *Arc-Baskers*. 

They are sessile leviathans that appear less like animals and more like weathered, basaltic caps upon the mountain heads. Their bodies are composed almost entirely of dead, layered horn and dense plates of mineralized bone, with no appendages, no sensory organs, and no digestive tract. They are entirely autotrophic, but their energy does not come from photosynthesis or chemical synthesis.

Protruding from the crown of an Arc-Basker is a five-story-tall spire of pure, crystalline magnetite, grown organically over centuries through the accretion of atmospheric iron-dust. This horn acts as a continuous lightning rod for the vast, silent electrical differentials that build between the mountain spires.

During the cold front crossings, when the moss sheets begin to discharge their amber sheets of spark, the Arc-Basker waits. When the lightning strikes—a massive, silent arc of several million volts slamming directly into its magnetite spire—the creature’s entire body becomes incandescent, glowing with a deep, internal cherry-red heat. 

Beneath its heavy armor, its frozen, crystalline organs liquefy in an instant. For the thirty or forty seconds following a direct strike, the Arc-Basker is truly alive: its heart beats in an explosive, high-speed flutter; its reproductive nodes generate and cast off clouds of microscopic, magnetically polarized spores that shoot upward along the ionizing lightning-channel into the upper stratosphere; and its deep, resonant brain registers a brief, ecstatic burst of consciousness.

Then, the voltage drains into the mountain’s roots. The red heat fades back to charcoal-gray. The organs gel, crystallize, and freeze solid once more. The Arc-Basker returns to stone, waiting in the violet-black cold for the next sky to tear open.
