In a landscape where the mortar is never given the leisure to dry, whatever lives must have a relationship not with stone, but with *tack*. 

They are things that cannot endure being set. To be cured, to reach that final, brittle ring of finished ceramic, is for them a form of petrifaction and death. They exist entirely in the long, wet afternoon between the molding and the firing, sustained by the lukewarm brine and the draft-gas that bubbles through the herringbone seams.

Across the great pans of red slip drift the **Skimmers**—though they are less like animals and more like the living skin that forms across a bowl of cooling porridge. They have no skeleton other than surface tension. From above, they appear only as wide, pale rhomboids of pale gray slip, fifty yards across, gliding across the flooded tiles with a low, tearing whisper like greaseproof paper being peeled from wet dough. They feed on the salt-efflorescence as it blooms, grazing the cauliflower-like heads of lye before the hot water can dissolve them. When a Skimmer feeds, it turns translucent at the margins, revealing the faint, ghosted impressions of whatever passed beneath it hours before: the cross-hatch of a reed basket, the whorl of an elbow-joint, the drag of a sodden rope. They cannot leave the water; if they wander onto a high, drying ledge, their edges curl instantly, crackling like dry parchment, until they are reduced to a scattering of red flakes that the rising steam re-hydrates by dusk.

Where the tiles bow and form deep, stagnant sumps, there are things with more weight—the **Pug-Wethers**. 

These are heavy, blunt-snouted lumps of dense, leathery grog, the size of draft oxen but shaped like dropped wedges of raw body-clay. They have no eyes; in the amber fog, vision is useless compared to the sense of moisture-gradients. They have four or six short, spade-like limbs that end not in claws, but in broad wooden ribs—scrapers of dense, unrotting ironwood that appear to have grown directly out of their joint-caps. The Pug-Wethers spend their lives wedging the soft ground. They heave their immense, damp bulk across the buckling floors, rhythmic and slow, driving their ribs into the seams to press out air pockets. As they move, they leave behind them long, perfectly leveled furrows of burnished tile, dense and mirror-smooth. They are drawn to voids; if an underground flue collapses or a pocket of steam threatens to blow a crater through the floor, a herd of them will gather, throwing their wet, heavy flanks over the breach, sealing it with their own substance until the pressure drops. Sometimes one will remain pressed into a seam too long, caught in a sudden updraft from the kilns below; its skin will redden, vitrify, and freeze into a permanent, glassy hump of dark stoneware, around which its living fellows must forever scrape and steer.

Higher up, in the unfinished ziggurats and the hollow earthenware cylinders, lives a dry, nervous ecology.

These are the **Tie-Weavers**, spindly and pale as salt-worms, whose bodies are woven from the same damp jute and sinew that holds the architecture together. They have the dry, rustling gait of harvestmen, their legs consisting of multiple jointed reeds tipped with sharp bone bodkins. They do not sleep; they are gripped by a ceaseless, twitching panic regarding the tension of the world. They scuttle across the sheer mudbrick faces, testing the lashings with their front limbs, singing to one another in thin, whistling clicks that mimic the sound of cooling glaze. Where a cylinder begins to splay under its own weight, hundreds of them descend in a frantic, uncoordinated swarm, spitting long, viscous threads of raw hide-glue and hemp from their bellies, binding the terra-cotta ribs together before the clay can tear. They are completely deaf to everything except the pitch of stretching fiber; if a lashing snaps with the sharp *thwip* of broken cordage, the Weavers in the vicinity will drop from the walls like dead leaves, paralyzed by the rupture.

And beneath everything, moving under the floor itself in the vast, unmapped labyrinth of the draft-flues, are the **Stampers**. 

No one sees them whole. They are known only by the rhythm of their passage—a deep, subterranean *thud-thump-thud* that travels through the soles of the feet through miles of wet tile. They are the great, clumsy engines of the world’s renewal. Where the floor sags too low, drowning in slip, a Stamper rises from the flues like a blunt knee or an immense, blind heel, lifting acres of paving on its leathery back. It pushes the mud upward into a new ridge, kneading the slurry with the crude, heavy pads of its belly until the water drains off into the gutters. Its hide is encrusted with broken sherds, kiln-slag, and thick rinds of vitrified sand, clinking softly as it breathes. When it vents, it does so through the hollow ceramic chimneys that pierce the flats, exhaling long, rhythmic sighs of white woodsmoke that smell of burning willow and damp loam.

Nothing in this place has a name for itself, because nothing is finished. The creatures are born directly from the mud when a wall folds over; they live for three days or three hundred years, depending on how long their particular terrace stays wet; and when they die, they simply lose their shape under the endless, warm condensation. They slump back into the mortar, their bones softening into chalk, their hides dissolving into red slip, ready to be gathered up by the next wave of mud and pressed once more into the face of a wall that never reaches the sky.
