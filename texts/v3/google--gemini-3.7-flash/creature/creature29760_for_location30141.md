To live here is to live in the company of a pulse.

Every creature that feeds, sleeps, or crawls through the Great Sump-Chains has its heartbeat subtly entrained to the forty-minute cycle of the balance-beams and the slow, sighing ventilation of the brass relief valves. Time is not measured by the sun—which remains a silver smudge, never setting so much as dimming into a pearlescent slate—but by the thermal tides that pulse through the ironwork. When the deep conduits flush with subterranean water, the metal swells and warms by three or four degrees; when the intake clears, the chill of the high altitude settles back into the moss. The entire fauna shifts its weight accordingly, leaning into or out of the iron.

### The Seam-Creepers and Condensation Spinners

At the smallest scale, life clings directly to the metallurgy. The massive zinc plates and cold cast-iron flanges are coated in a living velvet of iron-oxidizing bacteria, waxy lichens, and a peculiar genus of blind, flattened isopods known colloquially as *flange-lice*. These crustaceans, pale as peeled almonds and no larger than a fingernail, graze exclusively on the black, semi-fossilized tallow that still weeps from the machine joints. Their shells are impregnated with zinc and sulfur, giving them a dull, metallic luster; when threatened, they clamp themselves flush to the wet iron with suction-rimmed legs, resisting the tug of rushing condensate.

Between the spokes of the stationary idler wheels—some fifty feet in diameter, half-drowned in peat—spin the mist-spiders. They do not spin webs to catch flying prey, for few insects take to the dense, saturated air. Instead, they cast delicate, horizontal suspension bridges of hydroscopic silk between the iron teeth. These sheets gather the drifting steam until they sag under the weight of thousands of crystal beads. The spiders drink the mineral-rich dew and feed upon the tiny springtails and spore-motes trapped in the surface tension, moving across their sodden riggings with slow, high-kneed deliberation.

### The Pipe-Singers

The heavy-bodied birds that roost within the dry exhaust chimneys are *flue-grosbeaks*, though their thick, rounded bills and dense, oily down make them resemble diving alcids more than songbirds. Their plumage is the color of wet river-slate, waterproofed by an abundance of preen-oil that smells faintly of peat smoke and brass shavings. 

Their life is entirely acoustic. Because the saturated air deadens sound across open distances, the grosbeaks use the mountain’s vast labyrinth of empty iron pipes as an amplification system. A male will wedge himself into the throat of an abandoned draft-flue, brace his splayed feet against the soot-encrusted rivets, and cast a low, resonant drone into the shaft. The sound travels for miles through the interconnected conduits, reverberating inside the hollow balance-beams and emerging as a spectral, multi-toned hum from exhaust ports in distant valleys. Other birds press their breasts against the metal to feel the vibrations, answering not through the air, but directly into the iron, so that the entire mountain range functions as a single, distributed musical instrument.

### The Iron-Walkers

The slate-colored deer, whose fleeces have the oily, water-shedding density of otter fur, are creatures of pure balance. Their hooves are extraordinarily broad and split deep to the pastern, lined on the underside with a black, rubbery cushion that can find traction on polished, wet zinc without making a sound. 

They are not grazers of grasses, for true grasses cannot survive the wet gloom; they browse on the rubbery tongues of liverwort that carpet the aqueducts, and on the thick, gelatinous mats of red algae that flourish in the warm discharge channels. They have developed a symbiotic reliance on the machinery’s heat: pregnant does will bed down directly upon the warm, vibrating housing of the steam-chests, where the metal radiates a steady ninety degrees against the surrounding chill. 

They are hunted, though rarely, by the *sump-panther*—a squat, heavy-shouldered stalker with short, dense, seal-like pelt and wide, paddle-shaped paws. The predator does not chase, for running on the slippery iron or sinking peat would be fatal. Instead, it lies motionless for hours in the blind angles beneath reciprocating crossheads, disguised by the thick rime of club-moss that grows across its back. It waits for the piston to plunge—using the low shudder and the hiss of escaping vapor to mask its strike—pulling a deer down into the soft peat before the steam-stroke has fully finished its breath.

### The Deep Cistern Dwellers

Deep within the flooded granite ribs of the massif, where the cathedral-sized iron cylinders balance the mountain's hydrostatic weight, lives an ecology that has never seen even the silver daylight of the plateaus.

In these drowned chambers, where black water sits under immense, cyclical pressure, move the *great sirens*. These are neotenic salamanders of monstrous proportion, some reaching twelve feet in length, their skin translucent, milky, and completely devoid of pigment. Their external gills are not crimson like those of their smaller surface-dwelling kin, but a ghostly, luminous violet, glowing faintly in the pitch darkness through a slow chemical combustion of dissolved minerals.

They swim with a slow, undulating rhythm through the flooded flues and between the massive submerged valves, feeding on blind, white cave-shrimps and the thick mats of chemosynthetic fungi that coat the drowned ironwork. They are completely deaf to airborne noise, but they possess an organ running the entire length of their lateral line that can detect the microscopic flex of the iron cylinders as the pistons rise and fall. To them, the machines are not foreign intrusions, but the very lung-tissue of their world—the engine that pumps cold, oxygen-saturated surface water into their subterranean hunting grounds and expels their waste into the low valley bogs miles below.

### The Slow Tenders

Perhaps the strangest inhabitants of the high plateaus are the *moss-sloths*—hulking, slow-limbed creatures that have abandoned the dwarf junipers to live entirely upon the suspended three-mile balance-beams. 

Weighing several hundred pounds, with curved, spade-like claws that can hook securely over the iron flanges, they move only a few yards each day. Their long, shaggy coats are micro-habitats in themselves, supporting entire miniature bogs of liverwort, stonecrop, and nematodes. A moss-sloth will spend three weeks traversing the length of a single iron beam, methodically scraping away the corrosive scale with its heavy incisors to reach the sweet, fatty mineral salts deposited by the weeping rivets. 

In doing so, they inadvertently keep the great mechanical joints clear of choking debris, pruning back the moss that would otherwise foul the brass rocker-bearings. When the balance-beam slowly tilts—rising fifty feet into the mist over the course of twenty minutes, then sinking back with a long, muffled sigh—the sloth does not even open its milky eyes. It simply tightens its grip on the cold iron, buries its face into the damp emerald wool of its own chest, and rides the machine down into the fog.
