To move within this place, a thing cannot possess meat. Flesh is too soft, too slow to heal, and hopelessly vulnerable to the shearing shears of green wood and the razor-crests of the root-plates. What populates these erupting boulevards are beings spun entirely from the same hyper-pressurized xylem and tension-wood that forms the towers—creatures that are less like animals and more like breakaway boughs, mobile galls, and self-directing vascular engines.

They do not have muscles; they have hydraulics.

Watch one step down from a fresh-split terrace: it stands nine feet tall, a narrow, braided armature of pale willow-like lianas coiled around a central core of compressed white pith. It moves not with the smooth swing of a pendulum, but with violent, ratcheting releases of stored kinetic energy. Inside its joints, high-pressure sap bladders inflate until an organic catch-valve slips, snapping a limb forward with the whip-crack violence of a triggered mantis shrimp. When its foot—a hard, splayed cluster of lignified root-spurs—strikes the bucking pavement, the impact drives tiny anchor-tendrils directly into the ground’s fibrous mat. For half a second, it is rooted, drinking deep from the pressurized sap-veins beneath the surface; then, with a wet, splintering tearing sound, it rips its anchors free and snaps the next leg forward.

These are the *Cambial Drivers*, the walkers of the main avenues. They have no faces, but their crowns are swollen into broad, translucent galls of amber-capped parenchyma. Through this sunlit tissue, pale fluid swirls in rapid convection currents, carrying rhythmic pulses of calcium ions and ethylene gas—a chemical nervous system operating at the frantic speed of the city itself. They do not see in light and shadow as eyes do; they feel the ultrasonic squeal of water columns snapping under tension inside the surrounding wood. To them, the city is an acoustic landscape of stress-fractures, hydraulic loads, and phototropic hunger.

Where a tower begins to groan, leaning dangerously out over an avenue before its thirty-minute lifespan is complete, the Drivers gather. They do not build with tools; they *are* the tools. 

Three or four will fling their bodies against the tilting wicker-wall, driving their limbs deep into the lattice. Within seconds, their skin weeps a frothing, rubbery cambial glue that fuses them permanently to the structure. They become living buttresses. Their bodies swell, pumping sap from their own cores into the weakening joints of the tower, holding it rigid against the sky for a few vital minutes more so that its crowning flower-heads can burst and drop their heavy, melon-sized guttation bulbs. Once the spire finally snaps, the Drivers do not struggle. They are crushed, sheared, and dissolved into the splintered wreckage, their tissues instantly harvested by the runner-vines that knit the disaster into a new bridge. To them, individuality is not an enduring state, but a brief, rigid posture held against the light before being re-absorbed.

Lower down, in the dim, dripping tunnels formed beneath the overlapping root-plates, lives something more fluid.

These are the *Pith-Weavers*. If the Drivers are wood and hydraulic spring, the Weavers are soft, pale, and cellular—great mats of ambulatory, fungus-laced pith that creep through the dark like sheets of living dough. They inhabit the spaces where the city digests its own foundations. The Weavers have no fixed anatomy; they extrude exploratory pseudopods of dense, white fungal mycelium that infiltrate the dying wood, breaking down the lignin in a matter of minutes with caustic, boiling enzymes. 

As they feast, they generate immense internal heat. Steam whistles from venting fissures in their backs, smelling intensely of fermenting mash and ozone. When a rupture opens in the ceiling above them—when an avenue splits and sunlight pours down into the subterranean rot—the Weavers react instantly. They do not flee the sun; they launch themselves toward it. Inflating massive internal air-bladders with pressurized water vapor, they fountain upward into the streets as towering, pale columns of foam. In the dry, blazing sun, their outer layer cures in under twenty seconds, hardening into chalk-white, fibrous bridges and ramps that the ambulatory thickets immediately scramble across.

Communication among the inhabitants is neither vocal nor silent; it is percussive and chemical.

When two Drivers cross paths on a vibrating plaza, they do not pause—to pause is to be overgrown and pinned by the moss-carpets within minutes. Instead, as they brush past, they snap whip-like stipules against each other’s flanks. The crack is as loud as a rifle shot, driving a hollow thorn deep into the other’s vascular ring. In that microsecond of connection, a high-pressure jet of sap is exchanged—a dense burst of hormonal instructions, news of a light-well opening two miles east, or the chemical signature of a predatory strangler-bramble expanding in the lower tiers. The thorn snaps off, the wound froths green and seals instantly with amber glass, and both beings spring onward without breaking stride.

Even their lifecycles exist in a state of hyper-velocity. Nothing here grows old; things are either expanding, rigid with full turgor, or splintered into fertilizer. 

A Driver is born from a knot-gall on the flank of a dying spire: a swelling blister of wood that fills with sap until it ruptures, dropping the creature onto the pavement already twitching, already ratcheting its limbs, already searching for the brightest thermal plume. Its entire existence—its birth, its frantic strides across the heaving avenues, its chemical conversations, its fusion into a bridge or its crushing under a falling colonnade—spans perhaps four to six hours. By nightfall, not a single cell of the morning’s generation remains unconsumed.

At the highest pinnacles, where the spires narrow to needles of pure, pale heartwood swaying hundreds of feet above the roar, there exist the *Solar Cavitators*. 

These are delicate, wind-sculpted ribcages of dry, resonant reed-wood that never touch the ground. They cling to the tips of the tallest green towers with clawed tendrils, their hollow, paper-thin bodies acting as natural wind-harps. As the fierce thermal drafts rise from the boiling chlorophyll of the city below, these creatures do not fight the wind; they drink it. The moving air sets their interior reed-pipes screaming in harmonic unison with the hydraulic pulse of the spire beneath them. 

They are the city’s regulators. By vibrating at precise acoustic frequencies, their song vibrates the vascular bundles of the tower, preventing the high-pressure sap from foaming into vapor under the noon heat, keeping the water-columns intact so the spire can shoot another twenty yards into the blinding light before its inevitable, thunderous collapse.

They cling there, singing in the dry roar, utterly mindless of yesterday, entirely consumed by the frantic, mechanical violence of the next inch of growth.
