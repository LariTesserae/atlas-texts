In a world where moisture has been extinguished, life does not flow; it clicks, shears, and accretes. 

The things that inhabit the hanging city are made of the same exhausted substances as the avenues: desiccated sinew, hollowed marrow-cane, dry horn, and the piezoelectric chalk that flakes from the lintels. They contain no fluids—no bile, no lymph, no blood. If one were snapped in two, its interior would reveal only concentric rings of dry, papery fiber and chambers of fine, grey dust, smelling faintly of old lime and pulverized wasps’ nests.

### The Seam-Lice and the Efflorescence Feeders

Low to the flags, inside the microscopic fractures where the shale terraces threaten to cleave, live the smallest tenants: the *psalteries*, or slate-lice. 

They appear less like animals and more like stacks of grey, mica-thin plates, overlapping loosely like the pages of a warped book left in the sun. They possess neither eyes nor legs; instead, their undersides are carpeted with thousands of microscopic bone-spurs that vibrate in unison when exposed to the electrostatic tension of a shifting gravity well. They move across the sheer undersides of plazas with a dry, shuffling rustle, like playing cards being gently fanned over velvet.

The psalteries feed upon the mineral blooms. When a rosette of salt crystals erupts from an arcade joint, dozens of them gather, wedging their brittle rostrums into the lattice to shave away the chalk. They digest nothing in the chemical sense; rather, they crush the mineral against internal gizzards of hard talc, harvesting the faint, static sparks generated by the friction of fractured crystals, before expelling the pulverized meal as the fine talc that blankets the streets.

Where psalteries congregate in large numbers, they leave behind neat, geometric trails in the dust—not tracks of passage, but deliberate scoring marks etched into the stone, which act as acoustic guides for the larger things drifting through the gloom.

### The Column-Stalkers (The Slit-Born)

The billions of tall, narrow doorways were not cut for men, nor were they abandoned by them. They are precisely dimensioned for the *Stalkers*—the city’s primary, upright denizens, who spend centuries wedged inside the cubic cells, waiting for the alignment of their sectors.

A Stalker stands between nine and twelve feet tall, yet its torso is rarely thicker than a heavy timber, compressed laterally until it can slip edgewise through an opening no wider than a handspan. Its frame is composed of tubular, calcified ribs bound together by dry, yellowed tendons that have the tensile strength of braided steel wire. Its skin is a tight wrap of cured, ivory-toned rind, completely seamless, bearing no pores, no nostrils, and no mouth.

Instead of a face, the Stalker’s head is a tall, vertical blade of porous bone, perforated with thousands of microscopic acoustic chambers. As it stands in the doorway of a floating room, the dead air drifts through these perforations, producing a faint, harmonic drone—a dry, reed-like whistle that rises in pitch as a neighboring district approaches, and drops to a low, hollow hum when the quarter is drifting in isolation.

The Stalkers do not walk with a swinging stride. Their limbs are multi-hinged, moving in stiff, planar arcs. When traversing an avenue, they step with exquisite, calculated slowness, planting three-toed splay-feet whose pads are made of dense, spongy horn. They do not cross the plazas to forage or hunt; they move only to follow the shifting nodes of local gravity.

To a Stalker, the world is an invisible mesh of tension lines. They perceive the subtle tilt of a shearing avenue hours before the stone actually slips. When a district begins its ten-minute rotation, the Stalkers of that quarter awaken from their corner-slits. They gather along the shear lines, standing shoulder-to-shoulder at the very brink of the void, perfectly motionless, their reed-heads whistling in unison. 

When the terrace finally slams home against a new boulevard leagues away, the impact sends a shockwave through their rigid frames. They absorb the violent kinetic shock through their dry marrow-canes, converting the kinetic energy into the low, piezoelectric hum that keeps their nervous systems alive. They do not eat; they are sustained entirely by the violence of architectural docking.

### The Pendulum-Brood

Farther down, where the city’s lower galleries dissolve into the ivory drop, live things that have surrendered even the pretense of civil posture. These are the *Pendulums*.

A Pendulum is a colonial organism that begins when several dozen strata-weavers fail to disengage their needle-spurs from a sagging lintel. Over decades, their flattened, leathery bodies fuse along the margins, their ribcages interlocking into a flexible, segmented cable that can reach hundreds of yards in length. 

At the bottom of this living cable hangs a heavy, bulbous anchor-mass—a cluster of dense, ossified heads and interlocking limbs, blind and silent, whose sole purpose is to act as a counterweight.

As the district to which it is rooted drifts across the void, the Pendulum swings in lazy, sweeping parabolas through the empty air. Its cable segments expand and contract with a rhythmic *clack-clack-clack*, tasting the air for the subtle electrostatic plumes that precede a colliding causeway. 

When the swinging anchor-mass strikes the facade of an approaching quarter, its hook-shaped bone spurs bite into the shale with the sound of an iron pickaxe hitting slate. The Pendulum instantly locks its fibrous tissues, acting as a living hawser that arrests the momentum of the drifting district, drawing the two massive stone bodies together until their masonry teeth mesh and lock. 

Once the docking is complete, the Pendulum does not detach. It remains stretched taut between the two sectors, slowly calcifying, its fibers gradually replaced by the infiltration of salt and talc until it is no longer flesh at all, but a new, arching bridge of white stone, waiting for the dust to settle over its ribs.

### The Quiet Accretion

There is no birth here, and no rot. 

When a Stalker’s marrow finally loses its elasticity—after millennia of absorbing the shocks of shifting terraces—its tendons do not decay. They simply lose their tension, snapping one by one with the dry, sharp report of broken bowstrings. The Stalker falls forward onto the pavement, shattering into dry laths of bone and showers of fine white meal.

The psalteries arrive within hours. They do not devour the fallen; they sort it. With infinite, mindless patience, they nudge the shards of bone into the nearest wall-slits, stacking them in neat, vertical bundles along the corners. Over centuries, as the salt blooms grow through the pile, the mineral and bone knit together, crystallizing directly into the shale. 

Eventually, a new vertical flute of marrow forms within the wall. The acoustic chambers hollow themselves out as the stone breathes the cold air. One day, when a distant district grinds along its fault line, the new form shivers, steps out of its slit on multi-jointed legs, and takes its place along the curb, turning its faceless head toward the silent, ivory drop.
