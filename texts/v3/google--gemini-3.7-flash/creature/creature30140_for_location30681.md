They do not swim. To swim would be an admission of buoyancy, and buoyancy has long since been engineered or bred out of them. They are far too heavy for the water to lift, their bones dense as pig-iron, thickened by centuries of hydrostatic pressure until the marrow has compressed into a dark, greasy paste. 

When they move between the tenements and the boiler housings, they walk. Their boots are cast-bronze sabots with lead plates riveted to the soles, clacking against the iron catwalks with dull, vibrationless taps that travel through the metal rather than the water. 

They are recognizably descended from the municipal workforces that once tended foundries and rail-heads, but the architecture of their flesh has adjusted to the box-canyon’s logic. They stand somewhat short, broad-shouldered and thick-torsoed, with ribcages that do not expand or contract. Their chests are solid, barrel-like vaults of fused bone; they do not have lungs that hold air, for a pocket of air at four hundred fathoms would collapse like an eggshell. Instead, their internal cavities are entirely flooded with a dense, glycerin-thick fluid, isotonic to the deep brine, which circulates sluggishly through spongy, vascular tissues in their flanks. 

They have no use for voices. Their mouths are small, sphincter-like apertures fringed with pale, muscular ridges, used primarily for ingesting the dense, vitamin-rich bacterial scum they scrape from the catwalk railings with broad horn spatulas. When they must communicate, they lean forward until their foreheads touch, or they lay a broad, spade-like hand flat against the same iron beam. They speak by clicking their teeth together in rhythmic, staccato cadences, transmitting code through the skull and into the ironwork. An order given on the third tier of the tenement block—a command to adjust the stroke-length of a silt-press or to grease an idler bearing—travels as a faint, rapid drumming through fifty yards of submerged lattice to the fingers of an oiler standing on the bottom gantry.

Their skin is the color of skimmed milk left in an iron pail: grayish-white, hairless, and remarkably tough, like the boiled hide used for heavy drive belts. Over their shoulders and loins, they wear garments made of layered gutta-percha and oil-cured canvas, stiffened with salt and cinched tight with copper wire. These clothes are not for warmth—their blood has the consistency of mineral oil and does not freeze—but to provide anchorage for tools. Loops of tarred hemp hang from their harnesses, holding tallow-horns, brass calipers, and long, square-headed wrenches designed to fit the underwater gland-nuts of the piston jackets.

Around their heads they wear open-faced coifs of hammered copper, lined with felt to cushion their ears from the immense hydrostatic dead-weight. Their eyes are small, round, and covered by thick, convex corneas that have clouded over with a permanent, pearlescent haze. They do not see shapes so much as they perceive shifts in density and the pale, greenish smudges cast by the galvanic lanterns. They navigate primarily through their palms and the soles of their boots, reading the tension of every rivet, the balance of every eccentric cam, and the slow, hydraulic groaning of the trench walls.

They live in the tenement rooms, though the concept of domesticity has thinned to something purely mechanical. Inside the flooded brick chambers, where the wallpaper has dissolved into black mud and the furniture has petrified into stone-hard silt, they do not sleep as surface-dwellers do. Instead, they hook themselves to heavy brass eye-bolts set into the masonry using short lanyards of gutta-percha. There they hang, suspended in the neutral, dead water for three or four hours at a time, their limbs limp, their pulse dropping to five or six beats a minute, while small, translucent isopods crawl over their shoulders, cleaning away dead skin and bacterial filaments.

There are life-stages among them, though they are difficult for an outsider to distinguish. 

The youngest are small, smooth-skinned creatures, no longer than an arm, kept in the lower flooded basements where the warm-core brine vents from the soot-stacks. In these nursery-vaults, the water is mineral-heavy and warm as blood. The young cling to the brickwork like pale limpets, their bones gradually absorbing calcium and iron from the effluent, growing denser, heavier, and less buoyant month by month until their weight finally pulls them down to the tiled floor, ready to be fitted with their first small bronze shoes.

And in the deepest vaults—the silt-chambers where the basalt looms lay their woven mats—exist the oldest. 

These elders have ceased walking altogether. Over decades of tending the roller-tracks, their limbs have become encrusted with the same calcite rimes and iron-reducing bacteria that coat the machinery. They have wedged themselves into the masonry niches between the loom-frames, their lower bodies fused to the foundation stone by thick crusts of tubeworm shell and mineral deposit. They are no longer mechanics; they are components. Their long, spatulate fingers remain resting on the bronze shuttles, feeling the tension of the basalt threads, occasionally nudging a sticky valve or flicking away an eel that has strayed into the gear-teeth. 

They do not leave the trench. They have no concept of an "up" that ends in light or dry air; to them, upward is simply the direction from which cold, low-mineral brine occasionally drifts, and downward is where the work goes—where the black silt ingots are stacked into neat, infinite rows, paving the floor of the basalt abyss in silence, one heavy, perfect block at a time.
