The primary inhabitant is not an individual, but a density. 

To look closely at the floor is to see that the palm-sized plates do not merely overlap; they interlock like living shingles, their margins beveled so that as one moves forward, its neighbor is lifted slightly, passing the motion down the line in a dry, shuddering wave. They are called by no human name, but their anatomy is an absolute testament to the kiln. 

Their dorsal shells are not smooth horn. They are scored with tight, parallel ruts that run from head to tail like the cooling fins of an engine block. In the near-infrared dark, these ridges glow with a faint, dull bronze cast, holding and conducting the floor’s thermal throb into the body’s interior. They have no soft bellies. Where an ordinary arthropod would have a tender underbelly, these possess a secondary armor of pale, overlapping plates made of calcium fluoride and compacted silt. Their legs are short, triple-jointed wedges tipped with single, blunt claws of fused iron-silicate—pegs that do not slip on the greasy stone, but have, over uncounted generations, buffed the basalt to its strange, dark polish.

They have no eyes, no antennae to sweep the air. Instead, their leading edge is a thick, spade-shaped flange of horn, behind which sit three pairs of recessed, file-like mouthparts. These do not chew in the way of jaws; they grind sideways, a relentless, dry mill that shears away the thinnest micro-stratum of salt, sulfur, and shed powder from whatever surface they press against. 

When one dies, it does not rot. It contains scarcely three drops of moisture, and that fluid is not water or blood, but a heavy, non-volatile oil dense with dissolved minerals. When the heart-tube ceases its slow, hot squeeze, the oil is drawn outward through capillary pores in the shell, instantly consumed by the neighbors rubbing against it. The dry husk becomes brittle within seconds. Under the weight of the climbing tier above, it collapses with the sound of a stepped-on dry leaf, reduced to a coarse meal that is immediately funneled into the mouths beneath.

***

Underneath the churning stratum of the shale-backed grazers exists a sub-world of pure extraction. 

Here, in the suffocating heat between the under-carapaces and the rock face, move the needles. They are blind, pale filaments no thicker than coarse broom straws, four inches long, moving with a silent, rippling gait on dozens of paired, glass-like bristles. They do not eat the rock. They eat the friction. They scavenge the fine, hot flour produced by the grinding plates above, their bodies constructed entirely of hollow, translucent rings of silica. When they move, they do not clatter; they slide like fine sand pouring through an hourglass.

Below them still, anchored deep into the micro-fissures of the floor where the basalt is hottest and veined with sulfur, live the lithic borers. They are the true anchors of the system. Thick as a thumb and utterly immobile, their bodies are calcified tubes cemented into the rock itself, looking like small, blunt chimneys of gray slag. Inside each tube sits a soft, blind grub whose skin is impregnated with anaerobic, sulfur-oxidizing colonies. They do not draw breath. They consume the raw, hot chemistry of the mountain’s heart, extruding from their upper rims a constant, slow crown of dry, salt-rich excretion—a bitter, white crust that the needle-scavengers and the plate-crawlers shear away as quickly as it forms. 

Around the bases of the ancient slate pillars, where the crush of bodies is greatest, a different specialization takes hold.

Here, climbing is the only escape from being ground into flour. The things that scale the pillars are narrower, keeled like the hulls of racing shells. Their carapaces are black, glossy as pitch, and their legs are disproportionately long, tipped with curved hooks that fit precisely into the fine joints of the stacked slate. They form two constant, counter-flowing streams: one ascending toward the ceiling where the trapped heat is fiercest, their bodies starved and light; the other descending, heavy and bloated from days spent clinging upside-down to the baking basalt vault.

Near the ceiling, where the air sits entirely unmoving at temperatures that would scorch human lungs to ash, they gather in dense, hanging clusters like clusters of blackened grapes. Here, they undergo their final ecdysis. They do not grow larger; instead, their shells bake and split down the center line, not to shed a skin, but to expose a pair of broad, immobile cooling vanes that radiate their stored heat directly into the cooler upper stone. They hang there for months, suspended in a state of torpor so absolute that their internal oils barely circulate, acting as thermal capacitors for the colony below.

***

And then there are the pillars themselves. 

The dry-stacked slate is ancient, but it is not unoccupied. In the hollows between the stacked slabs, where neither the plate-crawlers nor the climbers can fully wedge their bodies, lives the maker of the chamber’s quietest work.

Deep in the internal voids of the stone piles dwells a creature that has surrendered movement entirely: the spindle-weavers. They are broad, flattened discs of grey hide, several feet across, wedged horizontally between the slate courses like mortar. They have no limbs, only a fringe of hundreds of fine, hair-like spinnerets that extrude a dry, non-sticky mineral thread made of excreted basalt-dust and resinous wax. For hundreds of years, they have woven these threads through the interior joints of the pillars, drawing the slate slabs tighter together with each dry contraction of their bodies. They do not hunt; they are fed by the falling dust of the climbers above, their spinnerets constantly lacing the masonry together, reinforcing the towers against the endless, scouring climb of the millions.

They are the reason the pillars still stand. They are the slow, ancient muscle that holds the architecture upright in the dark, preserving the only vertical paths in this closed, roasting world.

When the thermal pulse comes—when the mountain’s deep, iron lung breathes its invisible wave of heat through the floor—the spindle-weavers tighten. The borers in their chimneys pulse with a dull, internal orange glow. The climbers on the pillars flatten themselves against the stone until their edges seal. And on the floor, the vast, unbroken carpet of millions tilts its bronze ridges upward in unison, a single, room-wide gesture of absorption that turns the sound of grinding stone into a vast, unanimous intake of dry breath, before the dark settles back into its steady, endless, whispering crawl.
