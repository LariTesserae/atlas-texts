At first, there seems to be nothing here that can die, and therefore nothing that lives. What moves appears to move entirely by the mandate of the rocker arms and the thermal siphons. But life in this dry cleft does not distinguish itself from the machinery by softness or moisture; it distinguishes itself only by intent, and by a patience so absolute it resembles the tempering of steel.

Close to the journal bearings, where the friction-heat is greatest and the air is heavy with the warm, solvent tang of the plant-esters, the grease-scrapers keep their stations. 

They are flattened, disk-like things the size of iron washers, their carapaces formed from dense, overlapping shingles of amber-colored horn and leaf-wax. They have no eyes; in a space where light is merely an accidental by-product of static discharge and pilot flame, they navigate entirely by the micro-chatter of the iron beneath them. They possess dozens of dry, needle-sharp legs made of spun sulfur, tipped with microscopic burrs that lock into the grain of the cast iron. They ride the surfaces of the spinning flywheels without slipping, grazing upon the microscopic pelt of waxy pollen and scorched ester that coats the rim. 

When one touches another, there is no recoil or retreat. They click together like coins dropped on a stone floor, shear past each other without pausing, and continue their slow, concentric patrol. If one is crushed by the sudden descent of a crosshead guide, it does not burst or bleed. It shatters into a dry, aromatic gravel that smells faintly of turpentine and scorched almond, and within hours, its neighbors have scraped the fragments away, re-incorporating the dry resin into their own shells.

Deeper in the cleft, where the liver-colored moss forms thick, suspended mattresses between the high-pressure copper loops, live longer, more deliberate forms. 

They are articulated ribbons, four or five feet in length, built like flexible wooden rulers or strips of spring-steel wrapped in raw felt. These are the loom-weavers. Their bodies are dry and segmented, each knuckle jointed with a self-lubricating socket of hard graphite. They move with an unsettling, undulating precision through the crushing tolerances of the valve gear, timing their passage to the exact cadence of the stroke. They do not cross a track when the counterweight swings; their nervous systems are tuned to the exact sub-harmonic frequency of the eccentric rod, and they flow through the narrow gaps between piston-rod and cylinder wall with a fraction of an inch to spare, completely unhurried.

The loom-weavers do not eat the plants, nor do they consume the grease-scrapers. Instead, they graze upon the static electricity that builds across the edges of the dry fronds. Their heads are blunt, spade-shaped wedges of polished slate, flanked by twin clusters of flexible, copper-threaded antennae. As they weave through the brush of the oil-ferns, these antennae draw off the blue, brush-discharge sparks, feeding a slow, solid-state metabolic core that rests like an ingot of heavy ore inside their midsections. When they rest, they loop themselves loosely around the hot copper steam-less pipes, anchoring their tails to the granite floor and letting the massive, teeth-shaking thrum of the engines pass directly through their bodies. 

And then, wedged into the dead spaces behind the dynamos—in the stagnant, heated pockets where the rock narrows to a sharp V—there are the keepers of the clearance.

They are few, perhaps only three or four in the entire cleft, and they are so thoroughly incorporated into the machinery that one might look directly at them for minutes without seeing them. 

A keeper is broad-backed and low, shaped like an anvil wrapped in thick, soot-caked hide. Its flesh is not meat, but a dense, cellular tallow, dry and rubbery, through which an internal circulatory system moves the same heavy, amber ester that fills the surrounding ferns. Its four limbs are short, pillar-like columns of bone-dry cartilage, tipped with broad, flat pads of compressed moss-fiber that absorb the brutal vibration of the rock without bruising.

Its face is entirely flat, devoid of mouth or nostrils, dominated by three sunken, quartz-clear lenses that register the low infrared shimmer of the bearing blocks. A keeper does not breathe; instead, its sides expand and contract in a slow, rhythmic bellows-stroke, pulling the dry, volatile atmosphere across a set of internal filter-combs made of spun mica, which condense and reclaim the aromatics before expelling the desiccated air back into the draft.

A keeper’s existence is defined entirely by the state of the mechanisms around it. It does not possess intelligence in the mammalian sense, but an exhaustive, tactile comprehension of tolerances, clearances, and thermal balance. 

For days at a time, a keeper will sit motionless behind an eccentric linkage, its gray, felt-tufted head pressed firmly against the cast-iron casing, feeling the heat of the race through its temple. If the bearing begins to run hot—if the clearance tightens by a hundredth of a millimeter or the flow of aromatic pollen thins—the creature stirs. With movements of agonized slowness, it will reach into the adjacent shrubbery with a three-pronged, horn-sheathed claw, snap a cluster of brittle, resin-filled seed-heads, and crush them directly above the oil-cup, letting the thick, cold ester dribble into the brass feed-pipe until the frequency of the bearing settles back into its familiar, deep hum.

When its work is done, it tucks its limbs beneath its tallowy belly, closes the opaque shutters over its quartz lenses, and settles once more into the moss-mat. 

The vibration passes through the rock, through the iron bedplate, through the dry felt of the floorboards, and into the core of the beast, where its heavy, non-aqueous heart beats once for every ten strokes of the main piston. There is no hunger here, no predation, no decay, and no winter. There is only the continuous, deafening *clack-whir-thump* of the dry engines, the slow, static breathing of the ribbon-things in the gear-chambers, and the great, patient weight of things that have found an endless way to endure at the dry pinnacle of the world.
