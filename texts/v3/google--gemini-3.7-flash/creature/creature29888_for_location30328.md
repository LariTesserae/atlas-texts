Beneath the floating carpet of roots, in the narrow, scalding gap between the living weave and the black obsidian floor, exists a fauna shaped entirely by heat, pressure, and glass.

Here crawl the **Slick-Plates**—broad, disc-shaped creatures neither fully mollusk nor arthropod, as wide across as shield-bosses. Because the basalt beneath them is fractured into razor-sharp scarps, their bellies do not touch the stone; instead, they rest on thick cushions of dense, self-secreting tallow that boils away into greasy steam at the edges and is continually replenished from pores along their margins. They have no eyes—light has never reached the sub-mat—but navigate by the thermal thrum of the cooling magma below, grazing on the sulfur-eating bacterial carpets that coat the hot rock in pink, gelatinous quilts. Above them, their dorsal shells are rough and porous, fused directly into the descending rootlets of the canopy above. They do not merely inhabit the sub-floor; they tether the forest to the boiling stone, anchoring the entire basin against the updrafts of its own subterranean vents.

Where the root-mat thins over bubbling mineral pans, the water is occupied by the **Marrow-Eels**. These are long, finger-thick ribbons of milk-white tissue, completely devoid of skin, their bloodless vascular systems visible through flesh as clear as poached albumen. They live in water so hot it would slough mammalian meat from bone within seconds. Their internal chemistry relies on thermal convection rather than a beating heart: the temperature differential between their scalding bellies and their slightly cooler dorsal crests drives their pale blood through simple, valve-lined tubes. They swim with an eerie, mechanical rhythm, grazing on the shed chitin and dead cuticle that drifts down through the root-lattice, never sleeping, never settling, suspended in a continuous, boiling suspension.

***

In the mid-tier, between the springy root-floor and the lower surface of the vast, skiff-sized fronds, the density of life forces an architecture of total intimacy. Animals here do not possess territories; they possess niches of contact.

Among the thick stems of the giant liverworts move the **Pulp-Gorgers**—heavy, quadrupedal things the size of oxen, though possessing none of the swiftness or bone structure of dry-land herbivores. Their skeletons are not made of calcium, which would dissolve or warp under the ceaseless acidity of the basin’s air, but of dense, flexible cartilage impregnated with vulcanized silica. Their skins are completely bald, pale violet, and perpetually wet, covered in a thick, semi-living sludge of symbiotic yeast that breaks down the toxic mineral salts weeping from the air. 

The Pulp-Gorgers have no true faces. In place of a head is a broad, spade-like muscular funnel rimmed with thousands of blunt, horny nodules. They press this funnel against the rubbery fronds and simply walk forward, stripping and pulping the thick, chlorophyll-laden skin of the plants in endless, methodical strips. Because the vegetation grows at a pace matched only by mold in a sealed jar, a Gorger can circle a single cluster of horsetails over the course of three days, and by the time it returns to its starting point, the stems have already scarred over, swollen, and thickened into edible flesh once more. They have no natural predators in the traditional sense; nothing hunts them down or kills them swiftly. Instead, their lives are a slow, frictionless continuum of ingestion, shedding, and mechanical reproduction, their offspring dropping into the root-mat already half-grown and chewing.

Living in the moist folds of the Gorgers’ skin are colonies of **Needle-Flies**—hair-fine, wingless hexapods that do not bite, but rather groom the beast, sifting through its yeast-coat for mineral grains. When a Gorger brushes against another of its kind, the Needle-Flies do not scatter; they flow across the point of contact like water poured between vessels, a single, regional population that covers every large animal in the basin in an unbroken, shifting web of tiny, chitinous feet.

***

Higher up, clinging to the wet undersides of the emerald roof where the sulfurous fog is thickest, are the **Resonators**. 

These are the origin of the rhythmic, clicking drone that fills the basin. They appear at first like swollen galls or tumors on the ribs of the fronds—soft, bladder-like organisms the size of human torsos, clad in leathery, slate-grey mantles. Inside each Resonator is a hollow, spiraled chamber of rigid chitin, through which the creature pumps the pressurized steam that gathers under the leaves. 

They do not breathe air for oxygen; they are chemosynthetic converters that filter airborne sulfuric acid and elemental steam through internal chambers lined with iron-rich cilia. As the vapor is forced through their spiral valves, it produces a deep, percussive *clack-drone*, pitched precisely to vibrate the moisture droplets off the fronds above them. They are living de-misters. Without them, the weight of the condensed water would tear the giant leaves from their stems; the plants, in turn, feed the Resonators through specialized, vascular nectar-nodes that plug directly into the creatures' basal disks. 

Between the Resonators drift the **Silk-Sails**. These are the moth-like gliders of the upper canopy, though they bear little resemblance to insects upon close inspection. They have no mouthparts and no digestive tracts; their entire adult phase lasts only as long as the fat-stores accumulated during their larval crawl through the rotting sub-mat. Their wings are wide, rectangular sheets of translucent, waxen skin, held rigid by struts of spongy cartilage. 

The Silk-Sails do not flap; they launch themselves into the permanent thermal plumes rising from the floor cracks, riding the boiling columns of vapor up into the green gloom until they hit the cooler canopy roof, then gliding on long, slow diagonals across the basin until they catch the next plume. As they drift, they trail miles-long threads of sticky, gossamer silk behind them. In the thick, windless air, these threads do not fall; they remain suspended, tangling with millions of others to form a vast, ghostly spiderweb that fills the entire upper volume of the basin. 

This suspended web catches everything: fungal spores the size of marbles, dead midges, drifting flakes of volcanic ash, and microscopic larvae cast adrift on the steam. Periodically, the larval forms of the **Ribbon-Worms**—thin, luminous, amber-colored threads—haul themselves up the stems, climb out onto the suspended silk, and slowly eat their way through the accumulated harvest, glowing with a cold, yellow phosphorescence as they metabolize the aerial soup.

***

In this basin, nothing possesses an individual fate. 

The creatures do not have eyes because there are no distances to measure; there is only the perimeter of the skin, the warmth of the rock beneath, and the chemical flavor of the steam. There is no concept of a hunt, for hunting implies an absence of food; here, the animals move through a medium that is itself partially nutritive, breathing water that is half-mineral broth, resting upon a soil that is composed entirely of living, growing flesh.

The deafening sound—the click of the bladders, the rattle of the giant millipedes upon the basalt, the wet, sliding noise of billions of soft bellies over slick leaves—is not a series of distinct calls or warnings. It is simply the mechanical friction of a vast biological engine that was started at the dawn of the crust and has found no physical reason, no cold night, no dry wind, and no winter, to ever slow its stroke.
