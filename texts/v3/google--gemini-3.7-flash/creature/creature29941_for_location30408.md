In the deep seams of the masonry, where the metamorphic blocks meet without the cushion of mortar, life begins in the dark. 

Between the stones, where the cold of the mountain's core meets the mild moisture of the morning mist, dwell communities of flat-bodied bristletails and wingless, iron-colored ground beetles. They are ancient, blind, or pin-eyed, their shells hardened against the weight of settling rock. They feed on the invisible films of microscopic blue-green algae that bloom wherever condensation runs, navigating the labyrinthine interior of the walls as if the entire settlement were a single, porous cliff. When the sun swings around the massif and warms the outer faces, the small, slate-grey lizards creep out from these same fissures. Their scales are matte, mimicking the rough-toothed chisel marks on the stone so completely that they disappear until they blink; their throats pulse with an impossibly slow cadence, timing their heartbeats to the gradual warming of the rock.

Higher up, under the dry eaves of bone-cured pine, small colonies of high-altitude swifts nest in cups of hardened mud and shed down. They are silent tenants for most of the day, darting out over the drop-off in sudden, arrow-straight trajectories that drop three hundred feet before catching the cold thermals rising from the shadowed gorge.

And beneath the leather curtains of the doorways, in the dim, stone-vaulted interiors that burrow partly into the living rock of the cliff, the people move.

They are few, but they are not remnants; they are the exact inhabitants for whom these dimensions were struck. Physically, they are broad-shouldered and thick through the torso, possessing the expanded, barrel-shaped rib cages of those whose ancestors have drawn thin air for a thousand years. Their skin is dense, weathered to the shade of cured pine or dark tallow, deeply creased around the eyes from squinting into the clean, hard glare of high-altitude light. Their hair is coarse, dark, and cropped bluntly above the collar with copper shears to keep it clear of the wind.

Their clothing is an extension of the architecture: spare, seamless, and completely devoid of ornament. They wear tunics and wrappings of heavily felted mountain wool, fulled with grease and ash until the fabric is as stiff and weather-tight as slate. The colors are natural—the soot-black of high choughs, the pale oatmeal of washed fleece, and the ash-green of dried lichen. Their feet are bound in thick, triple-soled boots of rawhide, the undersides cross-hatched with small copper studs that bite into the slick, wet stair-treads without a sound.

Speech among them is rare, not out of solemnity or religious vow, but out of a strict physiological economy. In air this thin, breath is too valuable to spend on casual cadence. When they communicate, they use a language of micro-gestures: a brief incline of the jaw toward a shadow, a light tap of the knuckles against a stone lintel, or a single, low-register click of the tongue that carries across the narrow alleys without stirring the echoes of the cliff. 

Their daily labor is quiet, cyclic, and intimately bound to the mechanics of the terrace. At dawn, before the sun has cleared the ridge, figures emerge with long, horn-bladed scrapers and wide, shallow troughs of beaten bronze. Moving with practiced, rhythmic sweeps, they clear the condensation from the low-pitched shale roofs, channeling the water into the corner basins before the sharp morning sun can evaporate it back into the sky. 

They eat slowly and sparingly. Their sustenance comes largely from the cool interiors of the deep mountain storerooms: dried cakes of pressed sedge-meal, salted mountain-trout traded decades ago and cured in stone larders, and a pale, dense cheese pressed from the milk of high-plateau rodents that they trap on the upper scree slopes. In the narrow planting troughs along the alleys, they tend small, perennial tubers whose dark, fern-like leaves barely rise above the silt, drawing strength from the slow decay of slate-dust and the bone-cold water of the drains.

In the heat of midday, when the sun turns the stone terraces into mirrors of pale light, the settlement retreats into absolute stillness. The inhabitants sit inside their thick-walled thresholds, just behind the leather curtains, working quietly with their hands. They sharpen bronze chisels on wet river-stones; they re-ply the greased leather cords that bind their garments; they mend the flat, tight-woven baskets used to carry scree. They do not hurry. Their sense of time is calibrated to the stone beneath them—an unhurried, generational pacing where an entire season might be spent dressing three blocks of shale to repair a dished step.

Children are few, and they are raised in the same profound quietude. They learn balance before they learn to point, walking the narrow edge of the outer parapet with the calm, low-gravity certainty of mountain goats, their small handprints matching the bronze-toothed impressions left by masons long dead.

As the sun dips toward the sharp crest of the massif, casting the terrace into that sudden, cold blue twilight, the people step out once more to lower the heavy hide baffles over the cisterns, locking in the gathered water against the night’s freeze. They stand for a moment along the outer ramparts, looking out over the four-thousand-pace void where the lowlands dissolve into purple shadow. They do not watch for visitors or enemies; the path up the chimney is long since lost, or kept so strictly secret that the world below has forgotten the shelf exists. 

Then, one by one, the grease-rubbed curtains fall shut. The rock lizards pull themselves deep into the joints of the megaliths. The only movement left is the settling of the frost, creeping in crystalline threads over the dry sedge, and the slow, rhythmic *tink* of water dropping into the dark heart of the stone.
