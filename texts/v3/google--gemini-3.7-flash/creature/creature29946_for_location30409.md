To call the city uninhabited is to confuse the absence of twitching, scurrying motion with the absence of life. In a world where the air has the weight of warm river-water and the roar has petrified every acoustic boundary, life does not waste itself on frantic limb-work or the fragile mechanisms of flight. It has no use for wings; there is nowhere to fall that would not simply lead to another clinging surface, and the air is too dense to permit a clean glide.

What occupies the tiered tenements, the cavernous basilicas, and the mist-shrouded amphitheaters are things that have matching densities with the stone and the steam.

Inside the deep, shadowless niches of the habitation blocks—chambers that look from a distance like empty window-frames carved into the travertine—rest the **Columnars**. They are not statues, though a traveler waiting an hour or a week might take them for decorative pilasters. They are tall, upright masses of moist, compressed pith and dense, fibrous tallow, anchored directly into the floor-plates by thick, taproot pediments. Their surfaces are smooth, pale, and constantly weeping, slicked by the condensation that courses down their flanks. 

They have no eyes, for the light never shifts, and no ears, because the roar is already inside their marrow. Instead, their entire outer dermal layer is a field of microscopic, fluid-filled pores that vibrate in sympathy with the city’s flutes. The Columnars do not breathe by expanding and contracting; rather, the extreme atmospheric pressure forces the mineral-rich steam through internal vascular chimneys that run from their bases to crown-like vents at the tops of their bodies. They live in a state of continuous, effortless perfusion. They are the citizens of the high terraces, seated in their thousands along the cliff-faces, facing each other across vertical chasms without the need to speak, linked through the interlocking root-cables beneath the pavement by low-frequency harmonic pulses. A single thought—a slow adjustment of internal salt density or an acoustic alignment with the steam-pipes—takes fifty years to travel from the highest colonnade down to the abyssal foundations.

Beneath the broad lily-pad causeways, in the blind, inverted shadows where gravity pulls toward the sheer face of the stone, lives another order of being: the **Velamen**. 

These are broad, paper-thin sheets of living tissue, vast as the sails of ships, that stretch across the underbellies of the bridges and along the vertical water-channels. They are nearly two-dimensional, composed of transparent, rubbery chitin reinforced with crystalline silica. The Velamen cling to the wet stone by sheer hydrostatic tension, bathed in the sheets of water that flow sideways down the walls. They do not hunt; they are living filters, their tissue textured like velvet to snag the microscopic mineral flakes and sulfurous yeast carried in the runoff. 

When the steam pressure spikes—an event that happens with the rhythmic regularity of a geological pulse—the Velamen ripple. The wave travels through their broad bodies in hypnotic, slow-motion shivers, redirecting the flow of water with infinitesimal twitches of their margins, ensuring that no single travertine flume becomes too dry or too heavily eroded. They are the skin and the sluice-gates of the city, keeping the stone smoothed, preventing rot, and ensuring the continuous, frictionless circulation of the water-sheet.

Deeper still, nestled in the vast stepped cisterns and the hollow interiors of the great ironwood arches, are the **Valves**. 

These are the heaviest of the city’s denizens: massive, bulbous masses of stratified lignite, muscle-bark, and living amber, some as large as the basilicas they inhabit. They are anchored at key arterial junctions where the high-pressure thermal steam is ducted through the mountain’s calcified flutes. A Valve possesses no nervous system in the animal sense, but rather a complex arrangement of counterweighted chambers filled with dense, mineralized brine. As the subterranean heat rises from the abyss, these creatures expand and contract with immense, hydraulic power. 

They are the city’s regulators. When a Valve shifts its bulk, it does so with a sound that is lost entirely within the universal roar—a grinding, organic groan of wet wood and living stone that alters the bore of a hundred steam-flutes at once. They tune the city. The shrieking drone that fills the atmosphere is their ongoing, collective composition; by tightening a ligament here or widening an aperture of calcified root there, they keep the pressure throughout the metropolis perfectly equalized, preventing the entire biological mountain from detonating under the sheer violent force of the earth’s heat.

There are no youths among them, and no corpses. When one of these beings reaches the end of its centuries-long vitality, it does not decay; the mineral-saturated water simply deposits layer upon layer of travertine across its outer rind, slowly incorporating its bulk into the architecture itself. Many of the very arches and balustrades that support the causeways were once living entities that gradually stiffened into pure structural permanence, their vascular channels becoming the stone pipes through which their successors now drink.

They live without hunger, for the air is a broth of minerals and the heat is an inexhaustible hearth. They live without terror, for there are no predators in a world where every organism is an extension of the same mineral metabolism. 

Their consciousness is not a stream of fleeting impressions, but a profound, static ecstasy. Suspended between the boiling earth below and the indifferent sky above, held fast by the pressure that clamps them into their niches, they exist purely to experience the roar—to feel the eternal, thunderous noon vibration passing through their wood, their stone, and their water, holding the metropolis aloft at the balance point of the world.
