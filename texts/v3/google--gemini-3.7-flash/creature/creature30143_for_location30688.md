The life of these spires does not divide cleanly between what belongs to the mountain and what belongs to the machine. Flesh here has taken on the properties of the saturated iron and wet basalt: dense, perpetually weeping, calcified against heat, and tuned to the slow, heavy cadence of the buried engine.

### The Thermal Swimmers
The pale gliders visible from the ledges are known as *Vapour-Vanes*. They are neither true reptiles nor birds, but descendants of deep-canyon amphibia whose skin has thickened into a tough, cartilaginous hide that never dries. Broad-bellied and flat, their bodies act as airfoils spanning several meters, entirely wingless in the avian sense; instead, their ribcages extend outward into wide, ribbed fans spanned by translucent, vascular membranes. 

They do not possess lungs that inhale and exhale in rapid breaths; their chests house cavernous, vascularized steam-gills that process the atomized water directly from the air. Because the rising columns of mechanical exhaust are dense with dissolved minerals, the Vanes are buoyant in the thermal flues, climbing thousands of meters with absolute stillness. They orient their long, ruddered tails toward the mouths of the vents, basking in the scalding updrafts. 

Their eyes are vestigial, covered by milky shields of wet skin; they navigate instead through acoustic pits along their jaws that register the low-frequency thrum of the pistons below. When two Vanes cross paths in the mist, they produce no cry, but tilt their broad bodies to catch the light, flashing the copper-bright vascular networks beneath their skin like dull lantern slides.

### The Gasket-Lickers and Plate-Fauna
On the sheer vertical faces, where the iron boilers meet the black rock, life moves at the speed of oxidation. 

Embedded in the seams are the *Rivet-Clamps*—molluscoid vertebrates the size of shields, whose outer carapaces are formed from layered iron-oxide and biomineralized manganese. They are almost indistinguishable from the corroded bolt-heads that stud the plates. A Rivet-Clamp anchors itself over a fracture or a weeping rivet using a vast, muscular foot lined with microscopic, hooked radulae that bite directly into the rusted scale. 

They feed on the dense chemotrophic mats that coat the metal: thick, velvet crusts of iron-oxidizing bacteria and sulfurous sludge that brew in the constant trickle of boiling condensation. A Rivet-Clamp may remain affixed to a single iron plate for decades, shifting only a few inches every season. When the deep hydraulic pulse shudders through the rock, thousands of these creatures loosen their seal simultaneously, allowing a brief flush of superheated runoff to wash through their shell-grooves before clamping down again with a wet, hollow *clack* that echoes across the abyss.

### The Conduit-Eels of the Interior
Deep inside the flooded cylinders, where the mechanical organs still draw and plunge, exists an ecosystem of absolute darkness and immense hydraulic pressure. 

Inhabiting the ancient intake valves and drowned brass conduits are the *Strokers*. These are blind, elongated beasts—some reaching the length of tall pines—with pale, rubbery bodies insulated by layers of dense, lardaceous fat that can withstand temperatures near the boiling point. They have no limbs, but their sides are ringed with heavy, muscular ridges that allow them to wedge themselves against the fluted interior of the pipework.

The Strokers are rhythm-feeders. They wait in the dark throat of the mechanical passages, their wide, circular mouths lined with fibrous baleen made of flexible bone. When the subterranean stroke occurs, forcing thousands of gallons of scalding, mineral-thick water through the valves, the Strokers open their gullets to the surge, filtering out the drifting detritus of the lower chasms: drowned fern-matter, fungal nodules, and the dead carapaces of the upper ledge-dwellers that have washed down the drains. 

To reproduce, they rely entirely on the engine’s exhaust. Their larvae are microscopic, buoyant spheres that are ejected into the sky within the great geysers of steam, drifting on the clouds until they condense in the high sphagnum bogs and begin their slow, vertical descent through the drainage channels back into the iron guts of the range.

### The Strata-Crawlers of the Lianas
Between the spires, suspended on the ancient, braided cables of lianas and rusted wire-rope, live the *Cradle-Walkers*. 

These are massive, slow-moving quadrupeds with long, hook-curved limbs designed exclusively for under-hanging locomotion. They never set foot on flat earth; their entire existence is spent suspended upside-down beneath the iron struts and the vast root-tangles. Their bellies, turned upward toward the wet sky, are concave and deeply grooved, forming permanent, miniature peat-bogs where mosses, liverworts, and carnivorous sundews take root directly in their coarse, mossy fur.

A single Cradle-Walker is an island unto itself. In the wet depressions of its back, the small, translucent cliff-crabs build their burrows, cleaning the mammal-like beast of parasites and dead skin in exchange for shelter and the constant moisture trapped in its pelt. 

The Cradle-Walkers move with agonizing deliberate care, placing one hooked claw every few minutes, their heavy prehensile tails wrapped twice around the rusted core of an industrial cable. They feed on the split-leaved arums, stripping the heavy, rubbery foliage with blunt, crushing jaws that can splinter fossilized wood and rusted sheet metal alike.

### The Silence-Makers
Beneath all of this, in the hanging curtains of sphagnum that veil the basalt columns, lives the source of the mountain's peculiar acoustic deadness: the *Porous Moths*. 

These are palm-sized insects whose broad, heavy wings are covered not in ordinary powder, but in a thick, water-logged velvet that absorbs sound rather than reflecting it. They cluster by the millions in the dark recesses behind the water-sheets, their wings constantly trembling to pump moisture through their bodies. When they take flight in the twilight of the mist, they move in massive, silent drifts that blanket the cliff faces like flying moss. 

Their dense, sodden swarms swallow the mechanical scream of the iron vents and the roar of the falling water, softening the vast, tectonic violence of the peaks into a wet, universal murmur—a landscape where iron has become bone, steam has become breath, and every living thing is merely an extension of the great, forgotten machine that keeps the high world warm.
