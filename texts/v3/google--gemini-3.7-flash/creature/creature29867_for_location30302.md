In the spinning water-braids that hang suspended between the spires, there is a kind of life that has never touched stone. 

They are called *siphons* or *thread-marrows*—pale, hydrostatic organisms no thicker than a horsehair at their tips, swelling to the width of an arm where their transparent bellies collect the falling mist. They have no skeleton, no blood, and no true organs; their flesh is a dense, glass-clear gelatin that matches the refractive index of the water so perfectly they are invisible except when they cross a beam of refracted light. They drift inside the floating pillars of water, swimming upward against the upward-falling streams and letting themselves be dragged downward by the descending sheets, feeding entirely on the mineral salts and pressurized vapors torn free where space folds against itself. When two water-braids cross and shear, the siphons knot together in brief, shivering bundles, exchanging pale knots of tallow-like seed that float away into the glare like oil droplets.

Beneath them, plastered flat against the vertical sheets of weeping obsidian, live the *troughs*. These are broad, tile-like mollusks whose shells are not rounded, but grown in razor-thin polygonal plates of slate-grey aragonite. They fit together along the rock faces like tessellated paving stones, overlapping to form false ledges that divert the condensation into their own hinged underbellies. They do not move by crawling; they loosen the hydraulic vacuum beneath their shells and let the upward or sideways gravity slides them along the wet glass until they hit a dry fold, where they bite into the black stone with teeth of pure iron pyrite. They live on the acoustic friction of the mountain. Their shells are lined with piezoelectric crystals that turn the continuous, deafening chime of the flexing spires into a steady internal heat, keeping their cold flesh from freezing in the thin, searing air.

And then there are the ones who built the cradles.

They are not many, and they are not young. They are the *Weft-Keepers*, the remnants of a people whose anatomy has been bent and re-hardened by centuries of surviving along the seams where direction fails.

To look at them, they appear more like wet hides stretched over stone than men. Their skin is hairless, dead-chalk white, and perpetually slick with a greasy, waterproof sebum they secrete from glands along their spines—a natural tallow that keeps the cold condensation from rotting their flesh or leaching the warmth from their blood. They are short and barrel-chested, their ribs fused into thick, armor-like plates to withstand the immense acoustic pressure of the chasms. 

They have no use for eyes. The searing white light of the sky would burn out a normal pupil in hours; their eyelids are grown shut from birth, scarred over into thick, pinkish calluses of skin. In place of vision, their sense of the world is entirely tectonic and hydraulic. The bones of their feet and the knuckles of their long, spatulate fingers are honeycombed with fluid-filled chambers that act as internal plumb-lines, registering the slightest shift in gravity or spatial curvature. When a Weft-Keeper steps out onto a suspended slate ledge, they do not look down; they press their jaw against the stone, reading the resonant chime of the spire to map the chasms for miles around, knowing by the pitch of the rock exactly where a spatial seam has pinched closed or where an upward-falling cascade has snapped.

They move through the vertical world on their hands and knees, using tools of heavy, beaten bronze that have been passed down until the metal is worn thin as birch bark. Their fingers end in nails as thick and blunt as chisels, stained black from the tallow-grease they use to dress their suspension lines. 

Their tethers are their greatest craft and their only real possessions. They braid them from the fibrous nerve-strings of the siphons, dried and soaked in mineral brine until they are stronger than steel cables and immune to rot. A Weft-Keeper does not walk from spire to spire; they throw weighted bronze pendulums into the empty glare, aiming for the invisible gravity-wells where the stone walls repeat. When the pendulum catches the strange lateral pull of a fold, it snaps taut horizontally, allowing the keeper to pull themselves hand-over-hand through the mid-air gap, their legs trailing behind them in the howling, water-saturated void.

They do not speak. The sound of the spires is too loud for vocal cords, and the air is too thin to carry the soft nuance of words. When they meet in the dry recesses—crouched in the round stone shelters beneath the overhangs—they communicate by percussion, tapping the flint wedges driven into the rock with quick, rhythmic adze-strikes that carry clearly through the dense columnar calcite. 

They eat what the stone and water provide. They harvest the flat troughs from the cliff faces, prying them free with bronze spuds and cracking their slate shells to reach the sweet, iron-tasting meat within. They wedge shallow basins of shaved slate into the water-braids to catch the fat-laden seed-bundles of the siphons, boiling them down in unworked stone pots over small, flameless heat-stones—pyrite nodules they strike against the black glass until they glow with a long, smoldering internal heat that smells of crushed sulphur and wet lime.

In their shelters, they sleep curled tightly around the basalt pendulums that hang from their ceilings. Even in sleep, their fingers stay hooked around the braided gut-lines. They sleep tilted at impossible angles, their bodies leaning forty-five degrees into the empty room, held in place by the same spatial seams that keep the mountains standing. 

They know that the range is not permanent. They can feel, through the soles of their feet and the ringing in their jaws, that the spires are wearing themselves away—that the continuous roaring wash of condensation is slowly dissolving the calcite, and that one day the seams will snap shut, the gravity will right itself with a sound like a splitting world, and the forest of mercury will fall into the rifts. Until then, they wedge their bronze deeper into the glass, scrape the water from the stone, and sway in their cradles, as unmoving and stubborn as the rock itself.
