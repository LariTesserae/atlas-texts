To live here is not merely to endure the friction of stone and grease; it is to be a component in a mechanism that has no blueprint. The life that coats these newly cracked terraces does not distinguish between metabolic biology and kinematic necessity. Everything here is either a lubricant, a gasket, a counterweight, or a conduit.

At the lowest, most intimate scale are the **scap-feeders and shim-leeches**. Where the great iron cylinders stroke back and forth within their graphite beds, the interfaces are not bare. They are packed with dense, flattened colonies of translucent vermin whose bodies are less than a millimeter thick. These creatures possess no eyes, no respiratory vents, and no distinct limbs; they are essentially living films of pressurized lipids. They feed directly on the shear-heat and the microscopic swarf sheared from the sliding metals. As the iron pushes inward, they flatten to a smear of golden grease, dampening the shock; as the stroke withdraws, they drink the condensed aromatic oils that seep down the rock walls, swelling back into pale, ribbed ribbons that excrete fresh, slick graphite-paste into the seams. Without them, the stone pistons would weld themselves to their sleeves within a day; with them, the rock glides on an ocean of living marrow.

Where the fractures widen into warm, vaporous trenches, the **manifold-crabs** gather in deliberate, tightly engineered geometries. These organisms are shield-shaped, their carapaces formed of thick, laminated layers of native mica and slate-dust bound with vulcanized resin. The edges of their shells are not rounded or spined, but cut with precise, interlocking tongue-and-groove joints. When a subterranean fissure begins to scream with high-pressure, superheated petroleum gas, thousands of these creatures march into the breach. They interlock their shells with wet, pneumatic snaps, shingling over the vent to form a flexing, vaulted conduit. They do not fight the pressure; they channel it. The scalding vapor is forced down the length of their joined bellies, where arrays of internal, silken baleen strip the heavy hydrocarbons from the blast before venting the cooled, depleted steam into the mosses. When the pressure equalizes, the pipe dissolves—the crabs unlock with a chorus of dry clicks and crawl away to digest the captured pitch in the dark.

Higher up, amidst the ratcheting vines that span the gorges, move the **tensioners**. These are heavy, quadrupedal things whose bodies have the dense, unyielding weight of solid rubber. They have no faces—only a vertical, hydraulic split at the front of the head lined with rotating rasps of corundum. Their four limbs do not end in claws or hooves, but in wide, semi-circular clamps of reinforced cartilage that fit the exact diameter of the twisted drive-lianas. The tensioners hang beneath the turning braids, their immense weight keeping the organic belts taut between the crags. They sleep while hanging, their heartbeats slow, rhythmic thumps that match the stroke of the nearest iron plug. As they drift into deep torpor, their skin secretes a thick, amber-colored latex that cures rapidly in the air, wrapping the lianas in a fresh, wear-resistant sheath before the sheer torque of the landscape strips it away again.

Nothing here remains strictly animal for the entirety of its life cycle. The larval forms that twitch in the rainbow-sheened puddles—the stamped-tin grubs—undergo a metamorphosis that is entirely mineralogical. Born as soft, fat-bodied maggots that feed on falling soot, they accumulate heavy metals within their digestive diverticula. By their third month, their soft flesh has been entirely displaced by crystalline hematite and bismuth. They cease moving. Their legs fuse into stabilizing struts; their mouthparts calcify into open, fluted vents; their digestive tracts harden into porous ceramic filters. They become stationary mineral chimneys, drawing the oily rainwater through their bodies by capillary action, refining it, and weeping clear, combustible kerosene from their bases to feed the lower liverworts.

In the mid-air between the basalt spires, where the heat plumes rise from the iron seams, drift the **shuttle-ribbons**. These are the creatures whose skins resemble cured, vulcanized canvas. They have no skeletal structure; their forms are kept rigid by internal chambers filled with warm, pressurized naphtha vapor. They drift on the thermal updrafts with their wide, spade-shaped tail-planes locked, undulating in slow, mechanical figure-eights across the chasms. From their underbellies trail miles-long threads of sticky, carbon-rich silk. As they glide through the dense petroleum fog, these lines collect suspended soot, sulfur crystals, and airborne spore-capsules. When a line becomes heavy and laden with black grease, the creature draws it up through a ventral maw, stripping the thread clean with a sharp, spinning gizzard before paying the line back out into the mist. They are the atmospheric scrubbers of the plateau, flying looms that weave the soot from the sky and turn it into heavy, calorie-dense tallow.

And deep within the blind, echoing corridors where the plateau folds in on itself, there are the **plummets**—the nearest thing this shelf has to megafauna. 

A plummet is a creature of terrifying density. Its skeleton is composed largely of native lead and sintered iron, so heavy that the animal cannot walk upon ordinary soil without sinking; it can only exist on the bare, newly fractured basements of slate and basalt. A full-grown plummet resembles a blunt, four-sided wedge of pitted ironstone, twenty paces long, with thick, pillar-like legs that move with the slow, agonizing precision of heavy jacks. It possesses no sensory organs save for deep-set, fluid-filled channels along its flanks that detect microscopic variations in tectonic stress. 

The plummets do not hunt. They are structural regulators. When a mountain spur begins to shear prematurely under subterranean steam pressure—threatening to shatter a valley before its mineral veins have fully crystallized—the plummets feel the harmonic shift through the floor. They move toward the fault with crushing, deliberate steps, wedge their massive, blunt skulls directly into the fracture plane, and lock their limbs into rigid stanchions. There they remain for weeks, even years, their leaden bodies absorbing millions of foot-pounds of tectonic torque, acting as living shoring-timbers while the stone around them groans, heats, and slowly welds itself solid. 

When a plummet finally dies, it does not rot. Its flesh is so saturated with heavy oils and iron filings that it simply stiffens in place. The skin dries to a tough, vulcanized rind; the mineral marrow crystallizes; and the shifting slate closes around it, incorporating its bones seamlessly into the next layer of the rising, self-machining world.
