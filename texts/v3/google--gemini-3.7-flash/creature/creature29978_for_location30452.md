To understand what lives here, one must first understand that flesh, in the sense of wet red meat and soft weeping membranes, does not exist. Here, life is a matter of sintering, curing, and annealing. It has more in common with pottery, cast bronze, and dry tallow than with blood.

The people who tend the retort-towns—if they can still be called people—are long-limbed, narrow-chested, and spare. They have the slow, measured cadence of creatures that cannot afford the waste of friction. Their skin is not moist; it is a pale, horn-like rind, dry as parchment, dusted always with the talc-fine lime of the chalk plateaus. It does not sunburn; it hardens under the baked glare into a dark, glazed tortoiseshell. Beneath this armor, their muscles are dark, fibrous bundles, lubricated not by water, but by dense, sweet-smelling lipid chains distilled from the vent-vapors. They do not sweat. To lose a drop of internal grease is a structural failure, an injury requiring a plaster of sulfur-gum and shale-flour.

They wear heavy aprons of split shale-fiber and copper-wire mesh, with goggles cut from thin plates of smoky quartz set directly into iron frames. Their speech is quiet, almost entirely consonants and clicking palatals, pitched low to carry along the resonance of the iron deckplates. They rarely raise their voices; high pitch dies instantly in the thin, hot air. When they must speak across the gulfs, they use the cables themselves, striking the high-tension wire ropes with lead-headed mallets, sending code shivering miles through the empty air to the next anchor-station.

They do not sleep in beds. Instead, they hang. In the high, dry rafters of the lift-towers, out of reach of the floor-heat, they hook themselves into counterweighted webbing by the iron rings fused through their shoulder-blades. There they swing through the short, thin nights, rocking in time with the three-minute cycle of the master flywheels, their breath shallow, cool, and smelling of parched wax.

Around them, within the warm iron joints of the settlements, lives an entire verminry of the dry engine:

Inside the three-man pipes, where the heavy, paraffin-rich volcanic gas flows under pressure, live the *gland-leeches*. They are not soft, but stiff-bodied, articulated like copper concertinas. They clamp themselves over the pipe rivets with suction-plates of dense horn, grazing on the heavy condensates that form along the seams. The pipe-fitters do not kill them; they encourage them. A colony of leeches will seal a micro-fracture in a gas conduit with their own hardened secretions long before a human tender could spot the shimmering distortion of a leak in the heat-waves.

The giant flywheels—those house-sized discs of cold poured iron—have their own attendants: the *oil-beetles*. Flat, wide as a man’s chest, and completely blind, their carapaces are smooth, oiled hematite. They feed on the graphite dust worn off the bearings. In return, their bellies secrete a continuous film of dense, clear mineral fat that keeps the hundred-ton hubs turning without a screech. The relationship is ancient; no one oils the main shafts by hand. To do so would be to insult the beetles, who groom the brass journals with their brush-like forelimbs day and night, turning slowly with the wheel like clockwork ornaments.

Out beyond the bolted railings, where the iron gives way to sheer, five-mile drops of baked chalk, the vertical herds move.

The porcelain lithovores are called *the Steppers*. They are the size of draft horses, but flattened, built like multi-jointed crabs of dense, semi-crystalline bone that rings like china when struck. Their limbs end in pneumatic bulbs of thick, fibrous calloused leather that create a powerful dry vacuum against the polished stone faces. They do not eat plants—they eat the cliff itself. Their mouths are tripartite grinding cones of industrial diamond and hardened horn that chew methodically through the sulfur crusts and the calcite rind. 

As a Stepper grazes, it leaves behind a wide, polished track on the rock, smooth as a glass road. The townspeople use these ancient, intersecting tracks as pathways, anchoring their foot-lines to the deep pits the Steppers bore into the stone to anchor themselves while resting. A Stepper rarely falls; its center of mass is pulled flush against the cliff face, and its four lung-sacks pump with a slow, rhythmic *wheeze-clack* that echoes across the canyons like a distant bellows.

Higher still, in the blinding white wash of the upper thermals, drift the *Sails*. 

They are the true masters of the gulfs—creatures sixty paces from wing-tip to wing-tip, yet weighing no more than an empty cable bucket. Their bodies are light, ribbed cages of hollow silicate, transparent as amber, through which the slow, smoldering glow of their chemiluminescent digestion can be seen at dusk. They have no eyes; their heads are broad, shovel-nosed sensory shields of vibrating bone that read the micro-currents of rising heat and the distant scent of vent-plumes.

The Sails never land. Their wings are too broad, their bone-struts too brittle to support their weight on horizontal stone. Instead, they spend decades in the continuous, rising chimney of heat between the massifs, circling in immense, languid spirals that take full days to complete a single turn. When they mate, they grapple in mid-air with long, braided tendrils of dry tendon, falling together through three miles of empty sky before catching the updraft at the canyon floor and separating with a sound like tearing silk.

Occasionally, an old Sail will misjudge the thermal balance of a canyon mouth. As its lift decays, it drifts slowly toward the iron gantries of the towns, drawn by the warm exhaust of the smelters. The settlement dwellers do not hunt them with guns; they wait with long, hooked poles of yellow brass. When the beast brushes the lower cables, they snare its trailing anchor-tendrils and draw it down into the dry-docks. 

Nothing is wasted. The amber wing-membranes are cut into panes for the high observatory windows, letting in the fierce light while filtering the scorching heat. The hollow bones are cleaned and bored to serve as light gas-pipes. The dense, lipid-rich heart is rendered into the finest tallow, used to pack the seals of the deep pistons that pump the life-breath of the vents into the heart of the stone.

And when a worker of the cliffs dies, their end is without ceremony. They do not dig graves in the hard iron-stone; there is no soil to receive them. The corpse is stripped of its brass fittings and quartz lenses, then carried to the outermost spur of the extraction platform. There, where the hot wind draws upward with a steady, dry roar, the body is placed on an iron grid over the mouth of a sulfur flue. 

Within hours, the heat and the dry, sweet-smelling vapors have baked the flesh to stone, calcifying the marrow and turning the skin to a hard, ringing ceramic. The body is then hauled back inside, not as an ancestor to be mourned, but as a building block: a dry, rotless, perfectly stable lintel, set with mortar made of crushed shale into the ever-growing foundations of the cliff-city, holding up the weight of the engines against the sun.
