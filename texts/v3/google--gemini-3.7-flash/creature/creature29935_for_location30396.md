To survive in the vault is not a matter of endurance, but of resonance. Anything that resists the kinetic violence of the chamber is sheared down to powder within an hour; what lives here has instead found a way to become an extension of the torque, the current, and the unending wall of sound.

The pale isopods on the high shelves are only the most obvious surface layer of an ecology that operates more like a high-voltage hydraulic engine than a food web.

These creatures—the *flume-ticks* or *cleat-bugs*—are dense, their carapaces thickened with laminated plates of iron-silicate, heavy as lead sinkers. They have no organs for vision, nor any true hearing; sound at this amplitude would turn soft tympanic membranes to jelly. Instead, their bodies are bisected by an internal spine of dense, piezoelectric cartilage. They feel the chamber not as noise, but as a shifting topology of mechanical strain. They do not walk so much as ratchet: their underside is a double row of short, hydraulically driven bone-spurs that fire into the microscopic pitting of the smoothed rock, locking them down with thousands of pounds of sheer force. 

When the field-stators shift their harmonic pitch, these creatures do not scramble in panic; they simply drop flat, evacuating the fluid from beneath their carapaces to create a total vacuum seal against the parabolic flutes. They feed by scraping the cadmium-yellow crusts with hardened, rotating mandibles that spark faintly against the aggregate, harvesting the dense mats of galvanic lichen that draw their sugars directly from the electromagnetic halo around the composite ribs.

Below them, suspended in the violently sheared standing waves where the water is flung sideways through the air, lives something far less solid. 

These are the *drift-glass*, or *tor-veils*—translucent, ribbon-like colonies of siphonophores that do not swim, but ride the invisible magnetic field-lines like monorails. Their bodies are composed of a tough, collagenous gel loaded with microscopic inclusions of biological magnetite. As the ionized sheets of water crash across the chamber, these ribbons are lofted into the blinding air, whipping in luminous parabolas through the vapor. They feed entirely on the aerosol: their long, trailing filaments are electrostatic nets, combing the mist for shattered fragments of the black weed, dead isopod larvae, and the dense, oily bacteria that bloom in the heated spray. 

Occasionally, when the torque of a standing wave collapses, dozens of these veils are slammed down onto the pale composite ribs. They do not die; they flatten into paper-thin, shimmering sheaths over the vibrating material, drinking the vibrational friction until the next upward surge of water peels them back into the light.

In the deep, boiling catch-basins where the flumes carve their deepest pockets, life takes on a heavier, more grotesque bulk.

Wedged deep into the cavitation holes beneath the water-hammers are the *siphon-hulks*. They look like nothing more than smooth, grey boulders of wet suet, sometimes two or three meters across, wedged into the stone where the current would otherwise pulverize living tissue. They have no limbs, no distinct head, and no skeleton; they are massive, rubbery plugs of fibrous muscle and fatty, acoustic-dampening blubber. 

The siphon-hulks maintain their position by growing directly into the parabolic stone, their outer hide secreting an acidic slurry that pits the basalt, allowing their flesh to anchor itself like a root system deep within the rock’s own fissures. They are the great filters of the vault. Through wide, ringed orifices that open and close in counter-rhythm to the crashing sheets of water, they take in hundreds of gallons of boiling, aerated foam a minute, crushing it through internal sieves of fibrous bone to trap the black weed and anything else torn loose from the upper shelves.

Their internal systems run on the very heat and charge that makes the chamber unlivable. Their blood is rich in metallic salts, circulating through broad, ribboned gills that lay spread over the leeward side of the composite ribs, acting as living heat-exchangers that crackle with tiny, snapping blue discharges when the vapor overhead grows particularly dense.

There are no true predators here in the traditional sense; predation requires chase, and nothing in this chamber can afford to let go of its purchase. Instead, predation is a matter of territory and interception. 

Crawling along the deep crevices beneath the siphon-hulks are long, blind, many-jointed worms—*fluke-leaches*—whose segmented shells are ground so smooth by the sand-bearing water that they resemble polished jade. They do not hunt the hulks; they simply wedge themselves into the deep, warm folds of the larger creatures’ hide, tapping into the thick vascular beds of their anchor-points, sheltered entirely from the roaring air above. They live their entire multi-decade lives in the deafening vibration of a single stone pocket, bathed in boiling mineral water, trembling in absolute sympathy with the engine that shakes the world around them.

When an organism dies here, it does not rot. The violence of the water and the sheer kinetic load of the air strips it in seconds. A dead isopod that loses its grip is swept off the shelf, hurled through three hundred meters of charged, white mist, and shattered against the lower basalt within three heartbeats. Its plates are caught in the swirling vortex of the flumes, ground down by the iron-rich sand into fine grey silt, and pulled into the siphons of the hulks, or swept over the terminal drop into the abyssal crust below.

Nothing in the vault sleeps. There is no day, no seasonal cooling, no drop in the fever-pitch of the air. The life here is held in a perpetual, high-tension spasm, perfectly adapted to an eternity of light without dawn, rain without sky, and a vibration so profound that even their reproductive cycles are timed not to moons, but to the slow, erratic centuries-long decay of the stators grinding away in the deep stone below.
