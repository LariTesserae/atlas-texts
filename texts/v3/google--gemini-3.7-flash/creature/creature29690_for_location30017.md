In the dark spaces beneath the collapsed floor plates, where no direct glare ever penetrates, the guano drifts have accumulated into rolling, dune-like terraces. These drifts are not inert soil; they are an autonomous biome, dry on top and fermenting below, radiating a steady, feverish biological heat that rivals the warmth of the steel outside.

Within these banks live vast, blind societies of detritivores. Trillions of trogloxenic mealworms, flightless dermestid beetles, and pale bristletails churn the manure into an ultra-fine, talcum-like dust. The beetles have lost their pigmentation over uncounted generations, their shells an opalescent, pearlescent ivory. They move in silent, shifting drifts across the mounds, cleaning every fallen swallow fledgling and dying bat to polished, ivory bone within hours of impact. Above them, suspended from the underside of the floor grating, hang the silken nursery bags of cavern spiders whose leg spans stretch to the size of tea saucers. These arachnids do not spin webs for flight; they sit flat against the rust, their legs splayed to register the vibration of a single beetle clambering across a structural rivet.

In the submerged levels—the flooded boiler pits, pump rooms, and the maze of thirty-foot-wide culverts that once fed the cooling towers—the water is stratified. The top six inches are warm, yellowed, and skimmed with a persistent iridescent film of paraffin and tallow. Below this lies a cold, oxygen-poor, iron-dense brine. 

Here dwell the great, blunt-headed bottom fish: a strain of armored bullhead catfish that have adapted so thoroughly to the mineral wash that their skin has thickened into a rubbery, pebble-grained hide, pale underneath and marbled with slate-gray on their backs. Their barbels are long and fleshy, trailing across the silt-drowned rail tracks like reins. Because the water is an opaque tea of suspended limedust and iron hydroxide, their eyes are milky and degenerate—mere pinpricks beneath protective keratinous shields. They navigate exclusively by electro-reception and chemical taste, nosing into the submerged manifolds and rusted valve chests to suck down the slurry of organic detritus that sifts continuously from above.

Alongside them, sharing the submerged ironwork, are river prawns of unusual size, their translucent carapaces tinted a faint, unearthly turquoise from trace copper salts dissolved in the basin. They cling to the vertical rungs of drowned ladders and the blades of submerged intake impellers, using their extended, wire-thin pincers to comb the water for suspended flakes of bat guano and skin flakes shed by the reptiles.

The water monitors are the heavy apex of this drowned floor. They are extraordinarily fat, their jowls and the bases of their tails bulging with stored lipids, giving their gait a slow, heavy-swaying waddle when they haul themselves along the narrow concrete coping of the settling vats. Their hides are scarred, not from territorial brawling, but from the scraping edges of corroded iron plating through which they push to reach dry, heated nesting cavities in the hollow machine foundations. When they slip into the warm water, they do not splash; they sink like logs of wet teak, their sculling tails barely leaving a wake. They have developed a mutualism with the crabs: a basking monitor will lie utterly flaccid on an iron plate while dozens of tiny shore crabs scuttle over its scutes and claws, picking off ticks, leeches, and bits of decaying catfish flesh wedged between the lizard’s recurved teeth.

Midway up, in the structural skeleton of the mills—the zone of overhead crane rails, junction boxes, and sagging ventilation ducting—lives a community of ambushers that never touch either the water or the sky. 

Heavy, blunt-tailed mud snakes, their scales the exact color of dry iron rust, thread themselves through the electrical conduit pipes. They spend the hottest hours coiled within the hollow steel box-girders, where the ambient temperature remains constant. They emerge into the dim twilight of the upper trusses only to slide quietly into the mud-nest clusters of the swallows, swallowing eggs and nestlings whole before retracting into the ironwork like dark, muscular pistons.

The herons that line the catwalks are strange, stunted birds compared to their riverine cousins. Generations of living on high, narrow railings have shortened their legs and thickened their tarsal claws, turning their perching grip into something vice-like. Their plumage is perpetually soiled by the orange-red powder of oxidized steel and the white wash of birdlime, giving them the appearance of statues roughly chiseled from limestone and hematite. They do not migrate. The basin provides a perpetual summer, an unvarying bath of damp heat and limitless food. They breed year-round, weaving bulky platforms of rusted tie-wire, cable offcuts, and the dried ribs of catfish on top of the dead transformer banks.

And above everything, the swallows and bats share the vaulted ceilings in a vast, temporal partition. 

The cliff swallows own the daylight haze. Their mud nests are architectural marvels, built not from river mud alone, but from a masonry mix of crushed slag, iron dust, and bird saliva that dries as hard as Portland cement. Over decades, these nests have fused into continuous, multi-tiered honeycombs that encase whole thirty-meter spans of structural I-beams, adding tons of dead weight to the roof trusses. The birds themselves are small, compact, their wings clipped and agile, flying with extreme precision through the dense forest of vertical tie-rods and hanging cables.

As the flat glare fades into the heavy, purple-gray dusk, a seamless transition occurs. The swallows tuck their heads into their cement flasks, and the bats detach.

They are largely free-tailed bats, millions of them, whose colonies have completely choked the dry exhaust stacks and the high, unventilated roofs of the converter buildings. When they drop from the iron, they do not leave the shelter of the mills immediately. They swirl in a massive, counter-clockwise vortex within the high bays, a screaming column of warm air and wingbeats, rising through the collapsed roof sections to disperse over the marshes and the silt delta. 

Their bodies are muscular, smelling intensely of ammonia, musk, and wet slate. When they return before dawn, engorged on midges and moths, the heat they bring with them is palpable—a wave of biological warmth that rises into the roof rafters, maintaining the endless, humid cycle of the town’s interior weather, feeding the damp, sustaining the mold, and keeping the iron sweating in the dark.
