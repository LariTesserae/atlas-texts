They are smaller than a human thumb, pale as blanched parsnips, and they do not have names for colors, only for pressures and temperatures. 

To call them mammals is to lean on a taxonomy that loses its usefulness in this seam; they are something older and more compressed, a lineage of minute, hot-blooded chordates whose ancestors followed the hemlock roots down into the permafrost when the first glaciers ground across the bench. Their bodies are long, supple, and flattened laterally, like willow leaves, allowing them to pass through fissures no wider than a spruce needle. Their skin is entirely devoid of pigment—a translucent, tough vellum through which the frantic, scarlet machinery of their circulation is visible. Over this hide, they secrete a thin, musky oil that smells faintly of crushed elderberry; it repels the bitter hemlock sap and prevents the freezing mist from anchoring frost to their joints.

They have no eyes. Where eyes might have been in another epoch, their skulls have flattened into broad, spade-like wedges covered in concentric rings of micro-vibrissae—hairs so fine they do not register air, but rather the *shape* of the air, the subtle compression of drafts bouncing off a nearby root-cleat or the approach of a drop of condensate. 

Their hands are their primary mind. Each limb ends in four elongated digits tipped not with soft pads, but with needles of dense, black keratin that they file constantly against the grit of the peat. These claws are their chisels, their weaving shuttles, and their compasses. They do not build by sight, but by tension. When a builder lashes a suspension bridge of split rush to a living hemlock root, it tests the tautness by plucking the spider-silk sinew with its inner claw, listening through the resonance in its jawbone. If the pitch is too low, the bridge will sag under the weight of the harvestmen; if too high, the surging root will snap it when the tundra wind wrenches the forest above.

Their relationship with the animal tide around them is neither mastery nor submission, but a fierce, utilitarian entanglement. 

The shivering heaps of pygmy shrews are their hearths. A single builder, stripped of movement, would freeze solid in less than forty heartbeats in this draft. And so, the city is designed around the shrews' frantic metabolism. The builders build wicker cages—flexible, barrel-vaulted cribs of split rush—directly over the shrews' communal nests. They do not trap the shrews; they bribe them. Day and night, teams of foragers bring the shrews chopped grubs, amphipod paste, and the fatty larvae harvested from the cambium grain. In exchange, the builders sleep packed between the vibrating bodies of the shrews, burrowing directly into the scalding, hyperactive furnace of their fur, absorbing the radiant 104-degree heat before rolling out into the freezing mist to work another shift.

Beneath the streets, the millions of blind harvestmen are treated like living thatch. The builders train them with chemical trails laid down by gland-tipped sticks, coaxing the long-legged tangles to hang beneath the bridges and along the windward walls of the tenements. The massed, twitching legs break the bitter drafts, functioning as a moving, self-repairing insulation that keeps the interior alleys several degrees above the air outside. If a wall is torn by a shifting root, the builders do not immediately weave wood; they gather handfuls of harvestmen and press them into the breach, where the arachnids instinctively interlock their limbs, sealing the draft with a living, shivering gasket.

Life inside the tenements is silent and strictly choreographic. Because sound travels dangerously through the solid wood—alerting subterranean centipedes and parasitic wasps—the inhabitants speak almost entirely through a language of percussive vibrations. They tap their needle-claws against the taut cedar-bark floors: a dry, rapid morse that tells of a swelling flume upstream, a fissure widening in the northern taproot, or a pocket of fat-rich nematode eggs exposed in the lower gutters. 

When the hydraulic pulse arrives—the slow, crushing heave of freezing swamp-water forced through the peat—their society undergoes an immediate, coordinated shift. As the water begins its glass-smooth descent down the vertical alleys, the builders emerge from their bark huts in tight, disciplined lines. They wear harness-loops of knotted gut hooked into the structural thorn-pegs. Leaning out over the rushing flume at forty-five-degree angles, their spade-shaped heads pointed upstream to read the current's drag, they lower their fine thistle-down strainers into the torrent. 

Their movements during the flood are swift, rhythmic, and ecstatic. They pluck the strainers from the water every few seconds, stripping the caught drift—spores, microscopic water-mites, pale worm-flesh—with their second set of grooming claws and packing it into pouches sewn from hemlock-skin. They work until their limbs grow stiff and gray from the near-ice water, at which point a relief worker taps them on the flank, unhooks their harness, and shoves them back toward the nearest shrew-crib to thaw.

They do not bury their dead in the peat, for the earth here is constantly moving and would crush or eject them. Instead, when a builder dies, its body is stripped of its waxy musk and left to dry in the cold vertical drafts until the skin becomes as stiff and taut as parchment. The living then use these dried, lightweight husks as reinforcing struts within the architecture—their curved ribs lashed to the corners of the rush bridges, their spade-skulls pegged into the cambium to serve as anchors for the spider-silk lines. 

To walk the narrow alleys of this city—were one small enough to stand upon a rush-plank—would be to move through a place of relentless, mathematical industry that smells of bitter resin, hot rodent breath, and wet iron. It is an existence without sky, without warmth that is not stolen from another heartbeat, held together entirely by the tension of silk threads and the unyielding, stubborn refusal of tiny hands to let the cold wood close.
