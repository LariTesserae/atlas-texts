In the immense, lightless press of the high water, life does not cling to survival; it is built into the engineering of the place, moving through the timber-stone in long, unbroken cycles of maintenance, digestion, and acoustic navigation.

### The Grazers of the Grain
On the burnished surfaces of the buttresses, where the iron-wood meets the water in smooth, unyielding curves, live the **Ivory Ticks** and **Spindle-Crabs**. They are chalk-white, calcified, and entirely sightless, possessing no eyes or eye-sockets—only broad, fan-like antennules that quiver against the wood to read the grain’s tension. 

Millions of them carpet the lower spans. Their limbs end in hardened, obsidian-tipped picks that do not gouge the wood, but shave it. They graze upon the micro-coatings of iron-fixing slime and the slow, blooming hyphae of the botanical masonry. As they feed, their mandibles strike the dense timber with rhythmic, metallic *snaps*. Multiplied by billions across miles of open cathedral-ribs, this feeding creates a dry, persistent static—a high-frequency rain that coats the deeper bass of the hydraulic flutes. They are the balance: wherever the living wood tries to thicken too quickly, the ticks congregate in dense, clicking drifts to shear the excess away, keeping the hydrodynamic forms perfectly aerodynamic.

### The Pipe-Dwellers (The Reeds)
Inside the hollow flute-canals—where the cold, heavy current is forced through bored conduits at terrifying speeds—dwell the **Vane-Worms** and **Bore-Sturgeon**. 

The Vane-Worms anchor their root-like tails directly into the inner arterial walls of the timber. Their bodies are long, ribboned sheaths of flexible cartilage, extending into the rushing interior flow. They do not feed on prey, but filter the high-velocity water, vibrating like the reeds of a woodwind instrument. The organ-drone of the spires is partly their doing: as the water rushes past their segmented, tensed bodies, they modulate the tone, tightening or slackening their flesh to alter the pitch of the column. A single canal may house thousands of them, stacked in precise, resonant series, turning miles of botanical pipe into a living acoustic siren.

Weaving between these vibrating ribbons are the Bore-Sturgeon: long, needle-thin, jawless fish clad in slick, mucous-sheathed armor. They swim perpetually against the crushing interior current, feeding on the detritus caught in the Vane-Worms' bristles. Their bodies are so dense they lack swim-bladders entirely; if swept out of the flute-canals into the open abyss, they sink like dropped iron bolts into the unseen roots below.

### The Pelagic Drifters (The Shrouds)
In the three-mile abyssal voids between the colonnades, where there is no wood to hold, drift the **Sieve-Hulks**—the pale curtains of the open water. 

These creatures are vast, measuring hundreds of feet from their dorsal ridges to the tips of their trailing veils. They are nearly gelatinous, their flesh a milk-white, semi-translucent parchment that glows with a faint, non-luminous pearlescence in the pitch black. They possess no active propulsion; instead, they are shaped like enormous, concave hydrofoils. By subtly tilting the fibrous ribs that support their gossamer mantles, they tack against the cold, high-altitude currents, drifting in agonizingly slow, deliberate arcs between the spires.

The Sieve-Hulks navigate entirely by the chord. Because every timber spire is bored with unique flute-canals, each colossal structure sings in a distinct pitch and harmonic interval. To a Sieve-Hulk, the dark is not empty; it is a mapped acoustic landscape. A low C-sharp indicates the base of an aqueduct four leagues to the west; a whistling minor fifth marks the narrow archway of a soaring buttress. They drift from chord to chord, opening their vast, baleen-lined maws to swallow the drifting marine snow—the shed bark-dust and spent crustacean shells that fall continuously from the high vaults.

### The Stealth-Hunters
Following the Sieve-Hulks through the open gaps are the **Velvet Eels**. Growing up to forty feet in length, their bodies are completely black and covered in a dense, moss-like skin that absorbs sound rather than reflecting it. 

Where other creatures in the rift are loud—clicking, groaning, or humming—the Velvet Eel is an acoustic void. It hunts the ribbon-eels and small pelagic fauna by hovering in the "dead zones" of the spires' acoustic fields, where the turbulence cancels out the organ drone. When prey passes through these pockets of silence, disoriented by the sudden loss of their acoustic bearings, the Velvet Eel strikes with an uncoiling, hydraulic snap of its muscular spine, engulfing its target in a wide, circular jaw lined with inward-curving needles of brown cartilage.

### The Graft-Tenders
Deep within the intersections—where four-way bridges meet soaring spires in massive, vaulted nodes—there are forms of life that do not merely inhabit the architecture, but maintain its deliberate geometry. 

These are the **Suture-Folk** (though they bear no resemblance to anything terrestrial). They are radially symmetrical, star-bodied beings of dense, tallow-colored muscle, ten to twelve feet across, with limbs that split into thousands of fine, prehensile cilia. They possess no eyes, but their entire dorsal surface is covered in pressure-sensitive pits capable of detecting a deviation of a single millimeter in the arch of a mile-long bridge.

The Suture-Folk are the gardeners of the equilibrium. They live for centuries clinging to the joints of the iron-wood. From glands beneath their central discs, they secrete a thick, slow-curing resin that hardens underwater into calcified peat-mortar. When a spire experiences structural stress from the shifting high-altitude currents, the Suture-Folk sense the altered vibration in the timber. They crawl slowly toward the strain, weaving new strands of living kelp-tendril across the fault line and plastering it with their resin. 

They do not speak. When they must coordinate to bridge a new span—a process that takes generations of slow, guided growth—they press their central plates against the wood and drum with heavy, muscular pulses. The vibration travels through miles of solid iron-wood, signaling to distant colonies to direct their sap-shoots toward the center of the void, until two growing buttresses meet in the dark, touch, and fuse into a single, aerodynamically perfect arch.

Here, nothing dies without being absorbed into the substrate. The shed carapaces of the grazers become the mineral grit in the Suture-Folks' resin; the spent bodies of the Sieve-Hulks settle onto the lower roots to feed the sap-flow; and the water continues to force its way through the living pipes, playing a world that is simultaneously an ecosystem, an instrument, and an eternal, deliberate monument.
