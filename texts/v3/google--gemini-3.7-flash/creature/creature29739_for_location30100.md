In a place where the mechanical stroke is counted in minutes rather than seconds, the life that takes root must either slow itself to the cadence of the cambium or exist entirely in the margins between the piston-falls.

The most visible inhabitants are the **Linemen**—though to call them men is to rely on an old, increasingly imprecise taxonomy. They are a sparse, long-limbed folk whose lineage has spent generations within the mist-basin, taking on the slate-grey pallor and dry, unhurried density of the ironwood itself. Their skin is cool to the touch, hairless, and rubbed routinely with clarified tallow and cold-pressed pine wax to shed the perpetual micro-condensation of the ventilators. 

They do not hurry; a Lineman’s walking gait is an even, swaying stride that lands heel-first on the dry basalt crowns, perfectly timed so their weight shifts precisely as the three-ton counterweights thud into their sphagnum pits. To run in the yard is considered a dangerous physiological error—it desynchronizes the body from the ground-thrum, inducing a nauseating vertigo they call *bearing-sickness*.

These people live not on the yard floor, but in the structural seams: the **hollowed junction-nodes** where cast-iron manifolds meet living trunk. A dwelling here is an elliptical chamber thirty feet above the flagstones, entered via narrow climbing-staves of spliced ash pegged directly into the bark. Inside, their quarters are remarkably dry, lined with dry felt, cured leather, and compressed cedar shavings that smell of hot resin and wool. They have no open fires—flame is forbidden near the sap-pressures and dried oil coatings—so they cook and heat their water in double-walled brass jackets bolted over the central distribution shafts, drawing warmth directly from the friction of the hickory connecting rods.

Their food is drawn from the vascular system itself. At regulated tap-points, the Linemen draw a heavy, translucent sap which they ferment in slate vats into a pale, bitter curd called *marrow-whey*, rich in mineral salts and cold-hardy starches. They supplement this with shelf-bracket mushrooms cultivated on the shaded undersides of the lower purlins and the soft, white-fleshed grubs of the **oil-beetles** that pasture on the damp fittings.

These beetles—the *carabids* of the copper lines—are not wild pests, but an essential, semi-cultivated fauna. Large as tea-saucers, their black wing-cases have a dull, blued sheen like case-hardened steel. They are blind, guided entirely by delicate, branched antennae that taste the alkaline balance of the machine grease. They creep along the joints, their mandibles adapted not for crushing, but for scraping away the thin, corrosive micro-lichens and dead plant-scale that would otherwise bind the brass sleeve-bearings. Behind them, they leave a thin trail of an oily, paraffin-like excretion that polishes the copper to a dull, salmon-pink glare, sealing the metal against the cold air.

Higher up, where the pale sky meets the uppermost canopy of the trained ironwoods, lives an entirely different avian ecology: the **Chime-swallows**. 

These are small, wedge-winged birds with feathers the color of lead slag. They do not build nests of mud or twigs; instead, they gather the stray copper wire-twists, bronze filings, and fibrous shreds of hickory sloughed off by the swinging rods, weaving them with regurgitated sap into stiff, metallic baskets tucked deep within the accumulator cages. They do not possess a true song. Instead, their throats produce a series of clear, percussive metallic clicks—a staccato imitation of the brass valves tripping in the trunk-hollows. When a pressure change ripples through the high foliage before a rain, several thousand Chime-swallows will lift off in silence, circle the ventilator drafts once in a tight, slate-colored gyre, and descend instantly into the warm flue-gaps of the engine housings.

Beneath the basalt floor, in the dark labyrinths where the ironwood taproots wrap around the cold basalt anchors, there is a third, silent presence: the **Graft-weavers**. 

Whether they are an older offshoot of the Linemen or something that has drifted further into the botanical substrate is an idle question in the basin. They are rarely seen above the flagstone level, emerging only during the longest cold stretches of mid-winter, when the sap-rise falls to a near-dormant trickle. The Weavers are stocky, blind or near-blind, their fingers tipped with blunt, spade-like nails reinforced by thick keratin caps. They move through the root-trenches with hand-carved bone scrapers and balls of tarred twine, guiding young rootlets through iron alignment collars, easing new hydraulic leads through the underground basalt channels, and applying fresh poultices of peat and lard to deep friction-burns.

They speak, if they speak at all, through the rods. A Weaver kneeling on an underground conduit will strike it with a copper wedge: three short taps, a pause of four heartbeats, and a long dragging scrape. Two hundred yards away, an accumulator Lineman sitting high in an ironwood crotch will feel the vibration through the soles of his lead-weighted boots, nod to the grey, shadowless sky, and adjust a brass counter-bleed valve by a single thread.

Generations do not pass here with the drama of birth and burial. When an inhabitant dies, they are not laid in earth to rot, for earth is rare in the terrace. Instead, they are wrapped in porous linen, soaked in raw turpentine, and laid into the damping beds directly beneath the three-ton counterweights. Over decades, the rhythmic, muffled *thud* of the iron engine presses them down into the dense sphagnum peat, integrating their calcium and carbon into the hydraulic floor, until the stone, the wood, the machine, and the tenant are precisely the same substance.
