To live here, an ear is an injury. 

The first thing the void does to whatever enters is dismantle the apparatus of hearing. The tympanic membranes of the thrushes are dense, calcified discs, thickened against the concussion; the pale crickets have no tympanal slits on their forelegs at all, their nervous systems wired instead to the hairline spines along their shins, which register only the distinct impact of stone-fall or the heavier tremors of the floor.

Beneath the green carpets, the peat itself is an animal. In the deep, anaerobic rot where the mosses decay against the limestone, there is a low, constant fermentation that produces a faint, greasy warmth—a fraction of a degree higher than the air. In this warm seam lives a sprawling community of blind, translucent things: flattened land-leeches that feed on nothing but the slime of primitive worms; fat, slow-moving grubs the length of a thumb, whose skin is so clear their black, earth-filled guts can be seen churning as they turn centuries-old fern fronds into grease.

Higher up, where the notched pine poles jut into the black air, the life is larger, sparser, and profoundly slow.

There is someone who uses the poles. 

It is not a specter or a wild man of the woods, but something much more physically specific: an inhabitant whose lineage—whether of one man prolonged by the cold or three generations compressed into this throat of stone—has been molded entirely by the mechanics of the flue.

He sits for days on the high pine perches, wedged into the angle where timber meets stone, wrapped in a mantle made of sheep-fleece cured with rancid lard until it is as stiff and waterproof as boiled leather. The skin of his face and hands is not pale, but a bruised, water-logged grey, calloused at the knuckles and palms into thick, yellow plates of horn that do not soften in the mist. 

His jaw is unusually heavy, set in a permanent forward jut. This is an anatomical habit: to know anything of the world beyond the ledge, he must lean forward and press the enamel of his lower incisors directly into the wet limestone. Through the teeth and the skull bone, the roaring torrent below translates into a language of distinct frequencies. A thrum like a plucked wire means the dale above has frozen solid; a heavy, gut-churning grind tells of floodwater five miles upstream, carrying gravel through the lower siphons. 

He does not climb quickly. To move fast in this air is to invite condensation into the lungs, which never leaves. He moves with the heavy, deliberate leverage of a draught horse, testing each pine notch with the ball of a bare foot before shifting his mass. The soles of his feet are broad and flat, the skin so deeply ridged with cold-cracks and lime-crust that they grip the wet timber like iron rasp-files.

He is here for the harvest of the draft. 

High up, where the flue narrows before reaching the sealed boulders, the atmospheric pressure squeezes the violent air through natural stone grates. The draft carries down everything that dies or loses its footing in the dale above: wind-torn winter moths, the seed-heads of high alpine weeds, the frozen carcasses of pipits and field-voles swept into the limestone crack by blizzards. 

Along the top run of the scaffolding, he has set rows of low, flat baskets woven from split birch and horsehair, wedged into the stone flutes. Every three or four days, he works his way along the poles with a scraper made of split ox-bone, collecting the drift: a cold, fatty meal of dead insects, sodden fur, and frozen seeds that has gathered in the mesh.

When he sleeps, it is inside the low, four-slab shelter on the terrace. He does not use fire—there is neither the draft to clear the smoke nor wood dry enough to catch—but sleeps burrowed directly into the rotted heather and the deep, fermenting peat. The decay keeps him from freezing, steaming faintly around his shoulders in the blackness like a mound of manure in winter.

He is entirely mute. His vocal cords, unused against the roar for decades, have shrunk to dry gristle. If he opens his mouth, there is only the thick, white cloud of his breath mixing with the spray, and the wet click of his teeth against the stone as he listens to the bottom of the world.
