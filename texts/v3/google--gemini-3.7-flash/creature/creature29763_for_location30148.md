The creatures that fit into those sleeping-niches are neither mammal nor insect, though they borrow the architecture of both: they are the *Lithodact*, beings of dry horn, sintered bone, and dense, slate-like plating. 

To see one emerge from its hollow in the shale is like watching a tessellated box unfold itself. A full-grown adult is scarcely longer than a human arm, but heavy, its mass concentrated in a dense, barrel-shaped torso segmented into seven overlapping crests of burnished, black-grey keratin. Their bellies do not have soft undercarriages; instead, they are sealed by interlocking plates of pale, unhydrated cartilage that slide against one another with the crisp, dry rustle of shuffling playing cards.

They do not possess blood as the upper world understands it. Their internal capillaries carry a clear, heavy, sulfur-stable hydrocarbon—a biological naphtha distilled from the crushed oils of the mica-ferns. Because this fluid neither freezes nor evaporates at the chamber’s static heat, they have no pulse to speak of; their circulation is driven entirely by the rhythmic expansion and contraction of their dorsal plates as they move, a mechanical pump powered by locomotion. 

When a Lithodact is still, it is purely, utterly still. It does not pant; it does not blink, for its eyes are twin hemispherical lenses of polished, amber-tinted quartz set seamlessly into the skull. Behind these clear stones lies an array of photo-sensitive, crystalline nerve fibers that do not track motion by blur, but by registering subtle shifts in the chamber’s refractive amber light.

Their limbs are quadrupedal, ending in three-pronged, clawed digits tipped with a substance harder than basalt—grown by slowly concentrating the glass dust they groom from their carapaces into dense, vitreous talons. These claws make no sound on the razor edges of the obsidian floor because the pads behind them are thick, layered cushions of compressed, dry hydrocarbon felt. They walk with an eerie, high-stepping delicacy, testing each fractured sheet of chert before shifting their center of mass. They know the geography of the floor’s cleavage planes with the intimacy of blind scribes reading raised script; to them, a millimeter difference in a flake’s angle is an entire landmark.

In the deafening din of the vibrating filaments, their communication is tactile and resonant rather than vocal. Underneath the wide collar plate at the base of their skulls sits an array of ridged plectrums. By bowing these against the rim of their second thoracic segment, they stridulate. It is a sharp, percussive language—a rapid telegraphy of dry clicks, chitters, and high-tensile trills that cuts cleanly through the harpsichord drone of the glass brush. 

They do not merely endure the acoustic roar of the room; they navigate by it. Their antennae are not feathery tendrils, but long, stiffened tuning forks of mineralized bone that curve backward over their spines. When a draft-eddy shifts or a frond is cropped down, the ambient acoustic pitch changes minutely, and the Lithodact feel the frequency alter along the length of their crests.

Alongside them, grazing through the undergrowth of silicate plumes, are the porcelain beetles—the *Scritchers*. These are the room’s cattle and its gardeners. They are flat, dinner-plate-sized disks of brilliant, milky calcified enamel, completely blind, moving on eight short, gear-like legs that hook into the microscopic fissures of the stone. They do not have mouths in the conventional sense, but a spinning, circular rasp of diamond-hard chert nodules at the center of their underside. They clamp themselves to the stalk of an oil-brush and grind it down to coarse flour, swallowing the dry hydrocarbons and excreting small, hard pellets of pure, dehydrated mineral wax. 

The Lithodact harvest this wax. It is their primary adhesive, their lubricant, and their clay.

In the clearings, the Lithodact gather to work with a patience that could only exist where nothing rots. Because there is no hunger born of metabolic burn—their anhydrous chemistry operates at a sluggish, highly efficient thermal equilibrium—a single Lithodact might spend four human centuries sitting cross-legged before a solitary slab of chert, flaking its edge with a quartz punch to create a set of five identical scraper-blades. 

They do not gather in large numbers; the chamber’s closed ecosystem supports perhaps twelve or fifteen of them at any given time. Their reproduction is an event separated by geological epochs: a slow, mutual accretion where two individuals pool dry lipid reserves into a communal, hollow sphere of woven mica scales, coating it in layers of hot, regurgitated mineral wax until an egg-cairn is formed, solid and indestructible. Within it, an embryo takes fifty years to knit its skeleton out of the ambient nitrogen and lithic dust, motionless and silent until it is strong enough to split its stone cradle with a blow of its dorsal horn.

Death among them is rare, and purely physical. When a Lithodact’s joints finally wear down—when the felt of its pads frays to the bare bone and its oil-ducts calcify into solid sulfur-plugs—it simply walks to the perimeter of the clearing. It chooses an unoccupied hollow, tucks its four limbs beneath its thoracic plates, lowers its quartz eyes against the stone, and stops moving. 

It does not decay. It does not smell. It becomes another piece of the room’s architecture: a polished, segmented carving of stone and horn, slowly dusting over with fine glass powder, waiting for the ambient vibration of the harpsichord fronds to sing its shell into permanent, peaceful resonance.
