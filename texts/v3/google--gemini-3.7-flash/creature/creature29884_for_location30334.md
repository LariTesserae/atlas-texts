Where iron has replaced the earth, the lineage of blood and claw must renegotiate how it grips, sleeps, and hunts. 

At the smallest, most foundational scale, life here is a creature of the seams. Beneath the mats of saxifrage and strap-fern, where wet rust flakes off in brittle wafers, lives the **tallow-tick**—a broad, disk-shaped arachnid the size of a copper coin, whose carapace is entirely encrusted with orange hematite scale. They do not spin webs for the air, where mist would simply weigh the silk to ruin; instead, they string dense, resinous draglines inside the gaps between riveted plates. They graze on the dark, sulfurous biofilms that coat the iron and the rancid, centuries-old animal tallow weeping from the bearings of automated pulleys. 

Where the water-mains weep and cold mountain runoff gathers in the zinc troughs, there are **siphon-frogs**. They are entirely slate-black, flat as a wet leaf, with skin so thick and rubbery it resists the sharp edges of sheared metal. Their toes have evolved broad, pneumatic discs that seal against vibrating, oil-slicked wet iron. They possess no vocal sacs—sound in this damp, muffled labyrinth travels poorly and invites crushing by blind mechanical parts—so their courtship is purely seismic. A male presses his flattened throat against a hollow box-girder, drumming out rhythmic, hydraulic-sounding thumps that resonate through three tiers of structural steel, picked up by the sensitive jaw-bones of females miles away down the same tramway line.

Deeper inside the dead spaces, within the hollow iron caissons and the dry interiors of decommissioned steam drums, shelter the **gantry foxes**. Long-legged, narrow-ribbed, and entirely gray-furred, their coats are perpetually damp and smell sharply of wet wool and kerosene. Their pads are uniquely textured: rough, thick horn that never rots despite the ceaseless moisture, grooved like the tread of an industrial boot to gain purchase on slippery round pipe. They do not dig dens—there is no earth to scrape—so they whelp their litters inside cold boiler fireboxes lined with stripped ivy fibers and shredded hemp insulation. 

The foxes survive on the fall. They are creatures of dynamic patience. They know the automated rhythms of the valley—the shudder of an overhead trip-lever that signals a hopper is about to dump a ton of river aggregate; the sudden, boiling release of a turbine purge that will scald and flush hundreds of pipe-dwelling blind eels into the open sluices. When the mechanical diverters clatter and the gates slam, the foxes are already waiting downstream, their long, hook-toothed muzzles dipping into the foaming drainage to pull out stunned, pale river-things or crushed water-beetles before the current sweeps them into the bottomless lower darkness.

Higher up, where the mist hangs thickest across the suspension spans, move the **Wedge-Tenders**. 

They are human, or something so closely shaped by three centuries of surviving the superstructure that the distinction has ceased to matter. They are few—perhaps two or three dozen family lines scattered across a regional expanse of cold iron. They do not walk upright in the way of open-plains people; they move in a loose, low-center-of-gravity crouch, their bare or tallow-greased feet prehensile enough to hook the flange of an I-beam while both hands work a rope. Their fingernails are broad, blunt, and thick as horn, worn flat from scraping rust and clearing mechanical jam-points.

The Tenders do not maintain the machines out of reverence or design; they treat the machinery as a harsh, unchangeable geology that must be appeased to remain habitable. If a drainage weir clogs with ivy, the backed-up water will sheer a walkway off its mounts and drop their living-platforms a thousand feet into the lower dark; therefore, they prune the ivy. If a counterweight track seizes for lack of grease, the suspension cables will twist and twist until a deck shears in two; therefore, they render fox-fat and the oil from boiler-eels, crawling naked through narrow maintenance sleeves to smear the tracks.

Their dwellings are slung *beneath* the main levels, hung like swallows’ nests from the web-plates of the widest bridge spans. These shelters are light, teardrop-shaped pods woven from split wicker, river-rushes pulled from the tramway hoppers, and layered with sailcloth soaked in petroleum wax. Suspended on shock-absorbing hemp tethers, their homes swing gently with the perpetual vibration of the valley's unseen pumps, insulated from the bone-shattering shudder of the railway diverters.

The Tenders speak in a low, sibilant whisper—a tongue made of clicks, whistles, and the flat tapping of small lead rods against iron. They never shout; shouting is lost instantly in the sodden moss, but a sharp *ting* on a pressurized cold-water pipe can carry their names across two miles of gray, weeping air.

Their dead are not buried. When a Tender dies, their body is wrapped tightly in layers of sodden strap-fern, bound with copper wire, and wedged directly into the heavy mechanical joints of the counterweight systems. Over decades, the immense, sliding pressure of the iron weights and the steady weep of acidic, mineral-rich mountain water slowly compress and mineralize the remains, turning the dead into black, carbonaceous shims that stop the iron from grinding against itself. 

In this way, they become part of the baseline: another layer of organic silence wedged between the cold weight of the sky and the endless, turning steel below.
