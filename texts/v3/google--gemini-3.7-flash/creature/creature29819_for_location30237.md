To understand what lives in the cleft, one must first understand that life here does not merely inhabit the wood; it acts as its nervous system, its maintenance crew, and its internal circulation. In a place where timber takes the place of mountain granite and pressurized sap replaces stone, the boundary between the architecture and the organism is perpetually blurred.

### The Internal Fauna: Cleaners and Regulators

Within the deep plumbing—the ribbed, barrel-thick vines and swollen cistern-galls—lives a specialized biome that exists in total, rushing darkness. 

Long, ribbon-thin creatures known colloquially as *flume-eels* or *sieve-worms* cling to the interior walls of the primary conduits. Blind, with pearlescent, translucent skin that shows the rhythmic pulsing of their cold, pale blood, they anchor themselves against the torrent using chitinous mouth-rings. They do not feed on flesh, but graze incessantly upon the thick sulfurous slimes and mineral deposits that would otherwise fur and choke the hydraulic channels. When a valve-gall spasms from a drop in mountain pressure, these creatures shift in unison, their flattened bodies overlapping like living shingles to smooth the turbulence and prevent the living conduit from tearing itself apart under the water hammer.

Higher up, where the hollow stems breach the canopy to breathe, the vines are attended by *wax-beetles*. The size of a fist and plated in dull, oil-sheen elytra, these slow, heavily built insects never descend into the sub-canopy twilight. They spend their multi-year lives in the freezing wind, shearing off frozen dew crystals with their mandibles and harvesting the bitter resin produced by the needles. In return, they secrete a thick, pungent tallow from glands beneath their wing-cases, which they pack meticulously into the seams where branches have been grafted together, sealing the wounds against rot and frost-bite.

### The Alcove Dwellers

In the mid-tiers, where the great trunks swell into swollen, hollowed burls and the air is insulated from the high winds, the temperature rises by a few critical degrees. Here, within the domestic alcoves carved into the living grain, the settlement’s primary residents make their homes.

They are beings of dense, compact muscle and quiet, deliberate movement—humanoid in silhouette, but profoundly adapted to the physics of vertical, slippery, and oxygen-starved terrain. 

They are barrel-chested, possessing deep, expansive lungs that draw hard on the thin mountain air. Their skin is thick, pale as peeled birch, and covered in a fine, water-repellent down that causes the perpetual mist to bead and roll away without chilling their blood. Their extremities are remarkable: long, tendon-knotted fingers tipped with broad, hardened nails that can dig into fibrous bark like climbing irons, and wide, opposable toes that allow them to walk across a wet tension-root with the nonchalance of someone crossing a dry threshold. 

Because open flame is impossible—smoke would choke the tightly thatched canopy and suffocate the upper foliage, while fire is the absolute terror of a wooden city—they have no concept of hearths or metallurgy. Instead, they derive warmth from the city itself. They hollow their sleeping cells directly into the cambial layer of the largest boles, where the tree’s own slow, continuous metabolic respiration keeps the ambient air warm enough to sleep without shivering. 

Their clothing is not woven from wool or flax, but felted from beaten liverwort fibers and the shed silken linings of insect pupae, cured in pungent spruce-oils until it is utterly impermeable to the damp.

### Language, Sensation, and the Graft-Craft

They are nearly silent beings. In an environment where the roar of water through capillary wood and the groaning of stressed limbs creates a continuous, low-frequency hum, human-style vocalization is easily lost or disorienting. 

Instead, their communication is tactile and acoustic. They speak in low, percussive clicks and deep chest-humming that travels far better through the solid wood beneath their feet than through the heavy, water-logged air. A hunter or a climber three levels above can feel a neighbor’s approach by the specific harmonic ring of a tension-root plucked with a thumbnail, or by the slow, rhythmically tapped message against the grain of a structural pillar.

Their culture is centered entirely on the *listening*. The most respected members of their society are the *Tension-Tasters*—elderly individuals whose hearing and touch are so finely calibrated that they can lay an ear against the wet bark of a three-hundred-foot archway and tell whether the wood is dry-starved, under-pressurized, or shearing beneath the weight of ice on the ridge above. 

They carry few possessions: small bone awls for piercing bark, spools of tough, resin-soaked sinew, and horn gourds filled with grafting pastes made from ground liverwort and concentrated hormonal sap. When a wall begins to sag, they do not prop it up with dead beams; they score the cambium, train a young, flexible runner-stem across the fault line, bind it with wet cords, and wait for the two living surfaces to inosculate and knit into a single, indestructible truss.

### The Diet of the Mid-Levels

Because sunlight is scarce, agriculture in the traditional sense does not exist. Their sustenance is tapped directly from the living infrastructure.

They drink the phloem sap—a cloudy, viscous fluid drawn from specific "milking nodes" that tastes sharply of menthol, raw starch, and wintergreen. It is extremely high in simple sugars and metabolic stimulants, allowing them to endure the bitter cold without high-protein diets. 

For solid nourishment, they cultivate bracket fungi, broad shelf-mushrooms that grow directly from the peat-packed walls, and harvest the dense, mineral-rich eggs of the blind tree-frogs that nest in the water traps. Below the main settlements, near the sulfurous settling pools at the ravine’s floor, they tend sprawling beds of cold-hardy watercress and bitter, oily mosses that thrive on the rich mineral silt washed down from the sheared slate cliffs.

### Death in the Grain

When one of the cleft-dwellers dies, their passing leaves no tomb of stone. 

The body is stripped of its oiled felts, washed in cold mountain runoff, and laid inside a hollowed node deep within the central taproots that anchor the entire settlement to the tectonic fracture at the bottom of the cleft. There, surrounded by the moist, dense peat and the slow, inexorable thrust of root-hairs, they are sewn into the wood.

Within weeks, the fine rootlets penetrate the flesh; within a season, the living grain has swallowed the bone entirely. To the people of the cleft, this is neither a tragedy nor a fearful mystery, but an act of structural integrity: the dead become the floor plates their grandchildren will walk upon, the walls that will shelter them from the high gales, and the living pumps that carry the cold water skyward through the dark.
