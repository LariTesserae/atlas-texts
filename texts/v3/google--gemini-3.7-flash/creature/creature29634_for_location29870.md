To live on the stone floor is to be wide, flat, and made mostly of light.

Because the world here offers no depth to dive into and no height to climb, life has spread itself outward. It clings, creeps, and hovers. The creatures of this endless shelf have evolved not against gravity, but against the slickness of wet granite and the scouring directness of the sun.

### The Floor-Dwellers and the Creeping Film

At the very surface of the stone, where the water is only a skin of warmth, the world belongs to creatures that are almost two-dimensional. 

Among the olive and rust-colored algal crusts move the *shield-ticks* and *plate-crustaceans*—broad, amber-shelled arthropods no thicker than a fingernail, shaped like rounded coins of quartz. They do not walk so much as skate upon hydraulic cushions, their dozens of micro-legs tipped with soft, sticky pads that grip the slick diatom slime. When a shadow passes over them, they exhaust the water from under their carapaces and clamp flat against the gneiss with suction so fierce that not even a wading bird’s heavy bill can pry them free without shattering their shells.

Beside them in the silt-filled micro-troughs are the *pale worms* and *blind flat-leeches*, translucent as melted glass. They feed on the pink feldspar dust and the constant, microscopic rain of decaying algae, their digestive tracts tinted pale rose and gold by the minerals they ingest. When the sun is directly overhead, they become entirely invisible, their bodies refracting the sky and the wet rock so perfectly that a pool containing ten thousand of them looks like empty, polished stone.

### The Chorus: The Pearl-Throated Frogs

The roar that rolls across the flats belongs to hundreds of distinct lineages of frogs, none of them larger than a plum pit, whose entire existence is tuned to the acoustic resonance of thin water over bedrock.

Their bellies are broad, translucent disks that act like living suction cups, allowing them to anchor against the imperceptible creep of the current without expending muscle. Their backs are pigmented with the exact mottled pink, feldspar-white, and charcoal-black of the surrounding granite, so that when their vocal sacs are deflated, a pool appears to hold only gravel.

When they sing, however, they transform the landscape. Their vocal sacs do not expand upward into the air, but swell beneath their chins into large, pearlescent spheres that rest directly against the stone or dip into the water. As they chatter and drone, their calls transmit through the bedrock itself as much as through the air; the granite floor acts as a massive sounding board, carrying the vibration of millions of tiny throats for tens of miles. 

Their reproduction is bound to the shallows. They do not lay gelatinous clumps of eggs that would drift and bake in the sun. Instead, they produce wide, single-layer sheets of sticky, amber eggs that they paste directly to the granite in the lee of sedge tufts. The warmth of the stone incubates them in a matter of hours; the tadpoles hatch already broad-headed and flattened, possessing tiny rasping mouths that immediately begin mowing the felt of cyanobacteria from the rock.

### The Winged Mid-Air

Above the film of water, the air is thick with wings that have nowhere high to roost.

The *slate-winged dragonflies* are heavy-bodied, archaic insects with wingspans as broad as a hand. Unlike modern, darting dragonflies, these are slow, heavy cruisers that fly with an audible, paper-like rustle. Their wings are patterned with irregular black blotches that mimic the shadows cast by the wiry sedges. They spend hours hovering three inches above the water, their compound eyes—tinted deep copper—tracking the emergence of midges. When they rest, they do not perch on twigs; they splay themselves flat against the sun-warmed, dry knuckles of granite, absorbing the heat directly into their thoraxes.

The midges themselves form immense, vertical spires that can rise thirty feet into the still air, resembling pale pillars of smoke scattered across the flats. Each pillar is tied to a specific warm pan of water, drifting slowly with the barest movement of the breeze. To walk through the plain—if one could walk across this slick, wet stone—would be to pass continually from silence into the high-pitched, electric whining of these swarms, which fill the mouth and nostrils with the sweet, vegetative scent of sun-baked pond scum.

### The Stalkers and the Stone-Beaks

Pacing through these clouds are the waders—the *clack-bills* and *slate-herons*. They are archaic, heavy-set birds, descendants of lineages that never needed to develop long, graceful flight because their food is everywhere and has nowhere to hide.

Their legs are thick-jointed and relatively short compared to marsh birds of deeper waters, but their feet are enormous: the toes are long, flexible, and covered beneath with rough, pebbled skin like wet leather, preventing them from slipping on the diatomaceous scum that coats the granite. Their feathers are coarse and bleached to a pale, mineral gray that reflects the fierce sunlight.

They hunt with a deliberate, hypnotic slowness. A bird will lift one broad foot, hold it suspended above the water for a full minute, and set it down without generating a single ripple. Its gaze is fixed on the shallow glare. When it strikes, it does not spear; its bill is broad, flat, and made of dense, honey-colored horn, functioning like a pair of heavy wooden tongs. It claps the bill down against the stone with a dry, hollow *thwack* that startles the frogs for yards around, crushing shield-ticks, dragonfly nymphs, and small frogs instantly against the bedrock floor.

Because there are no tall trees, these birds nest directly on the low, rounded hummocks of stone that rise above the floodline. They build no nests of twigs; instead, they scrape small depressions into the lichen crusts and lay three or four pale, stone-colored eggs, which both parents shade with their broad, half-spread wings through the hottest hours of the afternoon.

### The Hidden Depths: Life in the Fractures

Where the bedrock is broken by joints and deep, hairline fractures, another world exists entirely—one of vertical shade in a landscape of horizontal light.

In these narrow cracks, where the roots of the coin-leaved scrub trees plunge into the cool, permanent damp beneath the crust, lives the *crevice-fauna*. Long, eel-like lungfish, with skin the color of damp slate and tiny, vestigial eyes, dwell in the dark crevices where the water pools two or three feet deep beneath the slab. They are ancient, slow-breathing survivors that can estivate in the silty grit of the joints for years if a rare, prolonged drought dries the surface sheets.

Here, too, are the *root-spiders*, pale and long-legged, which spin horizontal sheet-webs across the mouths of the fissures to catch midges that seek shelter from the midday heat. They share the cool damp with large, flattened newts whose skin is so saturated with defensive alkaloids that they smell distinctly of crushed bitter almonds.

### The Great Stone-Plates

The largest creatures of the peneplain are the *sun-plates*—sluggish, ancient reptiles resembling tortoises that have been ironed flat by time. 

Their shells are not domed, but are broad, circular disks of smooth, leathery bone, barely three inches thick at the center and tapering to paper-thin edges that rest flush against the granite. They spend days entirely motionless in two inches of water, their shells coated in living films of green algae until they are indistinguishable from the surrounding floor. 

They are herbivores, grazing with broad, serrated jaws on the tough, wiry sedges and the fallen, coin-like leaves of the scrub trees. They move with an almost geological patience, taking an hour to travel twenty paces across a sunlit slab, leaving behind them a clean, scrubbed trail of pink granite where their lower shells have wiped away the silt.

When evening approaches, and the direct sunlight softens into a long, pale rose reflection across the miles of shallow water, the sun-plates lift their blunt snouts to the humid air and let out low, resonant hisses that join the deafening, triumphant roar of the frogs. 

Nothing here is waiting for the world to change. The mountains have already fallen; the valleys are already filled. The creatures of the stone floor have settled into an eternity of warm inches, living upon the bones of the earth where there is nothing left to fall from, and nowhere higher to go.
