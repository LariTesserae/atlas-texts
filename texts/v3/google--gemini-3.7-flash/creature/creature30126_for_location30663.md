At the base of everything is the curd. 

It forms anywhere the mineral water slows down—in the gutters between low stone walls, inside the wide cisterns carved into the shale, and across the broad, shallow terraces that stairstep into the grey mist. It is not algae, but a dense, rubbery mat of chemotrophic bacteria and mineral-feeding colonial polyps that ranges in color from the grey of wet lead to a bruised, liverish violet. It smells faintly of warm copper and lye. It coats the razor edges of the shale like a slick skin, constantly precipitating chalk from the water and binding it into spongy, fibrous sheets. This is the valley’s pasture, bread, and pastureland combined: a continuous, self-replenishing carpet of grease and starch that grows thicker the more it is trampled and scraped.

Following the curd are the scraper-crabs. They do not walk so much as roll over the rock in interlocking herds, their white, porcelain-like carapaces flattened like heavy plates to resist the slip of the shallows. They are entirely blind, possessing neither eyes nor eye-stalks; instead, their shell rims are fringed with stiff, vibrating hairs that read the shudder of the bedrock. Their front limbs are short, spade-headed clubs capped with black mineral spurs, which they use to plane the bacterial sheets from the stone with a rhythmic, scissor-like clicking. When millions of them scrape at once, the sound against the shale is like the hiss of dry gravel poured down an endless iron chute. They breed continuously in the tepid pools, leaving clusters of heavy, calcareous eggs that weld themselves to the foundations of the stone huts like barnacles, cementing the city's bottommost layers into a single, concrete reef.

Above the crabs, wallowing in the wider drainage channels and beneath the arched rib-roofs of the causeways, are the great pale quadrupeds that provide the city with its timber and its grease. 

They are massive, barrel-shaped beasts with the heavy, unhurried bulk of manatees, though they possess four splayed, jointless pillars for limbs, ending in broad pads of horn that distribute their tonnage across the brittle shale without snapping it. Their skin is thick, rubbery, and entirely hairless, a uniform tallow-white that never tans under the diffused sky. They lack distinct necks; their heads simply taper into broad, spade-like snouts flanked by slit-like blowholes that wheeze with a wet, bellows-like regularity. 

These beasts live in a state of continuous, slow-motion grazing. They lower their shovel-heads into the warm mud, vacuuming up the scraper-crabs, the midge larvae, and the bacterial mats all at once, crushing the shells between broad plates of bone inside their throats with a wet, grinding crunch that vibrates through the flagstones underfoot. They are docile not from domestication, but from a profound, anatomical stupor. They have no natural predators large enough to threaten an adult, and they move among the low stone huts like living boulders, occasionally nudging a wall with a wet flank, leaving thick smears of insulating mucus behind.

The people who share these lanes are squat, heavy-boned, and built with low centers of gravity, shaped by generations of moving over wet, fractured calcium. 

Their skin is not pale, but a translucent, waterlogged grey-pink, constantly glistening with the moisture that condenses from the cloud ceiling. To protect themselves from the raw, ulcerating humidity and the bite of the chalky water, they smear their bodies with tallow rendered down from the great river-beasts, wiping it thick over their joints, ribs, and groin until their skin sheds the ambient sweat like slate. They have no body hair—they shave or scrape it away with sharpened shells to keep the blind midges from nesting in it—and their eyes are narrow, rimmed with thick, fleshy lids to shield against the constant, shadowless glare of the white sky.

Their feet are their most distinctive feature: the soles are broad, cracked, and calloused to the thickness of cured hide, deeply creased and blackened from contact with the mineral silts. They do not wear shoes; instead, the skin of their heels and pads has become an armor capable of stepping squarely on fresh-shattered shale without drawing blood.

Because the roar of the basin is total—the *tock* of the hammers, the scrape of the crabs, the wheeze of the mud-beasts, the hum of the midges—the people do not rely on speech as dry-land folk do. The spoken word here is useless past an arm’s length. 

Instead, their communication is physical and resonant. They speak in brief, low-frequency grunts produced deep in the diaphragm, which carry as vibrations through their heavy ribcages and the wet rock beneath their feet. When two masons work together on a slab of bone-and-mortar, they coordinate through the touch of their knees against the same stone, feeling the rhythm of the other’s strike through the vibrations of the cobble-hammer. In the alleys, their greetings are tactile: a firm, wet palm pressed against the base of the neck, or a deliberate heel-drop onto the causeway that sends a sharp, unmistakable click through the floor-slabs.

Their lives are structured around a continuous, non-linear maintenance. Because there is no night, they do not sleep together in long, dark stretches; they drop into brief, four-hour stupors whenever their limbs heavy, curling together in the dry, tallow-greased corners of the bone-roofed huts, only to rise and immediately resume the work. 

One group scrapes the bacterial curd from the walls of the cisterns, pressing it into grey cakes that are boiled into a bland, gelatinous broth in stone pots set over smoldering cakes of dried mud-beast dung. 
Another group tends the midge-screens—broad frames of interlaced rib-splints greased with fat, hung across the narrow alleys where the draft concentrates the insect clouds. Every few hours, a worker walks the line with a bone spatula, scraping pounds of suffocated, blind midges from the fat into heavy clay tubs, where the protein is pounded into a rich, salty paste that tastes of sediment and river-lime.

And everywhere, there is the chipping. 

The people do not build to finish; they build because the basin is continually precipitating. If they stopped clearing the channels, the calcium crust would rise by inches each year, choking the flow of warm water, swallowing the lower doorways, and petrifying the settlement from the floor up. They are continuously quarrying away the very ground they walk on, flaking off the fresh shelves of shale with their chert adzes, dressing the slabs, and hauling them upward to lay a new layer of causeway over the one that is slowly being submerged in white silt. 

They are not masters of the basin, nor are they its prisoners; they are simply the largest and most deliberate of its lime-forming organisms. They fit their stones, boil their curd, grease their skin, and sleep in the warm, perpetual noon, their heartbeats matched exactly to the physical thrum of the white floor beneath them.
