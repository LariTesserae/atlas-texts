To understand what lives here, one must abandon the standard cadence of biology—the fast flutter of lungs, the twitch of antennae, the frantic, calorie-burning rush of warm-blooded things. In this vault, life is not an event that occurs upon the stone; it is an extension of the stone’s own chemical decay and heat loss, moving at a speed so measured it can easily be mistaken for mineral rest.

The thickets themselves are not mere foliage. They are colonial, hydrostatic networks—monolithic organisms that occupy both the stone floor and the iron hulks. 

Beneath the waxy, bruised leaves, the ivory tubers do not simply root; they *infiltrate*. Where a pneumatic drill has left a channel, a root thickens, filling the bore perfectly, molding itself against the spiral grooves of the steel bit that cut it. These roots secrete a clear, acidic bile that slowly digests the basalt’s silicate matrix, drawing out the potassium and sulfur while leaving behind a pale, crumbly chalk. Deep within the core of the largest mounds, where the leaves rot under their own weight into a warm, odorless peat, the tubers swell into broad, chambered reservoirs. These reservoirs pulse—not with a heartbeat, but with the slow, convective shifting of trapped water, warmed from beneath by the buried steam conduits and cooled on top by the ambient air. Over the course of weeks, a single reservoir will draw in gallons of condensation through the waxy cuticle of its leaves, store it in its translucent flesh, and bleed it back into the root-tips to dissolve the grease caked around the dead turbines.

Living in the interstitial spaces of these botanical mounds are things that blur the line between animal, fungus, and sediment.

Wedged into the razor-sharp drill scars along the perimeter walls are the **Bellows-Skins**. They appear at first to be patches of wet, sloughed rubber, clinging to the vertical rock faces like discarded gaskets. In truth, they are blind, flattened organisms of pure connective tissue and spongy lung-flesh, devoid of limbs or sensory organs beyond a profound sensitivity to barometric pressure and thermal drift. They have no mouth; their underbellies are a dense thicket of pale, fibrous cilia that burrow directly into the sulfur crust lining the rock fissures. Over the course of four or five days, a Bellows-Skin will slowly engorge itself on the rising, heated air, expanding until it is the size of a shield and thick as a mattress, its translucent, purplish skin stretched taut over reservoirs of pale fluid. When the ambient temperature drops by even a fraction of a degree, the skin contracts with an agonizing, imperceptible slowness, squeezing the trapped vapors through internal baffles to extract microscopic mineral salts, releasing that faint, breath-like hiss that wanders through the quiet of the vault.

On the wide, desolate spans of crushed stone—in the open clearings between the foliage—move the **Slag-Creepers**. 

They are the room’s only mobile scavengers, though their movement is measured in inches per month. A Slag-Creeper has no interior skeleton; it is a heavy, muscular disc of tallow-colored gristle, three to four feet across, shaped like a flattened dome. Its dorsal surface is entirely covered in a thick, self-secreted mosaic of sharp basalt flakes, machine filings, and rusted iron scale, glued together with a hardened, amber-like resin. This gives it the appearance of an ordinary heap of blasting debris, perfectly camouflaged against the scree. 

Beneath this armored shield, however, the creature is a vast, slow mouth. Its ventral pad is coated in a carpet of rasps made of biological iron—hardened magnetite teeth that grind against the floor as it creeps. It glides over the gravel beds, systematically scraping the microscopic films of chemosynthetic bacteria from the stones and licking away the dry, sweetish grease that has settled into the rock dust over the decades. When two Slag-Creepers meet in the open, they do not fight or mate in any rapid sense; they simply press their margins together, exchanging fluids, hormones, and mineral stores across a shared seam of mucus for months before their slow, blind trajectories carry them apart again.

Inside the two-story iron bulk of the steam-manifold housing, where the maroon thermal light is strongest and the air is thickest with sulfur, lives something older and far more integrated with the machinery.

Within the dark, hollow belly of the cast-iron casing, occupying the void left by corroded pistons and burst copper pipes, is an aggregate organism that has used the machine itself as an external skeleton. It has no singular name or simple anatomy. It is a vast, gelatinous mass, dense and amber-colored, that has cast itself perfectly into the negative space of the engine. Its "nerves" are threads of conductive iron-oxide filaments that run through the verdigris-crusted pipes; its "organs" are partitioned sacs hanging inside the empty steam drums. 

It feeds on the residual core heat of the deep infrastructure, drawing thermal energy directly through the baseplate anchored into the floor. It does not move, nor does it grow outward into the light. Instead, it slowly, endlessly circulates a heavy, mineral-laden brine through the manifold's original pipework, using the dead machine's copper plumbing as a secondary circulatory system. On the occasions when a structural bolt finally gives way to rust and pops with a sharp *ping*, this central mass registers the shift in tension through its copper-threaded nerves, subtly adjusting its hydrostatic pressure to keep the iron plates from collapsing inward and crushing the dark, warm cavern it has claimed.

High overhead, on the weeping vault ceiling where the condensation gathers, there are the **Tear-Drinkers**. 

They are long, filamentous colonies, no thicker than sewing thread, that hang down from the ceiling like pale, wet cobwebs, sometimes dangling three or four fathoms into the empty air. They exist solely to catch the falling moisture. When a droplet of water coalesces on the basalt vault and begins to swell, the filament absorbs it before it can break free. The droplet slides down the interior of the thread, filtered of its heavy minerals, and is drawn into tiny, pearl-like bulbs spaced along the line. Only when the bulb is overfilled does the excess slip free, falling at that long, measured interval—*tink* against the boiler, *thwack* against the leaf. The Tear-Drinkers use this endless, falling distillation to wash their anchor-points in the stone, keeping their grip clean on rock that would otherwise crumble beneath them.

Nothing here competes. There is no hunting, no flight, no predation. There is only an excruciatingly patient division of the machine’s remains and the earth’s heat. 

The life in this room is not waiting for the sun to return, nor is it waiting for the air to cool. It has made a permanent home in the pause between human violence and geological oblivion, drinking iron, breathing steam, and quietly turning the cold steel back into living, pulsing mud.
