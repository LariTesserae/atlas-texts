Inside the hollow web-plates—where the sun never penetrates and the box-girders form echoing tunnels three stories high—lives an ecology of the interior, blind and acoustic.

Here, the air smells of old grease, warm condensate, and cold black loam. Long-legged whip-spiders, their antennae tipped with sensitive vibrational hairs rather than eyes, hunt along the rows of interior cross-braces. They feel the approach of prey not through the air, but through the micro-flexing of the steel plates as something shifts its weight on the outer flange sixty paces away. Beside them, blind, velvet-skinned crickets graze on the thick, rubbery mats of *ferro-fungus*—a pale, sulfurous growth that feeds directly upon the lard-based packing grease still seeping from forgotten pin-joints.

Where the great steam mains pass through the bulkheads, the heat creates micro-climates of sweltering tropical intensity within just feet of the thin, freezing mountain air outside. In these dark, sweltering sleeve-cavities nest the *copper-backed salamanders*. Their hides are rough and granular, mottled like corroded bronze, and their feet are tipped with broad, sticky spatulas that can adhere to polished, oil-slicked brass. They do not drink surface water; they flatten themselves against the condensation rings of the steam manifolds, absorbing the hot distilled mist directly through their bellies. When they call, their throats vibrate against the empty iron chambers, turning the girder into a tuning fork that thrums at a frequency felt in the teeth before it is heard.

Outside, in the daylight of the high chords, the fauna belongs to the wind and the balance-wire.

The agile climbers that run the suspension cables are not monkeys, though they have their intelligence; they are a lineage of arboreal civets whose claws have flattened and thickened into blunt, horn-like chocks that slip perfectly into the gap between parallel wire strands. They possess a second, semi-rigid set of ribs along their flanks that can lock in place, turning their bodies into low-profile wedges when the sixty-mile-an-hour crosswinds sweep off the cloud-sea. Their fur is dense and waxy, saturated with an aromatic oil derived from eating the fibrous nuts of the girder-palms. This grease repels the mist, prevents frost from anchoring to their guard hairs during the sub-zero dawns, and leaves a faint, amber scent along every catwalk they patrol.

Higher still, roosting under the eaves of the horizontal gantry rails, are the *camber-falcons*. They have broad, slotted wings like harriers, but their talons are unusually short and heavy, adapted not for snatching birds out of open sky, but for levering thick-shelled brass-beetles out of the drainage slots and crushing the armored crickets of the flues. They hunt the vertical drops. A camber-falcon will fold its wings and drop three thousand feet down the shear-wall of a vertical truss in absolute silence, using the updrafts of warm engine-exhaust like a thermal ladder to shoot back up into the sunlight without flapping a single primary feather.

And moving among them, slow and sure-footed as the giant balance-beams themselves, are the people of the upper trusses.

They are long-limbed and lean, with broad, leathery feet whose arches have an extraordinary prehensile grip, developed from generations of walking bare-soled on rounded pipe-sleeves and narrow angle-irons. They wear garments made from woven palm-linen and cured hide, rubbed repeatedly with boiled linseed oil and tallow until the cloth is as stiff and waterproof as the canvas lagging on the steam mains.

They do not think of the world in terms of north and south, but of *Tension* and *Compression*, *Windward* and *Lee*. They carry no walking sticks, but rather short, lead-weighted copper rods tipped with soft pine mallets. With these, they tap the structural chords as they walk, listening with their ears pressed against the wood to the tone of the iron. A high, sweet ring means the truss is sound and under load; a flat, dull thud warns of a choked expansion joint or a shear pin that has begun to rot within its socket.

These people are not builders; they are cultivators of equilibrium. They understand that if the sediment in a drainage trough becomes too deep, the added dead-load will twist the flange below; so they harvest the loam, carrying it in wicker baskets to the neutral axes of the cantilever arms where weight is needed to counter the pull of the long spans. They prune the thick fig vines away from the roller bearings, packing the bronze races with beeswax and palm fat, and in return, they tap the warm condensation pipes for drinking water and gather the sweet, oil-heavy fruit that grows in the drip-trays.

Their settlements are not towns, but *hangs*—clusters of light, suspension-strung dwellings woven from reed and split cane that swing gently beneath the sheltered bellies of the largest box-girders, anchored directly to the eye-bars. Here, sheltered from the raw glare of the noon sun and the bite of the altitude winds, their hearths burn dried fern-peat and fungus-slag in small copper braziers. 

At night, when the cloud-sea below turns the color of lead and the stars come out with a fierce, diamond hardness, the inhabitants lie with their ears against their suspended floors. They fall asleep to the language of the framework: the deep, rhythmic sigh of the ten-minute pump cycle, the sharp, gun-shot crack of an expansion shoe sliding four inches along its graphite bed, and the vast, slow, musical breathing of sixty miles of living iron settling against the night cold.
