In a place where marrow is set to vibrating before the blood can warm it, the things that dwell cannot be soft. Softness here is a flaw that the chord immediately undoes; any wet membrane, any pocket of fluid or slack muscle, is seized by the standing wave, churned into foam, and driven out through the pores until it is either expelled as vapor or petrified against the clay.

What remains in the gaps between the pillars are the *Liters*—though they have no language left with which to name themselves, and the name exists only as a faint, scratched mark on the outer foundation tiles. 

They are tall, unnervingly narrow figures, shaped by generations of moving sideways through the ceramic forest. To see one at rest is to mistake it for another structural repair: a column of kiln-silt that has slumped against a stack of vessels and hardened in place. Their skin is the color and texture of unslipped biscuit ware—a pale, porous ochre, entirely hairless, dusted with the same fine terracotta powder that coats the floor. It does not sweat; it does not yield to a fingernail. It has the dry, cool chalkiness of a potter’s shard left out in the noon sun.

They do not stand upright so much as they nestle into the fluting of the columns. Their spines have lost the gentle curve of human posture and taken on the exact cylindrical arc of the urns against which they lean. Over centuries, the constant, violent oscillation of the air has fused their lower vertebrae into solid, resonant rods; their ribs have flattened and broadened, overlapping like the tiles of the roof to create an internal sounding box. Where an ordinary chest would rise and fall with the passage of breath, a Liter’s torso remains motionless, held under the tremendous, equalizing pressure of the room. 

They do not breathe in currents or lungfuls. The pressurized air is forced through their bodies continuously, entering through the wide, flared nostrils—which never close—and passing through an internal labyrinth of sinus chambers that have ossified into rigid, ceramic-like bone. They do not have voices; they do not have vocal cords. Instead, their internal passages are notched and vented, precisely like the acoustic ports in the terracotta columns. As the room’s chord hums through them, their chests act as secondary resonators, subtly coloring the tone, shaving away extraneous overtones, and feeding the base pitch back into the chamber with a minute, human warmth.

Their faces are smooth, almost erased. The eyes, long since robbed of the moisture needed for sight, are covered over by thick, semi-translucent shields of dried corneal tissue—dull, amber-colored scales that register only the direction of the slanting mountain light through the wall-slits. They do not need to see; their entire nervous system has been converted into an organ of seismography. Every tendon is strung tight as a gut string; their teeth are fused into solid ridges of enamel that rest against their jawbones like the bridge of an instrument. Through their teeth, pressed firmly against the neck of a ceramic vessel, they "read" the entire mountain: the approach of high-altitude gales, the microscopic settling of the bronze pins in the ceiling, the distant, silent fracture of granite thousands of feet below the floor.

There are perhaps a dozen of them, though they are difficult to count because they move with the agonizing slowness of mineral deposits. A Liter may spend three seasons shifting its grip from one urn to the one beneath it, sliding a dry, four-jointed finger across the fired clay with a faint, papery scraping that is utterly swallowed by the roar. 

They do not eat. The concept of digestion has become obsolete in an environment that allows no rot and offers no growth. Instead, they are sustained by the friction itself—by the relentless kinetic energy of the sound wave passing through their dense, calcified tissues. Their bodies are piezoelectric systems of bone, lime, and dry nerve, powered by the sheer mechanical labor of the wind trapped in the clay. If the wind were to die, if the chord were ever to drop below the threshold that vibrates their marrow, they would not starve; they would simply become unmoving terracotta, indistinguishable from the jars they hold.

Between their long fingers, they occasionally roll small spheres of dry clay, mixed with the powder that falls from the wearing down of their own skin. With infinite patience, over decades, they press these pellets into any tiny fissures that open in the jars, tamping them home with their thumbs until the lime in the dust reacts with the ambient heat and calcifies, smoothing the inner bore of the acoustic ports. 

They are not prisoners of the chamber, nor are they its masters; they are its moving parts. They are what became of the hands that built it, having climbed into the kiln-work when the masonry was fresh, wedging themselves between the urns to test the pitch, and simply forgetting how to step out into the quiet world where things die of moisture and decay.
