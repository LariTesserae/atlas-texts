The carpet begins with the **tongue-crusts**. They are not single animals, though they share a single, unbroken pulse that ripples across sixty square yards of submerged iron plate at a time. To the touch, they feel like the underside of a horse’s lip—wet, warm, velvety, and faintly ridged with thousands of microscopic suction-clefts. They do not hunt. They secrete a clear, heavy acid that curdles the galvanic grease leaking from the machine housings, turning the industrial tallow into a warm curd which they draw inward through their pores. Where a tongue-crust has lived for more than a few cycles of the flywheels, the cold-rolled steel beneath it is pitted and clean, bright as surgical silver, stripped of all slag and scale.

Between them scuttle the **pith-spindles**. These are creatures made entirely of hard, sharp geometries, as if the sheared shale had learned to walk. Their bodies are no larger than an open hand, flattened like river stones, supported by twelve to sixteen needle-thin legs of dense, vitreous chitin. Because the rock is young and knife-edged, the spindles have evolved to be harder still; their footfalls produce a dry, rapid *snip-snip-snip* like tailors’ shears working through heavy canvas. They do not have mouths in any conventional sense. Their underside is an open, vibrating drum of stiff cilia that shears the kerosene-scented bacterial film from the basalt and crushes it into an oily paste through sheer acoustic friction. When two spindles meet, they do not fight; they press their carapace edges together in a sharp, sparking click, testing each other’s hardness, before sliding past like sheets of stacked mica.

In the flooded channels where the cables plunge deep into the brine, the **ribbon-shrouds** drift. They are animals of pure surface area—flat, paper-thin, some twenty feet long and less than an inch thick, composed of a muscle so soft and pale it resembles boiled tripe. They have no skeleton, no organs that can be felt as lumps, only an endless undulating margin fringed with chemical whiskers. 

The shrouds love the cables. They wrap themselves around the tarred hemp insulation in spiral sleeves, five or six deep, forming a living, twitching sheath over the copper. They are drawn to the high-frequency electrostatic hum. By pressing their bellies to the current, they induce a faint, reciprocal charge in their own blood, which glows with no visible light, but radiates a heavy, pleasant warmth that keeps the surrounding water at precisely 104 degrees. If you were to wade past a cable encrusted with ribbon-shrouds, you would feel them drift away from the hemp and drape against your legs—not to constrict, but to taste the salt of your skin with their entire bodies, their slick, velvet bulk dragging over your boots with the weight of wet drapery.

Higher up, where the physics begins to curl and fray around the summits of the towers, lives something that has adapted to the basin's elastic geometry: the **Gaugers**.

The Gaugers cannot be understood by looking at their shape, because their shape depends entirely on which direction you approach them from. Moving toward a Gauger from the south, it appears as a pale, swollen bladder the size of a hay-bale, wedged into the crossbeams of a coal-cracking tower, its surface heaving with slow, three-minute breaths. But approach it from the west, along the line of the warped condensate flow, and that same creature stretches out into a mile-long filament of translucent gut, pinned to the steel girders by thousands of hooked dewclaws. 

The Gaugers are the true inhabitants of the spatial folds. They anchor themselves across the discrepancies in distance. A Gauger feeds by simply existing in two places that should be near each other but are not; it draws its metabolic energy from the strain the terrain exerts upon itself. Inside their thick, rubbery hides, you can hear a sound like a wet hemp rope being twisted on a capstan—the sound of tissue absorbing the tension of bent space. They are entirely blind, possessing only clusters of deep-set vibration pits that register the three-day turn of the great flywheels. When a flywheel reaches its quarterly turn, the Gaugers let out a single, wet, pneumatic sigh that displaces the steam for hundreds of yards around them.

And deeper still, wading where the water is thick and the mud is rich with pulverized transformer oil, are the **Forgemen**—though they were never men, and they forge nothing.

They are bipeds only in the sense that they move upon two broad, column-like masses of muscle that have no knees, only wide, suction-ringed pads that spread the creature's immense weight over the razor-sharp shale. A Forgeman stands eleven feet tall, shaped like a wedge of tallow or a salt-cured ham set on end. Its hide is four inches thick, gray-white, and completely numb; it does not flinch when the fresh-fractured rock slices deep into its lower limbs, because its flesh seals instantly behind the cut with a thick, odorless resin that hardens faster than blood.

The Forgemen have no faces. In place of a head, the top of their torso terminates in a broad, concave basin lined with soft, finger-like papillae that catch the condensate weeping from the ceilings and the steel trusses. They are the basin's tenders, though they have no purpose or culture; their intelligence is slow, rhythmic, and heavy, operating on the same dampened clock that makes iron bolts sink like stones in honey.

A Forgeman will stand before a dead, silent transformer housing for eight months without shifting its feet. It presses its broad, flat chest against the cold metal, listening to the microscopic settling of the copper coils inside. With deliberate, agonizing slowness, its front limbs—short, spade-like appendages tipped with blunt, horn-capped digits—will reach out and clear the silt from the drain-plugs, or scrape away the dead tongue-crusts to allow fresh grease to reach the iron seams. They do not know why they do this; it is simply that to a Forgeman, an ungreased seam or a blocked valve feels like a grain of sand behind an eyelid. They correct the machines as a sleeping animal scratches an itch.

Sometimes, two Forgemen will meet in the narrow spaces between the storage tanks. Because space is soft there, they may spend three days passing one another, their tallowy flanks pressing together with a sound like wet leather being kneaded in grease. They do not vocalize. Instead, they trade heat. The warmer one cools slightly; the colder one warms; their heartbeats, which thump once every seven minutes like heavy wooden mallets wrapped in wool, fall into a slow, hydraulic synchrony. 

Once they are aligned, they move on, their vast, silent pads leaving suction-hollows in the black grease that take hours to fill, their passage marked only by the warm wake that curls through the steam, rocking the blind ribbons where they sleep against the copper wire.
