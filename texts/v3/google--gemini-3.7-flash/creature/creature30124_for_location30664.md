To live here is to be entirely divorced from the mechanics of fluid. 

Nothing in the basin possesses a pulse of blood, a mucous membrane, or a wet eye. To introduce a single droplet of water into this air would be an act of violent chemistry; it would boil into the vacuum of humidity instantly, cracking the delicate salt-crusts and turning the living tissues into shattered crisps. The life that occupies these self-assembling colonnades is built instead upon tension, static charge, and the endless, frictionless circulation of ultra-fine dust.

The long-jointed scavengers that drift across the elevated viaducts are perhaps the most visible, though they are neither rare nor singular. They are known to the architecture—if the city can be said to know anything—as *weft-walkers*. Their bodies are scarcely heavier than dried grass, built from overlapping ribbons of pale mica-plate and hollow, paper-thin calcium tubes held together by internal hair-springs of tensile graphite. They have no lungs; they do not breathe. Instead, their broad, oval carapaces are open at the margins, lined with dense banks of static combs that harvest electric potential from the dry air as they glide. 

The weft-walker's relationship to the city is not that of an inhabitant to a house, but of a needle to a loom. The velvet pads beneath their six splayed feet do not merely soften their tread; they are coated with millions of microscopic, branched cilia that read the static charge of the stone beneath them. As a weft-walker drifts down a narrowing alley, it senses the exact micro-second a calcium arch begins to suffer structural fatigue. It pauses, extends its feather-like appendages—which are not limbs for feeding, but dry spinnerets—and draws up drifted lime from the corners. Within its desiccated gizzard, two small spheres of rough slate grind the lime to a sub-micron aerosol, mixing it with an electrostatic charge before venting it in a precise, silent puff against the failing joint. The powder does not scatter; it snaps to the crack like iron filings to a magnet, sintering instantly into fresh, razor-sharp bone-mineral. The walker does not build the city with intention; it merely balances an equation of charges, compelled by the itch of unbalanced voltage across its skin.

Deeper within the tenements, inside the lightless honeycombs where the violet phosphorescence never reaches, lives the marrow of the precinct. 

These are the *bellows-forms*. They are entirely immobile, anchoring themselves within the vertical chimneys and flues of the towers like stacks of dry, pleated lanterns. Formed from flexible laminates of translucent horn-mineral, they expand and collapse with an agonizingly slow, accordion-like cadence. Because there is no wind to stir the deep chambers, the bellows-forms generate the draft. They draw the freezing, mineral-saturated atmosphere through the city’s hollow foundations, sifting it across thousands of internal vanes of compressed talc. 

When a bellows-form contracts, it produces no sound, but the air it expels is stripped of its heavy elements and saturated with a sharp, ozone-like tension. This dry current rises through the spires, causing the mica-foliage on the balconies to shiver and turn. If a bellows-form dies, its pleats freeze into brittle gypsum, the flue clogs with uncirculated dust, and the entire tower above it ceases to shift, growing rigid and calcified until the surrounding buildings sheer away from it, leaving it behind like a dead tooth.

Beneath the pavements, in the compressed talc and salt-floor, moves a fauna of pure dispersal: the *sieve-drifts*. 

They are flattened, disk-like entities, scarcely thicker than a sheet of heavy vellum, that move through the dry dust as a fish moves through water, though without the displacement of a single grain. Their bodies are composed of interlocking silicate scales that flex in micro-peristaltic waves. As they travel through the chalk depths, they consume the detritus of the falling spires—the collapsed walkways, the sheared balustrades, the shattered calcified lace that settles like snow. They digest nothing in the organic sense; they are mineral centrifuges. They separate the talc from the brittle silicates, compressing the latter into dense, geometrically perfect hexagonal rods which they leave behind in subterranean strata, forming the rigid pilings upon which the creeping facades of the upper city slide.

Communication among these creatures is entirely tactile and electrical. Because the chalk absorbs all sound, voice is impossible; because the violet light is dim and casts shadows like razors, vision is unreliable beyond a few paces. 

Instead, they touch. When two weft-walkers cross on an arched viaduct, their long, antenna-like reed legs brush against one another with an imperceptible scrape. In that instant, an exchange of static discharges passes between them—a brief, microscopic spark jumping from comb to comb. In that snap of current, the entire geography of the basin is shared: which streets have sunken into the salt-crust, which towers have spun new bridges toward the slate walls, and where the air has grown too thin to yield lime.

They are beings without warmth, without moisture, and without decay. When a creature reaches the end of its mechanical coherence, it does not rot. It simply stops moving. The dry air draws out the last residual tension from its hair-springs; the static charge bleeds into the surrounding masonry; and within hours, the extruded struts of the expanding city grow through its joints, incorporating its mica scales and calcium reeds into the lintel of a new, soundless doorway.
