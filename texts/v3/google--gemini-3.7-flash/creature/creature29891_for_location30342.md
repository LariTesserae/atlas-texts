To understand what lives in the seam, one must look below the scale of human stride and into the logic of the capillary.

Within the miniature obelisks—whose cane-tall diamondoid shells are compartmentalized into thousands of pressurized, pressurized-oil-lubricated floors—dwell the *Stria*, though they do not use a spoken name for themselves, communicating instead through harmonic tremors transmitted directly through the stone. 

Physically, an adult Stria is scarcely five millimeters from its vitreous rostral plate to its trailing filament. They are neither wholly biological nor entirely mechanical, but the outcome of a long refinement: spindle-shaped bodies of cast synthetic amber and flexible lithium-glass, jointed with fine, water-repellent seals. In the pale, high-altitude sun, they appear colorless and crystalline, but beneath the surface of their shells runs an intricate, pulsing vascular lattice of pale mercury-blue fluid—a liquid metal alloy that acts simultaneously as circulatory blood, logic gate, and counterweight against the fierce barometric shifts.

The Stria do not walk in the mammalian sense. At their scale, the continuous, sheeting film of water that coats the city is not a hazard, but a primary medium of transit and shelter. Their six needle-tipped limbs are tipped with microscopic hydrophobic pads, allowing them to skate with effortless speed across the tension of the runoff streams, or dive into the shimmering laminate of water clinging to a vertical wall, pinning themselves against the composite stone to avoid being snatched away by a stray eddy of the high gale.

Inside the obelisks, life is quiet, densely layered, and intensely civic. The interiors are arranged in spiral tiers around vertical thermal conduits, lit by the dull, warm phosphorescence of superconducting coils. Here, the Stria maintain their institutions:
- **The Liturgists of the Gradient**, who spend generations calculating the microscopic thermal margins that keep the cleft from freezing solid, manually trimming the thermal baffles with tiny levers of cold-forged wire.
- **The Core-Tenders**, closely related to the segmented silver micro-tenders seen on the exterior walls, who ride the sub-surface transit pods down into the spire’s hollow marrow to grease the immense structural dampeners that keep the mountain-sized tower from snapping in the jet stream.
- **The Cloud-Sifters**, who harvest the supercooled vapor drifting into the cleft. At this altitude, the mist carries high-atmosphere aeroplankton, flash-frozen spores, and crystalline particulate cast off by the upper stratosphere; the Stria filter these through diamond mesh screens, refining them into structural resins and nutrient-rich mineral waxes.

To a Stria, the city is not miniature; it is a sprawling, echoing metropolis of canyon-like alleys and soaring glass monoliths, where a single falling bead of water possesses the mass of a boulder and the gossamer transit-cables hum like great suspended bridges in the storm.

Outside the obelisks, the ecosystem is sparse but specialized:

Along the trembling transit-lines live the *Ampullae*—tiny, semi-autonomous siphon-spiders no larger than a grain of sand. They anchor themselves to the micro-apertures, acting as living valves. When a bead of condensation shudders down the cable, an Ampulla extends a ring of silica cilia, swallowing the droplet, stripping it of its dissolved trace-salts, and injecting the purified water into the spire’s internal cisterns with a minute, rhythmic click.

In the deep, dark runoff shafts beneath the deck, where the water plunges into the planetary core, there are reports of blind, thread-like colonial organisms—luminescent filament-drifters that feed entirely on the kinetic turbulence of the draining water and the magnetic leakage of the thermal cells. They produce the soft, cyclical chimes heard through the floor plates: tiny acoustic signals meant to guide the tenders through the lightless, wet machinery of the foundation.

The Stria possess no concept of sky as an empty ceiling. To them, the outward-facing threshold—where the platform ends and the atmosphere drops into thousands of feet of pale blue void—is a sheer precipice into pure velocity. They occasionally gather at the brink, anchored to the alloy lip by heavy, magnetic tail-clasps, to watch the atomized water blow away into the sun. They read the weather not in days, but in the microsecond shudder of the wind against their antennae, watching the crystalline spray scatter outward like diamonds into the great, empty ocean of air that sustains them.
