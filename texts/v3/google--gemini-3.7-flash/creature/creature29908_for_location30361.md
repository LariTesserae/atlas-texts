In the seams and sumps of the basin, life has taken the steady, unvarying stroke of the mountain into its own tissues. Nothing here scurries; nothing displays the ragged, skittering urgency of creatures that must hunt before sunset or hoard against winter. Where time is measured strictly in bore-strokes and flywheel revolutions, biology has settled into an immense, rhythmic composure.

At the smallest visible scale, the machinery is tended by creatures that resemble broad, flattened chitons, though their segmented dorsal plates are not shell but solid, lamellar ironstone, grown in thick overlapping shingles. These scrapers—scarcely two fingers wide—creep along the surfaces of the titaniferous magnetite journals. Their undersides are fleshy, pale suction-tracts rimmed with thousands of microscopic, silica-tipped radulae. They feed neither on plant matter nor prey, but graze upon the colloidal film of rock-grease and the microscopic spall shed by the turning metal. Wherever a journal begins to score or bind, these creatures congregate in dense, mosaic tiles, smoothing the contact face with their wet, mineralizing secretions until the metal shines with the mirror-finish of a wet river pebble.

Deeper in the lubricating reservoirs, where the lithomarge weeps thick and cool from the fissures, live the oil-worms: translucent, ribbon-like annelids that reach the length of a man’s forearm. Lacking red blood, their bodies are filled with an amber fluid that has the same specific gravity and viscosity as the rock-oil itself. They thread through the clearance spaces between teeth and bushings, trailing fine plumes of mucus that catch stray grit and carry it away from the moving faces, dropping the sediment harmlessly into the still gravel of the floor.

The translucent amphibians that ride the reciprocating rods are neither frogs nor true salamanders, but long-bodied, blind lung-breathers known to rest in place for decades. Their skin is so clear that the slow, hydraulic pulsing of their two-chambered hearts can be counted against the four-stroke cycle of the basalt pistons. They possess no claws; their digits have widened into broad, pale discs rimmed with dense capillary beds that produce a perpetual vacuum seal against the oiled stone. When a basalt shaft drives outward, the creature is carried through the mist, its throat-sac distending to catch the condensing droplets of mineral water; when the shaft retracts into the dark, steaming bore of the cliff, the animal is drawn into the earth’s hot, compressed interior, absorbing the radiant geothermal warmth before being thrust back into the chill silver air. Their breeding is keyed to the subtle micro-variations in the piston’s travel: they lay clutches of single, glass-like spheres directly onto the polished iron connecting rods, where the rolling motion of the bearings massages the embryos through their long, motionless development without ever crushing them.

Above them, the soot-colored swifts do not build nests of twigs or mud. Their plumage is extraordinarily stiff, composed of barbs that interlock into dense, slate-like plates that shed oil and pressurized steam without softening. They roost shoulder to shoulder inside the exhaust ports of the stone cylinders. When the rock-valves clack open and a pulse of spent, high-pressure vapor vents into the basin, the birds do not fly; they lean into the blast, spreading their narrow, scythe-like wings to let the hot sulfurous air wash through their feathers, scouring them clean of parasites. When they do take flight, they move with a mechanical, locked-wing precision, cutting through the heavy mist in tight, geometric loops determined entirely by the acoustic standing waves generated by the flywheels. They feed on the wing, snatching at the clouds of tiny, blind midges that hover permanently over the static-blue ionization at the tips of the giant ferns.

In the rectangular sumps, where the rocker-arms plunge and rise with the steady cadence of walking giants, the blind lungfish drift in suspended stillness. Their skulls are solid casements of native nickel-iron, fused directly to their spines, allowing them to withstand the enormous hydraulic concussions that ring through the water whenever a sub-aquatic valve seats itself. They have lost all trace of eyes, their orbits overgrown by dense arrays of electro-receptive pits that map the basin in humming lines of force: they can feel the rotation of a drive-spindle three hundred yards away through the trembling of the water table. They feed on the thick, fibrous roots of the cog-ferns that dangle into the deep pools, shearing the tough, oil-slicked fronds with beak-like jaw plates that never cease to grow.

Moving among the spacious clearances of black gravel are larger, solitary forms—beasts that carry the silence of the high massif in their deliberate, four-legged tread. 

One of these is a creature the size of a bull, low-slung and barrel-ribbed, its hide resembling wet, gray flagstone overgrown with yellow lichen. It possesses no neck to speak of; its broad, blunt head is set squarely into its shoulders, armed with a downward-curving plate of thick horn that acts as a natural wedge. It walks a precise, unvarying circuit between the isolated drive-spindles, pausing at each moss-crowned megalith to rub the flanks of its armor against the spinning stone shafts, grinding away its excess skin and allowing the wet moss to seed itself into the deep grooves of its back. Its steps are perfectly cadenced; it will stand for hours beneath the swinging arc of a massive bronze counterweight, its breath rising in slow, rhythmic plumes that match the exhaust of the nearest vent, waiting for the exact split-second in the cycle when the clearance opens wide enough for it to step through without breaking stride.

There are no carcasses here. When one of these creatures reaches the end of its immense lifespan, it does not collapse or rot. It simply ceases to move during the inward stroke of a piston or wades into the deep, oil-filmed basin beneath a slow flywheel, allowing its heavy, mineral-dense tissues to be gathered into the mechanism’s turning clearances. The gears, turning with the inexorable, unhurried torque of the mountain itself, draw the body down into the deep journals, where it is slowly, seamlessly ground into the fine, lubricating silt that keeps the stone from wearing against the iron.

In this place, to live is simply to be a moving tolerance within an engine that has no master, no purpose, and no end. The birds do not sing—they whistle in the exact, pure pitch of venting steam; the amphibians do not croak—their throats click with the dry, hollow report of valve-stones; and the basin breathes on, an immaculate, self-regulating assembly of blood and basalt, turning together beneath the eternal, silver dusk.
