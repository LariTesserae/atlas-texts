Beneath the dense, shifting crust of the basin floor, the anvil-sized organisms—the *loricates* of the trench—do not possess organs in any mammalian or insectile sense. Underneath the matte slate of their dorsal shields, there is no wet cavity of soft viscera. If one of their shells were pried open with a cold chisel, the interior would reveal a solid, laminated block of tissue the color and density of vulcanized tire rubber, stratified with paper-thin leaves of silver-gray nerve leafing. 

They have no blood to spill. Their internal fluid is a heavy, clear dielectric oil, odorless and viscous, that circulates without a heart, propelled entirely by the mechanical compression of their own movements as they churn against one another.

Their ventral side is flat and smooth as a pressing iron, completely devoid of walking legs. Instead, they move by the minute, coordinated undulation of four longitudinal bands of hardened, chisel-like teeth made of pure calcium phosphate. These teeth do not walk so much as slowly mill the ballast beneath them, gripping the crushed granite with enough force to turn stone chips into a talc-fine powder over centuries. 

At the anterior margin, beneath a downturned visor of heavy bone, sits their feeding apparatus: not a jaw, but a wide, copper-sheathed maxillary brush. They press this brush directly against the cast-iron housings of the converters and the wet stone flags. When an individual reaches a grounding strap or the base of a live dynamo coil, it clamps down with an audible, magnetic snap. It does not eat matter; it drinks the trickle of stray electrical potential, its gray plates trembling in micro-seizures as the current charges the laminated cells within its back. When it has drawn its fill, the magnetic tension releases with a dull *clunk*, and the animal is shoved aside by the blind weight of the others behind it, drifting back into the churning drift of the herd.

Their breathing is purely thermodynamic. The wet, rhythmic coughing that fills the drydock is the sound of lateral vent-valves snapping open to expel the heat generated by their electrical intake. These exhausts release small, forceful jets of cold, perfectly sterile steam that condense immediately into the heavy mist hanging over their shells.

Above them, the telegraph wires support a very different order of life. 

The things roosting in the lattice towers and along the sagging copper cables are small—scarcely larger than a clenched fist—and have the dry, stretched appearance of pickled leather. Their bodies are teardrop-shaped, tapering into a stiff, counterbalancing spine of fused vertebrae that ends in a blunt ground-spike. They do not possess eyes, nostrils, or ears; their entire skull is a solid, forward-pointing cone of dense, ivory-like dentine that resonates in direct harmonic sympathy with the dynamos below.

Their limbs are nothing more than three pairs of articulated iron-black pincer-arms, jointed like watchmaker’s calipers. They clamp onto the wire with a grip so total that their claws have worn bright, polished silver notches into the copper strands over generations. They do not let go, even in sleep. When they move along the wires, they do not hop or flutter; they slide in swift, terrifyingly smooth glides, moving hand-over-hand with the metallic click of a typewriter carriage.

Their pulsating, featherless throats are translucent. In the flat, linen light, one can see inside the throat sac: a cluster of tiny, amber-colored mineral nodules that click rhythmically together. These nodules act as piezoelectric spark gaps; as the creatures breathe the tallow-rich mist, their throats generate faint, rhythmic clicks of blue static, snapping in direct tempo with the slap of the converter belts. 

They feed on the heavier creatures below without ever descending. From the underside of the cable clusters, these wire-clingers lower long, hair-fine filaments of spun keratin—slick with a cold, non-drying grease—that dangle fifty feet down into the moving herd. When an anvil-crab is crushed beneath an engine’s counterweight, the filaments catch in the gray paste, drawing the mineralized tallow upward by capillary action like oil in a lamp wick.

Inside the converters themselves, where the iron gears mesh under dozens of tons of mechanical pressure, there lives a third, almost invisible inhabitant: the *gear-lice*. 

These are wafer-thin discs of flexible, graphite-impregnated chitin, no thicker than a playing card and no broader than a copper coin. They live exclusively between the contact surfaces of the rotating teeth. Far from being crushed, they require the immense pressure of the iron teeth to survive; their flat bodies act as biological shims, absorbing the impact of the heavy machinery and secreting the very mineral tallow that keeps the journals from seizing. They reproduce by fissure under the direct blow of the counterweights, their flattened halves sliding out of the gear-pinch to ride the spinning flywheels like flecks of wet carbon.

There is no death here that leads to decay. The basin is an absolute closed circuit. What dies is instantly ground by the tooth-plates of the herd into the ballast; what is ground is absorbed by the grazing brushes; what rises as vapor is caught by the hanging filaments. 

The entire sunken trench functions as a single, cold, self-lubricating engine, where the metal parts and the flesh parts have long since forgotten that they were ever distinct from one another. The creatures do not know a sky beyond the white linen fog; they do not know a sound other than the rhythmic, shuddering thrum of the dynamos; they live out their centuries in the cold, wet dark of the slate backs, shifting and coughing in an eternal, perfect, iron-bound peace.
