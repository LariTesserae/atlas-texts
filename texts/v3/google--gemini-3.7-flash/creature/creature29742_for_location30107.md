The life that takes hold on the floor does not fight its flatness; it conforms to it, becoming broad, thin, and relentlessly horizontal.

At the smallest scale, within the first weeks of the mortar setting, the seams become conduits for a fauna that possesses almost no vertical profile. In the millimeter-wide expansion gaps and along the chamfered edges of the limestone, there live colonies of *plinth-ticks* and pale, ribbon-like silverfish that have abandoned the dark under-rock world for the geometric canyons of the grout. They are the color of dry slaked lime, nearly invisible against the seams. At night, when the floor cools and the maritime air condenses, these creatures spill outward from the joints in their millions, grazing on the film of cyanobacteria that forms in the slick sheen of the dew. They have flattened, spatula-shaped limbs that allow them to skate across surface tension without breaking the delicate sheets of water draining toward the rills. By the time the sun clears the horizon and the water flashes to steam, they have retreated back into the joints, sealing themselves into the hairline cracks with hardened secretions of lime-paste to survive the forty-degree noon.

In the shallow collection channels, where water lingers an hour longer than on the pavers, a specialized amphibian has already found a niche. They are called *cistern-skinks* or *gutter-runners*—attenuated, blind-nosed creatures no thicker than a man’s finger, with skin the exact terracotta-orange of the surrounding tile. They have no scales to catch on the masonry; instead, their bellies are coated in a dense, velvety friction-pad that allows them to slide effortlessly down the long, gentle inclines of the drainage rills. They live entirely within the channeled hydrology of the floor, riding the morning runoff down toward the subterranean holding vaults, feeding on the drowned moths and seed-pods of the wiry brassicas that blow in on the upper winds. 

Because there are no trees, boulders, or natural depressions to provide shade, larger animals have had to solve the problem of the midday glare through architectural symbiosis. 

Across the wide terraces roam the *carapace-gazelles*—tall, stilt-legged ungulates whose hooves are broad, circular plates of horn, capped with soft, fibrous pads that do not chip or click against the dressed limestone. Their legs are nearly two yards high, elevating their narrow, sun-bleached bodies into the faster, slightly cooler convection breezes that blow four feet above the baking floor. Their underbellies are mirrored with dense, silvered bristles that bounce the intense radiant heat of the terracotta back downward, preventing their organs from cooking in the updraft. 

These herds never break into a gallop; the friction of running on dry, unyielding stone would shear their joints. Instead, they move with a slow, pacing glide, their heads held low to the stone only during the first hour of light, when they lap the condensation sheets with wide, shovel-shaped tongues before the sun can evaporate their water supply. When the localized noon storms sweep across the plain, the herds orient themselves in perfect, parallel lines facing the downpour, letting the rain wash the lime-crust from their hides and funnelling the runoff into the skin-folds of their flanks, which lead directly to their mouths.

Higher up, riding the towering cumulus heads born of the terrace’s own vapor, are the *chalk-gliders*. These are immense, wide-winged raptors that almost never alight upon the ground. Their wingspans are narrow and rigid, engineered for endless, effortless soaring along the knife-edge thermal walls created where the white limestone meets the heat-absorbent red terracotta. To a chalk-glider looking down, the floor is an immense checkerboard of rising and sinking air currents. They do not hunt by diving at the ground—a misjudged stoop against the unforgiving stone would be fatal. Instead, they trail the convective dust-devils, snatching up the brassica seeds, insects, and dead gutter-runners that are hoisted into the sky by the violent midday thermals.

And then there are the people who tend the seam.

They are few, scattered in small custodial retinues across thousands of leagues, living not *on* the floor, but *within* the drainage cisterns and maintenance culverts that were cast beneath it during the great pouring. 

To see one emerge is like watching the stone itself give birth. At dawn, square limestone plugs—indistinguishable from the surrounding pavers except for a single, finger-sized lifting hole—tilt upward on counterweighted stone pins. The cistern-dwellers rise into the pale, misty air to meet the water. 

They are a tall, quiet people, their limbs dusted permanently with a fine bloom of white ash and pink ceramic powder. They wear no leather or cloth, which would rot in the morning damp and bake to brittle parchment by afternoon; their garments are made of woven glasswort fiber and thin, overlapping scales of kiln-fired slip-clay sewn together with mineral-treated sinew. On their feet they wear thick, wide sandals carved from soft, porous volcanic pumice, which absorb the morning dew as they walk, keeping their soles continuously cooled through evaporative action as the heat rises later in the day.

Their entire culture is an elaboration of geometry and maintenance. They carry no metal blades or heavy timber, only long-handled wooden floats, bone scrapers, and wide, shallow troughs of fresh lime putty. They walk the hairline seams with a slow, meditative tread, scanning the immense flatness for the slightest imperfection—a spalled brick edge, an irregular pool where water has stagnated rather than draining toward the cistern lips, a brassica root that threatens to pry open a mortar bed.

When they find a flaw, they work in utter silence, their movements rhythmic and unhurried. They scrape away the salt crust, wet the stone with water drawn from their waist-flasks, and pack the relief joints with fresh paste, smoothing it down until the tool leaves the exact same wooden-grain signature that was stamped there when the world was finished. 

They do not speak above a low murmur, for on this vast, unhindered stone plain, the human voice carries for miles, a thin, lonely sound that shatters against the horizon. Instead, they communicate across distances by striking the flagstones with tuned ceramic mallets. A single, sharp strike on a hollow limestone vault sends a pure, bell-like tone humming through the horizontal bedrock, traveling leagues through the floor itself to be felt through the pumice-shod feet of another warden leagues away—a dry, ringing pulse that says the water has cleared, the rills are open, and the stone is whole.
