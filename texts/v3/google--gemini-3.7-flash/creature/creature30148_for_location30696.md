The people who live among the spires do not walk so much as they anchor. 

Their bodies have evolved or adapted—over generations of living on ninety-degree inclines—into low-slung, broad-shouldered forms with short, densely muscled calves and wide, spatulate feet. They move with an economy of motion so pronounced it can look like slow-motion rehearsal: a hand is never lifted from a safety rail until a foot has tested the grit of a wet iron tread; a harness clip is never shifted until the secondary tether is taut.

Because the rock never cools and the air never truly dries, their skin is perpetually slicked with a fine, briny film of sweat and mineral condensation. It gives their flesh a pale, boiled translucence, through which the blue branchings of veins show clearly. To protect themselves from the searing basalt and the raw friction of boiler plate, their hands and knees are covered in thick, yellowish calluses that feel like cured leather. They wear very little woven fabric—linen and cotton rot too swiftly in the endless lukewarm steam—relying instead on split-hide aprons, oiled leather wraps, and boots with soles made from layers of vulcanized gum and beaten lead, which mold to the wet bolt-heads and provide grip on the greasy volcanic glass.

In the vertical city, there are no voices raised in anger or excitement. Shouting is considered a dereliction of discipline; high-frequency sounds bounce erratically off the sheer obsidian walls, distorted by the rising heat-ripples. Instead, the inhabitants speak in low, breathy registers, their dialects stripped of hard consonants, leaning heavily into guttural vowels and chest-resonances that travel cleanly through the humid air without echoing. 

Between tiers, they communicate through the iron itself. Every worker carries a *tapper*—a short baton of solid copper with a lead core. By striking the massive catenary cables or the webbed ribs of the gantries in specific rhythmic meters, they can send messages humming through the alloy framework for miles. A dweller three kilometers down a spire can press their cheek to an iron stanchion and feel, through bone-conduction, the arrival of a freight lift, the shift of a thermal vent, or the death of a neighbor.

Their diet is narrow, dense, and entirely derived from the chemotrophic ecology of the heat-vents and runnels. 

Fastened along the undersides of the lowest catwalks, where the runoff condensation drips steadily onto hot iron pans, the inhabitants cultivate thick, rubbery mats of sulfur-eating bacteria and ferric yeasts. These mats grow in dense, liver-colored slabs that are scraped free with brass spades, pressed into cakes to squeeze out the bitter mineral brine, and cured over steam vents. The resulting food is gray, dense as tallow, and smells faintly of bruised iron and warm rain, but it provides the heavy caloric energy needed to work in the high-heat, high-altitude air.

They supplement this with the pale, shelled grazers—which they call *plate-bugs* or *chime-ticks*. These creatures, some as small as a palm, others the size of a furnace lid, are harvested with long hooked poles from the dark gaps where the iron pilings bite into the basalt. Their meat is white, fibrous, and tastes strongly of chalk and copper, while their heavy, segmented shells are ground down to create an abrasive paste used to polish valve seats and scour rust from the high-pressure seals.

Higher up, in the open voids between the needles where the rising heat plumes sheer violently against the freezing troposphere, lives a different kind of beast: the *drift-skates*. These are wide, leathery creatures with no eyes and no legs, consisting almost entirely of ribbed, parchment-thin mantles stretched across cartilage frames. They do not flap; they simply hang in the rising thermal chimneys, banking lazily on air currents that would tear a bird apart. They feed on the aerosolized nutrient-salts and microscopic spore-forms carried aloft in the steam columns, their wide, slit-like ventral mouths filtering the mist like whales in an aerial sea. 

To the people of the iron collars, the drift-skates are navigation marks. If the skates bank suddenly downward, it signals a cold-air intrusion from the upper atmosphere—a sudden downdraft that will flash-cool the outer gantries, causing the iron to shriek and buckle as it contracts against the unyielding, superheated stone.

Daily life here is measured not by day and night—for the sun is often merely a bright, diffuse pearl behind the mist, and the spires glow with their own dim, infrared heat—but by the *Stroke*. 

The great hydraulic pistons that balance the tension between the needle-cities operate on three-hour cycles. When the master piston at the apex of the central spire completes its upward stroke, the relief valves exhale. The sound is not loud, but it is immense: a soft, world-filling *whoosh* of warm vapor that washes down the face of the rock, cleansing the walkways of grit and signaling the change of work-shifts. 

When the Stroke sighs over them, the workers on the catwalks pause. They wipe the lukewarm water from their eyes with the backs of their scarred wrists, check the tension on their tethers, and watch the endless drops fall away beneath their boots—vanishing down into the shimmering, bottomless heat between the needles, where the world is still being made.
