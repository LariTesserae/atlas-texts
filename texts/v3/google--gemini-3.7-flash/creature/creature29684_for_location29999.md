At the lowest level, where the water is thick with boiler-scale and pulverized slate, the court belongs to things that graze upon tallow. 

The grease cups on the older eccentric shafts are fed with heavy animal fats—rendered mutton and tallow packed into brass cylinders—which seep down the cold iron faces in long, pale runners before reaching the sumps. In these slicks live colonies of flat, shell-less gastropods, nearly five inches long and translucent as scalded lard. Their bodies take the yellow stain of mineral oil and sulfur. They do not move against the current; they settle into the slipways where the eccentric collars turn, their broad, undulating skirts pressed flat against the polished bronze to lick the warm lubricating residue as it shears away from the steel. The slow rotation of the shafts does not dislodge them; it simply rolls under them, carrying a fresh film of fat into their mouthparts every seven seconds.

Beneath the diamond-grated floor, in the drown-ways where light only enters as broken squares of gold, there are blind river-carps that have forgotten the sun. They have blunt, spade-like skulls, thickened with calloused skin from years of nosing into the suction-screens to strip away the water-cabbages that clog the intake ports. They have grown deep-bodied and slab-sided on the warm outflow from the condenser ducts, their scales clouded over with a soft, green pelt of algae that makes them indistinguishable from the submerged masonry until they turn. When the primary displacement pistons reach the bottom of their stroke, the sudden vacuum draws these fish against the intake grates with a dull, rubbery *clump*; they wait out the backpressure with an ancient, sluggish patience, then drift free on the exhaust surge to root in the silt beds.

Higher up, where the warm mist gathers against the brick flues, the space is inhabited by people, though they have changed their habits to suit the court.

There are never more than three or four of them—the *tenders* or the *oil-minders*. They are entirely deaf to human speech, their inner ears long since flattened by the fifty-year roar of the displacement chambers and the percussive iron heartbeat that travels through the soles of their bare feet. They do not wear boots; boots rot within a month in the sluice-court, turning into pulpy cakes of fungus. Instead, their soles are thick, white, and ridged like the skin of turnip roots, deeply creased from gripping the wet iron lattice and the slimy edges of the weir boxes.

These minders live not in rooms, but in the dry pockets—the narrow, baking voids between the back of the masonry boilers and the retaining wall, where the air is dry enough to cure ham and the stone stays at a steady hundred degrees. There, they sling hammocks of oiled hemp that smell of mutton-fat and dried fern. 

Their days are governed not by clocks or daylight, but by the stroke-count of the three walking beams. Because they cannot hear the pinhole leaks or the dry squeal of an unlubricated bearing through the air, they carry *listening-rods*—lengths of cold-rolled iron five feet long, tipped with boxwood balls. A minder moves along the narrow catwalks with the slow, sliding gate of a barge-horse, leaning out over the machinery to press the boxwood end of the rod directly into his cheekbone, right against the jaw joint, while touching the iron tip to a valve chest or a cross-head guide. Through the bone of the skull, the internal state of the engine arrives with terrifying clarity: the scrape of a dry piston-ring sounds like gravel dragged through teeth; the healthy, water-cushioned seating of a brass poppet valve arrives as a clean, deep ring that vibrates straight into the gums.

Between the minders and the court's other creatures, there is no hostility, only a mutual reliance on the machine's topography. 

A minder will step carefully over a four-foot whip-snake that has coiled itself into the warm recess of a rocker-arm pillow block to shed its skin. The snake does not strike; it is dulled and heavy with the boiler heat, its belly full of the giant, waterlogged wood-roaches that crawl from the joints of the decaying cedar lagging. The minder simply reaches past the dry, papery coils with a long-spouted copper oiler, drizzles three measures of heavy cylinder stock into the oil-hole, and moves on, his bare heel leaving a pale, steaming print on the wet flagstone that vanishes under condensing mist within thirty seconds.

In the upper tier of the aerator, where the water is flung into the air and gathers the most light, a species of small, wingless water-cricket has taken over the underside of the iron pans. They build nests from the fiber stripped from the aerial fig roots, cementing the threads together with boiler-lime and spit. Thousands of them live in the warm underside of the fifth tier, sheltered from the spray by the curve of the lip, feeding on the microscopic debris that tumbles down from the garden canopy above. When the walking beams pause—an event that happens perhaps twice in a generation, when a main valve hangs on its seat—the sudden cessation of the rhythm causes the crickets to drop from the pans by the tens of thousands into the water below, like a sudden, rattling shower of dried peas, where the carp rise through the grating to take them before the pumps can draw their first, gasping restart.

The minders know this. When an engine stumbles, they do not look at the gauges. They look down into the dark water between their toes to see if the crickets are falling, their fingers already resting on the iron ladders, waiting for the stone beneath them to tell them where the stroke has died.
