The builders of the high basket-city are long-bodied and pale, their forms shaped entirely by an existence where falling is impossible and resting is merely a matter of finding a loop of cord to hook an elbow through. 

They are known to themselves through vibration and pressure rather than names called aloud. In anatomy, they are amphibious mammals that have long surrendered the dry world, though they still carry ancestral echoes of it: broad, spade-like ribcages that hold massive, slow-beating hearts, and four long limbs ending in five elongated, double-jointed fingers. Their skin is smooth, hairless, and the color of wet river-clay, but over their palms, forearms, and the soles of their splayed feet, it thickens into tough, horn-like pads as dense as sole-leather. These calluses are self-renewing and cut-resistant—an absolute necessity in a world where every outcropping of fresh basalt has the edge of a newly flaked razor.

They do not swim with the frantic, whipping motions of shallow-water fish. In this warm, heavy soup, they scull with deliberate, snake-like undulations of their spines, steering with wide, webbed wrists. Their necks are thick and ringed with three pairs of muscular branchial slits that flare and close like the gills of a manta, drawing in the silicate broth and passing it through rich, vascular sponges that strip it of dissolved oxygen and trace minerals. Because the water is warm as blood, their metabolism is slow, languid, and untroubled by the cold of typical abyssal depths.

Their eyes are large, lidless spheres of polished amber, protected by clear, gelatinous shields. They see little in the way of sharp form across long distances, for the emerald gloom scatters light after twenty cubits; instead, their vision is tuned to movement, bioluminescence, and the pale, thermal waverings of the vent chimneys. Above their brows and along the lateral lines of their flanks run clusters of sensory pits—nerve-rich depressions that read the tectonic ticks of the settling floor, the strain of a fraying rope three terraces away, or the low, rhythmic thrum of an approaching neighbor.

### The Life of the Basket-Dwellers

Within the woven tiers, life is an endless, rhythmic maintenance of tension. The people are weavers first, stone-splitters second. From childhood, they learn to gauge the pull of a kelp-line with the soles of their feet, knowing by the pitch of its vibration whether the anchor-stone below has shifted in the volcanic gravel.

They live in communal clutches inside the multi-tiered cages. Because gravity exerts so little tyranny here, their homes have no floors in the terrestrial sense—only layers of springy, braided webbing where they sleep tangled together in loose knots, buoyed by the natural lift of their oil-rich livers. They wear no fabric, for woven cloth would rot and drag in the dense water, but they ornament their bodies with patterns etched directly into their thick palm-calluses, or by looping fine, polished rings of vent-shrimp carapace through the cartilage of their gill-crests.

Their speech is felt rather than heard across distance. In their throats, they possess a paired set of resonant air-sacs that never vent to the outside; by contracting muscular walls around these chambers, they produce profound, sub-audible pulses that travel for miles through the dense water. A mother can soothe an infant drifting three chambers away with a rhythmic, low-frequency purr that vibrates directly into the child’s skull-bones. For close, domestic conversation, they use rapid, fluttering clicks made with their hard, enamel-sheathed teeth, accompanied by swift, intricate sign-languages flickered out by their long pale fingers.

Their tool-culture is entirely lithic and organic:
*   **Adzes and splitters** chipped from heavy, black volcanic glass, bound to handles of fossilized bone with boiled vine-sinew.
*   **Needles and bodkins** carved from the dense, mineralized plates of dead grazers, used to lace the broad vascular ribbons into roof-mats.
*   **Drift-nets and baskets** woven so fine they can trap the falling gray volcanic silt, which they mix with plant-slime to create a heavy, paste-like ballast used to weigh down the buoyant kelp-timber frames.

### Commensals and Domesticated Life

The city is not inhabited by the weavers alone; it is an entire, nested biome of mutualisms.

Living within the weaves of every dwelling are colonies of **Ash-Menders**—blind, many-legged arthropods the size of a human palm, with translucent, porcelain-white carapaces. These creatures are never harmed; they are welcomed into the lattice like domestic bees. They feed upon the constant snowfall of gray ash and decaying kelp fibers that settle on the roofs. In digesting this debris, they exude a sticky, calcium-rich mortar that hardens underwater, gradually turning the perishable braided vines into rigid, stone-hard lattice. An old dwelling is no longer held together by plant-cord, but by the petrified, mineralized trails of generations of Menders.

Suspended beneath the floating communal halls hang clusters of **Lantern-Polyps**—colonial siphonophores gathered from the open basin and tethered by their anchoring stems to the wicker underbellies. These creatures emit a steady, cold, greenish-gold luminescence, fueled by the rich plumes of organic broth rising from the vents below. They serve as the hearth-fires of the city. Beneath their glow, weavers gather to work, their long pale bodies illuminated in the emerald dusk as they split slate and splice vine.

At the bottom of the terraces, where the settlement meets the sharp obsidian floor, the people manage herds of **Pavement-Skates**—the heavy, slate-skinned grazers of the young basalt. These beasts are as wide as river barges, flat-bodied and armored with overlapping plates of mineralized shell that scrape against the glass-rock with a dry, shearing rattle. They possess no eyes, navigating entirely by scent and the heat of the floor. Their blunt, chisel-like jaws can shear through an inch of solid basalt to reach the rich endolithic liverworts growing inside the cooling cracks. 

The weavers guide these leviathans using long goads tipped with burning-hot vent-stone, steering them across newly cooled lava fields to "mow" and terrace the rock, leveling the shelves before new timber-frames are erected. When an old skate dies, every fragment of its carcass is utilized: its heavy, shell-like teeth become adze-heads, its thick hide is cured into impermeable roofing leather, and its buoyant, oil-filled spinal canal provides fuel and waterproofing fat.

### The Wild Periphery

Beyond the city’s tethered perimeter, the basin slopes away into shimmering thermal canyons where the water grows even thicker, clouded with pale clouds of dissolved sulfur and iron.

Here live the **Plume-Worms**, colossal tube-dwelling organisms that anchor themselves directly into the mouths of the volcanic chimneys. Their carapaces are tubes of black, fibrous chitin that grow twenty cubits tall, rising like fluted organ pipes through the hot current. From their crests, they deploy vast, feathery fans of brilliant crimson tissue, filtering the mineral broth. The weavers dive into these thermal plumes—risking severe scalding—to harvest the shed chitin tubes, which they use as lightweight, hollow beams for their highest floating towers.

Drifting through the open water above the spires are the **Sieve-Bells**: solitary, translucent creatures the size of haystacks, moving with slow, hydraulic pulses that occur only once every few minutes. They are nearly invisible, identifiable only by the pale emerald light refracting through their gelatinous bells and the long, beaded fishing-lines they trail through the water column. They are neither hunted nor feared; they drift through the city’s upper wicker-works like slow, benevolent clouds, occasionally becoming tangled in the moorings until a weaver swims up to gently cut them free.

### The Cycle of Return

Death among the basket-dwellers is integrated into the architecture itself. 

When a weaver dies, they are not buried, for there is no soft earth, nor are they cast adrift to float to the distant, forbidden surface. Instead, the body is wrapped in thick mats of live liverwort, bound with green sea-vines, and weighted with a heavy nodule of iron-basalt. The family carries the bundle down to the deepest fracture-lines near the thermal vents.

There, placed in the warm, mineral-thick upwelling, the creeping plants do not rot; they take root immediately in the flesh and the stone alike, fueled by the volcanic heat. Within a few months, the body has been entirely converted into a living, dense hillock of root-mat and hardened silt—a new, solid anchoring-point of basalt and bone, ready to receive the guy-lines of the next generation's home. 

In this way, the city never leaves its dead behind; it literally builds its heights upon them, tethering its floating sky to the bones of its past, rising higher into the emerald dusk as the mountain beneath continues to tick, cool, and grow.
