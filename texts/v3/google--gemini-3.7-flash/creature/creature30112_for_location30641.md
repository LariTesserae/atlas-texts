To live here is to be made of grease, grit, and tendon.

The people of the shelf—the *Vane-folk*, though they rarely call themselves anything other than *we who cling*—are short, dense-muscled, and low-slung, with wide, flat feet whose outer toes are long and distinctly articulate from generations of gripping notched ironwood and fraying vine-ropes. Their skin has the texture and dull sheen of tallow-rubbed goatskin, deeply folded at the joints, perpetually coated in a fine patina of ground beetle-shell and the golden flour of flailed seed-husks. They do not sweat as lowlanders might; their bodies are miserly with fluids. Instead, they give off a dry, baking heat, their breath smelling faintly of mastic resin and the bitter, toasted marrow of sun-dried locusts.

Their eyes have adapted to the amber gloom: the irises are huge, the color of wet river slate or cloudy amber, shielded by double rows of thick, interlocking lashes that sift out the floating chaff. When they step to the edge of the shelf to look out over the blinding white flats, they draw across their faces thin, polished slats of bone pierced with hair-thin horizontal slits, moving along the lip like blind seers guided by the wind on their bare shoulders.

They are rarely still, but they never rush. In an atmosphere so parched, a frantic movement is a waste of water. Their gait along the suspended gangways is a sliding, rhythmic shuffle, their bare soles testing the give of the woven fiber before putting down their weight. They speak in low, percussive palettes—glottal clicks, dry tongue-snaps, and soft, air-pinched consonants—that do not compete with the continuous roar of the roosts above them, but travel cleanly along the resonant limestone walls. 

Among them move the beasts, though the distinction between wild animal, vermin, and kin has blurred into something purely functional.

Underfoot, sharing the straw-matted floors of the mastic-plastered huts, are the *cushion-mice*—compact, round-bodied rodents with tails as thick and solid as sausages, swollen with stored metabolic fat. They are entirely docile, blinded by generations in the mid-tiers, and serve multiple roles: they clean the grain dust from the woven floorboards with their velvet snouts, their fur is sheared with flint shears to twist into bowstrings and caulking, and on the coldest nights, when the updrafts from the flats turn sharp, families sleep with three or four of them tucked beneath their hide wraps like living embers. When a cushion-mouse grows old and its tail begins to thin, it is dropped into a lidded clay pot with dried wild onion-bulbs and rendered slowly for lamp-fat and skin-salve; its skull, no larger than an acorn, is bound into the fringe of a child’s rattle.

Higher up, along the underside of the wicker bridges and clinging to the vertical rock faces with clawed, suction-rimmed toes, live the *drop-lizards*. Long, narrow, and colored like wet birch bark, they possess double-jointed jaws and pale, milky eyes that never blink. They are neither hunted nor petted; they are colleagues. A basket-weaver sitting on a ledge will pause mid-stitch to let a four-foot lizard scramble across her lap in pursuit of a fist-sized wood-wasp. The lizards keep the timber-boring grubs from devouring the structural supports of the lower quarters; in return, the people leave shallow stone depressions filled with crushed beetle-mash along the rooflines to keep the reptiles from snapping at sleeping infants.

The ceiling belongs to the *cloak-swifts* and the *soft-ears*. 

The swifts do not build with mud, for there is none, but with their own sticky, proteinaceous saliva, gluing together layers of shed lichen and moulted rodent fur until the underside of the rock ceiling is crusted in thousands of hard, gray pockets. They leave the shelf at dawn in great, screeching clouds that plunge out into the heat shimmers over the flats to harvest wind-blown spiders and high-altitude gnats, returning in the evening with crop-sacks full of water-rich pulp. The town’s boys climb the highest vine-tackles with long reed scoops, not to take the birds themselves, but to hook the dried, amber cakes of concentrated bird-lime that accumulate beneath the colonies, which the elders bake into fireless, slow-smoldering heating stones.

Intertwined with the bird roosts are the bats—broad, pale-furred creatures that hang in shuddering masses sixty feet above the highest platforms. The heat rising from their clustered bodies is perceptible twenty feet below: a sweet, suffocating draft of hot mammal-sweat and digested seed-pulp that wafts down in steady waves. 

Nothing that dies inside the fissure is ever cast out over the edge.

When a person reaches the end of their breath, their body is not committed to the earth, for the stone beneath them is solid and ancient. Instead, they are laid out in the highest, hottest alcoves near the ceiling, where the air is driest and the draft from the roosts is strongest. There, in a matter of weeks, the body is completely desalted and cured into hard, dark leather by the warm updrafts, preserved by the same resin-heavy smoke that cures the ironwood beams. 

Once cured, they are brought down. Their long bones are split to make the shuttle-pins for the looms and the rung-notches for the hanging ladders; their hair is spun with vine-bark to reinforce the main suspension cables that support the central plazas; and their flattened, dry ribs are carefully stitched into the undercarriages of the huts, providing the light, springy ribs upon which the next generation builds its wicker floors. 

In this way, the town is not merely built *upon* the cliff; it is made of those who have lived there, bound together by the sap of living vines and the slow, unending work of teeth, flint, and claw. There is no loneliness in the shelf; every floorboard creaks with the weight of someone remembered, and the air one breathes has already passed through a thousand pairs of lungs, warm, dry, and thick with the dust of the plateau.
