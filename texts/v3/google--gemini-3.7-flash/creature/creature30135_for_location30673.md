To survive here, flesh must abandon the ambition of softness. Nothing living in the basin possesses red blood or moist skin; such things would burst or bake to jerky before the first midday hour had run its course. The biology of the plateau is a chemistry of vitreous glazes, hot oils, concentrated alkalis, and calcium sheets. Life here does not rot when it dies; it calcifies, spalls in the dry wind, and is ground down by the tread of its descendants into the very silt that paves the sluices.

### The Floor-Scourers and Sluice-Lattice Dwellers

At the base of the food web are the porcelain-plated grazers—the *lithophages* that swarm the flats in millimeter-thin layers of boiling brine. Up close, their carapaces are not true chitin, but laminated aragonite, polished like glazed tableware and veined with threads of bright green malachite where they have metabolized the copper salts leaching from the machinery. They have no eyes; their broad, disc-like bodies are ringed with hundreds of thermal pits that read the shifting heat-fronts of the water.

They move on thousands of minute, vitreous cilia that sound like gravel stirred in a bucket as they scrape across the pavement. Beneath their bellies, rotating discs of quartz-hard denticles shear away the newly formed mineral crusts, preventing the shallow pans from choking on their own salt. When two plates meet, they do not fight; they grind their rims together with a low, dry friction, transferring oily clusters of mineral-coated cysts from one underbelly to the other. When they die, their carapaces simply settle into the travertine floor, fusing with the limestone within days, becoming part of the endless masonry.

Deep within the submerged footings of the kiln towers, where the water is dark and starved of air, the glass-eels thrive in the shadow of the great hydraulic pistons. These creatures are longer than a man’s arm, completely colorless, and clear as poured resin—their internal organs visible only as small, amber-colored nodes encased in oily bladders. They do not swim with undulating grace; they anchor themselves with hooked, lead-resistant barbs to the iron bolts and feed on the thick, yellow saponified grease that oozes from the packing glands of the pumps. Their bodies are saturated with heavy mineral salts that act as a biological antifreeze in reverse, keeping their clear tissues liquid even when the surrounding brine reaches temperatures that would melt lead solder.

### The Skimmers of the Terraces

The three-meter stilt-walkers—the *skimmers*—are the basin’s most conspicuous architects. Their bones are not marrow-filled, but structured like pumice: porous, rigid, and entirely composed of sintered tricalcium phosphate. Their long, needle-thin legs do not puncture the flagstones; their feet terminate in wide, flat pads of vulcanized connective tissue that distribute their weight so evenly they barely break the surface tension of the millimeter-deep brine.

Their great dorsal sails are marvels of thermoregulation. Held aloft by rib-struts of hollow bone, these sheets of translucent, parchment-like hide are laced with counter-current vascular networks carrying a clear, copper-based fluid. When a skimmer wades through a boiling channel, its sail blushes a deep, bruised violet as excess core heat is radiated out into the thin mountain atmosphere. When cold winds sweep down from the upper glaciers at twilight, the sails turn milky and opaque, folding tightly against their narrow, keel-shaped torsos like umbrella ribs.

Skimmers are filter-feeders of a peculiar sort. They do not eat plants or meat; their long, beakless muzzles are fitted with interlocking ceramic sieves. As they stride through the shallows, they dip their heads into the rising steam-vents, catching the thick, sulfurous lipid-blooms produced by colonies of extremophile archaea that coat the interior of the boiling pipes. They nest exclusively on the hot brickwork at the summits of the kiln towers. Their eggs are spherical, thick-walled flasks of raw chalcedony, laid directly upon the steaming iron flue-grates, where the unceasing heat incubates them over months of blinding sun.

### The Air-Plume Kites

High above the distillation towers, where the ascending columns of white vapor meet the razor-thin cold of the five-thousand-meter sky, dwell the *plume-serpents*. They are creatures of pure buoyancy, ten to twenty meters in length, their bodies constructed of segmented, gas-filled bladders skinned in a mirror-bright, metallic foil that deflects the brutal ultraviolet radiation of the high plateau.

These aerial ribbons have no wings. They maintain their altitude by drifting over the thermal updrafts of the boiling vents, adjusting their trim with rows of tiny, muscular exhaust-pores along their flanks. Their undersides are coated in a perpetual, sticky film of alkaline wax. As the steam carrying microscopic particles of sublimated zinc, sulfur, and carbonate dust billows past them, the minerals stick to their bellies. Periodically, a serpent will loop through its own length, using a comb-like mouthpart at the tip of its head to scrape its own underbelly clean, swallowing the caked mineral harvest.

They never land. They mate in mid-air, intertwining like long strands of tinsel caught in a dust devil, and their young are born alive as tiny, needle-thin threads of foil that immediately seek the warmth of the nearest chimney plume.

### The Sluice-Wardens (The Keepers of the Lattice)

In the rare, dry spaces—inside the hollow voids of the great kiln towers, or perched upon the narrow iron catwalks above the distillation manifolds—dwell the largest and most enigmatic residents of the plateau. Whether they were once men who adapted to this machine-desert or beings that evolved alongside the apparatus, they are now inseparable from it.

They are broad, low-slung humanoids whose skin has taken on the texture and hue of dry shale. They wear no fabric, for cloth rots or burns in the alkali spray; instead, their bodies are smeared with a pale, crusting paste of gypsum and tallow that they renew daily from the mineral vats. Their eyes are shielded by double eyelids: a heavy outer fold of leathery skin, and an inner, transparent nictitating membrane that is naturally tinted dark amber to filter the blinding glare of the bleached travertine.

These Wardens do not speak. The damp, thin air swallows sound, so they communicate through vibrations sent through the ironwork. By striking the aqueducts and conduits with heavy, bronze-capped staves, they send sharp, ringing pulses through the submerged grid—signals that travel for miles through the metal, reporting blockages in the sluices, the failure of a counterweight, or the shifting of a brine-vein deep in the limestone.

Their diet is derived entirely from the system: they tap the clear, distilled water from the high condenser coils, and they harvest the dense, rubbery mats of sulfur-eating molds that line the interiors of the low-pressure steam conduits. They use heavy iron hooks to clear jammed skimmer-bones from the intake grates, and they guide the drifting herds of plate-arthropods toward newly encrusted channels to ensure the water keeps moving.

They live long, dry, quiet lives. When a Warden grows old and their joints stiffen with lime deposits, they climb down into the deep settling vats beneath the central manifolds. There, in the warm, quiet dark where the heavy minerals settle before distillation, they allow the water to take them. Within weeks, the brine encases them in a brilliant, sparkling crust of calcite crystals, preserving their forms forever as new cornerstones in the foundations of the sun-drowned city.
