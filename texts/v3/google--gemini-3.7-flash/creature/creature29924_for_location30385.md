To live on the plateau is to live within an acoustic vacuum, and so the creatures here have surrendered the voice entirely. They are beings of pure surface: mirror-backed, glass-skinned, oil-sheened, built to translate the fierce light into language, shelter, and heat.

The herds that graze the stone terraces—the *lithophages*—move with the synchronized grace of oil on water. Their six legs do not step so much as glide on broad, fibrous cushions saturated with a natural lipid that prevents the polished sandstone from grinding their joints to powder. Because the air carries no warning cries, their survival rests in their skin. Their flat, overlapping scutes are grown from layered aragonite and organic prisms, capable of shifting from a blinding, sun-deflecting chalk-white to deep, saturated hues in fractions of a second. 

When a herd feeds, its communication is a continuous, fluid cinema. A solitary bull at the perimeter, catching the high-contrast twitch of a predator between the rocks, does not roar; he tilts his flank thirty degrees toward the sun and flashes a hard, rhythmic pulse of cobalt blue. Within a heartbeat, the signal cascades through three hundred bodies—a sudden, blinding ripple of violet and gold that rolls across the terrace like a thrown silk sheet. The herd shifts as one organism, sliding away from the danger zone, their soft pads whispering across the pavement with no more noise than dry grass brushing stone. 

Beneath their pearlescent carapaces, their biology is calibrated for extreme conservation. They do not sweat or pant; their respiratory slits, tucked safely beneath the rim of their lower shell plates, open only during the deepest cool of the night. Their mouths are wide, horizontal rasps of black diamond-chitin, sweeping back and forth across the stone runnels to sheer away the paper-thin sheets of red cyanobacteria that coat every damp surface.

Where the grazers keep to the open plains, the needle-towers themselves possess their own microscopic, vertical biome. Climbing the matte, light-devouring surfaces are colonies of *vessel-mites*—creatures no larger than a grain of sand, with bodies made of semi-flexible silica. They live entirely within the micro-climatic boundary layer of the towers, where the temperature plunges toward freezing and the air is perpetually choked with distilling vapor. They anchor themselves in tiny, interlocking chains, forming living, hair-thin aqueducts that guide the cold water downward, skimming dissolved atmospheric salts and microscopic airborne spore-dust before the runnels reach the stone below.

High above the plain, carving the deadened air, the gliders dwell in an architecture of invisible forces. Their wings—vast, semi-translucent sails of amber cartilage and stretched tendon—do not possess the musculature for flapping. If grounded, a glider is clumsy, dragging its light-boned chassis across the rock like a beached kite. But aloft, they are masters of the subterranean anomalies. 

The gliders possess organ-ridges along their snouts that glow faintly with piezoelectric friction, allowing them to *feel* the geometric conduits beneath the plateau—the invisible plumes of localized antigravity and thermal uplift that leak upward from the buried roots of the needle-towers. They ride these invisible pillars of force to the very edge of the stratosphere, circling for hours without a single expenditure of metabolic energy. 

When they drink, they dive: falling in a sheer, vertical stoop that exceeds two hundred kilometers per hour, pulling out at the last fraction of a second to skim a stone basin. Their lower jaw, elongated into an elastic, hair-thin siphon, cuts the water surface like a razor, drawing up several liters in a microsecond before the glider’s momentum catches the next thermal spine and carries it back into the white sky. They lay their single, spherical eggs in the basalt hollows atop the highest mesas, coating them in a reflective, leaden lacquer that shields the developing embryo from the raw solar glare.

In the interstitial spaces—the rubble of fallen sandstone and the shade of the wire-bushes—the hunters keep their vigil. The stutter-stalkers are long, reedy quadrupeds whose muscles do not contract smoothly; instead, their limbs utilize a biomechanical ratchet system, storing elastic energy in heavy, coiled tendon-spools until a latch-joint releases. To an outside observer, they appear to flicker through space. A hunter stands frozen behind a basalt boulder; an instant later, without the eye tracking the transit, it stands ten paces closer. 

They possess no lungs in the mammalian sense, breathing through passive spiracles that eliminate the rhythmic heave of flanks that might betray their presence. When a stalker strikes, its jaw snaps forward on a telescoping neck-strut, crushing the head of a grazer with a dry, muffled *clack* that dies the moment it leaves the bone. They feed quickly, drinking the cool, lipid-rich blood before the dry sun can evaporate the moisture, then dissolve back into the pale apricots and dark basalts of the plateau.

Yet the truest witnesses to the plateau’s antiquity are the four-eyed quadrupeds sheltered beneath the fallen monolith. 

They are heavy-bodied, slow-metabolizing creatures whose ancestors must have known the builders of the towers. Their velvet-coated ears are broad, spoon-shaped resonators turned perpetually toward the earth, monitoring the faint, deep-frequency hum of the planetary machinery buried miles below the crust. They do not hunt the grazers, nor do they fear the stutter-stalkers; their skin secretes a bitter, chalky compound that renders them unpalatable to the predators, and their thick, low-slung frames are impervious to the light gliders.

Their four faceted eyes possess an ocular range that extends far beyond the visible spectrum. Where a human eye would see only empty, baking air and silent sandstone, these den-dwellers watch the intricate, looping lattices of force that bind the needle-towers to the sun. They watch the towers drink—seeing the air part into iridescent bands of pressure as the water is drawn out of the nothingness, molecule by molecule, condensing into the dark stone cups.

In the heat of the midday peak, when the basalt radiates a dry, shimmering warmth and the plain is blindingly bright, the family clusters together at the razor-edge of the monolith’s absolute shadow. The young—pale, soft-furred things with ears still floppy and oversized—lie across their mother’s flanks, their multi-faceted eyes slowly rotating, tracking the silent, chromatic conversations of the herds wandering the basins. 

They are creatures of a finished world. In their stillness, in the steady, rhythmic pulse of the towers, and in the soundless passage of thousands of gleaming bodies across the warm stone, there is no sense of struggle or haste—only a vast, intricate clockwork of stone, light, and water, winding itself down through millennia of unbroken sun.
