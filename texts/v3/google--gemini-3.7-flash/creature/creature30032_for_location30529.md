To live here is not a matter of nesting or staking territory; it is a matter of timing. 

The things that inhabit the basin do not fight the machine’s rhythm, nor do they seek shelter from it. Shelter, in a place where iron plates tilt without warning and three feet of water vanish down a siphon in the space of a heartbeat, is a fatal illusion. Instead, life here has become hydraulic, its life cycles, body plans, and nervous systems calibrated strictly to the cycle rates of the drowned linkages.

At the lowest tier—in the black mud and the frothing silt-chambers—are the **grease-scabs**. They are larger cousins of the coin-sized crustaceans that graze on the rising piston rods, but these have abandoned mobility almost entirely. Shaped like flat, dished iron rivets, they anchor themselves with serrated, horn-rimmed skirts directly over the weeping seams where mineral oil oozes from the axle journals. Their carapaces are not calcium, but a dense, flexible compound of chitin and accumulated zinc scales sloughed from old sacrificial anodes. They feed by straining the pressurized spray that bursts through worn gaskets, their feathery mouthparts fluttering in the cold slipstream. When a linkage swings near, their shells do not shatter; they yield, compressing flat against the steel like rubber washers, then popping back into shape the moment the iron passes.

Deeper within the flooded conduit pipes, where the water is forced through tapered brass throats under terrifying pressure, live the **flute-eels**. They are blind, pale ribbons of pure muscle, entirely lacking in bones, their bodies reinforced by hydrostatic pressure rings that let them survive sudden, violent cavitation. They have no eyes, no fins, and no scales—their skin is smooth, gray-white, and continually secretes an oily, friction-reducing mucus that keeps them from being torn to ribbons by the interior edges of the sluice-valves. 

The flute-eels live entirely in the suck and surge. When a valve slams shut, rebounding a hammer of compressed water backward through the conduit, they ride the shockwave, snapping up the pulverized debris—ground shale, drowned grubs, and grease-motes—that the churn shears apart. They mate only during the deepest hydraulic draws, releasing strings of buoyant, petroleum-scented eggs that drift into the dead-water pockets behind dead-centers and balance weights, where the water moves too slowly to crush them.

Above the water line, wedged into the clearances between the rocking beams and the weeping limestone, is something stranger.

They are called **cadence-crawlers**, though there is no one left to name them. In the flat, gray light, they look like slick, river-smoothed bundles of wet chamois or pale tallow. They are quadrupedal, but their limbs have no fixed shoulders or hips; instead, their skeleton is mostly cartilage, allowing their bodies to stretch long and thin to slide into an empty cylinder sleeve, or flatten until they are scarcely two inches thick to ride the space between a descending counterweight and its bedplate.

Their heads are blunt and wedge-shaped, devoid of eyes or ears, but ringed with broad, fan-like sensory wattles that rest directly against the vibrating metal. Through these wattles, a crawler does not merely feel vibration—it reads the entire basin as an acoustic clockwork map. It knows precisely how many seconds remain before the copper valve beside it vents steam; it knows when the great walking beam above will drop its three-ton knee; it knows the micro-slack in an overhead cable four beats before it snaps taut.

The crawlers feed on the grease-scabs and the pale sheets of bacterial tallow that coat the iron, stripping the metal down to cold, gleaming brass with rasp-like tongues. In doing so, they perform an accidental, essential service: they keep the delicate clearances clear of mineral calcification and gravel-jams. 

They do not vocalize—any breath spent on sound would be smothered instantly by the wet, silty fog. Instead, they communicate by rhythmic tapping: striking their hard, horn-capped elbows against the resonant bellies of the conduits in rapid, percussive pulses that carry through hundreds of feet of interlocking pipework. A mother guides her soft, pliable young across the churning floor entirely by these taps, teaching them the basin’s dead zones, its safe pockets, and its intervals of rest.

There is a terrible, quiet intimacy to their existence. A crawler sleeping in the hollow of an oscillating trunnion moves as the trunnion moves, swaying eight degrees left, eight degrees right, endlessly rocked in the wet, dark grease of an engine that has not stopped since long before its ancestors were born. 

If the water were to dry up, or if the weeping limestone were to finally run dry and the iron seize in its tracks, everything living in the basin would die in hours—not for lack of food, but because their bodies, born into an eternity of measured strokes and silent hydrostatic violence, would not know how to exist in the stillness.
