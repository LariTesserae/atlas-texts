In this place where nothing rots and nothing dries, the things that live do not experience hunger as a lack, nor life as an expenditure. They are not engines burning fuel; they are vessels at capacity, perpetually topped up by the warm, sweating skin of the world.

Close to the brickwork, the small lives exist in an ecology without predation. The long-bodied skinks have no teeth to speak of; their mouths, when opened in their slow, synchronized yawns, reveal clean, pink-white arches of smooth cartilage. They do not hunt the double-winged dragonflies, nor do the dragonflies descend upon the chalk-white frogs. To watch them for hours is to realize that none of them eat. Their nourishment is entirely osmotic. Through the paper-thin, translucent skin of their undersides, they draw up the mineral-rich film weeping from the terracotta, their clear internal organs visible against the pink tiles, glowing faintly with filtered white light. When they move, they do not skitter; they slide with the slow, deliberate grace of oil over wet glass, leaving faint trails in the surface tension that close seamlessly behind them.

In the cisterns and along the broader terraces, the water is rarely more than an ankle deep, yet it harbors creatures whose bodies have flattened to adapt to an existence lived in two dimensions. There are ribbon-like eels, no thicker than a single linen thread and broad as palm fronds, that glide across the submerged tesserae. Their bodies are completely transparent, save for a delicate, branching system of golden veins that mirror the root patterns of the liverwort on the walls. They do not swim through depth; they ripple laterally through the millimeter-thin sheets of water, occasionally coming to rest over a sunken tile pattern, aligning their ribs with the geometric lines pressed into the clay until they become entirely invisible.

Above the terraces, the air is occasionally parted by larger, heavier shapes that drift rather than fly. These are the pale, long-necked ibises of the basin. Their plumage is not made of individual feathers, but of solid, supple sheets of downy white skin that never shed. They possess no calls and no hollow bones; their bodies are dense and cool, like unbaked porcelain. They do not flap their wings to stay aloft; the thick, saturated air supports them like brine. They step from the tops of the unadorned clay colonnades into the white void, falling so slowly that minutes pass before their broad, webbed feet touch the courtyard floor three terraces below. They walk with high, ceremonial steps, bending their necks to touch their horn-colored beaks to the wet mortar, tasting the age of the lime, testing the moisture of the seam.

Where the courtyards widen into vast, blinding flats, one finds the dwellers who laid—or who continue to resemble—the floor itself. 

They are tall, hairless, and cast in the same soft, rounded geometry as the mud bricks. Their skin has the texture of beaten lime-plaster, smooth and cool to the touch, tinted the pale, dusty ochre of sun-cured clay. They have no ears, for there is nothing to hear beyond the ambient hiss of condensation, and their faces are almost entirely unfeatured except for wide, shallow indentations where the eyes rest—eyes that are dark, lidless, and completely flat, like disks of wet river slate.

These beings do not gather in groups, nor do they build dwellings upon the masonry; the masonry *is* their dwelling. They exist in postures of absolute repose. One may be found sitting at the edge of a shallow basin, knees drawn up to its chest, palms resting flat against the weeping tiles, remaining entirely motionless for what might be centuries. Another lies fully supine on a wide terrace riser, its long limbs aligned with the grout lines, its back absorbing the steady, microscopic pulse of water from the clay. 

They do not speak, and they do not work with tools. When one of them finally moves—rising with a slow, hydraulic ease that makes no sound—it is only to tend to the geometry. A dweller will walk twelve paces to an elephant ear that has grown a fraction of an inch beyond its allotted perforation, and with the soft, blunt edge of its thumb, gently press the leaf back toward the center of the tile, holding it there until the plant accepts the posture. They will knead a tiny fissure in the lime with their knuckles, smoothing the wet paste of their own weeping skin into the gap until the surface is once again unbroken, seamless, and fresh.

Their consciousness is not linear; it does not move from a memory toward an ambition. It is spread out evenly across the terracotta basin, as broad and shallow as the water that covers it. They feel the entirety of the world at once: the coolness of a turtle’s carapace shifting on a step half a mile away, the opening of a single liverwort spore in a distant court, the weightless descent of an ibis through the white glare.

Occasionally, at the edges where the floor curves up toward the ceiling of the bowl, new life appears not through birth, but through condensation. In the corners where the dampness gathers thickest in the joint between a parapet and a floor, the water beads do not simply slide; they cluster, thicken, and take on opacity. Over days of unvarying white light, the puddle curdles into slip, the slip firms into pale flesh, and a new frog, a new skink, or the long, serene limb of a new dweller slowly disengages itself from the brickwork, fully formed, cool, and already complete, leaving behind only the crisp, sharp impression of a palm in the wet floor to mark where it rose.
