The people who move through these vaults have the low, folding posture of those whose sky has always had a radius. They are short-limbed, broad in the collar, and their spines have an easy, habitual suppleness that allows them to squat for half a day on the dished tiles without setting a stool. To look at their bodies is to see the borough’s red dust made mobile: their skin, naturally the color of dried tallow or peeled birch, is stained permanently to a dark, powdered apricot by the pulverized fired earth that hangs in the draft. It is worked into the grain of their palms, under the crescent of every nail, and into the fine creases at the corners of their eyes, so that when they weep or sweat, pale clean lines run down their cheeks like water drawn down a brick.

Their feet are their most remarkable feature. They go barefoot without exception; leather rots too quickly in the warm, sweating runnels, and wood clatters too loudly against the terracotta. Over generations, the soles of their feet have become thick, seamless pads—curved slightly convex to fit into the concave wear of the floor tiles, yellow-brown and calloused like the rind of an aged cheese. They step with a rolling, heel-less tread, their toes splayed wide to grip the wet glaze of lime that fringes the drainage troughs.

Because the acoustic roar of the vaults turns open speech into a mush of booming echoes, their language has sheared away most vowels. They speak in the palate and the teeth—a tight, clicking dialect of dry consonants, tongue-taps, and soft, breathy fricatives that drop dead two paces from the speaker’s mouth without exciting the resonant frequencies of the domes. For long-distance communication across the borough’s breadth, they do not shout; they strike the ceramic flue-pipes with hardwood batons. Each major pier has its own resonant note—a dull, hollow gonging that travels along the terracotta ductwork like blood through a vein, carrying messages of draft adjustments, kiln-temperatures, or water distributions.

They are keepers of moisture and masters of the slip. A dweller’s primary implement is not a blade or a hammer, but a wooden rib—a flat, curved paddle of polished plum-wood used to dress clay. They carry these tucked into their hemp girdles alongside small skin-pouches of fine-ground grog and wet grey silt. Their lives are spent in constant, quiet maintenance: wherever an arch begins to show a hair-crack from the shifting earth above, they pack it with fresh slip; where condensation pools too deeply on a vault belly and threatens to drop onto a sleeping bench, they scrape a new thumbnail-groove into the brick to lead the bead away toward a collection runnel.

They sleep stacked three or four high in the hollowed sides of the piers, lying in shallow, scooped niches shaped like the bread-ovens of the upper world. These sleeping cells are lined with thick layers of dried swift-down and stripped reed-pith, smelling faintly of bird lime, scorched flour, and skin. 

Beside them, the animals are not pets or pests, but parts of a singular digestive cycle. The dwellers cultivate the swifts with an obsessive, gentle care. They mold the clay nesting cups with their own thumbs, ensuring the lip is just rough enough for the birds’ tiny claws to grip. In return, they harvest the excess eggs with long bamboo spoons, scrape the nitrogen-rich droppings from beneath the roosts to feed their tub-grown chard and pale, lightless onions, and catch the old, clumsy birds with fine-meshed hair-nets flung across the light shafts.

Along the floor, the brick-dusted land-crabs are herded rather than hunted. The dwellers know which sumps produce the fattest crabs and drop parings of squash-rind and dried beetle husks into the water to feed them. Children—round-bellied and silent, whose hair is cropped close to the scalp to keep it free of mites—sit beside the runnels with short reed sticks, guiding the crabs into woven traps with quick, rhythmic taps against the tiles. The crabs are boiled whole in lidded clay jars set directly into the flue-drafts; their meat is sweet and earthy, tasting distinctly of the mineral crust that lines the water-troughs.

There is another creature here, rarely seen by the light of the pierced lanterns: a species of small, blind polecat, long-bodied and loose-skinned, that the dwellers slip into the hollow spaces behind the wall-tiles. These animals, which they call *casing-dogs*, are no thicker than a man’s wrist and wear collars of braided rawhide tipped with copper bells. They hunt the fat woodlice, scorpions, and cave-crickets that nest in the dry dead-air chambers behind the arches. The dwellers track their unseen progress through the walls by listening with an ear pressed to the warm brick, following the faint, tinny chime of the bell through ten inches of fired clay.

When a dweller dies, the architecture does not release them. Wood is far too precious to burn for a pyre, and there is no soft soil to dig. Instead, the body is washed in the mineral brine of the lowest sump, folded into the fetal posture of a sleeper in an amphora, and placed within an empty cavity inside one of the massive corner buttresses. The opening is walled over with green mud-brick, mortared with lime, and plastered smooth. The heat of the borough’s flues cures the brick over weeks, baking the tomb into the permanent fabric of the pier. The living then take their wooden ribs and carve the outline of the deceased’s hands into the wet outer plaster, thumb to thumb, over the belly of the vault. 

Generations of these handprints cover the lower arches—thousands of shallow, finger-shaped hollows pressed into the ochre slip, worn slick and dark by the passing palms of descendants who brush against them as they carry their water-jars through the warm, whistling twilight.
