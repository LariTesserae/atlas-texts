To survive the hydraulic violence of the nave, an organism cannot possess air, hollow cavities, or soft membranes. Anything with a lung would be pulped in milliseconds by the cavitation waves; anything with a fragile eye would have the organ vibrated out of its socket by the continuous, ringing thunder of the water. 

What lives here has the density of cured meat, wet horn, and saturated timber.

Clinging to the lee sides of the basalt, beneath the thick pelt of liver-colored lichen, is a secondary layer of life that treats the rock’s razor edges not as a hazard, but as an anchor. These are the *cleats*: flat, disc-shaped animals no thicker than a fingernail, built of overlapping scales of dull, calcified keratin. They possess no faces, no limbs, and no blood. Instead, their bodies are filled with the same heavy, alkali-dense brine that surrounds them, equalizing their internal pressure perfectly against the 120-decibel shockwaves. They feed by capillary action, drinking the grease-like film of iron bacteria that coats the stone. When the basalt underneath them snaps under the hydraulic load, releasing a cold blue spark of triboluminescence, the cleats reflexively clamp down with hydraulic suction so absolute that the stone will fracture a second time before the creature can be torn loose.

Deeper in the fractures—specifically within the narrow, shuddering seams between the imported granite lintels and the native basalt—exist the things that survived the mountain’s drowning. 

When the warm sea first tore through the sky, whatever beasts were brought up by the builders—pack-mules, heavy-horned goats, or mountain oxen used to haul the dressed granite—were crushed against the frames. But their biology did not simply decay; the bitter alkalis, total absence of air, and extreme dissolved iron pickled and mineralized their carcasses before rot could set in. 

In the blind doorways, wedged tightly into the corners where the braided hide cords still strain against bronze pins, these drowned bodies have become nurseries for something new. Out of the preserved marrow and water-tanned hide grow clusters of *strikers*: blind, jointless stalks of gristle, each capped with a heavy, spade-like tip of modified bone. They do not swim. They are anchored into the joints of the old skeletons, swaying in the liquid gale like heavy kelp made of muscle. When a piece of shattered obsidian or volcanic grit tumbles past in the current, the striker’s nerve-fibers detect the acoustic shift and the stalk snaps outward like a spring, batting the debris away or smashing smaller drifting matter into pulp, which its porous, tongue-like skin immediately absorbs.

Higher up, where the water loops violently toward the drowned ceiling, the pale ribbons drift.

Seen close, these ribbons are not single creatures, but colonial chains of blind, muscular segments—each one little more than a pair of calcified ribs linked by thick, white bands of tonic tendon. They have no digestive tract, no gills, and no brains. They are living tension-harnesses. The ribbons exist entirely to exploit the chaotic mechanics of the broken gravity; their ribbed structures are curved in such a way that the violent upwellings catch them like sails, hurtling them toward the liquid dome, while their heavier, iron-sheathed tails drag them back down through the vortex. 

As they tumble end over end through the foam, they flex and contract rhythmically, not to propel themselves, but to pump the dense, iron-rich water through internal sieves that line their ribcages. They catch the flakes of liver-lichen torn from the cliffs, the drifting marrow-fleece shed by the strikers, and the fine, floating silt of pulverized basalt. They do not die when they strike the rock at speed; their gristle is self-healing, snapping back into shape like thick vulcanized rubber, leaving only pale smears of coagulated protein on the black glass before the torrent drags them back into the loop.

And in the very center of the chamber, where gravity loses its orientation and the water churns in a suspended, buoyant eye, there is a slow, drifting mass. 

It is a ball of tangled matter the size of a hay-wain, rolling lazily in the dead-spot of the vortex. It is formed from centuries of shed ribbons, braided hide cords that snapped and drifted free from the lintels, floating chips of porous pumice, and the dense, fibrous mats of detached lichen. This mass is not dead; it is a communal engine. Inside its tangled core, shielded from the worst of the hydraulic hammer, live pale, slug-like worms with mouths of jagged bronze-grit, chewing through the fibrous debris, digesting the old leather and the lichen, and excreting a thick, buoyant mucus that binds the entire rolling sphere together. 

Whenever a flash of blue light sparks from a snapping spire, the mass can be seen drifting sluggishly across the dark, negative space—an artificial, breathing island of gristle and stone, suspended forever inside a drowned thunderclap that never ends.
