To survive here, a body cannot be soft, and it cannot be buoyant. Buoyancy in this vault is a death sentence: anything that floats is drawn into the shearing vortex of a four-story impeller within minutes, pulverized against the magnetite-crusted blades, and dispersed into the tea-colored wash as greasy foam. 

Everything living here has learned to be heavy, or to be hydrodynamic to the point of near-frictionlessness.

The lowest tier of life clings directly to the metal skin of the facility. The pale crustaceans noted in the eddies are not fragile things; they are heavy, flattened arthropods the size of foundry shovels, their carapaces thickened with incorporated iron oxide and slate dust until they look like chipped pieces of slag. They have no eyes—only twin rows of dense, oil-filled sensory pits along the margins of their shells that read the high-frequency screaming of cavitating brass. Their legs do not end in claws, but in hardened, chisel-like picks that wedge directly into the pitted rivets and rust-scale of the bulkheads. They move with a deliberate, ratcheting gait, never lifting more than one limb at a time against the violent draw of the down-sluices. They graze with rasping, manganese-hardened mouthparts, scraping down the rubbery sulfur-mats and licking the mineral grease that weeps from the joints of old pressure valves.

Where the electrical lines short and spark, a different sort of tenant gathers. Clustered around the submerged junction boxes are colonies of *galvanic siphons*—tubular, leathery organisms that resemble bundles of severed hydraulic hoses. Rooted into the soft lead insulation and copper braiding, they feed directly on the stray galvanic charge and the rich dissolved zinc leaching from ancient sacrificial anodes. When a junction arcs, their translucent fronds flare with sudden, internal neon-blue phosphor, discharging brief clouds of iron-fixing spores into the turbulence before snapping shut against the concussive pressure wave.

Higher in the mid-water, in the great open voids between the catwalks, the current is too wild for anything anchored. Here live the *shear-sliders*.

They are long, ribbon-like vertebrates, ten to twelve feet from blunt snout to tapering tail, with flesh the color of boiled lard. They have no bone, only dense, oily gristle that can deform under immense pressure without fracturing. Their skins are completely smooth, lubricated by a continuous secretion of thick, bitter slime that allows water to pass over them with zero resistance. They do not swim against the current; they hunt by falling through it. Pitching themselves into the downward vortices of the thermal plumes, they ride the spinning water at terrifying speeds, their mouths agape—wide, circular funnels lined with concentric rings of fine, black, horny sieves. They filter the churned slurry: torn fragments of bacterial mat, pulverized crustacean shell, and the thick flakes of magnetite thrown off by the grinding machinery.

The shear-sliders communicate through the water using the vault’s own acoustic brutality. By inflating hollow, cartilaginous resonance chambers located along their flanks, they drum against the mechanical hum of the engines, sending sharp, metallic *pings* through the water that bounce off the brick arches and steel trusses. To an ear submerged here, their calls sound like loose bolts vibrating inside a spinning pipe.

Deepest of all, in the blind dead-zones behind the massive intake housings—where the current curls back on itself and forms pockets of stagnant, superheated water—lie the true anchors of this ecosystem. 

These are the *ballast-matrons*. They are immense, sessile masses of muscle, fat, and calcified grit, wedged immovable into the corners between the floor plates and the intake grates. Some are as large as steam boilers. Their outer skin is a thick, blackened rind of dead tissue, rust, and silt, nearly indistinguishable from the iron foundations they rest upon. They have lived here for centuries, sustained by the endless river of nutrients driven past their resting places by the pumps. 

A ballast-matron does not chase food; the machines deliver it to her. She breathes through cavernous, siphon-like vents that open and close in counter-rhythm to the stroke of the hydraulic pistons nearby. Within her vast, warm interior, thousands of the pale scavengers nest, crawling in and out of her wrinkled respiratory folds to clean her of parasites and dead tissue in exchange for shelter from the crushing velocity of the open floor.

The matrons are the acoustic centers of the vault. Their enormous, liquid-filled cranial domes act as massive hydrophones, drinking in the entire vibration of the drowned city—the groan of every failing gear, the micro-fractures forming in the cast iron, the subtle shifts in water temperature as sluice gates jam or open thousands of feet away. They know the geography of the chamber not as space, but as a shifting map of pressure, resonance, and torque. 

When a component of the great engine finally shears apart—when a gear tooth snaps or a copper pipe bursts under the steam pressure—the shockwave rings through the water like a cannon shot. In that instant, before the current can even sweep the metal shrapnel away, the entire vault reacts: the siphons pulse their dying blues, the shear-sliders roll into the newly opened wake, and the scavengers ratchet forward through the dark, their chisel-claws ringing against the iron, perfectly adapted to live off the slow, eternal unmaking of the machine that holds them.
