At first, one might look into the terraced lime basins and see only geology: layers of pale, fluted calcium deposited millimeter by millimeter over iron bolt-heads. But where the alkaline spray strikes the hottest copper plates, the mineral crust gives way to something thicker, rubbery and saponified. 

Here, in the overflow channels, live the *tallow-blooms*. They are not fungi, nor are they true plants, but vast, colonial sheets of living wax and tallow-fat that have taken up the mineral drift of the water. They feed on the graphite flaking from the packing glands, the trace oils thrown off by the six-foot balance beams, and the copper salts leaching from the conduits. They have no organs to speak of, only a rhythmic, slow-pulsing surface that tenses and relaxes with the pressure changes in the room. When a cylinder completes its slow downstroke, the sudden vacuum in the air causes the margins of the tallow-sheets to curl slightly, drinking in the atomized oil before settling flat against the stone once more. They are pale as suet, veined with faint, dendritic lines of oxidized iron-green, and they smell faintly of mutton fat, wet slate, and cold river bottom.

Further back, in the crawlways behind the cylinder jackets where the heat is dry and stifling, exist the caretakers of these margins.

They are few—perhaps three or four in the entire square—and they move so seldom that moss has begun to establish itself in the deep wrinkles of their outer wraps. It is impossible to tell whether they were once men who descended forty feet from the city above and never found a reason to climb back out, or if they are something the engine itself condensed out of steam, graphite, and saturated wool over the span of a century.

They are shaped like human beings, but elongated and flattened, as if built to slip between narrow clearances of iron staging. Their clothing is not woven fabric anymore; it has become an armor of laminated canvas, thick felt, and horsehair gasket-rope, all of it soaked through with black cylinder oil and mineralized hard water until it is as stiff as cured leather. Where their skin shows—at the wrists, the throat, the broad, flat planes of their faces—it has the dull, polished texture of river pebbles. It is hairless, gray-pink, and entirely smooth, lacking pores or sweat glands; the moisture that runs down their cheeks is simply the ambient steam condensing on their cold, dense flesh.

They have no eyes to speak of, or rather, their eyes have clouded over behind thick, milky calluses that distinguish only between the tea-colored amber of the ceiling gratings and the hot, cherry-red glow of dynamo brushes. They do not need sight. Their bare feet, splayed and flat, are pressed permanently against the trembling flagstones. They navigate entirely through the floor’s vibrations, feeling the health of the cloister through their marrow: the slight hitch of a dry slide-valve, the cavitation of water in a blocked intake, the subtle change in pitch when a fern root worms its way past a lead washer.

Their life is measured in the long, unhurried arcs of the machinery. One will stand for three days beside a primary wrist-pin, entirely motionless except for its chest, which expands not on biological time, but in exact, synchronized counterpoint to the *whump-hiss* of the steam stroke. 

When it moves, the motion is heavy, deliberate, and soundless. It will reach into a pouch stiff with lard, extract a braided hemp twist impregnated with lead paste, and press it into a leaking gland with a blunt, slate-gray thumb. It does not hurry; to hurry in this air would be to tear the delicate liverwort from the iron, to disturb the quiet, calcifying equilibrium that keeps the retaining walls from buckling under the miles of city above.

They do not speak. The acoustics of the manifold would turn a voice into meaningless mud, drowned beneath the immense, lung-shaking hum of the dynamos. Instead, when two of them pass on the wet flagstones, they lean forward until their foreheads touch with a wet, dull clink of calcified brow-bones. Through that contact, bone to bone, they exchange the tremors of the engine—sharing the precise mechanical balance of the deep floor, the pressure of the subterranean river beneath them, and the slow, inexorable growth of the lime terraces in the dark.
