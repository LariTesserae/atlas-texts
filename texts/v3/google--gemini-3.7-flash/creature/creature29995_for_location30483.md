The life that belongs to this floor does not fight the rock; it has simply surrendered every softness that could be cut, torn, or infected by brine. What remains is a biology of horn, tendon, slate, and oil.

### The Lattice-Gleaners

Beneath the towering laths, where the mist hangs thickest and the stone needles grow so densely that nothing wider than a hand can pass, live the *flute-weavers*. They are not spiders, though they work with line, but long, flattened arthropods whose bodies are segmented into plates of translucent, amber-colored chitin as hard as river flint. They possess twelve pairs of jointed legs tipped with curved, diamond-hard stilettos that find purchase in the microscopic pits of the selenite crystals.

The weavers do not spin silk from glands; instead, they harvest the thick, viscous mucus trail of the tallow-slugs, mixing it with their own acidic bile and the weeping gypsum dust to draw long, elastic tethers between the stone flutes. These tethers dry in the cold air into taut, glassy filaments. When a droplet of dense brine strikes a needle higher up, the vibration travels down through the stone and sets the filament singing. By pressing the flat underside of their carapaces against the rock, the weavers read these frequencies with startling precision. They know when a column is cracking three hundred yards away, when a neighbor has snagged a dislodged isopod, or when the subterranean pressure is rising toward an eruption.

They feed primarily on what falls: the calcified husks of their own young, dead isopods dislodged by snapping crystals, and the thick, sulfurous mats of anaerobic bacteria that peel away from the wet vents at the base of the laths.

### The Chime-Hunters

Where the spires give way to the wider tabular plates of basalt, the acoustic pressure is at its most violent. Here, wedged into the vertical seams, waits the *anvil-jaw*. 

The anvil-jaw is a creature of immense mass and agonizing patience. It is nearly entirely head and shell—a squat, wedge-shaped predator resembling a tortoise forged from slag iron. Its outer shell does not grow from within like bone; it is a bio-mineralized crust formed by centuries of weeping lime and iron water washing over its back while it lies motionless. Over decades, its shell becomes an actual geological extension of the basalt slab behind it, cemented in place by the basin’s own precipitation.

Its only moving parts are a set of horizontal, shear-like mandibles capable of pulverizing slate. It possesses no eyes—the shadowless gloom renders sight an unnecessary metabolic burden—but its jawbones are tuned to the precise harmonic pitch of the hollow selenite columns. When the pale, shield-sized isopods creep upward to escape the rising condensation, their heavy, dragging gait creates a subtle, rhythmic dampening in the stone’s resonance. The anvil-jaw detects this shift through its anchored shell. When the isopod passes within reach of the crevice, the trap springs—a single, hydraulic snap of the jaws that sounds precisely like two paving stones being smashed together. The sound is swallowed instantly by the roaring basin, and the hunter resumes its decades-long stillness, digesting the hard chitin in a stomach lined with swallowed quartz grit.

### The Cairn-Keepers

The dry-stacked causeways and split basalt mounds are not the abandoned relics of an extinct species; they are the active, continuously maintained territory of the *Liths*—the basin’s sole humanoid inhabitants, though they have diverged so radically from common humanity that the term is largely anatomical.

They are short, barrel-chested, and drastically stooped, their spines curved to keep their centers of gravity low against the acoustic buffet and the treacherous slick of the floor. Their skin is not soft, but thick, grey, and cornified, resembling the rind of salt-cured leather. Because their sweat contains unusually high concentrations of calcium and silica, their joints, shoulders, and the crowns of their hairless skulls are encrusted with thin, chalky scales that flake away and reform constantly.

The Liths have no functional external ears. The delicate drums of human hearing would have been ruptured within days by the basin’s deafening chime. Instead, their auditory canals have grown over with thick plugs of cartilage, and their hearing is entirely skeletal. They navigate and communicate by bone conduction. A Lith will press the flat, bony shelf of their lower jaw directly against a basalt pillar or the surface of a causeway to "listen" to the valley. Through the stone, they can hear the footsteps of their kin a mile off, the rhythmic filling of subterranean steam pockets, and the distinctive, low-frequency groan of a spire about to collapse under its own weight.

Their clothing is constructed entirely from the dried carapaces of giant isopods, drilled with flint points and bound together with the sinewy gut-cords of the tallow-slugs. Over their feet they lash thick slabs of dense, fine-grained basalt, shaped by patient grinding against the floor. These stone soles protect them from the glass-keen edges of the newly sprouted gypsum needles, allowing them to walk heavily, if slowly, across the jagged terrain.

The Liths do not build houses because no roof could withstand the steady accretion of heavy stone or the concussive vibrations of the vents. Instead, they live inside the hollows formed beneath the largest collapsed basalt slabs, where the causeways cross. They do not use fire—there is nothing here dry enough to burn, and no wood to feed it—nor do they forge metal, which would dissolve into rust in the acidic brine mist within months.

Their entire culture is an endless, silent war against the growth of the stone. 

Armed with heavy mauls made of dense river-cobbles (carried down from the rim in generations past) lashed to bone handles, they patrol the causeways. With slow, rhythmic blows, they smash the young gypsum laths that sprout like weeds between the basalt blocks. If left untended for five years, the needles would split the causeways into gravel and leave the Liths marooned in the razor thicket, unable to reach the sulfur pools or the slug beds.

They eat the tallow-slugs raw, slicing the pale, oily flesh with knives struck from volcanic glass. They drink the condensed water that weeps from the selenite tubes, but only after filtering it through sacks of crushed basalt sand to draw out the harshest lime and copper salts. 

When a Lith dies, there is no burial in earth. Their kin carry the body to a grove where the gypsum needles grow most rapidly and lay it across a cradle of stone laths. Within months, the weeping brine and the frantic crystallization claim the corpse. The white needles push through the leathered skin, fill the chest cavity, and encase the bones, until the dead hunter is transformed into a pale, translucent pillar of selenite—ringing in harmony with the rest of the deafening, unyielding valley, indistinguishable from the stone that birthed it.
