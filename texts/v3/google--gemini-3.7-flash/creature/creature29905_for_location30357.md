To live here is to be made of weight, fat, and stone.

The people of the whaleback ridges—who call themselves only by the vibration of their hearth-stones, a deep double-thud that registers in the shins—are broad-shouldered and squat, their centers of gravity pulled low toward the wet shale. Generations of walking upon slick, iron-oxidizing films and fighting the lateral drag of the continuous water-sheet have thickened their ankles and flattened their feet into wide, spade-like pads. Their calf muscles are knotted like burls on dwarf birch. 

Underneath the stiffened layers of seal-pale blubber-hide, their skin is the color of curdled fat, rubbed perpetually with yellow tallow to keep the cold mist from rotting the pores. They have little body hair; what they have is sheared away to prevent the condensed lead-gray moisture from crusting against their skin. Their eyes, visible only when the thin-scraped plates of translucent horn are lifted inside the domes, are pale, milky gray, with pupils that widen into horizontal slits. They do not look toward the horizon—for there is no horizon to measure—but downward, reading the micro-ripples in the water-skin and the fluctuating color of the iron-mats, which blush from saffron to bruised violet hours before a vent-surge or a change in the deep currents.

Because the air is a howling chamber of steam and water-roar, their hearing has retreated. The external ear is small, tucked tight against the skull and often sealed with plugs of boiled lard; the temporal bones of their skulls, however, have grown extraordinarily dense. They hear through their skeletons. When two people meet upon the open rock, they do not shout; they step close, lean inward, and press their foreheads firmly together. In this way, bypassing the air entirely, the resonance of the vocal cords passes through the frontal bone directly into the inner ear—a low, humming murmur of skull-to-skull speech that sounds like stones grinding together in the surf.

Inside the low-domed shelters, the atmosphere is stifling, greasy, and fiercely warm. The interior is excavated only a few inches into the mudstone—to dig deeper is to invite the pressurized water of the fissures—and the air is thick with the sweet, heavy smoke of burning marrow-oil. Here, three or four families live huddled together upon platforms constructed from the broad, flat shoulder blades of benthic drift-whales, raised on slate pillars just high enough to let the shallow seepage wash beneath their beds. 

Their domestic life is rhythmic, measured entirely by the tide and the lung-strokes of the land. 

They scrape the slate-colored horn-scavengers that drift into the hollows, cracking their carapace-plates with heavy granite mauls to extract the gray, gelatinous meat within. The empty shells are not discarded; they are trimmed with chert chisels, drilled with bone awls, and sewn together with sinew to make the overlapping scale-armor they wear against the snapping snouts of the deep-channel hunters. 

These hunters—the pale, rubber-skinned things of the waist-deep channels—are called the *Slappers*. They are neither seal nor fish, but neotenic amphibians that have lost the need for air, absorbing the hyper-oxygenated spray of the vents through thick, folded cloaks of vascular skin along their flanks. The ridge-dwellers hunt them from the edges of the swales using long, dead-weighted spears tipped with jagged chert. It is a dangerous, silent harvest: a hunter stands motionless in a foot of rushing water, their feet locked into stone footholds worn into the rock by ancestors, waiting for the dull *thwack* of a paddle against the surface. When the strike comes, it is a matter of pure leverage—heaving the thrashing, white-skinned bulk onto the dry slab before its blunt teeth can find an ankle.

Below the human world, deeper in the fissures where the air screams through the stone pipes, lives an entirely different biology. Inside the natural flutes of the shale, blind, fibrous things cling to the polished walls. There are *vent-weavers*—pale, colonial vermiforms that spin nets of tough, mucus-coated hair across the mouths of the blowholes, catching the spray of pulverized tube-marrow and sulfur-crust that the steam blasts upward. When the vents whistle high and thin, it is because these nets are full and taut; when a vent falls silent, the people know the net has torn, or that something larger has crawled up from the abyssal conduits to feed.

In the deepest swales, where the water never drains even at the lowest slack, grow the *bellows-sponges*: vast, liver-colored masses of tough, cartilaginous tissue anchored directly into the mudstone. They do not photosynthesize; they breathe the hydraulic shock. Every time the sea forces a wave through the subterranean chambers, the sponges compress with a wet, heavy sigh, then expand, drawing gallons of the iron-thick water through their porous cores and filtering the red bacterial sludge. The people harvest their tough, fibrous interior skins to make the diaphragms for their storm-boots and the watertight seals for their shelter-doors.

When the deep tide turns and begins its slow, slate-colored crawl across the plains, the world belongs entirely to the stone. 

The people retreat into their domes, dropping the heavy, tallow-greased stone slabs into the door-frames and wedging them tight with rib-bones. Outside, the water climbs from an inch to three feet, turning the whaleback ridges into low, black reefs surrounded by an endless expanse of boiling pewter foam. The roar of the vents drowns into a massive, earth-quaking thrum—a frequency so low that inside the dark shelters, the marrow-oil trembles in its stone cups, and the children fall asleep with their hands pressed flat against the bedrock floor, lulled by the massive, churning heart of the stone continent as it drinks the sea.
