The skin of the place begins with the rasp-slugs and the plate-lice—the primary harvest of the iron.

They are creatures without eyes, or with eyes reduced to milky blisters beneath their armor, for sight is of little use in a world where food is a matter of touch and taste across wet plate. The plate-lice are wide, flattened like spade-heads, their carapaces segmented in overlapping slate-gray tiles that mimic the mineral crusts of the plateau. They do not walk so much as hydraulically wedge themselves along the seams of rivets. Their undersides are a pale, quivering forest of hydraulic tube-feet and hardened scraping pincer-mouths made of iron-hard organic silicate. They work continuously over the verdigris blooms on the copper conduit, grazing down the blue-green dusting and the warm bacterial lard that sweats from the seams of the pipe. When a pump-stroke shudders through the metal beneath them, thousands of these plates lift slightly in unison, venting trapped steam from their gills in a collective, whistling hiss, before clamping down tight once more.

Interspersed among them are the great cone-limpets—solid, chalky pyramids the size of a man’s fist, cemented permanently to the vertical faces of the flues and aqueducts. Their shells are not smooth like marine snails, but ringed with ridged tiers that channel the weeping runoff directly into their mantles. Within each cone lies a dense, muscular plug of meat that never dries out, kept bathed in the sulfur-rich dew. Over decades, their base secretions chemically pit the basalt, anchoring them so deeply that to tear one away would be to take a chunk of the bedrock with it.

Higher up, where the copper towers narrow into trusses and cross-braces against the bright glare, the life becomes warm-blooded, lean, and nervous.

These are the shelf-kites, or *skin-gliders*—creatures that seem part mammal, part bat, with long, rib-stiffened flaps of oily, hairless leather that stretch from their wrists to their elongated ankle bones. Their hide is the color of wet tallow or bruised plum, naturally water-repellent, beaded perpetually with greasy drops of mist. They do not build nests of twigs or hair; instead, they anchor themselves with curved, needle-sharp dewclaws into the rust-pits and bolt-holes beneath the eaves. In the heat of the midday sun, they hang in trembling rows, packed so tightly together that their collective body heat radiates down into the walkways like the breath of a furnace.

When the warm lowland drafts catch the sharp lip of the plateau, the kites drop by the hundreds from their perches. They do not flap; their broad, diamond-shaped silhouettes simply cup the rising thermal currents, wheeling in wide, silent circles above the cooling towers. They feed on the wing, snatching up the cloud-spiders—minute, ballooning arachnids that drift on gossamer parachutes through the rising steam—or diving with tucked wings toward the canals to gaff the surface-swimming crust-worms with their long, tooth-lined lower jaws. Their cries are high, brief clicks, like pebbles struck together underwater, used to map the dense forest of girders through the blinding fog of the vents.

Down along the dark floor of the machine-canyons, where the runoff gathers into the stone-paved canals, a heavier, more sluggish fauna rules the gutters.

Here, in the warm, mineral-slick channels where water runs thick as broth with dissolved salts and tallow, live the *trough-hogs*. They are barrel-bodied, six-limbed quadrupeds with thick, folded skin like damp leather boots, entirely hairless and stained the liver-brown of the surrounding iron. Their feet are broad, fleshy pads ringed with tough horn that grip the mossless, slippery cobbles without sliding. The trough-hogs are the cleaners of the works. With broad, spade-like snouts, they dredge the canal bottoms, sifting through the silt of discarded shells, iron flakes, and tallow-scum. Their metabolisms are slow, volcanic; their gullets can grind down basalt gravel and calcified molts, extracting the rich sulfur-fixing bacteria that coat every submerged surface. 

They are remarkably placid, leaning their massive flanks against the vibrating casings of the steam cylinders to soak up the heat, their small, dark nostrils flaring in rhythm with the engine strokes. When they sleep, they submerge entirely in the warm mineral runoff, leaving only the ridged crests of their snouts exposed, looking like discarded pieces of industrial scrap half-sunk in the silt.

And moving among these beasts, inhabiting the dry, hollowed-out chambers within the thickest foundation walls and the hollow spaces between piston jackets, are the *Tenders*.

They are not visitors to this infrastructure, nor are they its prisoners; they are its tenants, evolved or modified alongside the brass and the basalt until their lives are indistinguishable from the machine’s operation. They are human-shaped, but shortened and widened, their chests broad and barrel-like to drink deeply of the thin, warm air. Their skin is thick, yellowish, and perpetually slick with a natural sebum that prevents the acidic, mineral-heavy waters of the plateau from eating away at their flesh. Their eyes are small, set deep beneath heavy, hairless brow-ridges, shaded against the brutal, unsoftened sun that glares off miles of wet metal.

The Tenders wear no textiles, for cloth would rot within days in this sweating air. Instead, their garments are aprons and greaves of cured trough-hog hide, fastened with heavy copper pins and weighted with lead sinkers so the rising drafts do not whip them about.

Their culture is an oral and tactile one, measured in degrees of vibration, pressure, and the sound of water through stone. They do not write on paper; they engrave with hardened steel punches directly into the verdigris crusts of the dead pipes, marking dates of flow, pressure drops, and the cycles of the animal swarms. 

A Tender’s day is spent walking the narrow gangways with long hooked poles of ash-colored iron. They clear the intake screens where the plate-lice have clustered too thickly, scraping bushels of the clattering, segmented shells into leather baskets to be ground into paste for food or boiled down for oil. They tend the great tallow-traps where the fat of dead canal-hogs floats to the surface, skimming the grease with wide copper ladles to pour into the oil-cups of the rocker arms and piston slides.

They speak little, for the continuous roar of the spillways and the heavy thump of the pumps drowns out human speech. Instead, they communicate across the spans through a language of pipe-striking—clanging out rhythmic sequences on the structural girders with brass-headed hammers, the notes traveling through the frame of the plateau faster than sound through air.

In the late afternoon, when the sun drops low and throws long, razor-edged shadows of the cooling towers across the gleaming basalt shelves, the Tenders gather on the upper galleries. There, sitting among the sleeping clusters of leathery gliders, they wash their hands in the warm, clean condensation that bleeds from the steam flues. They eat the rich, salt-crusted meat of the limpet plugs and watch the light leave the high shelf. Around them, the pistons beat on into the dusk, the mollusks scrape the rust in the dark, and the plateau breathes its warm, wet breath into the cooling sky, complete, enclosed, and without end.
