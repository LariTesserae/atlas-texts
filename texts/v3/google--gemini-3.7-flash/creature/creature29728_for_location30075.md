They have no eyes, because there has never been anything to look at except the unchanging glare of the phosphor tubes, and no ears, because the single, continuous C-sharp hum of the deep manifolds provides neither warning nor information. Instead, the things that live across the plates are entirely creatures of voltage, lipid-density, and millimetric touch.

Their bodies are shaped by the geometry of the floor. Because the zinc plates are flat and the stamped lead gratings are set with crisp, right-angled perforations, the organisms have evolved an unnatural, rectilinear symmetry. There are no curled shells, no spiral horns, no radial symmetries reminiscent of sea stars. Everything is flattened, slotted, or segmented into neat, modular bands that fit neatly between the raised traction-nibs and bolt-heads of the decking.

### The Grate-Feeders and Tallow-Mouths

The most numerous are the *tongue-plates*—broad, pale squabs of muscular gristle, roughly the size and thickness of a leather sole. Their upper surfaces are smooth, water-repellent, and pearlescent, shedding the continuous drip from above like greased rubber. Their undersides, however, are a complex machinery of ingestion: an apron of thousands of microscopic, brass-hard chitinous plates that vibrate at the exact frequency of the floor’s mechanical hum. By resting flat against the wet metal, they use the deck's own vibration to drive their scrapers, shaving off the microscopic scum of synthetic lipids and sulfur salts without expending their own metabolic energy. 

When a tongue-plate moves, it does not crawl so much as melt forward, riding a self-secreted cushion of warm slime. They have no heads. A peripheral ring of galvanic sensory pits informs them of the nearest copper seam or zinc weld; they steer simply by turning toward the strongest micro-current, crawling toward the junctions where the galvanic tallow forms thickest.

Beneath the tongue-plates, wedged directly into the slots of the drainage grating, are the *plugs*. These are cylindrical, blind bags of soft white tissue that have surrendered all locomotion. A plug drops into a stamped square hole as a microscopic larva and swells until it matches the precise imperial or metric tolerance of the perforation. Its upper rim flares out like a rubber flange, catching the warm grease-wash as it drains toward the pipes, while its lower body hangs in the warm, roaring vapor-spaces below the deck, dangling long, feathery gills into the subterranean exhaust stream. A square kilometer of grating contains millions of them, side by side, transforming the floor into a living, porous sponge that breathes with the pressure differentials of the ventilation system.

### The Riser-Climbers and Truss-Lace

Above the floor, where the galvanized stanchions and structural I-beams rise toward the lights, the biomass changes form. The creatures here are vertical specialists, built to capture the condensation before it hits the deck.

Great sheets of *curtain-flesh*—creatures no thicker than parchment and several meters wide—cling to the vertical faces of the iron columns. They hang like wet, ivory tapestries, held fast by millions of suction-discs that anchor themselves in the microscopic pits of the rolled metal. As the warm mist condenses on the upper reaches of the beam, it runs downward in sheets over their bodies; their skin is covered in a microscopic herringbone pattern of channels that directs the falling water through internal digestive cavities, filtering out the suspended sulfur particles before releasing the water from their lower hem, purified and crystal-clear.

Higher still, in the web of trusses sixty meters up, live the *cable-spiders*—though they have no venom, no webs, and no kinship with terrestrial arachnids. They are jointed ribbons of translucent, water-filled flesh that span the gaps between conduits. Their limbs are long, tubular hydraulic struts filled with the same warm, mineralized condensate that drips through the air. They spend centuries creeping along the electrical conduit runs, their bellies pressed against the warm iron casings of the wiring. 

Directly encrusting the sealed phosphor tubes are the *scum-burners*: thin, moss-like colonial zooids that are semi-transparent and pale amber. They do not feed on grease, for the tubes are too hot and dry for lipid-films to form. Instead, they feed directly on the high-frequency electromagnetic leakage and radiant heat of the lighting elements, their tissues rich with metallic ions that metabolize the electrical field itself. They glow with a faint, ghostly second-hand phosphorescence, casting a soft, yellow-green halo around every suspended lamp.

### The Gutter-Strands

In the drainage runnels—where the weeping from miles of metal gathers into sluggish, lukewarm canals an inch deep—the biomass is liquid in its movement.

The parsnip-eels mentioned before do not possess skeletons or blood vessels in the familiar sense. Their flesh is a homogeneous, dense albumen, like the white of a hard-boiled egg, through which clear, copper-rich lymph circulates via the gentle, squeezing motion of their outer skin. They have no teeth; their mouths are soft, pleated vacuums that engulf the runnels, drinking in the fallen scrapings, sloughed skins, and biological sediment produced by the floor-grazers.

They are so densely packed in the gutters that they cease to act as individual animals. When the fluid flow increases, the entire mass slides downstream together, a single river of white muscle shifting through the channels, turning corners with fluid obedience to the floor’s drainage angles, looping endlessly through the circulation system, through sumps and settling tanks, only to be pumped back out across the high manifolds to start the drift anew.

### Reproduction and Dissolution

There are no mating seasons, because there are no seasons. There is no courtship, because there are no eyes to witness display and no brains to process desire.

Reproduction is continuous, quiet, and mechanical. An organism simply reaches a critical mass of lipid storage, pauses its grazing, and divides along clean, geometric lines that follow the seams of the floor plate beneath it. A tongue-plate splits into four smaller rectangles; a cable-spider drops a bulbous, water-filled cyst that bursts upon striking the deck below, scattering thousands of microscopic, oil-seeking discs across the backs of the herd.

When an organism’s cellular machinery finally exhausts itself—when its galvanic ion-pumps can no longer maintain the differential against the warm water—it does not rot, because there are no putrefying bacteria in this distilled, abiotic air. Instead, the chemical bonds of its skin soften at 32 degrees Celsius. Its flesh becomes slightly more soluble than the grease around it. The neighboring tongue-plates, sensing the change in surface tension and the sudden release of potassium, slide over the carcass without pause. Within minutes, the rasping brass cilia have scraped the metal clean, down to the original machine-stamped steel, leaving only a faint, bright silver patch where the metal was polished by their feeding.

### The Totality

It is a world without hunger, because the food is generated by the floor itself; without fear, because there are no carnivores; and without silence, though nothing speaks.

The animals exist as an extension of the factory’s mechanical engineering. They are the scrubbers, the filters, the seals, and the thermal buffers of a system that was built to idle forever. If a human were to sit upon the warm zinc plate, the creatures would not scatter. Within an hour, the tongue-plates would begin to explore the soles of the boots with their vibrating scrapers, testing the rubber for digestible lipids. The white eels would coil gently around the ankles, seeking the warmth of the shins, their blind, smooth snouts nudging patiently against the fabric of the trousers, treating the intruder not as an alien presence, but simply as a slightly irregular section of pipe that has not yet begun to weep.
