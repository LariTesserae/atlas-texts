To survive in the cleft, the body must become an instrument of friction and weight. 

The people who live along the chasm walls are thick through the torso and short-limbed, built with the low center of gravity belonging to creatures that cannot afford to stumble. Because the diorite cuts like flint, their clothing is an assemblage of thick, boiled hides—repaired constantly with copper wire and waxed sinew—and heavy, stiff aprons of split rawhide that hang to their shins, shielding their legs from the razor-like margins of fresh-cut blocks. Their hands are distinctive: broad-palmed, the skin stained a faint, indelible grey-blue from diorite dust, with fingertips so hardened by contact with the raw rock that they seem almost clawed. Most bear fine, white cross-hatchings across their forearms and knuckles, the permanent record of working against stone that has not yet had its edges worn smooth.

Because the acoustic roar of the chasm drowns out normal speech, these people are largely silent. They do not shout; shouting is swallowed whole by the drone of the wind and the percussive slap of water on stone. Instead, they have developed an intricate, distance-spanning language of the hands and shoulders—rapid, sharp gestures that can be read clearly across three hundred yards of empty air against the dark timber of the galleries. 

For communication inside the stacked dwellings, where bodies are close, they use touch and vibration. A hand pressed flat against a companion’s collarbone transmits intent through bone; a deliberate, rhythmic tapping of an ash mallet on a primary cedar joist carries warnings of shifts, tension failures, or descending cargo through the entire frame of the settlement. When they do speak, it is with their foreheads touching, mouth pressed directly to the cup of the listener’s ear, murmuring in a low, breathy register that slips beneath the chasm’s thunder.

Their culture is governed by the state of the rock. There are individuals among them—often the older builders whose knees have stiffened from cold—whose sole task is to listen to the mountain. They spend hours seated on low stools at the deepest recesses of the galleries, pressing their bare temples against the weeping stone faces. They are listening for *the release*: the microscopic, glassy pings and subterranean groans of stone that is still relieving the immense tectonic stress that broke it from the earth. When a seam decides to breathe, they know whether to loosen the guy-wires or drive fresh cedar wedges into the joint.

In the horizontal pockets where the liverwort and ferns thrive, women and younger apprentices tend the hanging gardens. Their work is quiet and damp. They harvest the thick, fleshy leaves of the alpine succulents, which they press for an astringent, pale oil used to prevent their skin from cracking in the freezing, dry wind. From the black-stemmed ferns and the cushions of moss, they scrape a bitter, dark-green paste that is boiled with mineral salts and dried rations brought up on the winches. Their diet is simple, dominated by these bitter greens, cakes of ground seed stored in dry rock-cavities, and the cold, dense water that drips ceaselessly from the seams—water so heavily laden with dissolved minerals that it leaves a white, chalky crust on their clay cups.

Alongside the humans, a small, highly specialized ecology has found purchase in the seams between raw stone and timber.

The pale skinks live in the air-gaps of the dry-stacked masonry. They are nearly blind, with scales the color of skimmed milk and long, splayed toes tipped with hooked claws that can grip the glassy facets of the diorite. They move not with the quick dart of lowland lizards, but with a slow, deliberate slither, emerging only during the three hours of direct, searing noon sunlight to lick the mineral condensation from the timber posts. They have no fear of the human builders; they will crawl over a motionless stonemason’s boot to reach a patch of wet liverwort, their cold, dry bellies sliding across the leather.

The moths that drift through the chasm do not fly so much as navigate the turbulence. They are massive, heavily built insects, their wings covered in dense, charcoal-grey fur that sheds water like a duck’s back. During the violent afternoon wind-surges, they flatten themselves into the crevices of the cedar beams, folding their wings tightly over their bodies to become small, velvety wedges that cannot be pried loose by the gale. They feed on the pale, microscopic fungi that bloom in the dark spaces where wet timber meets wet stone, and their larvae—plump, translucent grubs that spin silk as tough as sewing thread—are gathered by the children as a delicacy or boiled down for adhesive.

Even deeper within the water-flumes, where the seep is constant and light never penetrates, lives a species of blind, flat-bodied crustacean, no larger than a pumpkin seed. They cling to the undersides of the hemlock sluice-boxes, grazing on the film of algae that coats the wood. When a sluice is cleaned or repaired, the workers toss them into their soup pots, where they turn bright vermilion and taste of iron and river-silt.

There is a strange, suspended serenity to life in this place. Because the world below is invisible, cut off by sheer drop and mist, the inhabitants’ entire universe consists of this three-walled corridor of light, stone, and wood. Their children grow up without an understanding of horizontal horizons; to them, distance is measured either up toward the dazzling sky or down into the misted, thundering throat of the cleft. 

At dusk, when the wind momentarily drops and the sun casts the shadow of one needle across the face of another, the settlement comes to rest. Small charcoal braziers are coaxed to life inside the dry-stone shelters, sending thin, blue threads of birch-smoke into the arcades. The people sit on the outer edges of the cedar platforms, their legs dangling over hundreds of feet of empty space, watching the opposite wall turn from pale blue to slate, and finally to charcoal. In the gathering dark, with the roar of the water settling into an all-encompassing, rhythmic pulse that feels like their own blood, they sign to one another across the void with small, swinging lanterns—brief, bright sparks of yellow fire speaking across the cold gap of the unfinished mountain.
