In the Basin of the Third Piston, life does not occur as an accident or an intrusion; it has grown into the tolerances of the machinery, filling the millimeters of clearance between iron and timber until the distinction between a biome and an engine has ceased to matter.

### The Scum-Scrapers and the Masticators

The smallest visible animal life exists as a shifting, mineral crust. The terracotta-shelled crustaceans—often called *boiler-ticks* or *potters* by those who once maintained the pumps—do not have true carapaces of chitin. Over generations spent grazing on the mineralized slime of the iron columns, their shells have incorporated heavy loads of oxidized hematite and scale. They are the color of dried brick, dry and powdery to the touch until the condensation cycle strikes, at which point their backs turn the glossy, dark hue of fresh liver.

They live in colonies so dense that their shells grind together like river gravel whenever the main walking beam completes its stroke. They have no eyes; in their place are pairs of sensitive, whip-like antennules tipped with oily pads that taste the temperature and sulfur content of the iron. A potter lives for six or seven years, cementing itself to the iron during its final months. When it dies, its lime-and-iron shell remains welded to the pipe, forming a raised, corrugated scab that provides foot-traction for the lianas and root-shoots climbing toward the heat.

Beneath the scabs, in the rotting layers of cork-mantle where the masonry has decayed, live the *tallow-grubs*. These are the larvae of the great grease-moths—soft, white, segmented tubes as thick as a wrist, with jaws made of black, serrated horn capable of cutting through seasoned oak and lead pipe-gaskets alike. They exude an alkaline slime that neutralizes the caustic acids of the engine’s exhaust, turning the surrounding root-wood into a sweet, rancid meal. They are entirely deaf to airborne sound, but they possess a deep-set nerve organ along their bellies that requires the low, four-stroke engine thud to stimulate peristalsis; if the engine stops, the grubs become impacted and die within hours.

When they emerge as grease-moths, their forms are broad and heavy. Their wings, spanning nearly two feet, are not dusted with powder but saturated with a dense, clear lard that drips from their trailing edges in the heat. They do not feed as adults. They have no mouthparts; they spend their brief four-day life drifting on the greasy thermal plumes that rise from the firebox seams, mating in mid-air and dropping their heavy, wax-coated egg clusters into the open lubrication cups of the reduction gears, where the warmth of the friction incubates them.

### The Mid-Vault Hunters

Between the pillars, where the dangling air-roots hang like the rigging of a sunken ship, the air belongs to the *skin-pipits*. 

These are not true birds, though they carry the vestige of a keel and avian lung. They are entirely hairless and featherless, their bodies covered in a pale, greasy skin the color of lard, veined with violet. Their wings are elongated finger-membranes, thin enough to reveal the pulse of blood beneath, and permanently damp from the oily fog. Their skulls are long, needle-nosed, and completely smooth where the eye-sockets have fused shut under a layer of cartilaginous armor. 

A pipit hunts by reading the air currents created by the engine’s exhaust. When the boiler vents every twenty minutes, the sudden gust of warm air throws hundreds of moths and flying beetles into turbulence; the pipits drop from the ceiling roots in coordinated drops, catching the insects against their leathery wing-webs and scooping them into their toothless, scissor-like beaks. 

They nest directly on the iron surfaces of the walking beams. The continuous vibration—which would shatter the eggs of any surface creature—is required by their embryos, whose circulatory systems rely on the seventy-ton rocking motion to move blood through the yolk. Pipit chicks are born with specialized, hooked claws on their elbows, instantly anchoring themselves to the weeping ferns and iron-moss before their first breath can be knocked out of them by the downstroke.

### The Sump Trench and the Floor-Marrow

The floor itself is alive, not merely as a root system, but as a digestive plain. The three-yard-thick mattress of compressed root-stock is threaded with the mycelium of the *cold-fire fungus*—the pale blue phosphor that lines the catwalks. The fungus feeds on the engine’s drippings: spent mineral tallow, dead crustaceans, pipit guano, and the black, unrefined crude that seeps from the pump packings.

Wallowing in the lowest drainage gutters are the *tallow-eels*. These creatures are blind, six feet long, and entirely legless, with bodies like waterlogged fire-hoses. Their skin is covered in a thick, sulfurous mucus that prevents the boiling machine oil in the sump trenches from blistering their flesh. They have no teeth; instead, their round, muscular mouths are ringed with hard, horn-plates that rasp the lipid cakes that form where oil meets standing water. 

When a piston misfires or the valve gear chatters out of time, the shockwave through the mud sends the eels into a frenzy. They knot together in writhing rafts of twenty or thirty, rolling over one another to scrape the rich grease-films off the sump walls, their pale bellies flashing in the dull cherry glow of the firebox slits.

### The Rhythm-Striders

Farther back, in the dim clearances between the six taproots where the air is hottest and the iron pillars sweat dark tannins, live the vault’s largest active dwellers: the *Striders*.

They are quadrupeds, roughly the size of a stabled pony, but flattened sideways as if they were pressed between two slabs of iron. Their hides are thick, hairless, and plated with calloused horn that has taken on the texture of the cork-wood they climb upon. They have long, multi-jointed limbs ending in massive, spoon-like claws designed to hook into the fluting of the iron columns and the thick bark of the roots.

The Striders are scavengers and regulators. They move with an unsettling, mechanical deliberation that is entirely governed by the beam engines. They do not possess a continuous, fluid stride; instead, they move in four distinct hitches:
* On the **intake stroke**, they reach forward and set their talons.
* On the **compression**, their thick shoulders bunch and lock.
* On the **power stroke**, they heave their mass upward or forward with explosive speed.
* On the **exhaust**, they pause, their broad, slit-like nostrils dilating to take in the greasy mist, venting a long, wet hiss through spiracles along their flanks.

They feed on the crustacean sheets, scraping yards of the terracotta shells away with their lower jaws, which are shovel-shaped and reinforced with dense, stone-like bone. They are also drawn to the lianas that thread the secondary reduction gears. A Strider will spend hours licking the clear tallow-wax from the vine nodes, its broad, rasping tongue keeping the plant nodes clean and polished, preventing the gears from fouling with bark debris while receiving a high-calorie diet of pure vegetable lipid.

They have no natural enemies in the vault, though they are intensely territorial about their respective pillars. A Strider will claim a single cast-iron column, defending it against others of its kind by drumming its heavy breastbone against the fluting—a rhythmic, booming clang that travels through the ironwork and mixes indistinguishably with the chatter of the valves.

### The Valve-Keepers

In the highest, hottest reaches of the chamber—where the six massive taproots pierce the ceiling masonry and the air is thick with 110-degree steam—there are nests made of braided lianas, grease-moth wings, and felted root-marrow. These are inhabited by the *Tenders*.

Whether they were once human beings who climbed down the drainage shafts centuries ago during the construction of the drainage works, or whether they are something that drifted in through the city’s sewer-veins and adapted to the dark, is impossible to tell from their anatomy. They are long-limbed and slight, rarely standing more than four feet tall, with skin that is completely pigmentless, tough as vulcanized rubber, and permanently stained with machine grease and yellow iron-moss spores.

Their eyes have not disappeared; rather, they have enlarged into wide, wet, amber globes that have no eyelids, protected instead by a clear, nictitating membrane that washes them with oily tears against the sulfur steam. They are completely mute, their vocal cords having atrophied in an environment where no sound softer than a bronze valve-slam can be heard.

The Tenders live in absolute symbiosis with the machine-forest. They do not build fires, nor do they make tools of stone or bone; their only instruments are discarded iron bolts, cotter pins, and bronze shims they have pried from defunct catwalks and ground sharp against the flywheel housing.

They spend their waking hours tending the lianas that run through the reduction gears. With terrifying agility, swinging from dangling air-roots with their long, prehensile toes, they prune the excess leaf-growth from the gear teeth using their bronze scrapers. They guide the young shoots through the spokes of the turning wheels, weaving them into the existing braided cables so that the plant's natural growth does not jam the bronze bushings. 

In return, the Tenders harvest the "boiler-manna"—the thick, nutrient-rich sap that the taproots secrete where their wood is scorched by the steam lines. It is a dense, yellow resin, tasting of turpentine and sugar, which they store in hollowed-out gourds formed by the root-nodes. They drink the condensation that runs down the fluting of the pumping pillars, licking the iron clean with long, pale tongues that are calloused against the rust.

When a Tender dies, their companions do not bury them in the root-floor, which would rot too slowly in the oily peat. Instead, they carry the body to the central flywheel housing. There, waiting for the massive, forty-span wheel to reach its slow dead-center, they slide the corpse between the rim and the cork-wood bumper. In a single, heavy revolution, the body is drawn into the clearance, pressed into the fibrous mantle, and converted into tallow, fiber, and bone-meal—becoming part of the grease that keeps the Third Piston turning through the deep, wet dark.
