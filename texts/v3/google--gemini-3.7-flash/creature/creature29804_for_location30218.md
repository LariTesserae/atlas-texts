To live here, a thing must not be soft. It cannot rely on the wet chemistry of cells, the slosh of blood, or the fragile evaporation of breath. In an atmosphere without moisture and a city without decay, life has long since shed the liabilities of organic tissue. What remains has migrated into the mineral kingdom: an ecology of dry friction, sintered ceramics, heavy hydrocarbons, and thermoelectric exchange.

These beings do not have names, for there is no language to call them, but their forms and habits belong entirely to the plateau’s enduring thermal balance.

***

### The Grazers of the Foundation

Beneath the rounded skirts of the blast furnaces and along the oily margins of the viaduct piers dwell the flat things. 

They are wide, low-profile carapaces that resemble paving stones or discarded hatch-plates, roughly the size of heavy iron shields. Their upper shells are formed from laminations of dense magnetite, slag-glass, and compressed shale, worn by time into smooth, domed lenses that shed the dry drifting dust without a seam. 

Beneath this mineral armor, there is no meat. Instead, their undercarriages are packed with intricate arrays of dry, flexible reed-levers made of hardened vulcanite and thin brass leaf. They walk on several dozen needle-tipped ceramic pins. When they move across the shale or the purple-black iron plates, they do so with a sound like fine sand trickling through a brass funnel—a dry, continuous *shhh-tink* that carries only an inch into the thin air before vanishing into the bedrock.

They are the city’s slow cleansers. Their mouths are not jaws, but counter-rotating abrasive discs of hard corundum. They press these against the foundations, shaving away the microscopic frost of sublimated zinc, sulfur bloom, and ancient, polymerized lubricants that sweat from the stone. 

They derive their energy from the difference in temperature between their bellies and their backs. Their undersides absorb the heavy, dark heat of the basalt floor; their upper shells radiate that heat away into the starless pitch above. In that slow, constant thermal siphon, a faint electrical current flows through their internal copper braids, keeping their escapements turning. A single square yard of sulfurous masonry can sustain one of these creatures for three centuries.

### The Catenary Drapers

High above the boulevards, suspended from the thick, sagging bitumen power conduits, lives another kind of entity entirely: long, slender, tension-based forms that look like frayed lengths of braided cable or strips of dry, oil-cured leather.

They do not touch the ground. Their limbs are caliper-like clamps made of cold-rolled spring steel, wrapped around the overhead lines with a grip so total that only the microscopic creep of the metal allows them to slide. They travel upside-down along the catenaries at a rate of perhaps three feet a year.

Their bodies hang downward into the dry warmth: long, segmented ribbons of woven mica and lead-cloth that sway with imperceptible slowness. They have no thermal nerve-plates on their fronts; instead, their entire length is strung with fine, resonant wires like an unplucked harp. 

These drapers do not feed on minerals. They feed on the tectonic and structural frequencies of the city. 

Whenever a deep piston strokes leagues away—that rare, subterranean *thum*—or when a cooling block shifts a micron on its bed of shale, the suspended cables vibrate. The drapers catch these vibrations in their hanging ribbons. Internal crystalline organs, squeezed by the rhythmic shudder of the wires, generate piezo-electric charges that store energy in heavy lead-oxide nodes along their spines.

They are the listeners. When two drapers meet along the same conduit—an event that requires centuries of slow approach—they do not fight or mate in any conventional sense. They simply interlace their hanging wires, sharing the accumulated tension and vibration of their respective spans, equalizing their internal charges until they resonate as a single, doubled instrument.

***

### The Sump-Weavers

Deep inside the thermal sumps, where the air shimmers with dry heat and the bottom of the shafts glow with that faint, sullen cherry-red, exist the closest things the city has to stationary colonies.

They resemble vast, drooping tapestries or crystalline soot-webs hung across the throat of the vents. They are composed of pure carbon: million-layered sheets of flexible graphite, woven into porous, honeycomb sails that billow upward on the rising heat currents without ever tearing.

The sumps exhale faint plumes of dry metallic vapors—trace bismuth, lead gas, and volatile hydrocarbons that have been boiling in the planet’s crust for eons. The weavers act as condensing filters. Their graphite webs catch these atoms, precipitating them into tiny, perfectly spherical beads of dense alloy that grow along the web’s fringes like lead fruit.

When a bead becomes too heavy—perhaps once every fifty years—it snaps its thread and drops into the cherry-red depths below, striking the sump floor with a clear, bell-like chime that rings through the foundations. The recoil of the dropping weight sends a ripple through the web, triggering the release of an ultra-fine, microscopic spore: a single flake of carbon, carrying a magnetic imprint of the parent web, that drifts out of the chimney and settles on a distant conduit or uncolonized flue.

***

### The Monoliths of the Boulevards

Rarely—so rarely that an observer would need to watch for an epoch to confirm it—something moves across the wide, empty expanses of powdered shale.

These are the city’s largest inhabitants: massive, low, segmented monoliths that could easily be mistaken for derailed locomotive boilers or shifting sections of the viaduct itself. They are twenty paces long, low to the ground, and built of immense blocks of cast iron and basalt held together by thick bands of vulcanized rubber. 

They have no limbs. They move by the principle of thermal expansion and contraction. 

The front of the monolith slowly absorbs bedrock heat until it expands forward by a fraction of an inch, its textured iron belly gripping the shale with directional microscopic teeth. Then, as the heat migrates toward its cooler rear, the tail contracts, drawing the entire multi-ton mass forward. 

They are the great balancers. Their interiors are hollow, packed with tons of paraffin wax and heavy mineral tallow that melt and solidify in long, predictable cycles. As they crawl across the grid of the city, they act as mobile thermal capacitors, soaking up excess heat from overly warm manifolds and bleeding it off into the colder, open shale wastes.

They have no eyes, no ears, and no desires. Their intelligence, if it exists at all, is a form of deep, mechanical contemplation that operates at the speed of cooling rock. When two of these monoliths cross paths in the wide boulevards, their immense iron bulks brush against one another with an agonizingly slow, dry shriek of metal on metal—a dry, polishing friction that leaves both surfaces as reflective and black as obsidian before they pass, taking a century to put a mile of silence between them once again.

***

### The Life of No Ending

Nothing ever truly dies here, because there is no biological engine to break down.

When a grazer’s ceramic pins finally wear down to flat stubs after a million leagues of travel, it does not perish in pain. Its corundum disc merely slows. Its internal escapement misses a beat, then another, and finally comes to rest against a dead boiler plate. 

The warmth from the floor continues to rise through it; the cold void above continues to draw heat away. It remains there, perfectly preserved, a shiny purple-black nodule on the side of a smelting hall, indistinguishable from the bolt-heads and flanges that surround it. 

After a thousand years of dry dust-drift, a younger grazer may crawl over its shell, delicately shaving the sublimated sulfur from its dormant back, leaving the old machine smooth, polished, and permanently at rest in the eternal, starless warmth.
