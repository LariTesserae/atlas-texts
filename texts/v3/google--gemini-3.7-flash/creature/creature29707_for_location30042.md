The ones who laid the fish-teeth were not strangers to the seam; they are what happens when marrow is asked to survive under forty atmospheres of silent brine.

They are small enough to sleep within the hollow of a single broken liverwort leaf—creatures of pale, curdled tallow and jointed glass, whose bodies have no bones, only a series of interlocking calcified plates that slide against one another with the dry, microscopic rasp of mica sheets. They have no eyes, for there is nothing to meet an eye, but their forelegs terminate in tripartite needles of extraordinary hardness, grown from the concentrated lime they leach out of the water.

They do not swim. To swim in this syrupy, stubborn fluid would be like trying to fly through cold pitch. Instead, they *lever*. They insert their needle-claws into the pores of the limestone and haul their dense, coin-thin bodies forward, a single micron at a time, their plate-edges clicking softly against the jade bladders as they squeeze through the mesh. 

A single lifetime for one of these masons is measured not by breaths, but by the placement of perhaps four teeth. 

They do not hunt the fish whose teeth they stack; those teeth are ancient drift, washed into the fissure during the sudden, violent hour of its drowning a hundred years ago and trapped there forever. The masons harvest them from the silt-cracks, scrubbing each ivory peg with a corrosive secretion from their lower palps until the root is squared and true. They set them with a mortar made of their own shed cuticle mixed with dissolved chalk—a paste that cures under pressure into something harder than the bedrock itself.

Inside the wax-sealed niches, there is no dead dust. The wax—rendered from the lipid-rich fat that collects at the joints of the wire-moss—protects their clutches: not eggs in the soft, biological sense, but faceted, dodecahedral cysts of semi-solid tallow. Within each cyst, an embryo sits perfectly suspended in an oil denser than the surrounding water, its limbs folded in the exact posture of the slate lintels above it. These cysts do not hatch on a calendar; they wait for a change in hydrostatic tension, a infinitesimal shudder through the stone that signals a tooth has settled or a lintel has found its true bearing. Some have sat in their dark alcoves since the pocket was first sealed, their transparent hearts beating once every three weeks, sending a faint, pale pulse of green light through the wax wall—a rhythmic bioluminescent grease that never quite dies.

Between these builders moves a second presence, less formed, almost fluid. 

Where the flat, ribbon-like planarian drags its four segmented horns, it leaves more than mucus. It is not an intruder, but the seam’s only true courier, an organism whose flesh is so thin that its digestive tract is merely a branching pattern of empty space within its clear gelatin. As it glides across the jade-colored bladders, its horns tap the masonry with a rhythm so faint it can only be felt as a vibration through the dense liquid. It tastes the lime-settlement; it gauges whether the apex of the wedge has narrowed by a hair’s width under the weight of the sea above.

Within its body, carried in the translucent folds behind its head, are tiny, spherical colonies of silver rotifers—animals so small they exist entirely between the ripples of the ribbon-worm’s crawl. They live by grazing the microscopic phosphorescence from the wire-tips, their spinning crowns creating minute vortices that do not move the water, but merely churn its mineral salt into fine, edible threads. When the planarian reaches the dark crevice where the stone pinches shut, these passengers do not disembark; they calcify against the rock, adding their tiny, pearl-like shells to the natural grain of the limestone, slowly smoothing the jagged faults into polished, marble flutes.

Nothing here eats violently. In this cold syrup, ingestion is a form of osmosis, an exchange of chalk and lipid so quiet it resembles the growth of crystals more than metabolism. Even the plants are partially sentient in the way that tension is sentient: the jade bladders are sensory organs for the masons, hollow resonance chambers that amplify the deep, tectonic groans of the reef outside. When an outer ocean storm passes miles above, the bladders do not sway—they contract, their interior gas pockets shrinking by a tenth of a percent, emitting a rapid, dry *click-tink-tink* that tells the creatures deep in the tangle to wedge themselves tighter beneath their lintels.

They have no language, but they have alignment. When a slate slab is set perfectly horizontal to the hydrostatic plane, the masonry hums in sympathy with the water’s pressure, creating a dead-spot of absolute stillness in the pocket’s core. In that dead-spot, where even the cold syrup ceases to press, the builders gather to shed their outer plates, leaving behind clear, glass-like casts of themselves that remain wedged between the plant-stems forever—translucent ghosts standing upright in the porticos, slowly being swallowed by the green liverworts until they are simply stone again, holding up the roof of a world that does not know the sky exists.
