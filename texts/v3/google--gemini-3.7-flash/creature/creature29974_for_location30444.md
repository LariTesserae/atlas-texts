The shelters on the summits are not abandoned. 

To mistake them for ruins is an error born of a faster, wetter world. Here, where a single millimeter of rock takes a hundred years to rise from the depths, an inhabitant may sit motionless for three centuries simply listening to the tonal shift of a single basalt facet as it cools, its attention fixed upon the microscopic widening of a joint.

They have no flesh, for flesh is an unstable sponge of moisture that would boil and freeze simultaneously in this dry neon vacuum. What occupies these spires are long, narrow figures—tessellated beings whose frames are composed of dry, porous apatite, chalk-marrow, and dense bands of calcified sinew. They stand eight to ten feet tall, but their silhouettes are so thin, so strictly aligned with the vertical striations of the hexagonal pillars, that even from a few paces away they appear as natural fluting in the stone. 

Their outer surface is neither skin nor shell, but an aggregate of compressed slate-dust and gray talc, constantly sloughing and replenishing through the absorption of airborne silica. They have no nostrils, no lungs, and no need for the heavy neon gas that pools in the chasms; their broad, flattened ribcages are not bellows, but open xylophonic chambers. The deafening, monolithic scream of the tectonic crystallization does not deafen them—it is their metabolism. The continuous acoustic assault enters the hollow bone-spokes of their chests, vibrating the internal lattice of dry crystals that forms their nervous core. They do not eat; they resonate. To move is to bleed off acoustic pressure; to remain still is to charge.

Their heads are smooth, planar wedges of dense orthoclase feldspar, unmarred by eyes or mouths. They perceive the realm entirely through seismic conduction and electro-reception. When they press the four-jointed talons of their hands into the sulfur-moss, they do not feel texture; they "see" the stress-fractures running five leagues deep into the planet's root. They track the invisible, drifting gravity wells by the way the hairs of spun glass on their wrists bend toward the sky.

When they move, they do not walk with a cadence of steps. They move with a slow, deliberate levering, using heavy mallets of diorite and chert wedges to test the grain of the rock before committing their weight. Gravity here is not a single downward command, but a patchwork of contradictory loyalties. An elder of these spires—distinguishable by the deep coats of plum-colored electro-lichen thriving across its shoulders—will lean out over a five-mile drop, place its foot onto empty space, and step downward onto a ceiling of invisible tension, its long bone-fibers creaking in dry, perfect equilibrium between two opposing spires.

The suspension bridges are their work, but they are not merely paths for travel; they are nervous systems strung between severed geometries. The braided silicate vines and calcified willows act as acoustic conduits, carrying the warning hum of a shifting pillar from one redoubt to the next. When a bridge begins to shriek with too high a pitch, the beings know that the chasm beneath it is about to twist—that its local gravity plane is about to invert. 

And the ritual piles of chalky slivers around their perches are neither trash nor offerings, but their dead, their tools, and their chronometers, all at once. 

When one of these beings reaches the end of its resonance—when its marrow becomes too densely calcified to vibrate with the planet’s scream—it does not decay. It stiffens into true stone. Its kin do not bury it; they bring it to the edge of the highest sheer face and carve its limbs into thin, resonant needles. These are the chimes hung along the windy voids. As the crushing acoustic roar beats against the dead bone, it sings for a decade, a century, until the violent frequencies finally snap the calcified sliver with a clean, dry crack. 

The splinter falls, drifting downward, then sideways, then settling upon the ledge. The living gather the shard and place it neatly into the pile: one more measure of the world’s birth-cry recorded, numbered, and laid to rest in the cold, dry dark.
