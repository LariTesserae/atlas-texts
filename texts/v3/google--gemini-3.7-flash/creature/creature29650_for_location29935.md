To understand what lives here, one must first understand that there is no boundary where the stone ends and the flesh begins. The creatures of the basin do not inhabit the rock so much as they are an extension of its erosion—calcified, mineral-fed extensions of the black slate, driven by cold pulses of colorless blood.

At the lowest stratum, welded directly to the moisture-slicked floor, are the **Lithophages**, the plate-grazers. They are broad, overlapping shingles of dark, dense keratin and incorporated iron-silicates, shaped like flattened shields with beveled edges. They possess no eyes, no distinct heads, and no dorsal appendages that might catch or tear in the crush. Their entire underbelly is a single, muscular crawler-sole lined with four parallel rows of hardened rasping plates—radulae made of bio-grown pyrite that grind against the bedrock with the tireless, oscillating rhythm of piston heads. 

They do not merely scrape the pale bacterial grease from the stone; they mill the stone itself, ingesting the slick chemosynthetic mat along with the weathered top millimeter of slate. Their digestive tracts are crushing mills that extract sulfur compounds and trace minerals, voiding behind them a constant, micro-fine slurry of polished mineral flour. In doing so, they lubricate their own passage and create the very mud in which their young will brood. Because they move in contiguous, unbroken sheets thousands of individuals wide, their carapace edges rub together perpetually, producing that dry, deafening sound of a mountain being turned to gravel in a mortar.

Above this grinding floor of shields walks a second, completely different tier of life: the **Stilt-Walkers** and **Gleaners**. These animals never touch the bedrock. Their entire world is the shifting, hard-shelled terrain of the Lithophages’ backs. 

They are spindly, ghost-pale things, some the size of outstretched hands, others spanning meters across their ultra-fine, multi-jointed limbs. Their legs terminate in sharp, recurved needle-claws designed specifically to hook into the breathing notches and suture lines between the plates below. They move with an erratic, mechanical high-stepping gait, picking delicately through the moving floor. Their heads are dominated by bundles of hyper-sensitive, velvet-textured antennae that constantly lash the humid air, tasting for the sharp tang of freshly cracked shell, spilled hemolymph, or the sulfuric musk of an expired grazer.

Among these scavengers are the **Needle-Mouths**, possessing elongated, three-jointed proboscises tipped with diamond-grit drills. When a grazer slows—either exhausted by the crush or fouled by its own mineral excrement—the Needle-Mouths crowd onto its dome in dozens, anchoring their legs, and drive their drilling mouthparts through the shell seams. They pump in digestive enzymes that liquefy the grazer’s inner musculature while it is still crawling, sucking the nutritious broth out through the narrow boreholes until the hollowed shell buckles under the weight of the swarm and is crushed into flakes by its neighbors.

In the sunken pans where mineral-heavy runoff gathers into shallow, icy tarns, the ecology swells into massive, sedentary forms known as the **Cistern-Beasts**. These are the siphon-feeders—great, pulsing mounds of gray, blubbery muscle the size of haystacks or wagons, anchored into the deep bedrock by thick roots of calcified tissue. 

A Cistern-Beast is little more than a living bilge pump. It draws the stagnant, ammonia-tainted water through massive, fringed intake rings that dilate and contract with an agonizing slowness, straining the liquid through internal forest-gills of mucus-coated filaments that trap suspended bacteria and free-swimming larvae. Every few minutes, having built up hydraulic pressure within its rubbery mantle, it expels the filtered water through a vertical chimney in a violent, chuffing spout that atomizes into the heavy air, adding to the eternal condensation dripping from the overcast sky. 

The skins of these great boulders of flesh are not smooth; they are pitted, wrinkled, and encrusted with miniature ecosystems. Colonies of blind, barnacle-like **Cup-Worms** bore into their outer hide, snapping their feathery mouthparts out to catch the fallout of the beast's exhalations. In the deep folds of their skin, small, soft-bodied vermin hide from the hunters that patrol the fringes of the pools.

These hunters are the **Wedge-Strikers**—the apex efficiency of the basin. Heavy, low-slung, and armored in glossy, oil-black chitin, they do not crawl; they lunge. Their bodies are built around massive, internal hydraulic chambers filled with pressurized hemolymph. When a Striker identifies prey—using a lateral line of vibration-sensitive pits along its flanks that read the acoustic signature of the bedrock—it triggers a release valve. 

In a fraction of a second, its fore-limbs, which are thick, chisel-tipped pickaxes of dense bone-chitin, strike forward with explosive force, shattering carapaces like porcelain. A Striker does not linger over its meal. It feeds frantically, ramming its serrated mouthparts into the breach, tearing away soft meat while violently thrashing its spined, muscular tail to clear a space around itself. It has only seconds to feed before the unstoppable, crawling tide of grazers and scavenging legs pushes in from all sides, burying the kill under a mountain of blind, indifferent mass.

And above this entire crawling floor, filling the damp, gray air between the rock and the low clouds, is the acoustic layer. The frantic, high-frequency chirping comes from the **Chitter-Drifts**—thousands of small, moth-like organisms whose wings have hardened into acoustic clappers. They have no eyes; they fly in erratic, bounding arcs through the dense air, vibrating their hardened wing-covers against their thoraxes to generate sonic pulses, navigating entirely by the echo thrown back by the heaving floor below and the sheer mudstone bluffs around them. 

They feed on the aerosolized grease and drifting spores cast up by the Cistern-Beasts’ exhalations, hovering in thick, dancing swarms over the pans. When their energy fails, or when their delicate wings become too heavy with moisture, they drop like lead sinkers into the living carpet below, where a hundred blind claws tear them to nothing before they have even ceased to twitch.

Nothing in this place has a name for itself, nor any conception of a world beyond the touch of cold stone and the scrape of neighbor against neighbor. It is an ecosystem that has perfected a closed loop: stone becomes slime, slime becomes shell, shell is ground back into stone. There is no birth that is not an immediate scramble to find footing on the backs of the living, and no death that leaves even an ash of waste behind. It is pure, blind function, turning upon itself forever under the gray, sweating sky.
