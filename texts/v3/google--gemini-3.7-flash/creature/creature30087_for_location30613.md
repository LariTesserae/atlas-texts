To speak of what lives here as “flora” or “inhabitants” is already to use the wrong taxonomy. Nothing in the basin possesses a nervous system, yet nothing is inert. What resides here is a spectrum of violent, self-machining vegetative forms—organisms whose biology is calibrated not in calories or cell division, but in torque, amperage, and burst-pressure. 

The primary rulers of the basin floor are the **Stator-Trees**. These are not singular plants, but massive, parasitic vascular scaffolds that have fused permanently with the drowned multi-story generators. Their trunks do not grow outward in rings of soft wood; they deposit concentric layers of slag-lignin, heavily impregnated with iron filings and vulcanized rubber. Their taproots penetrate the dynamo casings, splitting the water-logged cast iron with the slow, unstoppable hydraulic pressure of growing roots, wrapping their fibrous root-veins directly around the spinning copper armatures. They do not eat; they *ground*. They draw the roaring, high-voltage inductive fields through dense, braid-like networks of copper-xylem, bleeding the excess current into towering fronds of corrugated sheet-iron that shimmer with purple corona discharge. When a stator-tree reaches electrical saturation, its crown does not flower—it arcs, discharging thousands of volts into the soup of the basin, flash-boiling the surrounding water and shattering its own top-tier branches into jagged, sinking shrapnel that instantly takes root in the basalt below.

In the mid-water currents, suspended between the floor and the silent miles of black ocean above, drift the **Auger-Thorns**. These are mobile, though entirely non-animal. Their bodies are rigid, helical stalks of brass-veined timber, up to forty feet long, spiraled like industrial drill bits. Lacking leaves, they are ringed with hard, chattering fins made of bone-dense, silicified cellulose that are tuned to the precise vibrational frequency of the submerged turbines. The acoustic trauma of the basin does not damage them; it drives them. The deafening, low-frequency sledgehammer-blows through the water cause their harmonic fins to ratchet against internal, fibrous escapements, spinning the entire helical organism continuously on its axis. They drill perpetually through the brine, propelled forward by the roar of the engines, boring through kelp forests, grinding into exposed concrete bridges, and shredding one another in blind, mechanical collisions. When they breach a pocket of superheated brine, the sudden thermal expansion triggers a violent, internal spring-release: the stalk splinters explosively, firing barbed, needle-like splinters into the current that embed themselves in the rubber gaskets and iron joints of distant machinery.

Closer to the chasms, where the basalt is split open and the water is hot enough to melt lead, live the **Bellows-Galls**. These grow like massive, clustered tumors directly over the high-pressure exhaust ports and thermal vents. Formed from thick, corrugated sheets of oil-cured bark that function like heavy industrial bellows, they expand and contract with the shuddering rhythm of the steam venting beneath them. Inside their hollow, cavernous interiors, they do not hold nectar or seeds, but boiling slurry and pressurized gas-ice. The Bellows-Galls act as thermal exchangers: they suck in the cold, zinc-stained water through fibrous, one-way valves, compress it using the kinetic force of the vent’s own exhaust, and blast it back out as a jet of hyper-saline fluid mixed with oily, embryonic spore-grafts. These spore-grafts are heavy, brassy nodules the size of cannonballs, rolling along the basalt floor, driven by the violent underwater gales until their barbed anchors catch on a vibrating turbine casing.

Creeping over every stationary surface—the abandoned pump stations, the dead conveyor spans, the razor edges of the basalt—is the **Galvanic Scum**. It is an unbroken, sheet-like colonial organism that looks like peeling lead foil and feels like cold industrial grease. It is neither moss nor lichen, but a vast, continuous printed circuit of metallic plant tissue. The scum is acutely thigmotropic and electro-receptive; it grows along lines of structural stress and electrical leakage, laying down microscopic traces of native copper and zinc within its cellulose walls. Where the current is strongest, the scum thickens into dense, throbbing mats that open thousands of tiny, pore-like orifices, exhaling thin ribbons of diesel-smelling sap and fine bubbles of hydrogen. It effectively "eats" the decay of the machines, digesting rust and mechanical vibration, while simultaneously insulating and protecting the iron beneath it to preserve its own thermal foundation.

In the gravity anomalies, where the physics of the rift begin to buckle, life takes on an even more disorienting, recursive geometry. Here drift the **Inverted Drifts**—clumps of hollow, iron-plated bulb-weed that are lighter than the surrounding brine only while subjected to extreme acoustic violence. In the dead center of a turbine’s whine, these plants "fall" rapidly upward, their broad, tin-like shears slicing through the brine toward the surface. But as soon as they rise above the acoustic threshold of the basin, their internal gas bladders collapse under the cold ambient pressure, their buoyancy fails, and they plunge back down into the hellish din like falling anvils, only to be caught, energized, and hurled upward again in an eternal, oscillating kinetic cycle.

Nothing here rests, nothing sleeps, and nothing dies of old age. Death in the basin is exclusively mechanical: a stalk is sheared by a propeller blade, a root-system is vaporized by a thermal eruption, or an overcharged dynamo explodes, reducing a forest of metal-kelp to drifting mulch. But the mulch is instantly re-consumed; the oily sap coats the gears, the seed-spindles wedge into the cracked housings, and the frantic, deafening engine of the basin continues to build, tear, and rebuild itself out of timber, copper, and scalded brine, utterly blind to the soft world of flesh and bone that exists so far above it.
