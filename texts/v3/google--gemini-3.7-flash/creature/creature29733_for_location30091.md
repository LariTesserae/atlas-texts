In a place where space folds upon its own seams and the air does not permit an echo, life does not occur as an intrusion upon the mineral. It begins as a condensation—an intensification of the plateau’s own grease, weight, and geometry.

The shallow cisterns, which appear to be nothing more than sheets of black glass reflecting the indigo sky, are not empty. Their surfaces are skinned with *pellicles*—broad, paper-thin plates of living tallow that float flush with the meniscus. They have no underside and no eyes; they are entirely two-dimensional organisms, sometimes thirty yards wide, composed of an oily, cellular velvet the color of wet slate. They feed on the raw ultraviolet light of the unobstructed sun, metabolizing it into a clear, viscous gelatin that keeps the cisterns from ever icing over. When the moss on the distant plains exhales its four-minute plume of steam, the pellicles shiver, sending a single, concentric wave across the water that does not splash against the stone banks, but simply ceases to exist when it reaches the edge.

Beneath the liverwort mats, where the bedrock sweats its cold lubricant, move the *sledges*. 

They are not animals in any familiar sense, having neither limbs nor breath, but are thick, segmented cushions of dense, liver-colored muscle, encased in a hard, horn-like cuticle derived from swallowed grit. A sledge may take four months to cross a single terrace. As it moves, it does not crawl; it relies on the rhythmic, hyperventilating expansion of the moss above it. Each time the tundra swells and sighs out its steam, the sledge slides forward a fraction of an inch upon the film of oil, propelled by the collective respiration of the vegetable carpet. In their bellies, which are cold and acidic, they carry pounds of ancient quartzite silt, slowly grinding the plateau even flatter, functioning as living planing-irons that have spent hundred-thousand-year cycles smoothing the bedrock to its mirror finish.

Within the cyclopean cities, however, the life takes on the horrifying logic of the masonry.

The tenements are not abandoned; they are inhabited by things that could only have been formed by the sheer pressure of repetitive, unrecorded time. These are the *Pillarmen*—though they are not men, nor are they stone, but something caught in the petrified midpoint between the two.

They stand in the unglazed window-frames and deep lintel-recesses of the five-story drystone blocks. Their bodies are tall, dry, and columnar, covered in a tough, gray hide that resembles the bark of a birch tree if it were cast in lead. They possess no faces, only a vertical fissure down the center of the head, packed tightly with fine, pale bristles that sift the drifting iron-fog for microscopic flakes of dried liverwort.

Because of the sheared space within the avenues, a Pillarman does not occupy a single location. One may stand perfectly motionless in a second-floor doorway on the western avenue, while its heavy, spade-like hands rest on a windowsill three streets away, dangling into an alley that loops through the base of the same building. They do not perceive this as distance or distortion; their nervous systems are dispersed along the stress-fractures of the city’s geometry. If you were to touch the rough, cold flank of one standing in a courtyard, a dry, wooden tremor would run through the masonry beneath your feet, and thirty cubits above, an arm of the same creature would subtly shift its grip upon a coping stone.

They move only during the twice-daily fog, when the acoustic collapse silences the impact of their weight. When the iron-scented vapor rolls in from the horizon, the Pillarmen descend from their recesses with a slow, hydraulic deliberate-ness. They do not walk with a stride, but lever themselves along the drystone walls using their long, jointless forearms, their bodies dragging like plumb-bobs. 

They are drawn to the quarry pits. 

There, among the split blocks and the damp hemp ropes, they gather in absolute, soundless congress. They do not use the antler picks or the hammers—those tools were made for hands that possessed thumbs and hurried pulses—but they tend to them. With their broad, spatula-like fingers, they turn the wedges, clear the slate dust from the cut-grooves, and moisten the swollen wood with droplets of cold oil secreted from their palms. They are maintaining the state of *interruption*. They keep the quarry precisely as it was at the moment the builders vanished, as if the work were scheduled to resume within the hour, preserving the half-split megaliths against the erosion of the thin wind.

Higher up, where the walls intersect at impossible non-angles, there are the *Shadow-skins*.

These are the drifting rectangular silhouettes that detach from the masonry. They are not absences of light, but ultra-thin, airborne organisms consisting of a dense, light-absorbing pigment held together by a gossamer matrix of fungal thread. They sail flat against the vertical faces of the slate towers, feeding on the intense, cold radiation that beats down from the black zenith. When two Shadow-skins meet on the surface of a wall, they do not collide; they pass through one another with a faint, papery rasp—a sound that, in the inverted acoustics of the Shelf, rattles the teeth like a wooden mallet struck against a beam. When they die, they do not fall; they simply dry into gray scale and are incorporated into the lichen, giving the city walls their mottled, charcoal patina.

Nothing here dies quickly, because there is no heat to waste on decay. When a creature reaches the end of its centuries, its fluids do not spoil; they are drawn outward by the dry, tropospheric air, crystallizing into delicate, greasy crusts that the liverwort slowly overgrows and absorbs. 

And deeper in the labyrinths—in those central plazas where a single straight street crosses itself four times without ever turning—there sits the *Consensus*. 

It is a singular, communal mass of gray marrow, several hundred cubits long, that fills the lower floors of an entire city block like a subterranean river of cold fat. It does not speak, it does not build, and it has no eyes. But at four-minute intervals, precisely synchronized with the tundra that stretches for five thousand miles around the stone walls, the Consensus contracts. It draws the cold, oily condensation out of the gutters, pulls the heavy vapor down through the chimneys, and then, with a sound like a great ship’s timbers groaning under a wave, exhales it back into the sky as a plume of pure, white steam—keeping the air thin, keeping the slate damp, and ensuring that the Shelf remains balanced upon the peak of the world, suspended forever between freezing and the fall.
