To understand what lives in this vault, one must first understand that sound here is not heard; it is inhabited. The layered roar of the conduits and the deep, seismic cycling of the floorplates do not leave room for ears as the surface knows them. The things that walk, cling, and drift through the warm gloom have replaced tympanic membranes with solid acoustic wedges—broad plates of cartilage or synthetic-bonded bone fused directly into their jaw hinges and clavicles. They do not hear the infrastructure; they ride its frequencies, navigating the vast spans of empty air by reading the interference patterns of the hum.

Down in the condensation hollows, where the water is warm and tastes of sweet, degraded ethers, life begins as grease. The bacterial mats that coat the submerged composite gratings are thick as boiled hide, marbled in ochre and dull copper from the leaching of copper-alloy pipework miles upstream. Grazing upon these mats are the *wafer-lice*—the pale, paper-thin isopods whose carapaces are so thin that the pulse of their amber-colored digestive tracts can be counted against the faint blue gleam of the status diodes. They have no eyes, only fan-like antennae that drift in the lukewarm currents, catching microscopic flakes of shed gasket-compound and dead lichen. They move without hurry, for they have no sudden predators in the pools; their only threat is the cyclic dry-out of a blocked drain, which leaves them pressed into the composite like dried vellum until the next shower reconstitutes them.

Above the pools, on the vertical faces of the charcoal pylons, the ecology grows predatory and dry.

The *clackers*—the creatures whose long legs tap out rhythms on the overhead girders—are not spiders, though they have their posture. They are long-bodied, jointed things whose chitin has incorporated the fine ceramic dust that hangs in the air, giving their limbs the matte, chalky texture of unglazed porcelain. Their legs are five or six times the length of their bodies, tipped with magnetic claws that lock onto the composite’s flux-lines with a dry *snick*. A clacker spends weeks motionless along the underside of a superheated gas pipe, its broad belly pressed flat against the lagging to absorb the radiant heat. It does not look for prey; it waits for the thermal silhouette of a climber crossing the gap between cold structural ribs and hot junctions. When it strikes, it does not bite—it uses a pair of modified forelegs to pierce the prey's shell and siphon out fluid that has already been warmed and partially thinned by the machine’s own heat.

Higher still, in the gulf of warm, stagnant air between the floorplates and the ceiling vaults, lives the largest native of the void: the *pelt-kites*. 

They are descendants of nothing recognizable, perhaps born from the synthetic tissue cultures once used to seal high-altitude pressure doors, now feral and self-replicating. They resemble great, rectangular sheets of leathery, translucent skin, four to six meters across, stiffened by a light framework of hollow, cartilage-like spars. The pelt-kites do not fly by flapping; they are pure aerostats. They sleep during the quiet hours, clinging to the underside of the cantilevered catwalks like dropped drop-cloths. When the great pressure differential shifts—when the low, concussive thud ripples through the vault and the warm lung-gust sweeps across the chasm—the kites let go.

They rise on the thermal updrafts in complete silence, their pale membranes billowing, catching the microscopic fallout of the high conduit leaks: vaporized fats, yeast-spores from the cooling towers, and airborne lichen dust. To watch them from below is to see faint, pale squares drifting across the darkness, momentarily blotting out the cold constellations of the status diodes. After hours of drifting, as the air cools and the gust subsides, they glide in flat, slow spirals back toward the perimeter pylons, settling with a wet, heavy slap against the warm composites to digest their aerial gleanings.

Yet the space is not entirely devoid of hands.

In the dead-ends of the service towers, inside hollow junction boxes where the air is still and smells deeply of cured phenolic resins, live the *Cablesmiths*—or what remains of whatever lineage was left behind to watch the diodes blink.

They are few, perhaps numbering only a few score across the entire multi-mile hub, and they are long-bodied and nearly hairless, their skin having taken on the slate-gray pallor of the composite surfaces they sleep upon. Their fingers are unusually long, with flattened, leathery pads that can touch a 300-degree conduit without blistering, their palms scarred by the geometric cross-hatchings of the catwalk gratings.

They do not speak. In an environment where a shout is swallowed within three feet by the choral drone of the pipes, the Cablesmiths communicate through bone-resonance. When two meet on a hyper-slender bridge over the void, they do not look at each other—their eyes are milky, reduced to small, light-sensitive slits that register only the sudden flash of an electrical fault or the blue blink of a diode. Instead, they lean inward until the broad, thickened ridges of their foreheads touch. Through this contact, they vibrate their vocal cords, sending low-frequency hums directly from skull to skull—passing information about which conduits are running cold, where the condensation has turned bitter, and which bridges have begun to peel from their anchor-bolts.

The Cablesmiths do not maintain the cathedral; they *attend* it. They have forgotten what the transit hub was built to connect, or what travels through the superheated conduits far above their heads. To them, the complex is an ancient, benevolent animal whose metabolism keeps the frost away and whose sweat provides them with water. They spend their days climbing the vertical dendritic tracks, stripping dead wire-lichen from heat-sinks with stone-hard fingernails to keep the vents clear, and carrying damp mats of bacterial slime in their cheek-pouches to pack into leaking pipe-joints.

When a Cablesmith dies, they are not buried in the stone, for there is no earth to dig. They are carried to the highest cantilever—the one that ends cleanly in midair over the black center of the vault. There, during the peak of the concussive pressure-pulse, the others tip the body out into the rising warm gust. 

The body does not fall immediately; the immense upward draft catches it, tumbling it slowly through the thermal haze toward the ceiling, where the clackers and the wire-lichen wait in the dark. By the time the air stills, the hub has accepted the offering, folding the carbon back into its long, closed-system loop, while the diodes continue their cold, repeating arithmetic upon the walls.
