Under the hot sheets of running water, where the basalt is so flat and slick it acts as a black mirror, the first true inhabitants are almost invisible: ribbons of living film no thicker than a single layer of onion skin. 

They are neither algae nor flatworms, though they share the patience of both. Lying pressed against the glass-edged stone, they hold their ground against the constant laminar rush by means of microscopic vacuum cups that line their underbellies. Their upper tissue is perfectly transparent, revealing the iridescent cleavage planes of the rock beneath them, but as the water rolls over their backs, they harvest the minute electric charges generated by the friction of liquid over dry-cleaved stone. When one of the ceramic pylons hums—adjusting its draw against the high sky—these ribbons shiver in unison, their margins flaring with a faint, phosphor-green hem that fades as quickly as the tone dissolves into the rock.

Beneath the rubbery, plum-colored fronds that root in the hairline fractures, another life congregates in the cool shadows between the basalt's hot skin and the plants' insulated undersides. 

These are the *mint-ticks*—though they are soft-bodied, segmented like pale millipedes, and entirely blind. They graze exclusively on the aromatic wax that the ferns secrete to shed the water. In doing so, their bodies become saturated with the volatile oils, giving the terrace its sharp, crushed-herbal scent whenever they shift or rub against one another. They never touch the water; the hydrophobic tension of the fronds forms a dry, pressurized tent over them. If a drop of the boiling runoff breaches their pocket, they curl instantly into tight, pearl-hard beads of calcified shell, waiting until the heat evaporates before unfolding their feathery legs to continue chewing the purple rind of the stems.

Higher up, clinging to the needle-smooth flanks of the three ceramic pylons, are things that look like cast-iron barnacles. They do not feed on organic matter. Instead, they position themselves precisely along the invisible field lines where the air’s moisture begins to nucleate. As the trembling globes of warm water gather in the empty air, these barnacles extend fine, hair-like antennae of spun copper-wire into the swelling drops, siphoning trace minerals and atmospheric static directly out of the suspension before the sphere grows too heavy and snaps downward to the floor. They grow with agonizing slowness, their shells accreted out of airborne iron dust pulled in from the troposphere, forming geometric ridges that mirror the crystalline structure of the basalt beneath them.

The slate-winged skimmers that dart into the frozen blue sky are the only creatures that bridge the terrace and the void beyond. 

Their life cycle is brutal, dictated by the vicious thermal gradient at the lip of the notch. In the freezing air seven thousand meters up, their hemolymph crystallizes into a glassy slush within thirty seconds, locking their wings into fixed glides. They dive back into the sweltering bath of the terrace just before freezing entirely, crashing through the steam veils to skim the boiling sheets of water. The heat instantly re-liquefies their circulatory fluid with a faint, audible crackle, like ice cubes dropped in hot tea. 

They do not mate in the air; they lay their clutches of metallic, needle-shaped eggs directly into the floor of the circular boiling basin, wedging them into the fine tracings of the pale gold alloy. The eggs require the unnatural, static heat of the basin floor to incubate. The larvae that hatch from them do not swim; they are heavy, faceted things made of iron-rich chitin that crawl along the golden geometric tooling three feet beneath the boiling surface, grazing on the white mineral crusts that precipitate from the agitated water, entirely indifferent to temperatures that would cook ordinary flesh in seconds.

And then there is the watcher of the basin.

It spends weeks at a time submerged in the circular pool, resting flat against the golden tracings at the bottom, its outline blurred by three feet of perfectly clear, boiling water. It is shaped vaguely like a broad, legless turtle carved from matte obsidian, but its shell is not bone—it is a thick, porous composite of compressed basalt fibers and compressed pumice, through which the scalding water circulates freely to warm an oil-filled core.

When it rises, it does so without a ripple, displacing the hot sheet-flow with a slow, hydraulic heave. It possesses no eyes; its sensory apparatus consists of four wide, slit-like pits beneath its armored brow that read the thermal topography of the terrace in blinding detail. To this creature, the notch is a glowing amphitheater: the freezing sky above is a void of absolute blackness, the running water is a web of blinding white-gold ribbons, and the three pylons are towering pillars of pale, pulsing violet.

It drags itself across the terrace on broad, calloused knuckles of horn that are immune to the razor-edges of the fresh-fractured rock. Its movements have no urgency; it lives on a timescale closer to the cooling of lava than the beating of wings. As it crawls, it uses a blunt, spade-like snout to clear the hairline fractures where the plum-colored ferns grow, gently nudging aside loose fragments of volcanic glass that might block the water from reaching the roots, or using its heavy breath—which smells heavily of wet iron and sulfur—to clear the sediment from the succulents' pulsing hexagonal pores.

It is not an engineer, nor is it a wild beast; it is an organ of the terrace itself, as integral to the closed loop as the pylons and the gold wire. It eats nothing that lives. Instead, it periodically positions its mouth over the outlet of the boiling basin, swallowing the heavy, mineral-dense slurry that settles in the bottom, filtering the pale gold dust through internal grates and excreting it as a fine, bonding cement that coats the razor-sharp edges of the basalt steps, preventing the mountain from wearing itself away under the relentless work of the water.

When the daylight fades and the bruised indigo sky turns black and fills with stars that do not twinkle through the thin air, the creature crawls back into the basin. It sinks beneath the surface, settling into the golden grooves, and closes its thermal pits. 

Above it, the three pylons hum their low, resonant chord into the mountain's roots. The warm globes of water continue to gather in the darkness, snapping down onto the stone with regular, rhythmic *tocks*, keeping the steam rising, keeping the notch alive, while three feet away, across the sheer lip of the terrace, the wind howls against the bare tooth of the world at forty degrees below zero.
