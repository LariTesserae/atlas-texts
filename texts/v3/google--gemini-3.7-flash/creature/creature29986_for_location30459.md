In the still water of the shallowest knuckle-deep basin, where the film of seepage gathers before it can evaporate, lives a colony of creatures no larger than caraway seeds. 

They are flat, translucent, and hexapodal, with shells of flexible, glass-clear chitin that take on the red-brown hue of the silt beneath them. They do not swim; swimming would break the tension of the mirror. Instead, they walk the stone floor of the basin on legs tipped with microscopic hairs, moving so deliberately that a human eye would have to fix upon a single grain of sand for an entire morning to register their transit. They graze upon the invisible mantle of iron-fixing bacteria that coats the bedding planes. When they die, their carapaces do not decay; they slowly mineralize, settling into the basin's floor to form the very rim that keeps the water from spilling into the sand. Over millennia, they have built their own world half a millimeter taller.

Higher up, where the sandstone wall weeps its moisture in an unbroken sheen, lives a solitary mantle-moth. It is wingless, flattened against the rock like an extra layer of liverwort. Its broad, shield-shaped back is textured with the identical pitted grain of the arenite, veined with faint lines of slate gray that match the surrounding lichen. It does not fly, nor does it possess mouthparts for chewing. It absorbs ambient moisture through a porous ventral fringe, siphoning the mineral-laden capillary water directly into its tissues. It has occupied the same two handspans of damp wall for what may be decades, shifting only an inch to the left when the seam of moisture migrates slightly with the turning of the seasons.

And beneath the overhanging lip of the western wall, wedged in the sheltered angle between the rock face and the second limestone block, lies the alcove’s true tenant.

It is a creature of long, low, heavy habit, stretched out in the dry red silt with its limbs tucked flush beneath its carapace. In outline, it resembles a broad, unkeeled tortoise or a sprawling, paedomorphic lung-lizard, some four paces from its blunt, spade-like snout to the thick root of its tail. But its skin has long since ceased to look like animal hide. It is plated in dense, polygonal scutes the color of dried sumac and river iron, each tile separated from the next by a narrow fissure filled with pale, settled dust.

It has no ears to hear the silence, and its eyes are small, cloudy globes covered by thick, semi-opaque corneal scales that register only the amber shift of the daylight above. It does not need vision; nothing changes in the chapel of stone. 

Its life is measured in the slowest physiological increments. Its lung—a single, cavernous chamber running the length of its dorsal cavity—expands and contracts with such subtle power that the red silt around its flanks remains entirely undisturbed. A single breath is drawn in over the course of an hour, held while the blood absorbs the dry warmth of the chamber, and released in an unhurried, imperceptible sigh. Its heartbeat is a subterranean thud, spaced so widely that minutes of complete stillness pass between each pulse.

It does not hunt, for there is nothing here to chase. It feeds upon the ancient, slow-growing crust of the liverwort and the dried starch stored in the deep, bulbous taproots of the solitary rushes. Once every three or four years, when the subterranean pressure behind the rock wall causes the seepage to swell by a fraction of a dram, the creature stirs. 

The waking is a matter of days. First, a tremor in the legs; then, the scraping sound of dust parting as its heavy, clawed feet find traction in the sand. It drags its belly across the forty paces of floor, leaving a shallow, polished trough that the falling dust will take half a century to erase. It approaches the lowest basin, presses its broad snout to the stone adjacent to the water, and lowers its lower jaw into the pool. It does not lap. It allows the water to draw up through the porous, sponge-like keratin of its lip by simple capillary action, drinking the dissolved iron and silica until its ancient tissues are heavy and dark.

When it has finished, it turns with the ponderous grace of an unmoored barge and returns to the pale limestone blocks. 

The blocks were dragged here for it, or perhaps by others of its kind before their hands lost the shape of making, or by men who found the creature already ancient and built it a low dry bench so that its ventral scales would not rot in the damp sand. It rests its chin upon the edge of the limestone, fitting its throat into a smooth, curved depression that centuries of its own weight have worn into the pale rock.

There it stays, perfectly suspended between life and geology. The falling drop ticks from the ceiling every five minutes, marking off an eternity that means nothing to the alcove, while the warm, tea-colored light filters down from the high rim, bathing the sleeper in the permanent, dustless noon of the earth.
