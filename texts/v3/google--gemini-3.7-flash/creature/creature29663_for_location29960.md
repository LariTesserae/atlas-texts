In the deep, lightless interstices between the dry-stacked blocks, where the granite never dries and the temperature never wavers from the chill of deep earth, lives a slow, pale ecology that predates the people who walk above them.

Beneath the paved alleys, within the vast lattice of subterranean drainage conduits, dwell creatures whose bodies have taken on the properties of the water they inhabit. Small, blind salamanders—scarcely longer than a finger, with translucent pink gills that flare like moss in the current—cling to the vertical faces of the cistern walls with suction-tipped digits. They share the weeping mortarless gaps with flat-bodied river crabs whose shells are as soft and gray as wet slate. These scavengers feed on the endless drift of fine organic silt: the microscopic fallout of airborne spores, wind-cast pollen carried from lowlands thousands of meters below, and the decaying fronds of liverwort washed down from the roof troughs.

Higher up, where the mist meets the open air, the city’s human inhabitants move with a gait shaped entirely by the terrain. 

They are a compact, barrel-chested people, their ribcages expanded to draw deep draughts of the thin, saturated air. Their skin is smooth, thick, and naturally conditioned by the perpetually damp atmosphere, taking on a cool, slate-pale tone in the shadowed lower lanes and a dark, weathered copper among those who work the high parapets during the midday burn. Because wet granite is treacherous, they do not wear rigid shoes; their feet are bare or bound in strips of oiled, vegetable-tanned hide faced with dense, cross-hatched cordage that bites into the rough grain of the stone without making a sound. 

To encounter them is to understand how deeply they belong to the acoustic hush of the plateau. They do not raise their voices. The dry-laid megaliths scatter and swallow high frequencies, so their spoken language has evolved into a register of low, chest-resonant murmurs and guttural fricatives that travel cleanly through the narrow alleys without bouncing. Across longer distances—from terrace to terrace, or across the broad expanse of a tiered collection plaza—they communicate not with horns or shouting, but with small, tuned wooden strikers used to tap upon specific, resonant lintel stones, sending rhythmic vibrations thrumming through the bedrock itself.

Their garments are designed to interact with the water rather than merely repel it. They wear long, tiered tunics made of felted sedge-fiber and wool from high-altitude hare-like rodents that live in the perimeter crevices. These tunics are treated with thick lanolin and mineral waxes, layered like shingles so that the condensing mist beads and sheets off their shoulders, channeled down their backs and directly into the street gutters as they walk. When the cloud deck descends, they pull deep, stiff-brimmed hoods low over their faces, creating small, dry microclimates of warm breath before their lips and eyes.

Their diet is sparse, disciplined, and entirely bound to the masonry.

In the shaded, baffled courtyards where the direct sun never reaches, they cultivate sprawling, shallow beds of dark, edible cyanobacteria and gelatinous stone-algae, fed by the constant trickle of diverted runnels. In the sun-warmed silt traps along the southern parapets, they grow low carpets of a cold-tolerant, peppery brassica that roots in the thinnest layer of captured dust. Protein comes from the deep cisterns, where the pale crabs and small, blind fish are systematically harvested from stone weirs, and from vast, fine-meshed nets of twisted sedge strung across the high gaps between buildings. These nets catch nothing from the ground, but rather strain the updrafts, gathering millions of migrating windborne insects that are swept over the plateau’s rim and stunned by the sudden drop in temperature. These insects are pressed into dense, oily cakes and cured in the cold, dry air of the lower storage vaults.

The rhythm of their lives is governed not by sunrise and sunset, but by the breathing of the clouds.

During the *fall*—the hours when the hill-fog rolls in, turning the city into a dripping, gray maze of silhouettes—the people retreat into the thick-walled inner chambers of their homes. These rooms, buried deep within the mass of shared party walls, are warmed by small, smokeless braziers burning dried peat gathered from the perimeter basins and cakes of compressed sedge. Here, in the damp, quiet gloom, they work on fine, repetitive tasks: splitting slate tiles with horn wedges, weaving cloud-nets, and carving the tiny, precise sluice-gates that regulate the flow of the roof troughs.

When the *lift* arrives and the raw mountain sun burns the vapor away, the city undergoes an immediate, coordinated emergence. Hundreds of figures appear on the stepped parapets, carrying long-handled scrapers made of smoothed bone and bronze. They work quickly while the sun is high, scouring the water-channels to clear away encroaching lichen and scraping the black silt from the gutter traps before it can bake hard in the dry heat. They move in silent, practiced unison, their oiled capes drying and stiffening in the wind, their eyes squinting against the blinding, mirrored glare of leagues of wet, sunlit granite.

There is no sense of urgency in their labor, only a continuous, generational vigilance. They view themselves not as masters of the plateau, nor even as builders, but as a lubricating element within a vast stone machine. They know that if they cease their scraping, if they allow the sedge to take too deep a hold or the ice to lodge in an uncleared gutter during the bitter winter nights, the frost will slowly wedge the great stones apart. Their entire culture is an unspoken agreement with the mountain: in exchange for the shelter of these indestructible walls, they offer their hands to keep the water moving, forever falling from terrace to terrace, down toward the invisible edges of the world.
