To find what lives here, one must first unlearn the signs of life. There are no wet throats to draw breath, no soft membranes to hold fluids, and no eyes that require light to see. In a place where a single drop of water would explode into steam with the violence of a spark on black powder, life has had to abandon the hydraulic entirely. It does not flow; it grinds, expands, latches, and snaps.

At first, the city appears populated only by its own destruction. What seem to be tumbling tiles or fallen lintels rolling down the wide avenues of black sand are, in truth, the small and ubiquitous foragers of the floor: the *scourers*. They are creatures of dry aggregate, roughly the size of tortoises, but constructed like layered sheaves of slate bound together by dense, braided cords of carbon fiber. They possess no organs in any familiar sense; their interiors are chambers of porous pumice through which the superheated draft roars continuously. 

The scourers move by thermal ratchet. On one side of their low bodies, thin bimetallic plates of copper-rich mineral expand in the radiant heat of the floor fissures; on the other, shielded in shadow, the plates contract. This uneven, frantic warping drives tiny graphite-tipped spurs into the sand, jerking them forward in dry, shuddering lurches. They gather in the eddies behind the grain-towers where the airborne mineral pollen drifts into thick, glowing drifts. They do not eat the pollen—they pack it into their internal cavities, using the high static charge of the dust to maintain a crackling electrical potential that keeps their fibrous joints from seizing. When they brush against one another in the dark, they do not hiss or growl; they discharge in blinding, silent arcs of pale blue static that light the alleys for a fraction of a second.

Higher up, within the multi-story tenements and along the lintels of the dry-stacked buildings, the ecology grows larger and more deliberate.

These are the *weavers of the flues*, the things that keep the masonry standing. They are tall, spindle-limbed entities that hang in the draft-ways of the empty chimneys and the high window apertures. Composed almost entirely of flexible silica ribbons and thick, jointed rods of hollow carbonate, they look like desiccated marionettes woven from dry wicker and mica foil. Their long, multi-jointed limbs end in rough chisels of basalt. 

The weavers are creatures of pure resonance. Because the convective roar through the city’s chimneys acts as an enormous acoustic engine, the weavers suspend themselves directly in the air-paths, letting the screaming thermal winds vibrate their hollow ribs. They tune themselves to the city. When a wall begins to lean under the stress of root-prying, the frequency of the wind through its windows drops; the weavers feel this shudder in their joints, descend along the carbon vines with a dry, clattering swiftness like bundles of falling firewood, and wedge themselves into the mortar-gaps. 

They do not use mortar; they use the aggressive flora. With meticulous, clawed gestures, they pull the creeping carbon-fiber vines across the stress-fractures, weaving them tight across the cracked shale like living bandages, anchoring the masonry against the heat-shiver of the basement rock. They are not merely inhabitants of the city—they are its dry immune system, holding the ancient, hand-hewn blocks together because the houses are the only things that create the specific wind-chords they require to stay conscious.

And then there are the makers—or what remains of them.

Deep within the cavernous, square grain-towers, sheltered from the shearing force of the convection gales, the air settles into a baking, breathless stillness. Here, resting in the tiered galleries of baked clay, sit the *quarry-born*. 

They are massive, upright bipeds made of the same materials as the city itself: dry-pressed clay, segmented slate, and thick, interlocking scales of burnt mica that slide over one another with the heavy sound of a spade turning over gravel. Whether they were once organic beings who calcified and replaced their failing tissues with mineral solutions over geological ages, or whether they are an architecture that simply learned to walk, is impossible to tell. 

Their heads are blocky, featureless keystones split by narrow, horizontal vents that filter the static dust. They move with an agonizing, geologic slowness—a single step down a plaza stairway may take an hour of careful, creaking contraction as mineral tendons heat and draw taut. Inside their chest vaults, dry seed-pods of dense silicates are held in tight, heated compression chambers; when the quarry-born must speak or deliberate, they loosen these chambers, allowing the seeds to rattle, ricochet, and crack within their hollow torsos like rhythmic dice, producing complex, percussive sequences that echo through the floor rock for miles.

They tend the grain-towers, which are not filled with food, but with millions of kinetic seed-casings harvested from the frantic plants outside. In the dry dark, they sort these pods by weight and tension, building towering, unstable piles of potential energy that occasionally detonate, showering the dark chambers with sparks and flinty fragments.

In the public plazas, where the black sand gathers in dunes against the stepped terraces, dozens of them stand entirely motionless for decades, their arms raised to catch the descending rain of static-charged pollen. They let the copper-colored fronds climb their legs, let the papery lichens crust their shoulders and flake away, let the roaring wind polish their slate faces until they are as smooth as river glass. 

They are not waiting for the heat to die, nor for water to return. To them, the presence of liquid would be a catastrophic rot, a terrifying acid that would dissolve their joints and quench their static fires. They exist in absolute, roaring perfection within their dry furnace, endlessly mending their stone boxes, listening to the music of the wind through their chimneys, and vibrating in harmony with the great, unquenchable furnace of the earth below.
