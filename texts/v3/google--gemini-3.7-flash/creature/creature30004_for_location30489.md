The life here has long since ceased to look for the sun. Eyes, where they exist at all, are milky, vestigial beads or have been completely overgrown by thick, protective lids and greasy scales. In their place, the creatures have developed a hyper-acute topography of touch and heat: broad sensory pads beneath the chin, rows of lateral vibration-pits along the flanks, and skin that can register a fraction-of-a-degree shift in the draft coming off a cooling vent.

At the lowest level, churning the silt, are the **cobble-frogs**. They are wide, disc-shaped amphibians with skin the color of wet slate, mottled with sulfur-yellow grease stains from the machine sumps. Their limbs are short, thick, and clawed for continuous, rhythmic treading through the mud. They do not possess lungs that expand outward; instead, they breathe through heavy, vascularized folds along their thighs and bellies, taking in oxygen from the sodden, rotting mulch. They live in a state of constant, passive collision, thousands of them sliding over one another like stones in a swollen creek, feeding on the blind midge larvae and fungal mycelium that coat the submerged root systems. When a machine piston drives downward, the shockwave travels through the mud, and the entire carpet of frogs contracts in a single, rolling shudder before relaxing back into the warmth.

Navigating the frog-beds are the **iron-skinks**. These are the lizards stacked along the pipe flanges—long-bodied, blunt-snouted creatures whose bellies are covered in thickened, horn-like plates that allow them to rest directly against bare, steam-hot metal without blistering. Their toes have lost distinct claws, fusing into broad, fleshy spatulas coated in a natural tacky secretion that grips greasy steel even at a ninety-degree angle. They do not hunt with sudden bursts of speed. Instead, they browse with agonizing slowness, extending thick, black tongues to scrape the oily algae from the pipe seams or plucking torpid beetles directly from the brass housings. Because they are kept in perpetual thermal optimum by the conduits, they never sleep in the traditional sense; they exist in a continuous, low-gear consciousness, their heartbeats synchronized to the deep 60-cycle hum of the electrical mains.

Higher up, wound through the junction boxes and sagging insulated lines, live the **tallow-snakes**. These are heavy, sluggish constrictors, thick around as a man’s thigh, with skin that feels like cool, powdered rubber. They have evolved to mimic the cables themselves: uniformly dull, dark gray, and segmented by ringed scales that match the ribbed sheathing of the high-voltage lines. A tallow-snake will remain draped over a single pipe junction for months, absorbing radiant heat, its jaws resting open in the darkness. It does not chase prey. When a skink or a moth crawls across its lower jaw, the mouth simply snaps shut on reflex. Their metabolism is so suppressed by the eternal, unchanging heat that a single meal of three or four bats will sustain one through a half-year of motionless basking.

The smell of fur that hangs in the heavy air comes from the **basin tapirs**, or what has become of them. These are massive, pig-bodied mammals that move through the understory along deep, muddy runnels worn alongside the conduit routes. They are entirely blind, their small ears folded back against thick, hairless skulls to protect them from the relentless, wet foliage. Their coats are not sleek; their hair grows in coarse, bristling mats soaked through with a thick, tallowy sebum that repels the constant condensation and prevents skin rot. 

These beasts are the engineers of the basin’s floor. They use the iron pipes as navigational rails, keeping one broad flank pressed against the warm metal as they plow their way through the aroids and ferns. Their snouts are elongated into flexible, muscular spades with which they root out the giant, starchy tubers that swell around the heat-leaking machinery. When they come upon a pump station, they will stand for hours beneath the rhythmic swing of the rocker arm, letting the warm, oily mist from the hydraulic seals settle into their fur, leaning their great bulk against the vibrating iron to aid their slow digestion. They have no natural predators; nothing in the basin is fast enough or large enough to bring them down, and they die only when their teeth are finally ground flat by the abrasive silt, their massive carcasses collapsing into the mud where the skinks, beetles, and fungus reduce them to clean bone in forty-eight hours.

Suspended above, in the thick black canopy where the hot steam gathers before condensing, are the **felt-moths**. They are as large as dinner plates, with broad, rounded wings covered in a dense, mossy pile that absorbs sound completely. They do not fly so much as parachute; their heavy, fat-filled bodies drop from the canopy with a soft, wet *thud* onto the foliage below, where they drag themselves along the ficus roots by their forelegs, seeking the rotting crowns of dead ferns. Their caterpillars are pale, translucent grubs that live directly on the machines, using hardened mandibles to chew through the outer rubberized insulation of the cables to feed on the petroleum-based binders beneath.

There are no distinct seasons in the basin, and so there are no mating calls, no migrations, and no sudden flowerings. The life here is a continuous, thick fluid: the frogs push through the mud, the skinks scrape the pipes, the blind tapirs shoulder through the dark, and the tallow-snakes digest their motionless meals against the humming iron. Everything feeds on the excess heat of the deep conduits, and everything returns directly to the warm, wet floor, indistinguishable from the grease and the silt that sustains it.
