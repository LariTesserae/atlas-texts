At the base of everything is the yellow film. It does not belong purely to the rock, nor to the municipal grease that slumps down the cable jackets, but to a third thing born of both: a dense, rubbery zoogleal felt that carpets the shale wherever the mist strikes hard. It is thick enough to hold the imprint of a boot for twenty minutes before the elastic slime wells back up to fill the tread. Where the sulfur runoff is hottest, this mat thickens into liver-colored sheets that peel away under their own wet weight, dropping with wet slaps into the drain-channels, where they do not rot so much as dissolve into a cloudy, proteinaceous milk that feeds the lower sumps.

Beneath this skin, in the micro-fissures where the diamond drills left parallel ridges of crushed quartz and powdered basalt, live the thread-worms—billions of them, no thicker than a single eyelash, moving in unison so that the stone itself appears to have a slow, rhythmic grain. They do not have eyes, nor distinct organs for mouth or vent; they are little more than digestive capillaries that absorb the mineral broth straight through their translucent flanks. When a hydraulic line shudders or a drilling seam groans beneath tectonic shift, the whole colony contracts at once, turning a square yard of wet rock from dull cream to deep slate-grey in an instant, as though the wall had held its breath.

Out on the ironwork, where the air is five degrees hotter and smells of scorched graphite, the harvestmen have achieved an unnatural, leggy architecture. Their bodies are small, no larger than dried peppercorns, but their legs span the width of a dinner plate. They do not spin webs; they *are* the web. They hang in tiered draperies from the flange-joints of the ceiling rings, interlocking their tarsal claws until they form suspended hammocks that swing slightly with the passage of the ventilation drafts. 

When a cloud of sewer-gnats rises from the open gutter below, the lower layer of harvestmen does not strike; they simply let their legs drift through the thermal current, collecting the insects on sticky, microscopic setae like pollen on grass. A single disturbance—a burst of water from a cracked gasket or the heavy drop of a grease-clot—sends a shiver through fifty feet of these living curtains. The motion is not frantic; it is dry and communal, a silent, rustling wave that travels the perimeter of the chamber and dies away against the cold gneiss of the untooled ceiling.

Deeper in the shadows of the conduit trays, where the high-voltage bundles hum at sixty cycles, live the true scavengers: the grease-beetles. They are broad, flat, and entirely blind, their carapaces polished to the dull sheen of wet coal by years of squeezing between cable-sheaths and iron brackets. They live entirely on the tallow and paraffin used to pack the packing-glands of the pumps, supplemented by the carcasses of crickets that fall into their runs. 

Their life cycle is wedded to the heat. In the cooler corners near the floor, their larvae—white, fat-bodied grubs the length of a thumb—burrow through the sediment in the drainage gutters, digesting the oil-sands until their flesh is saturated with petroleum distallates. When they pupate, they glue themselves directly to the hot casing of the steam-mains with a secretion that hardens into an amber-like crust. The emerging adults are slow, heavy, and hot to the touch, their bodies radiating the stored heat of the pipe-metal like small, scuttling bricks.

Above them all, inhabiting the dead airspace between the iron arches and the blasted ceiling, are the pipistrelles. They are not the sleek, dusky bats of the surface church-steeples. Three or four generations in the perpetual 110-degree soup have thinned their fur to a sparse, reddish fuzz through which their grey-pink skin shows bald and greasy. Their ears have enlarged to monstrous, translucent funnels, crisscrossed with crimson veins that flare when they bank against the sodium lights. 

Because the chamber is an echo-box of iron and steam, their echolocation has flattened into a low, chittering click that sounds less like an animal and more like a telegraph key working behind a thick wall. They roost not by hanging from stone, which is too hot and slick with condensation, but by wedging themselves by the dozens into the hollow bolt-recesses of the cast-iron ribs. There, packed belly-to-back, they generate a collective heat so fierce that the iron surrounding their pockets never cools, sweating a greasy, dark guano that streaks the columns down to the walkplates in long, tarry ribbons.

And on the catwalks themselves—at the very center of the iron ribs, where the floor grates are worn smooth and silver by the soles of heavy leather workboots—there are the men who have stayed.

They are not many; perhaps four or five to a twelve-hour shift, though some seem never to ascend the hoist-cages to the surface levels. They are known to the line-masters as the *Sump-Tenders*, but among themselves they use no titles. They are shaped by the pressure and the wet. Their skin is the pale, waterlogged white of peeled willow, except around the eyes and knuckles where the rock-dust and graphite grease have worked permanently into the pores. 

They wear almost nothing: heavy, lead-toed boots to keep their footing on the slime-slicked steel, duck-canvas trousers cut off at the knee and tied with hemp, and broad bands of flannel wound around their foreheads to keep the salty sweat from blinding them in the mist. Over their mouths and noses they wear simple respirators made of tin cans packed with crushed charcoal and vinegar-soaked sponge, though after an hour on the lower walkplates they usually let these dangle around their necks, preferring the thick, hot stench of the sump to the suffocating restriction of the filter.

They move with the same economy as the beetles. A tender does not run; he does not even walk with a normal stride. He shuffles, his boots never fully leaving the grating, feeling through the iron for the specific vibrations that signal a failing bearing in the main lift-pump or an obstruction in the grease-traps forty feet below the floor. 

In their hands they carry *sludge-hooks*—long ash poles tipped with bronze scrapers—and small, battered tallow-pots that they use to grease the valve-stems by hand. They do not talk much. The roar of the exhaust is too wide, too deep for the human voice, which simply unravels in the mist three feet from the lips. Instead, they communicate by striking the conduit pipes with the heads of their wrenches: two sharp *clangs* to signal the opening of a bleed-valve, a rolling rattle along the guardrail to warn that a hot-water backflow is rising through the lower trench.

They have developed a strange, symbiotic tolerance for the fauna. They do not sweep the crickets from the instrument panels; they blow on them gently, clearing the dials with a quick puff of breath before reading the pressure gauges. When a gecko drops from the steam-pipe onto a tender’s bare shoulder, he does not slap it; he simply reaches up with a greasy thumb and nudges the creature until it skitters down his forearm and leaps back onto the warm lagging of the conduit. 

They sleep, when they must, in the small, dry pockets behind the transformer housings, where the air is blown clear of mist by the exhaust of the pilot fans. They string low hammocks made of greasy conveyor-belting between the iron struts, sleeping naked under the pale, jaundiced glare of the sodium bulbs with their forearms thrown across their eyes. Above them, the bats wheel and chatter in the amber fog; beneath them, the crickets graze on the crumbs of hardtack and the rind of salt pork dropped into the grating; and all around them, the mountain settles against the iron, millimeter by millimeter, groaning through the dark like an animal that cannot find its rest.
