To live here is to abandon the concept of the step. Nothing in the eye of the rock walks; everything clings, anchors, or falls.

### The Dwellers of the Rim and Crevice

In the wet shadow under the overhanging lip, where the limestone has been eaten into honeycombs of sharp-edged pockets, live the *Cleft-weavers*. They are pale, long-shanked harvestmen the size of a man’s hand, with bodies like flattened seeds. Unlike low-country spiders, they spin no flat webs; the violent, shearing updrafts would shred silk in seconds. Instead, they produce thick, cable-like stays of greasy, non-adhesive line, anchored between dwarf laurel roots and the stone face, forming elastic trip-lattices across the moss cushions. They do not wait for prey to become entangled; they hunt by the shudder of the rock. When the great drafts whistle through the chimney, setting the mountain’s spine into a deep, bone-resonant hum, the harvestmen press their jointed shins flat against the wet schist, feeling for the frantic, micro-vibrations of stunned midges or downy prions that have misjudged a landing on the slippery liverwort.

Deeper in the solution tubs—the cold, emerald-ringed baths that never spill because they are constantly replenished from above—lives an endemic, lungless salamander, nearly colorless, its skin the milky gray of wet slate. Its ribs are broad and flattened like armor to resist the pressure of rushing water, and its tail has grown a broad, paddle-like keel edged in soft cartilage. They graze slowly upon the thick, gelatinous mats of cold-water cyanobacteria that coat the submerged stone, sharing the pools with the larvae of the black-wing stonefly. When the updraft surges and tears the surface of a pool into airborne foam, the salamanders wedge their heads into the hair-cracks of the rock, locking their curved, needle-sharp claws into the silt, immovable as fossils.

The prions and cliff-swallows are the true owners of the central air, but they share the heights with a solitary scavenger: the *Vane-hawk*. It is a bird built entirely of sharp angles—narrow, scythe-like wings that do not flap so much as adjust their camber with dry, snapping sounds, and a tail split deep like an earwig’s pincers. The hawk never crosses the chimney horizontally. It spends its life banked in an eternal, ascending spiral, riding the roaring thermal core up from the blue haze, snatching midges and exhausted swallows out of the column with talons that close like spring-traps, then spilling over the outer rim into the cloud deck to feed while falling.

### The Perimeter-Folk

The people who occupy the timber platforms in the undercut call themselves the *Tethered*, though they rarely speak the word aloud. Speech is an inefficient, fragile tool in a place where the air itself roars like a bronze bell. 

They are small-bodied, thick in the torso, with lung capacities enlarged by generations of breathing the sheared, high-altitude air. Their shoulders are abnormally broad, their arms longer and heavier than their legs, developed from a lifetime of hauling their own weight along wet guide-ropes. Their skin has the cured, yellowish texture of fat-rubbed sheepskin, perpetually damp and smelling faintly of the tallow they use to grease their bodies against the freezing mist. Their hands are their primary sensory organs: the skin of the palms is as tough and hairless as shoe leather, scored with deep, permanent creases from grip-friction, while their fingertips remain hyper-sensitive, able to distinguish between stable limestone and rotten, crumbling shale by the barest change in surface chill.

#### The Language of Taps and Gut-Lines
In the chimney, a shouted word is shredded by the wind and lost in the organ-drone of the shaft. The Tethered communicate through two systems:
*   **Whistle-speech:** Short, piercing, high-frequency bursts blown through dried bone reeds wedged behind their lower teeth, tuned precisely to cut through the low-frequency rumble of the updraft.
*   **The Line-Code:** Their timber platforms, sleeping nets, and the narrow perimeter ledges are linked by a nervous system of taut rawhide cordage. By tapping bone mallets against these cords, or plucking them with a thumb-ring, they send rhythmic vibrations traveling hundreds of feet along the wet wall to alert others of a loose anchor, a full water-bladder, or an incoming cloud-surge.

#### Their Dwellings and Domestic Life
They do not build walls; walls offer resistance to the drafts and are ripped out by the roots. They live in suspension.

Their shelters are *slings*—deep, cocoon-like hammocks woven from split sedge and lined with layers of prion down and greased gut-skins. These are suspended beneath the deepest rock shelves by clusters of ironwood pins driven into the bedding planes. A family sleeps stacked vertically: the children in the lowest, smallest hammocks closest to the platform planks, the elders higher up where the rock is marginally drier and warmed by the faint, rising thermal seepage from deep mountain fissures.

Fire is rare and holy. They burn dried cake made from pressed swallow guano, bone shavings, and bird oil, kept in small, hooded ironstone basins clamped directly to the rock face. A single thumb-sized flame is shielded with slate plates, used not for light—they navigate the perimeter by memory and tactile guide-lines—but to warm stone cups of a bitter, dense broth brewed from dried sedge roots, lichen paste, and salted stonefly grubs.

#### The Purpose of the Winch
The people do not live in the Eye out of exile; they are harvesters of the *Vapor-Drift*.

The chimney acts as a continental chimney-sweep, drawing warm, rich, heavy air from the lowland river jungles thousands of feet below and blasting it into the freezing upper atmosphere. The winch, with its split-willow basket, is lowered into the roaring throat to trawl the column. It catches things that have no business at this altitude:
*   Great mats of lowland silk-moths and cloud-borne spiders swept up in seasonal storms.
*   High-altitude *skimmings*—the oily, sweet condensation that forms when lowland warmth hits the glacial stone, which they scrape from the clay baffles and store in cured bladder-skins. This water is extraordinarily pure, carrying microscopic spores and mineral salts valued by lowland herbalists and alchemists far below, for which the mountain folk trade dried ironwood, tallow, and raw copper pins during the brief summer descents to the outer screes.
*   The molt-down of the nesting colonies, gathered from the lip of the tubs before the wind can scatter it into the void.

#### The Old and the Void
To grow old on the platforms is to slowly lose the ability to open one's hands. The elders suffer from a hardening of the tendons that curls their fingers into permanent, rigid hooks—an affliction they consider a mercy, as it allows them to hang securely from the safety lines even when asleep or exhausted.

When a member of the Tethered dies, they are not buried; there is no earth to hold them, and rock is too precious to spend chipping out a tomb. The body is stripped of its cordage, its bone hooks, and its grease-skins. The kin gather at the edge of the lowest timber beam, where the spray turns to blue mist. Without words, in the ringing silence between two gusts of the great stone whistle, they slip the body out into the empty center. 

It does not drop like a stone. The furious updraft catches the light, dry frame, lifting it twenty or thirty feet into the diagonal sunbeams—a brief, tumbling ascent among the midges and swifts—before the cold shearing winds take it over the outer lip, scattering it into the lower clouds that roll forever against the mountain's knees.
