To survive here, an organism must make peace with three things: the tallow, the lime, and the pulse.

At the lowest tier, where the runoff from the settling trench spills into the dark sumps, life begins as a skin. The oil that mists continuously from the piston packings does not wash away; it settles onto the water’s surface and the wet masonry, where it is met by dense, velvety mats of grease-metabolizing fungi. These colonies are not white or mold-gray, but a deep, bruised aubergine and oil-slick bronze. They carpet the lower flanges of the beam engines, feeding on animal fat and petroleum grease, converting the industrial lubricants into a thick, rubbery mulch that smells faintly of warm paraffin and fermenting malt.

Plowing through this mulch are the vault’s primary scavengers: blind, flattened woodlice the size of plum stones, their carapaces bleached porcelain-white and translucent enough that the dark sludge of their digestive tracts is visible through their backs. They have lost all circadian rhythm; instead, their feeding cycles are governed by the six-strokes-per-minute heave of the engines. When the great iron walking beams rock downward, shuddering through the brick floor, these isopods freeze against the iron; in the two-second lull of the stroke’s recovery, they surge forward across the hemp lagging to graze on the fungal mats and the shed leaf-meal.

In the warmer niches—the dry hollows tucked directly behind the steam cylinders, where the heat climbs past ninety-five degrees—lives a strain of silk-spinning moth that exists nowhere else. Their ancestors arrived sixty years ago as pupae embedded in the roots of nursery ferns brought down during the vault’s brief period of human maintenance. Over generations in the dark, they have lost their pigment and the power of true flight. Instead, they are heavy-bodied, velvet-pale, and scuttle like spiders along the braided ficus roots, laying their eggs exclusively within the fraying seams of the tarred-hemp pipe insulation. Their larvae eat the rotting hemp and the dry spores of the bird’s-nest ferns, spinning dense, golden-brown webbing that binds the deteriorating industrial lagging together, unintentionally preserving the insulation that keeps the steam lines hot.

Higher up, in the wet mid-canopy where the iron catwalks are choked with wild ginger and broadleaf aroids, small, lungless salamanders cling to the undersides of the steel grating. Descended from escaped greenhouse stock, they have developed broad, sucker-like pads on their toes to grip the vibrating, algae-greased iron. Their skin is smooth and completely black, matching the wet coal-iron of the girders, save for bright, rust-orange markings along their flanks that mimic the flaking paint of the pump housings. They do not hunt by sight; their eyes are clouded over with milky cataracts, unnecessary in the murk. Instead, their throats rest flat against the metal, sensing the micro-vibrations of the insects that walk the iron meshes.

And then there is the tender.

Whether there is one or two, or a lineage that has thinned to a solitary survivor, is impossible to tell from a distance. A figure moves along the upper crane rails, navigating the dense hanging curtains of Spanish moss and pale aerial roots without disturbing their wet hang. 

The keeper has adapted entirely to the conditions of the vault. Hearing is long gone, destroyed decades ago by the unyielding, concussive *clack-hiss-thud* of the horizontal engines; in its place, the body has become a massive acoustic receiver. The soles of the keeper’s feet are thick, leathery, and bare, hardened into pads that read the health of the machinery through the iron catwalks. By stepping onto a high I-beam, the keeper can feel if the left cylinder’s valve-stem is sticking, or if the main bearings are running dry of oil, simply through the harmonic buzz traveling through the skeleton.

The keeper’s skin is permanently stained with a mixture of lime-dust, soot, and aerosolized cylinder oil, giving it the dark, water-repellent patina of an oiled leather boot. Clothing is minimal—shreds of old canvas and hemp sacking tied together with tough, stripped lengths of aerial root, more like a harness for carrying tools than garments for warmth. Across the chest and shoulders, where the condensation drips steadily from the vault ceiling sixty feet above, the skin is grown over with a faint, harmless dusting of green epiphytic algae.

The keeper’s existence is not one of struggle, but of continuous, slow-motion maintenance. The routine is ancient: wedging broad, waxy leaves into the drip-catchers to direct soft condensation water into the boiler supply lines; clearing choking clumps of sword fern away from the brass grease cups so the automated lubricators can turn freely; slicing back the thickest ficus roots when they threaten to pinch the copper governor linkages. 

When the keeper rests, it is not on the floor—where the peat is too sodden—but suspended twenty feet up, cradled in a natural hammock formed by the thick, living basket of an ancient bird’s-nest fern rooted to the central column. There, wrapped in the deafening, cradle-rocking thrum of the Cornish engines, breathing the hot, mineral-thick vapor that tastes of iron and crushed leaf, the keeper sleeps in sync with the pumps. 

Every six seconds, the steam hisses, the iron sighs, the floor shudders, and the sleeper’s chest rises in time with the stroke, neither fully separate from the machinery nor distinct from the roots that hold the iron together.
