To live here, a thing cannot possess an inside that is softer than its outside. 

There are no wet membranes, no pulpy brains kept in dark bone vaults, no veins that would boil into steam the moment the sun dropped its vertical noon-lance down the split. What stirs within the dry thickets of iron and copper is not carved out of flesh, yet it possesses appetite, territorial panic, and the slow, agonizing ambition of things that must manufacture their own kind from the crust.

The smallest of them are the *Scrapers*—though they are less like insects and more like loose sets of unjoined leaf-springs that have learned to wander.

They reside in the shallow talc drifts beneath the mica canopy. Roughly the length of a human hand, their bodies are composed of paper-thin sheets of cold-rolled hematite, stacked like the leaves of an unriveted ledger. They have no limbs in the traditional sense; instead, the bottom-most leaf expands under the baking midday heat a fraction of a millisecond faster than the leaf above it. This microscopic disparity in thermal expansion causes the entire stack to curl, bite the basalt with an edge sharper than a surgical tool, and vault forward with a sharp, dry *tink*. 

They graze upon the microscopic frost of volatilized zinc and sulfur that settles on the rock faces. They do not digest it. They pass it between their laminated plates, using the abrasive dust to grind away their own imperfections, lapping their mating surfaces until they achieve an airtight, mirror-bright tolerance. When three or four Scrapers collide on a narrow ledge, they do not fight; they interlock their plates by mutual magnetic attraction, forming a momentary, rigid bridge across a gap, only to uncouple and scatter when the shadow of a terrace shifts the local temperature by two degrees.

Higher up, where the copper briars coil around the dry pyroxene crankshafts, dwell the *Strut-Walkers*.

These creatures are spindly, towering cantilevers of sintered rutile and pale, green-white beryl, standing five to six spans tall but weighing no more than an armful of dry laths. They resemble the framework of a crane or an oil derrick that has narrowed its footprint down to three needle-tipped stilts. They possess no heads, no eyes, and no mouths; at their apex hangs a heavy, spherical counterweight of pure, dense magnetite, suspended within an open cage of tensioned wire.

The Strut-Walkers are creatures of pure resonance. They do not see the fissure; they feel the torque within the rock. 

They move with a staggering, high-friction majesty. A Strut-Walker will stand motionless for eighteen hours, wedged across a vertical chimney, waiting for the cliff to heat to its maximum shear stress. When a basalt linkage fifty feet away groans and contracts, the Walker feels the seismic vibration travel up through its needles. It steps—a rigid, sweeping arc of a limb that does not bend at a knee, but ratchets along an internal circular rack—and plants itself precisely where the rock’s structural strain is greatest.

There, it feeds. Its "digestion" is an act of piezoelectric theft: by bracing its crystal frame against two opposing faces of the fault line as they expand, it allows the mountain to compress its limbs with tons of dry force. Deep within its beryl core, this mechanical pressure is converted into a silent, crackling reservoir of static electricity, which bleeds off its joints in minute, hair-thin arcs of violet light that leave behind the smell of cold, dry ozone.

Predation here is not a matter of consuming substance, but of stealing alignment.

Prowling the middle ledges, where gravity leans sideways and the metal foliage grows out at impossible angles, are the *Shear-Dogs*. They are dense, low-slung armatures of black, unpolished iron, shaped like heavy wedges or hydraulic splitters. They have no lungs, but their flanks feature small, sliding louvers of mica that cycle open and shut with a dry, rhythmic hiss, cooling the immense friction generated by their internal drive-trains.

A Shear-Dog does not stalk the Scrapers; it hunts the tensioned rods of the Strut-Walkers. It crawls along the stone faces with its underbelly pressed flat to the basalt, held in place by powerful internal electromagnets. When it finds a Strut-Walker locked in place across a fissure—braced, trembling, and saturated with tectonic compression—the Shear-Dog strikes. 

It does not bite. It wedges its chisel-snouted head beneath the Walker’s primary load-bearing strut and releases a sudden, massive burst of its own stored kinetic spring-tension. 

The sound is like an anvil struck by an iron sledge: a single, deafening *PING* that does not echo, deadened instantly by the talc-dusted walls. The Walker’s strut buckles; its stored piezoelectric charge discharges instantly into the Shear-Dog’s hull, annealing its iron hide and hardening its gears, while the crippled Walker topples into the gulf below, shattering into a spray of clear, unblemished beryl shards that the Scrapers will spend the next three decades grinding into dust.

And yet, none of these are the true inhabitants; they are merely the moving components, the loose tooling of a deeper gestation.

In the deepest pocket of the nave—where the light from the overhead rift is focused by natural quartz lenses into a blinding, steady point that could melt lead—lies the *Cast*.

It is an immense, embryonic bulk, half-fused with the western wall of obsidian. It does not possess a silhouette that the eye can easily hold; it is a sprawling, geometric mass of interlocking flywheels, heavy basalt pendulums, and thick, seamless slabs of cast iron that are slowly, cell by crystalline cell, displacing the native stone of the mountain.

It has no mind, but it has an escapement. 

Deep inside its core, an axle of dense, meteoric nickel turns at the speed of a glacier, driven by the thermal differential between the sunlit surface and the deep subterranean chill. As it turns, it draws the copper briars into itself, winding them onto vast internal spools, stripping their zinc needles, and using the metal to cast new linkages within dry, vacuum-tight voids in the rock.

The Cast is building an extremity. Out from its lower flank, suspended fifty feet above the talc floor, a massive, jointed arm of layered basalt and cold-forged iron is slowly taking form. It does not grow by cellular division; it grows by spalling. Every forty days, a section of the surrounding cliff face fractures along a razor-straight geometric line, the waste rock drops away into the void, and an entirely new, fully machined gear-tooth or socket-joint is left behind, integrated perfectly into the arm’s linkage.

The creatures of the fissure serve this assembly without knowing it. The Scrapers lap the joints of the arm until they are frictionless; the Strut-Walkers discharge their static loads into its structural pins to temper the metal; the Shear-Dogs curl themselves around its cold-worked pivots at night, their cooling iron bodies keeping the tolerances tight against the freezing mountain dark.

It is an ecology that has no memory of water, no concept of greenness, and no use for the fragile, wet chemistry of the world below. It is waiting for the rift to widen. It is waiting for the sun to drop low enough to illuminate the bottom-most pin of the great arm. 

And when the final linkage is dropped into place with that dry, iron *clack*, the thing in the fissure will not wake up—for it was never asleep—but it will finally, with the heavy, unhurried patience of a stone falling through the earth, begin to uncoil.
