To live in the pocket, a thing cannot possess an eardrum, a wet tongue, or a fluid eye. Any membrane soft enough to register a human voice would be ruptured in seconds by the standing wave of the vents; any drop of exposed blood or mucous would be sucked into vapor by the blast-furnace draft before it could complete a single pulse. 

Yet the bone-pale tenements are inhabited, not by ghosts, but by a precise, dry-bodied hierarchy whose biology is entirely integrated into the acoustic physics of the rift.

Deep within the mortar-joints and under the vibrating floor-slabs crawl the *tally-beetles*—dense, flattened discs of pressed shale and iron-rich grit that resemble loose gaming pieces. They have no legs in the conventional sense, but travel on hundreds of microscopic, piezoelectric bristles that shudder in sympathy with the canyon’s bass notes. When the giant wind-catchers on the rooftops hit a resonant low *C*, the tally-beetles drift in smooth, frictionless swarms across the plazas, pushed along the chalk by the sound alone. They do not eat flesh or greens; they graze upon the static electricity that crackles between the mica-weed leaves and scrape the golden, friction-baked spore dust from the steps, leaving behind faint, polished tracks that gleam like graphite.

Higher up, clinging to the vertical alleyways and the underside of the sun-hardened timber catwalks, are the city’s weavers: long-limbed, brittle arachnoids known as *lath-spinners*. Their bodies are as thin as slivers of cedar, constructed entirely of horn-plate, dried sinew, and hollow air-bladders. The spinners do not make silk from glands; instead, they harvest the tough, fibrous cords of the dried reed-canes, splitting them with pincer-jaws as sharp as obsidian chisels. With frantic, percussive jerks of their needle-like forelegs, they knot and lash the canyon's suspension bridges, replacing the dry-rot ropes as fast as the hot gales fray them. Their joints pop like dry twigs as they scuttle, their movements strictly timed to the whip-crack rhythms of the bursting ferns so their frail bodies are never caught mid-stride by the lateral air-spikes.

And within the tiered apartments—sheltered from the direct glare of the ceiling slits but bathed in the furnace-heat of the flues—reside the builders and masters of the shelf. 

They are bipedal, but barely so; their frames are extraordinarily tall, narrow, and laterally compressed, built like wooden laths so they present virtually no surface area to the wind. Their skin is an ivory-white, desiccated rind, indistinguishable from the sun-cured clay of their houses, cross-hatched with millions of microscopic stress-grooves that absorb and deaden harmonic shock. 

They have no faces in the mammal sense. Where eyes and nostrils might have been, their heads terminate in a flared, fluted crest of hollow cartilage, intricately bored with small, lateral vents like an ancient bone ocarina. They do not hear sound; they *are* sound. Their entire skeletal structure is an interlocking lattice of hollow acoustic chambers, calcified and tuned to the chamber’s native frequencies. A change in the canyon’s barometric pressure or the whistling pitch of a single rooftop vent vibrates through their rib-cages, translating the geometry of the entire four-block chasm into an instantaneous, physical sensation of weight and location.

They communicate across the deafening roar not with calls, but with chorded venting. By contracting their dry, accordion-pleated thoracic plates, they force the ambient, high-pressure air through the flutes in their skulls and the slits along their forearms, releasing piercing, counter-frequency whistles that cut cleanly through the roar. A conversation between two figures standing on opposing terraces sounds like the rapid tuning of distant violins, punctuated by the rhythmic clatter of their wooden fingers against the stone balustrades.

These beings drink the static charge of the air directly through their horn-tipped extremities, grounding themselves against the calcite cliffs to draw electrical current through their dry, wire-like nervous systems. They move with an unsettling, mathematical stillness. When the noon-glare is at its fiercest and the reeds scream at full pitch, dozens of them emerge onto the cantilevered lookouts. They do not seek shelter from the violence; they lean out over the yawning drops, spreading the hinged, papery ribs of their chests to let the roaring wind scour their hollow interiors clean of dust.

They are continuously engaged in an endless, communal tuning of the city. Armed with heavy, flint-headed adzes, they patrol the tenements, chipping away at corners that have begun to whistle at an incorrect pitch, clearing choked reed-chimneys, and wedging flat stones into vibrating cracks. If an archway rattles out of tune, they rebuild it until it hums with the proper, load-bearing frequency. 

They are the engine's caretakers, and its product. They do not sleep; they wedge themselves during the cold, windless hours of night into the acoustic dead-zones behind the mudbrick hearths, going stiff and dormant as kiln-fired pottery until the first sun-blade strikes the roof, the draft begins to scream, and the rock beneath them begins to sing them awake.
