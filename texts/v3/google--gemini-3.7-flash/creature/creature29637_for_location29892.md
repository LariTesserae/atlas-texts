In the heart of this locked mid-morning, the people who raised the cyclopean walls remain among them, as vivid and uncorrupted as the stone they shaped.

They are a high-altitude people, deep-chested and broad-shouldered, their lungs built for the rare, blade-thin air of the heights. Their skin is the color of oiled cedar, weathered across the cheekbones by the ferocious clarity of mountain sun, but unmarred by the slow creases of age; whatever youth or maturity was theirs at the moment of the pause remains fixed upon them forever. They wear tunics of dense, coarse-spun vicuña and caprid wool, dyed in muted minerals—iron-red, chalk-white, and the slate-blue of the valley’s shadows—fastened with bone toggles and clasps of cold-hammered copper.

They do not stand like statues; they stand like workers arrested between one heartbeat and the next. 

On the great southern ramp, a team of twenty hauliers leans into a thick braided hemp cable, their bodies angled forward at forty-five degrees against the grade of the rock. The strain in their calves is absolute: every tendon stands out like a drawn bowstring, and the sweat beaded across the napes of their necks catches the sun like seed pearls, never trickling downward to cool their skin. Yet their expressions carry no agony. There is only the supreme, concentrated focus of shared weight—the quiet, muscular fellowship of people who know precisely how much stone will yield to collective mass. 

Further up, in the open megalithic courts, the life of the city is domestic, civic, and serene. On a low stone bench cushioned with dry woven rushes, two women sit with an unrolled sheet of hammered copper between them, their fingers poised over a row of incised calculations for a granary’s timbering. One is speaking; her lips are parted around a vowel that has no sound in the air, yet the warmth of her voice seems to linger as a palpable density between them. Across the plaza, a child leans over the rim of an unworked basalt basin, his hand hovering two inches above the surface of the water, drawn to the reflection of the raptors overhead. The light illuminates the fine, pale down on his forearm, each hair glowing like a filament of raw quartz.

Inside the flat-topped palaces, where the cool mountain shadow pools deep and blue, life is gathered in quiet densities. These interiors are dry, clean, and smelling of dried sage, split pine, and cold hearth-ash. In a wide hall supported by monolithic pillars, elders and architects sit in a low circle on thick pelt-rugs. A clay jug is caught in the act of pouring: a curved ribbon of dark, fermented mountain berry wine bridges the lip of the vessel and the rim of a shallow stone cup, glassy, unbroken, and perfectly clear. 

There is no terror in their faces, nor any hint of sudden calamity. This stasis was not a disaster that overtook them; it was their architecture’s ultimate consequence. They built on a scale meant to outlast the crumbling of low-lying empires, and in doing so, they seem to have pinned the morning to the rock with the sheer mass of their masonry. They inhabit their city with a calm, eternal competence.

And within this frozen hour, their consciousness is not extinguished, but gathered into an infinite depth. 

Because time does not carry them away from the moment, they do not forget what they are feeling. The haulier feels the burn of the rope against his palms not as pain that will pass, but as a deep, permanent communion with the stone on the sledge. The woman calculating copper measurements is not rushing toward the completion of the granary; she is suspended in the pure, lucid joy of the problem itself. Their inner lives are like the dewdrop hanging from the split-timber lintel—self-contained, utterly full, holding the whole landscape without spilling a single drop.

Alongside the people, the non-human life of the plateau exists in the same crystalline intensity.

In the seams between the cyclopean foundations, where the emerald star-moss forms thick, velvet carpets, miniature worlds thrive in unbroken vigor. The sap within the saxifrage leaves is pressurized to bursting, holding the thick, jelly-like moisture of the heights without drying or rotting. Among the moss-fronds, small, heavy-shelled beetles with shells of metallic green sit with their antennae raised, tasting the unchanging mineral drift of the basalt. They do not starve, because their hunger is paused at the exact threshold of satiety; they do not tire, because energy here does not bleed into the void.

Beneath the paved thoroughfares, deep within the dark, dry gaps of the foundation stones where no daylight reaches, small mountain weasels with thick, cream-colored winter coats lie curled in nests of dry grass. Their eyes are half-closed, their small paws tucked beneath their bellies, breathing an air that never stales, sleeping a sleep that knows neither morning alarm nor nightfall. 

High above them all, on the topmost pinnacles where the basalt meets the sheer sky, the slate raptors maintain their vigil. Their sightlines cross the amphitheater from end to end, taking in the hauliers, the wine frozen in its arc, the caprids with their frosted wool, and the immense, unmoving mountains beyond. Their golden irises do not adjust; they drink the full, unwavering mid-morning sun without blinking, their talons locked around the dry-stone ledges with a grip that the centuries cannot tire.

Together, the people, the beasts, and the stone form a single, living organism. They are not waiting for time to resume. They have achieved the plateau's true nature: an eternal, unwearied morning, held aloft in the clean air between the razor-edged rock and the pale, unshifting sky.
