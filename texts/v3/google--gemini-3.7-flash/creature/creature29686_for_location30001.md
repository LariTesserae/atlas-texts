Where there is no moisture to yield, flesh does not exist. Nothing soft could survive the first brush against a landscape where every boundary is a micro-fractured blade; a falling wing or an exploratory paw would be reduced to ribboned shavings before it found purchase. 

The life that occupies the glassy heights is constructed of the same parched, uncompromising matter as the spires themselves, yet it is not static. It exists along the gradients of strain, heat, and static charge, animated by a chemistry of molten sulfur, dry metallic vapors, and the high-voltage crackle of shearing stone.

***

Clinging to the mirror-smooth vertical planes are things that resemble sheets of flexible slate or layered mica. They are broad, extraordinarily thin, and dark as soot, adhering to the vitrophyre not with suction—which requires a wet seal—but through dense fields of dry van der Waals fibrils, like the pads of geckos amplified to a titanic, mineral scale. 

These are the *Shear-Plates*. They do not crawl in the animal sense; they ripple. Their movement is a slow, rhythmic shivering that sounds like a deck of heavy slate cards being shuffled against the dark. Because the spires are constantly being extruded upward, the Shear-Plates spend their centuries in a state of deliberate counter-descent. They feed on the radiant thermal gradients of the rock face, their underside absorbing the oven-like heat from the depths while their dry, faceted dorsal scales radiate that energy into the cold void of the corridors. This thermal siphon drives a sluggish, dry-phase metabolism within their silicate lattices, converting heat directly into kinetic tension.

When an obsidian tower accelerates its climb, the Shear-Plates feel the rising strain hours before the rock snaps. They orient themselves along the coming fault lines, pressing their flat bodies flush across the microscopic fissures. When the fracture finally detonates—that deafening, concussive *crack* that rings through the abyss—the Shear-Plates drink the violent release of energy. They absorb the high-frequency vibrations and the sudden burst of triboelectric lightning that sparks between the freshly parted surfaces. To them, an earthquake is not a disaster; it is a feeding event.

***

In the vast, empty corridors of warm air that separate the basalt spines, the atmosphere carries buoyant, paper-thin wanderers. They resemble geometric kites or faceted leaves of hammered bronze, no thicker than a pane of mica and spanning dozens of yards.

These are the *Gliders of the Void*, creatures of pure surface area. They have no organs, no blood, and no interior volume to speak of—only a two-dimensional lattice of carbon-doped silica that rides the thermal plumes rising from the fissure floors. They drift in absolute silence through the gloom, invisible except when they catch the dull-red underglow of an open vent, flashing maroon against the black ceiling of the troposphere.

The Gliders subsist on the mineral vapors carried aloft in the heat—sublimated sulfur, arsenic dust, and vaporized iron. As they drift, their upper surfaces, chilled by the upper reaches of the atmosphere, act as condensation plates. The vapors crystallize directly onto their skins in delicate, powdery rimes. When a Glider becomes too heavy with accumulated mineral crust, it tilts on edge, slicing through the rising thermals in a terrifying, silent dive that lasts for miles, until the intense heat near the floor bakes the crust away, restoring its buoyancy and sending it climbing once again.

They do not mate or reproduce through division. Instead, when two Gliders collide along a thermal shear, their razor-thin margins clash and chip. The microscopic shards knocked loose into the rising currents do not fall; they act as crystalline seeds, nucleating new sheets from the vaporous air over thousands of years, slowly growing from floating dust-motes into vast, drifting planes.

***

Higher up, where the megaliths and dry-stacked basalt prisms sit perched on the precipices, lives the closest thing this world has to an architect.

They are slow, segmented colossi, shaped like angular wedges of dense, unpolished hematite. Their bodies are built like bridge trusses, formed of interlocking triangular plates that articulate with the dry, grinding screech of iron on iron. They have no eyes; their perception of the world is entirely acoustic and piezoelectric. They listen to the ringing frequencies of the stone beneath them, feeling the song of rising magma and the groan of shifting tectonic plates through their heavy, chisel-ended limbs.

These entities are the *Balusters*. The colossal stone rings and dolmens scattered across the ridges are not ruins left by some vanished precursor; they are the Balusters’ communal work, ongoing and eternal. 

The Balusters do not build for shelter—shelter is a concept for things that can be bruised by falling rain or cooled by wind. They build to anchor. They understand the mountain-spines as wild, eruptive beasts that must be pinned, weighted, and counterbalanced. When an obsidian needle begins to tilt precariously over a void, a herd of Balusters will spend generations gathering prisms of columnar basalt, wedging them into the fault lines, stacking mammoth lintels across the fracture to brace the stone against its own ascent.

Their life is a slow, Sisyphean struggle against the upward-shattering world. A Baluster may spend three centuries hauling a three-hundred-ton block of basalt up a vertical shear, only for a sudden tectonic snap to cleave the ridge beneath it, carrying the block miles into the sky while the builder is dropped into the gulf below. They survive such falls; their bodies do not break so much as they shatter along pre-formed cleavage planes, the fragments slowly drawing back together over decades, pulled by intense static and magnetic affinities until the creature is whole again, scarred with new, glassy seams.

***

At the very bottom, in the narrow, lightless chasms where the maroon glow is born, there is a final, strange ecology: the *Glass-weavers*.

They live where the heat is so intense that the basalt is not quite solid, but ductile—moving with the slow, terrifying viscosity of chilled tar. The Weavers resemble long, articulated skeins of glassy filament, like animate spools of spun basalt. They do not walk or fly; they weave themselves directly into the emerging fault lines. 

As the molten glass cools and hardens into obsidian on its journey upward, the Weavers draw out the cooling threads, lacing them back and forth across the rising faces. They leave behind intricate, tensioned webs of micro-filament that span miles of negative space between adjacent spires. 

In the pitch-black void, these webs are invisible, but they hum. As the spires rise at different speeds, the filaments are stretched taut, vibrating at frequencies so deep and powerful that they liquefy the dust in the air. The entire sunless world is strung with these colossal, singing cat's-cradles. 

When a traveler—or a drifting Glider, or a falling block of stone—strikes one of these unseen webs, the impact does not break the thread. The thread, under millions of pounds of tectonic tension, instantly shears through whatever touched it, slicing stone, crystal, or organism into perfect, geometrically flawless halves. The severed pieces tumble down toward the glowing fissures, while the web rings on in the dark, a harp played by the upward violence of the earth, celebrating a world that has never known a drop of water, and has never learned to yield.
