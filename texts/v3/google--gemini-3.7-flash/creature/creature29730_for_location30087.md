To understand the life of the Upper Sump-Plateau, one must understand that to these creatures, the world is not a space defined by boundaries of view, but a dense, shifting architecture of temperature, vibration, and chemical draft. Nothing here possesses the luxury of an eye; the socket is an evolutionary dead end in a realm where the only constant is black vapor. In place of sight, the organisms have bloomed into monstrously sophisticated sensory instruments: skin that reads the thermal drift of a steam pipe from fifty paces, jawbones suspended in oily cartilage to parse the seismic chatter of the deep reciprocating engines, and broad, fan-like sensory antennae that taste the air for the sour, telltale tang of copper grease or the iron-rich musk of a herd in rut.

At the base of this living web are the things that graze directly upon the Machine’s metabolic exhaust. 

The most common of the great herbivores are the **Tallow-Wallowers**—broad, low-slung quadrupeds with pale, greasy hides thick as boot-soles, hanging in heavy, waterlogged dewlaps around their knees. Their heads are blunt wedges of reinforced bone, lacking ears or eyes entirely, capped instead with a broad, honeycomb-textured shield of thermal pits that glow faintly pink beneath translucent skin. They move with the glacial, rhythmic persistence of bellows, wandering the pipe-forests in loose pods. With broad, spade-like lower beaks, they scour the sweating flanks of high-pressure conduits, scraping off the thick, mineralized cakes of chemotrophic moss, sulfur-crusted fungi, and the fibrous, asbestos-like lagging that insulates the iron. Their guts are massive fermentation vats capable of breaking down industrial silicates, dead grease, and dense celluloid fibers; they rumble internally with a deep, liquid clatter that harmonizes strangely with the distant turbines. When they sleep, they do not lie upon the saturated stone, which leaches body heat; they drape themselves directly across the warm iron junction-boxes, their soft underbellies conforming to the rivets, kept alive by the steady, scalding hum beneath them.

Trailing the herds are the **Slag-Lickers**—smaller, long-necked scavengers resembling flightless, six-legged cranes. Their limbs are tipped with soft, suction-rimmed pads that allow them to walk upside-down along the underside of overhead pipe arrays. They dip long, needle-fine proboscises into the weeping gaskets and seam-leaks, drinking the boiling, mineral-saturated condensate directly before it hits the moss below. Their saliva is heavily alkaline, neutralizing the acidic runoff and precipitating bright crusts of blue and green copper salts along their throat-sacs, which clink together like dry pottery as they hop blindly from conduit to conduit.

In the mid-canopy—the howling, high-tension labyrinth of grounding cables, dangling rusted catenary lines, and thick, ropey root-systems—the predators hold dominion. 

The apex stalker of the cable-ways is the **Anvil-Tail**, or *Strikecrawler*. Sinuous, thirty feet of banded, segmented muscle sheathed in a wet, leathery carapace that resists both high heat and electrical arcs, it travels along the cable networks with a fluid, centipede-like gait. Its primary hunting organ is not in its mouth, but at the tip of its heavy, spatulate tail: a massive, iron-dense bulb of calcified bone. 

The Anvil-Tail does not stalk by scent alone; it hunts by resonance. Clamping its forward claspers to an iron pylon or a suspension cable spanning an abyss, it brings its tail down against the metal with a sudden, deafening *CRACK* that rings out like a dropped sledgehammer. For several seconds afterward, it presses the flat underside of its chin against the steel, feeling the reverberation return through the metal. It reads the acoustic landscape through the iron: the weight of a tallow-wallower crossing a bridge half a mile away, the frayed weakness of a cable, or the frantic flutter of an animal startled by the strike. When it strikes, it drops from above like a loosened anchor, suffocating its prey against the hot moss with its muscular coils while using serrated, scissor-like mouthparts to pierce the soft flesh beneath the beast’s thermal shield.

Farther up, within the hollow, roaring throats of the abandoned cooling towers and the shattered glass-and-iron observation domes, live the **Vapor-Condors**. These are not birds, but massive, warm-blooded dermapterans whose leathery, translucent flight membranes span forty feet. They cannot take off from flat ground; instead, they launch themselves into the permanent, boiling updrafts that vent from the plateau’s central pressure chimneys. 

They ride these vertical rivers of steam for days without landing, their wings ribbed with hollow, air-filled struts that whistle a continuous, ghostly chord as they circle. They feed upon the aerial plankton of the Sump—swarms of blind, moth-like grease-skimmers and the trailing, buoyant seed-spores of the liverworts—filtering the air through hairy, comb-like palps beneath their chins. When they mate, pairs lock their gripping-hooks together at the apex of a steam column, thousands of feet up in the thermal fog, tumbling through the pitch dark for thousands of feet in complete silence before breaking apart just above the churning surface of the black tarns.

Down in the black waters themselves—the vast, boiling-warm condensation basins that pool in the hollowed granite sinks—an entirely separate, liquid darkness churns. 

Here dwell the **Blind Dredgers**, crabs the size of wagon wheels whose shells are completely encrusted with galvanic moss and tiny, tubiculous worms. They walk the sunken, silt-choked remains of old ore-tracks and submerged machinery, using heavy, asymmetric claws to crush the thick shells of oil-drinking mollusks that cluster around underwater coolant vents. 

Alongside them swim the **Cable-Eels**, smooth, muscular ribbon-fish six paces in length, which have abandoned eyes for lateral lines so sensitive they can detect the magnetic field generated by energized cables submerged in the muck. They coil around active high-voltage lines, using the ambient electrical leakage to stimulate their metabolism and warm their blood, occasionally discharging their own stored current into the shoals of amphipods that drift too close to their iron perches.

Yet the most unsettling inhabitants of the Upper Sump-Plateau are the ones that work with hands.

They are called the **Gasket-Folk** or the **Pipe-Men**, though they are barely recognizable as kindred to anything human. Pale, squat, entirely hairless, their skin is permanently stained with a slick patina of machine-oil and copper grime, functioning like a waterproof second hide. Their fingers are disproportionately long, double-jointed, and tipped with thick, shovel-like nails capable of prying loose rusted bolts and tearing through dense mats of rubber-moss. They have no eyes; their faces are flat, smooth expanses of skin dominated by large, flaring nostrils and wide, mobile ears that can swivel independently to triangulate the hiss of a failing valve or the approach of a predator through the steam.

The Gasket-Folk do not speak in words; they communicate through a rapid, percussive code of knuckle-taps against the pipes. A single tap on an overhead steam main can carry for leagues, passed along the iron spine from clan to clan: a message warning of a pressure-blowout, a migrating herd of tallow-wallowers, or an Anvil-Tail hunting along the high cables.

They do not build structures; the Machine has already built their world. Instead, they inhabit the dead spaces: dry transformer vaults, the hollow interiors of dead boiler drums, and the narrow crawlspaces behind maintenance bulkheads. They live in a state of deep, instinctual symbiosis with the architecture. They do not understand the mechanics of the Plateau as engineers did, but they understand it as an ecology. They know which valves must be levered open with bone-bars to keep their nesting caverns warm, which weeping flanges yield sweet, drinkable water, and which vibrating floorplates herald the catastrophic rise of a scalding mud-geyser.

They harvest the blind liverworts, cultivate mats of pale, greasy shelf-fungi in the moisture-rich air of the exhaust ducts, and hunt the wallower calves using long, fire-hardened pikes tipped with scrap-iron shards. They treat the great, thrumming turbines buried in the granite not as machines, but as the dormant, titanic hearts of the earth itself—gods whose rhythmic, bone-shaking pulse keeps the killing frost of the upper atmosphere away from their dark, dripping cradle. 

In the Plateau, nothing is separate from the iron. The bone of the grazer incorporates the calcium and rust of the ground down pipes; the hunter tracks by the acoustics of the truss; the fungi digest the lubricants of an ancient, unremembered industry; and the Gasket-Folk sleep with their ears pressed against the cold steel of the floor, their dreams shaped entirely by the deep, eternal heartbeat of the steam.
