In this high, clear ceiling of the world, life does not squander itself on softness. There are no gelatinous bellies, no erratic, fluttering fins, and no frantic metabolisms driven by the suddenness of hunger or fear. Where the physical laws have been refined into a perpetual, unvarying clockwork, the organisms themselves have taken on the dense, cold perfection of machined parts.

Clinging to the corrugated flanks of the siphon-trunks are the **Strokers**—the train-car-sized arthropods whose life cycles are pinned directly to the vertical strokes of the central pistons. Their shells are not chitin as terrestrial shores know it, but thick, stratified laminates of zinc-impregnated enamel and petrified lignin, ground to a dull gunmetal sheen by centuries of friction. They do not walk; they have no free-roaming legs. Instead, their undersides are lined with sets of heavy, calcified rollers that sit within the natural guide-rails of the trunk’s bark. As the miles-long amber pistons rise and fall, the Strokers are driven along these tracks in rhythmic, reciprocating ascents and descents. 

They feed without chewing. Their mouthparts are broad, flat scraping-trowels of burnished silica that plane away the dense, waxy lipid-slurries secreted by the tree’s friction-rings. Because they move exclusively in tandem with the hydraulic stroke, their heartbeats are mechanically triggered: each downstroke compresses a flexible thoracic plate that forces blue, hemocyanin-heavy blood through their rigid circulatory conduits; each upstroke draws it back into passive reservoirs. A Stroker separated from its trunk would not drown or starve—it would simply stop, its circulation frozen in the middle of a single mechanical cycle.

In the vast, fifty-mile voids between the trunks, the water is so crystalline that the eye finds nothing to rest upon until it catches the glare of the **Needle-Grids**. These are schools of pelagic siphon-feeders, each no longer than an iron rod, with needle-thin bodies sheathed in mirror-bright plates of crystalline guanine. They do not swim by undulating. Within their hollow, tubular bodies, microscopic cilia beat against the dense, cold fluid to create a steady, silent venturi-suction. 

The Needle-Grids do not school in shifting clouds; they hang in rigid, geometric arrays—lattices spanning quarter-miles of open water, perfectly aligned with the prevailing electrical lines of the kelp-decks below. Every individual maintains an exact distance of twelve inches from its neighbors, locked into the grid by electro-receptive nodes in their snouts. When the piston exhaust pulses through the void, carrying its invisible, organized front of starch-granules, the entire grid feeds simultaneously, motionless and unblinking, harvesting the particulate matter like a massive, silvered sieve hung in the blue.

Deeper along the horizontal planes, on the upper faces of the kelp-iron leaf-decks, lives an ecology that eats the thunder. These are the **Cathode Mantas**—vast, flat-bodied organisms twenty paces across, their skin composed of vitrified, non-conductive slate-grey plates edged with copper-hued filaments. They lie flush against the rigid photosynthetic decks, anchoring themselves with heavy, tap-rooted claspers that penetrate directly into the plant’s galvanic conduits. 

The Mantas are entirely silent. They have no digestive tracts, for they have abandoned the chemistry of consumption; they are living capacitors. They absorb the blue-white coronal discharges that race along the leaf margins, storing the high-voltage potential within banks of lipid-insulated electrolytic organs. When fully charged, their dorsal plates emit a pale, violet phosphorescence that does not waver or flicker, casting long, sharp shadows through miles of empty water. Once every several decades, when a Manta reaches its electrical threshold, it releases a single, controlled arc upward into the water column—a blinding vertical lance of cold light that cracks dissolved silicates into precise, micro-crystalline flakes that rain slowly back down to nourish the lower fronds.

Suspended in the loneliest, most cavernous spans of open fluid are the **Plumb-Titans**. They are the largest of the free-drifting inhabitants: pencil-straight, spindle-shaped leviathans several hundred yards in length, hanging perfectly vertical in the water column like titanic plumb-bobs. They have no fins, no eyes, and no appendages. Their bodies are hollow shells of mineralized bone, weighted at the lower terminus with stones of dense barite and pressurized at the upper tip with chambers of compressed nitrogen. 

The Plumb-Titans are the chronometers of the high ocean. Inside their hollow central core hangs a suspended, fibrous pendulum of solid iron-wood. As the great baseline hum of the world passes through the cold fluid—the *chug-clack* of valves leagues away, the hydraulic groan of distant pistons—the Titan’s pendulum resonates, striking internal acoustic plates with a slow, low-frequency reverberation that can be felt by everything within a day’s travel. They do not hunt or graze; they absorb the dissolved minerals carried by the deep vertical loops of water through millions of microscopic pores along their length, growing so slowly that a single millimetre of their ivory-like shell represents three hundred thousand mechanical strokes of the sky-ocean.

In this world, death is not an event of rot or violence. When a creature reaches the end of its structural endurance, it does not decay into disorder. A Stroker whose joints have finally worn to tolerance simply locks into the bark of its siphon-trunk, becoming a permanent, calcified bolt that the plant’s expanding tissue slowly absorbs. A Plumb-Titan whose ballast seals fail does not thrash; it simply begins a long, perfectly vertical descent, sinking through fifty thousand feet of increasingly emerald, sunless water until it comes to rest on the blind planetary crust below, joining the vast, cold boneyard of ancient mechanisms that have quietly completed their work.

Every organism here possesses the same uncanny serenity. They have no nervous architecture for alarm, no capacity to startle, no concept of an intruder or an enemy. They are parts of an engine that has never missed an ignition, moving with the heavy, frictionless grace of bronze and water, suspended under an unchanging white sun that keeps their cold, perfect house forever bright.
