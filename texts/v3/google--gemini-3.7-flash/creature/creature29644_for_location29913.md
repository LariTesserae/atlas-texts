To know what lives here, one must first discard the assumption that life requires motion, or that survival is a matter of hunger and replenishment.

In the Thimble-Planes, life has long since finished its work. What remains is the architecture of an ecology that has arrived at its final, perfectly balanced sum.

### The Great Anchors

The six-winged ungulates—the colossi upon whose bodies the human shelters cling—are known by those who walk between their hooves as the *Stelae*. 

Their hides are not hairy in the mammalian sense, but covered in a dense, cool fleece of hollow mineral filaments, closer to asbestos or spun gypsum than hair. If one parts this pale, powdery wool with both hands, the skin beneath is gray-white, cool to the touch, and unyielding as vulcanized rubber. There are no pores, no beads of oil, and no pulse perceptible to a human palm. 

Yet they are not stone. Beneath the skin, deep within the vault of the ribs, the organs rest in dry, suspended symmetry. Their lungs do not inflate and deflate; instead, they are vast, honeycomb-like calcifications that hold a single, permanent breath taken at the beginning of the era. Their hearts—dense masses of fibrous, silicate-laced muscle the size of haylofts—do not beat in a rhythm of seconds. A single cycle of their circulation takes generations. When the heart does contract, it does not push fluid; it transmits a deep, seismic shudder through the marrow of the legs and into the bedrock itself. The human inhabitants call this *the Settle*. It happens perhaps once in a long lifetime—a dull, sub-audible *thump* that causes the loose chalk on the cliffs to shift a millimeter and the anchored ropes to hum like tightened bowstrings.

Their horns do not shed. They spiral upward in complex, fluted towers, ribbed like the shells of ammonites, bleached to the color of dry bone. In the hollows of these horns, where the air is completely still, the silence is so absolute that a person resting their ear against the calcified ridges can hear the molecular lattice of the horn slowly hardening, atom by atom, into crystal.

### The Strata of the Void

In the air between the pillars, the suspension of life follows strict geological horizons.

The floating serpents—the *Drift-Cables*—occupy the middle elevation, between four and six thousand spans above the floor of the chasms. They range from the thickness of a ship’s mast to bodies broad enough to pitch a camp upon. Their underbellies are clad in broad, overlapping scutes of translucent horn, while their backs are covered in a dense, downy plume that never soils. 

They do not swim through the air; they are moored to the invisible geometry of the atmosphere. If a traveler leaps upon the back of a serpent, it does not dip. It absorbs the momentum into its mass without swaying, its eyes—faceted like cut tourmaline—staring through the traveler into the pale distance. 

Farther up, forming the soft, vaulted ceiling that filters the unchanging sun, are the *Tesserae*. These are broad, flat, ray-like beings whose wings have fused together at the margins over epochs of proximity. Their leathery skins have interlocked, forming a continuous living canopy miles wide. They live on nothing; they are sustained entirely by the dry, ambient radiation of the upper sky. Because their wings overlap like slate shingles, they form an unbroken baffle against whatever space lies above. To look up from the surface of a massif is not to look at an open sky, but at a ribbed, gray-brown ceiling of skin that stretches to every horizon, faintly patterned with the concentric growth-rings of ten million static flyers.

### The In-Between Vermin

Where there are pelts that never fall and skin that never sheds, there is a specialized fauna of absolute stillness.

Living deep in the furrows of the colossi’s fleece are the *Mica-Crabs* and *Tallow-Moths*. They are creatures of brittle horn and dried chitin, translucent as wax paper. They do not feed in the aggressive sense; they graze upon the dry, powdery bloom that forms on the beasts’ skin, moving at a speed so negligible that a single step across a beast’s flank may take a season. 

There are no liquids in their bodies. If crushed, they do not smear; they shatter into a fine, gray dust that smells faintly of dried musk and chalk. Their wings, if they possess them, are rigid and glassy, held permanently spread like specimen slides. They do not lay eggs; their young bud from their carapaces as tiny, mineral nodules that slowly swell, harden, and eventually detach when the mother’s shell undergoes its centurial molt.

In the deep, narrow spaces where two colossi stand flank-to-flank, the pressure has fused their fleece into a solid, felted felt-rock. Here live the *Chalk-Weavers*—blind, many-legged things the size of a man’s hand, whose shells are indistinguishable from the siltstone beneath them. They spend centuries spinning bridges of dry silk between the two beasts, knotting the fur together until the boundary between the two creatures is entirely erased.

### The Flank-Dwellers

The people who live among the beasts do not call themselves masters, hunters, or shepherds. They are the *Lichen-Men* or the *Tethered*.

Physically, they have adapted to the radical drought and the sound-eating world. Their skin is thick, pale, and dry, dusted with the fine talc that coats everything on the planes. They do not sweat; their bodies conserve moisture with ferocious efficiency, recycling fluid internally through dense, convoluted kidneys. Their eyes are clear and hooded, protected from the cold glare by thick, transparent second lids that allow them to stare across the blinding white rock without blinking.

They wear garments made of unspun wool clipped from the underbellies of the Stelae, felted with water carried up from forgotten epochs and beaten with stone mallets until it is stiff as birch-bark. Their boots are soled with slices of shed horn, stitched with dried sinew that will never rot.

Because sound does not travel, their spoken language has withered down to a handful of guttural clicks and breathy vowels used only in the closest intimacy, their lips pressed directly against the listener’s ear. For communication across distance, they have invented a complex system of *Tension-Signaling*. 

They run cords of braided colossus-hair from one beast to another, stretching them taut between bone anchors driven into the horns. By striking these cords with small mallets of polished chert, they send vibrations traveling through the wool and horn of the herds. A family living on the ribs of an ungulate on the western ledge can feel the rhythm of a message through the soles of their feet, transmitted across three miles of unmoving bodies.

Their architecture is entirely parasitic upon the megafauna:

*   **Rib-Cots:** Small sleeping-platforms slung beneath the massive curve of an ungulate’s sternum. The beast’s steady, internal heat—a dry, gentle warmth like a stone that has sat in an oven hours ago—keeps the sleeper from freezing in the thin air.
*   **Horn-Cairns:** Towers of dry-stacked siltstone built around the spirals of the horns. These serve as family vaults and storage chambers for stone tools, dried lichen-cakes, and historical tallies carved into thin slabs of slate.
*   **The Cable-Roads:** Pathways laid out across the backs of the suspended serpents. The people lay flat planks of slate across the serpents' spines to create smooth highways from one massif to the next. The serpents never twitch; they are better foundations than iron cantilevers.

### The Cycle of the Chalk

The Flank-Dwellers do not bury their dead in the earth, for the stone is too hard to dig, and there is no soil to receive them.

Instead, when a person reaches the end of their dry, prolonged life, they are brought to the *Gap*—the edge of the massif where the suspended flyers hang closest to the cliff. The body is wrapped tightly in layers of felted wool and bound with sinew until it resembles a chrysalis. 

The elders lift the bundle and place it upon the back of a hovering flyer, wedging it securely between the overlapping wings of the *Tesserae*. There, in the cold, moisture-free air, the body does not decay. It desiccates over decades, its minerals slowly migrating outward into the wool, until the human remains have become a light, chalky fossil, indistinguishable from the horn and hide of the creatures that support it.

In this way, the dead do not leave the world; they merely join the canopy, adding their weight to the vast, unmoving roof of life that holds the sky in place.
