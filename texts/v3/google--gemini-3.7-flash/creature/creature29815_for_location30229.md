In the cool, damp cradle of the notch, life has adapted to the severe geometry of fresh-cut stone, moving with a deliberate, low-friction patience that mirrors the building itself.

The first to truly claim the masonry are not human, but they share the space with an easy, unbothered familiarity. The slate-skinned geckos are stout and thick-tailed, their bellies perpetually dusted with pale rock flour. They do not merely scuttle over the walls; they use the razor-sharp chisel grooves as highways, their soft lamellae anchoring effortlessly into microscopic crosshatches. They linger where the ribbons of condensation slip downward, drinking with rapid, hair-thin tongues directly from the vertical basalt. Beneath them, in the subterranean gaps between the unmortared pavers, pale myriapods and soft-bodied cave crickets with antennae four times the length of their bodies navigate the black voids. They feed on the fungal bloom that rises from the quarry dust, entirely blind to the filtered daylight above, content in the steady thermal blanket of the floor.

Those who cut this place and walk its alleys are a quiet, broad-chested folk whose bodies seem shaped by the same tectonic patience that laid the shelf. 

They are people of low center and thick sinew, their movements rhythmic and unhurried. Because the basalt paver-edges are still keen enough to slice leather, their feet are bare, protected by immense, horn-thick calluses that have formed over years of balancing on fractured stone. The soles of their feet and the palms of their broad hands are stained an indelible slate-gray, ingrained with the fine, powdery silt of ground mineral. Their skin is smooth and cool to the touch, deep umber or olive-ash, lightly oiled with tallow and pressed fig seed to turn away the persistent cling of the greenhouse damp. 

They wear very little: kilts of hammered bast fiber stripped from the upper fig trees, cross-belted with plaited nettle cord that does not rot in perpetual moisture. In this warm, windless under-croft, clothing is an encumbrance; it catches on the sharp corners of the lintels and holds the sweat. When they work or rest, their bare shoulders gleam faintly in the green-tinted gloom, catching the same dim, silver highlights as the wet stone behind them.

They do not shout. The sunken basalt court acts as an acoustic sink, absorbing high frequencies into its velvet mosses and damp fissures while rumbling low pitches through the floor. Consequently, their language has evolved away from sharp consonants. They speak in low, chest-resonant murmurs, guttural clicks, and soft whistling hums that carry along the stone faces without waking the canopy above. A worker three alleys away understands a command not by hearing a name called, but by the rhythmic, deliberate tap of a hard river-stone against a lintel—a code of vibrations felt through the soles of their feet.

Their tools lie neatly arranged in the shallow, dry alcoves, devoid of bronze or iron: rounded pounders of dense, imported diorite; heavy mauls carved from dense river-flint; long pry-bars of green ironwood polished smooth by sweat; and wide, flat wedges of split bone. They are in no haste to roof the courtyards or finish the town. For them, the settlement is already alive and functional; the shelter is not a ceiling, but the depth of the earth itself. 

In the open, roofless chambers, they live collectively and loosely. Where the monolithic lintel stands alone against the ferns, family groups gather on wide mats woven from split arum stems. They sit with their backs pressed directly against the cool, sheer faces of the basalt, finding comfort in the rock's steady, unyielding thermal mass. They eat quietly, sharing meals of river snails, boiled bitter arum roots mashed into thick gray paste, and heavy, purple-skinned figs dropped down from the rim ropes in wide-mesh baskets. 

Around their ankles, the small burrowing toads shuffle through the crushed rock, partially buried, their throats pulsing in slow synchrony with the breathing of the resting folk. No one steps on them; no one drives away the pale, heavy moths that settle on the sleeping mats to drink the salted sweat from discarded fiber belts. The hunting spiders that stretch their gossamer nets between the lintels are treated as domestic fixtures, their webs left unbroken to catch the biting midges that occasionally drift down from the upper jungle.

When sleep comes, it is taken in shifts in the deep alcoves where the light never fully reaches. There, sheltered from the occasional falling droplet, they sleep on low shelves of dressed stone padded with dried liverwort. Their sleep is heavy, soundless, and deep—entrenched six fathoms below the roaring winds of the plain, cradled in warm stone, breathing the rich, damp exhales of a city still being born from the rock.
