In the narrow under-ribs of the plates—where the iron sheets overlap like tectonic shingles over the machinery below—life begins as a film.

It is a slick, grease-nourished culture of pale organisms that neither crawl nor walk, but slide. They have the consistency of cured lard, flat as hand-towels, moving with a steady hydraulic creep across the damp steel to graze on the yellow condensate that seeps from bearing-housings. Where two plates grind together in their slow, planetary shifts, these tallow-leeches gather in dense, quivering mats. They do not get crushed; their bodies are non-Newtonian, firming to dense cartilage under sudden pressure and liquifying under sustained weight to flow into the microscopic pores of the mill-scale. They are the base weight of this world’s flesh: blind, silent, tasting the iron for sulfur and tallow, leaving behind trails of clean, polished metal that never rust.

Feeding on them are the seam-crawlers—creatures built like long, flexible calipers. Their legs are many-jointed rods of dense, black keratin tipped with magnetic hooks that make a dry, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* as they clamber inverted across the ceilings of the lower galleries. They have no faces, only a rosette of stiff sensory hairs and a central drilling mouthparts capable of puncturing the tallow-mats and siphoning the warm, mineral-rich fats within. When a seam-crawler dies, its limbs do not relax; the magnetic tips remain locked to the iron overhead, leaving hollow, rust-crusted ribcages hanging like chandeliers in the steam-filled undercrofts for centuries.

Up on the open flats, where the air is wider and the red velvet moss spreads across the plates, the scale of life expands.

The grazers that pick their way through the mist are known by the heavy, rhythmic thud of their footfalls. Their legs are long, column-like, and lack knees as flesh-animals know them; instead, their limbs are segmented with thick, fibrous pads of self-healing vulcanized tissue that absorb the relentless shock of walking on unyielding iron. Their hides are heavy, pebbled, and perpetually wet, smelling of old raincoats and iron filings. 

Because they cannot see, their spade-shaped snouts are marvels of tactile mapping. The lower edge of the spade is a continuous ridge of self-sharpening, iron-hard tooth that acts as a chisel, peeling the sulfur-moss from the plates in clean, dark strips. Behind this chisel lies a deep, wet chamber lined with thousands of microscopic pits that sense the weak electrical potentials of the ground. They can feel the presence of a buried copper bus-bar fifty feet down, or the approach of another animal by the static charge it builds against the humid air. 

These grazers move in small, silent wedges, usually three or four together, their snouts pressed low. They do not communicate with sound; their throats are voiceless bellows used only to vent the warm, moist air of their lungs. Instead, they drum. By dropping their heavy breastbones sharply against the plate, they send low-frequency pings through the metal shelf. A mile away, another wedge feels the ring through the soles of its feet, registering whether the ground ahead is clear, occupied, or tilting toward a purge.

Above them, the air belongs to the mantles.

These are not birds, nor truly bats; they are vast, kite-like sheets of tough, translucent skin stretched over a light frame of hollow, oil-filled cartilage. They do not flap so much as adjust their trim, riding the immense columns of warm exhaust that rise out of the deep planetary vents. A mantle can remain aloft for months without descending, sleeping in the thermal updrafts with its wide, diamond-shaped body bowed into a gentle parachute.

The underbelly of a mantle is a map of pale, specialized organs. In the center sits an array of electro-receptive slits that read the plate-floor below as a glowing grid of magnetic currents and animal heat. When a mantle detects a herd of grazers disturbing a nest of seam-crawlers, it pulls its outer wings inward and descends.

It makes no sound as it drops. It does not strike with claws or talons; it smothers. The mantle spreads itself across the metal floor like a cast-net, its wet, heavy margins sealing against the iron plate to trap whatever scuttles beneath. The inner skin then secretes a rapid, acidic slime that breaks down the soft joints of the prey while leaving the iron untouched. When it has finished feeding, it waits for the next surge of steam from a nearby vent, cups the rising heat with its vast wings, and lifts back into the oily fog, leaving only a pale, clean square on the rusted floor.

In the deepest pans, where the magnetic mud gathers into thick, still lakes, there are lifeforms that have abandoned motion entirely.

These are the bellows-trees—sessile colonies that take root directly in the expansion bolts and drain-ports. They look like clusters of ribbed, black pipes, four to twelve feet tall, their walls constructed of layered mineral plates glued with animal resin. As the atmosphere breathes—cooling slightly when the fog thickens, heating when a subterranean shaft vents—the bellows-trees expand and contract with a low, wheezing sigh. 

They are living condensers. Their ribbed skins draw moisture and atomized grease directly from the warm air, channeling it down through their root-structures into the machinery below. In return, the vent-plumes keep them warm. In their hollow trunks, small, wingless vermin nest by the thousands, living off the grease-droplets that trickle down the internal flues. When the plates occasionally tilt and the cascading wash of condensate sweeps across the flats, the bellows-trees close their upper valves with a sound like heavy leather doors slamming shut, standing firm against the torrent while the water rushes around their rooted feet.

There are no seasons here, no day and night to divide the hours. The only rhythm is the long, slow heartbeat of the machine beneath: the century-long cycles of the grease-baths, the turning of the giant planetary pins, the occasional three-degree tilt of an iron continent shedding its water into the dark. 

The things that live here are patient. They do not hurry. Their blood runs thick and dark with iron compounds, their metabolisms are slow as deep-sea vents, and their lives are paced to the quiet, rust-damp eternity of the shelf. They exist in an enduring equilibrium with the iron, neither conquering it nor being destroyed by it, perfectly content in the warm, blind fog between the clouds and the gears.
