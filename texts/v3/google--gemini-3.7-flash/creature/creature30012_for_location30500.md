Where there are no insects, there is no twitching. The life that inhabits the vault has abandoned the frantic economy of antennae and wings; it operates on the heavier leverage of tallow, cartilage, and hydraulic tension.

The smallest of them are the **Pellicles**, though they are less like creatures and more like wandering sheets of wet, liver-colored skin. They exist wherever the condensation is thickest, plastered flat against the leeward flanks of the steam chests. No thicker than a cobbler’s apron and often five or six feet wide, a Pellicle moves without limbs, creeping across the rivet heads by an infinitesimal, peristaltic ripple that leaves behind a trail of clear, neutral lipid. They are blind, deaf, and mouthless in any conventional sense; their entire undersides are carpets of micro-cilia that rasp away the microscopic crust of iron-feeding bacteria and the paraffin-wax crusts shed by the plants. When a drop of hot, amber-tinted condensate falls from an overhead flange, a Pellicle will sense the localized impact of heat and slowly—over the course of several hours—funnel its broad margins around the droplet, absorbing the iron salts directly into its rubbery mass.

Higher up, where the copper pneumatic lines cross the vaulted ceiling, live the **Tension-Crawlers**. 

These are long, segmented things, the color of wet slate and cured bacon. Their bodies are built like heavy, flexible gaskets, thirty joints or more, each ringed with a collar of pale, vestigial blubber that buffers them against the scalding copper. They have no eyes; their heads are blunt, spade-like wedges of solid horn, designed to wedge into the seams between pipe-flanges to prize loose the ancient mineral scale within. 

Their locomotion belongs entirely to the four-minute cadence of the floor. For three minutes and fifty seconds, a Crawler hangs motionless beneath a pipe, suspended by four pairs of hooked, caliper-like limbs whose inner edges are lined with dense, horn-toothed pads. Its metabolism is so slow that its heartbeat cannot be distinguished from the background hum of the bleed-off valves. 

Then, when the bedrock shifts—when the subterranean piston strikes its deep, concussive *thoom-clack*—the Crawler uses the shockwave. In the single second that the copper pipe shudders and the air pressure spikes, the creature releases its grip, lurches forward eight inches in the violent slack of the vibration, and clamps down again before the pipe settles. Between strokes, it is as rigid as cast iron. To watch a column of them migrating along an overhead manifold is to watch a series of heavy, jointed levers advancing in sudden, single-inch jerks, perfectly synchronized with the subterranean heart of the vault.

And then there are the **Gasket-Bows**, the true apex of the vault’s quiet biomass. 

They do not hunt; there is nothing to chase. Instead, they nest within the empty expansion chambers and the dead, water-logged crankcases of the largest flywheels. A mature Bow is roughly the size of a draught horse, but flattened, as if it had spent generations being squeezed through a roller-mill. Its hide is hairless, gray-black, and nearly two inches thick, scarred with the geometric patterns of the floor-gratings it has rested upon for centuries. It possesses four thick, pillar-like legs that terminate not in hooves or claws, but in broad, suctioning pads that can lock onto wet, oil-slick iron with enough force to resist a steam-jet.

The Gasket-Bow feeds on the plum-colored fronds, but its digestion is geological. It does not chew. Its lower jaw is a single, broad plate of dense bone that grinds against a matching upper plate with an agonized, hydraulic groan, crushing the fibrous, waxen leaves into a dense, oily meal. Inside its vast, tripartite belly, this vegetable starch is fermented alongside sulfur water and flakes of iron crust, producing an internal heat so intense that the creature’s breath smells of boiling battery acid and wet coal.

A Bow will spend forty years in a single twelve-foot stretch of catwalk. It knows the terrain not by sight—its eyes are merely milk-white beads sunken beneath protective ridges of calloused fat—but by the thermal map of the ironwork beneath it. It knows which rivet is warm, which valve leaks greasy tallow, and which plate vibrates with the coming of the stroke. 

When a Bow breathes, it does so in long, shuddering drafts that last three minutes each. The air passes through large, lateral spiracles lined with wet, felt-like lung-leaves that filter the oil-mist from the vapor. As it exhales, it vents a thin, warm plume of grey steam from behind its shoulder blades, adding its own small moisture to the condensation cycle that feeds the plants at its feet.

Reproduction among them is so rare and so slow that it resembles an industrial process. Every few decades, a female Bow will deposit a single, heavy egg—encased in a black, bituminous shell that hardens on contact with the sulfurous air until it resembles a round iron casting—directly inside the open intake of an idle cylinder. There, sheltered from the occasional drops of boiling grease and warmed by the eternal geothermal glow from the grates below, the embryo develops over nine years, drinking in the iron-rich rust-sweat through micro-pores in its shell.

In this place, life has forgotten the sky, the seasons, and the speed of things that run. It has become an adjunct to the machinery—a soft, patient bronze that grows over the hard bronze, measuring its life not in days, but in the millions of slow, deafening strokes of the pump below.
