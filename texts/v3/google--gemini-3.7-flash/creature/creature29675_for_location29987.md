To exist in the trench is to have surrendered the need for dry edges. Everything that lives here is slightly permeable, coated in an unctuous, protective lipid or a skin that drinks the air directly.

At the lowest tier, where the black compost meets the foot of the basalt retaining walls, the ground belongs to the **girdled snails**. They do not have the brittle, chalky shells of their plateau kin; instead, their whorls are formed of a dense, flexible chitin the color of wet horn, soft enough to yield under the foot of a bat and spring back without cracking. They feed not on living leaves, which are too tough with defensive tannins, but on the grey fleece of mold that coats the underside of fallen limbs. Their paths across the basalt are marked by trails of pale, iridescent grease that never dries, forming permanent, frictionless highways that smaller things—soft-shelled mites, minuscule orange springtails no larger than grains of pollen—use to travel without drowning in the surface tension of the stone’s constant sweat.

Wedged into the vertical seams between the masonry blocks live the **wax-bellies**. These are lungless salamanders, eight inches long, their bodies pale as lard except for their throats, which pulse with a dull, blood-rich salmon pink. Because they have no lungs, their skin must remain perpetually flooded with condensation to breathe; they position themselves precisely along the stone’s drainage grooves, hanging vertical for days at a time, head-downward, letting the slow distillation of the plateau above wash over their flanks. When they mate, they do not enter pools—for there are none—but deposit their clutches in the water-filled axils of the broad, maroon-veined arums. The larvae do not swim; they wriggle through a soup of drowned pollen and bacterial jelly, breathing with feathery external gills that are purple and thick as velvet moss.

Higher in the tangle, where the broad leaves overlap into tiered platforms, the **velvet-winged moths** roost. They are large, triangular, with bodies as thick as a thumb, covered in a dense, water-repellent pelt of charcoal fur that insulates their high internal heat. Because the stagnant air cannot carry sound, it carries scent with suffocating fidelity; the males have antennae that spread like twin ferns of black lace, sensitive enough to track a female’s musky, fermented-fruit pheromone through three hundred yards of dense canopy without a draft to guide it. At twilight, when the hairline fissure above dims from copper to slate, these moths begin their *thermo-dance*: they cling to the underside of the leather-leaves and vibrate their flight muscles at extreme speeds, making no sound, yet generating small, localized halos of heat that can be felt against the bare skin inches away.

This heat is their undoing and their necessity. It attracts the **green-banded vipers**, whose sensory pits are tuned not to the cool air, but to those precise, throbbing points of warmth. These snakes are heavy-bodied, their bellies broad and flat, lined with keeled scales that grip the slick stone like tire tread. They are nearly blind; their eyes have clouded over beneath thick, protective spectacles that shield them from the acidic drippings of the ceiling. They hunt entirely by temperature and the thrum of the vines. A viper may remain coiled around a single basalt corbel for three months, its heartbeat dropping to two beats a minute, its digestion so protracted that the skull of a mouse-sized bat takes forty days to dissolve within its gut.

In the deep crevices beneath the collapsed plinths, where the soil is untouched by light and stays at the constant, feverish temperature of the deep rock, lives the oldest resident: the **burrowing lung-toad**. These creatures are nearly spherical, the size of a human skull, with smooth, rubbery skin of a mottled plum-black that secretes a bitter, milky slime fatal to fungi. They have no use for legs beyond short, spade-like nubs with which they churn the bottom layer of compost, swallowing the rotting fiber whole to digest the nematodes and blind millipedes trapped within it. They have survived here since before the ceiling fractured. When the air pressure drops before a rare, violent plateau storm sixty feet above, the lung-toads inflate their massive, throat-spanning vocal sacs and produce a single, subsonic groan—a frequency so low it does not travel as sound, but causes the condensation drops on the vaulted ceiling to lose their grip all at once, showering the trench in a sudden, silent, interior rain.

Even the micro-bats that hang from the highest vault have adapted to the breathless chamber. They are no larger than walnuts, with translucent, pink-veined wing membranes that are completely naked. They do not hunt flying insects; the space is too crowded for chase. Instead, they *glean*. They drop from the ceiling in silent parachuting glides, landing on the broad leaves with padded feet to lap the greasy nectar from the wax-scented orchids, or to pick off the sluggish, soft-bodied katydids that graze there. Their guano does not fall to earth dry; it dissolves into the root-curtains on the descent, swallowed by aerial ferns that have abandoned roots entirely, living solely on the bats' warm, ammonia-rich wash.

Nothing leaves the trench. A beetle that reaches the hairline fracture above is shriveled in seconds by the dry, scouring winds of the upper desert; a desert lizard that slips into the fissure drowns in the heat and fungal rot within a day, its carcass claimed by the white threads of mycelium before its nerves have fully stopped twitching. The life down here is a closed circuit, an ancient, damp engine turning slowly upon itself, greased by its own sweat, thriving in the dark pressure of its own uninterrupted breath.
