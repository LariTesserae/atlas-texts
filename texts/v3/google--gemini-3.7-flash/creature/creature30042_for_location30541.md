In the grease-wells between the knees of the locomotive ungulates, where the skin is not plate but folded, oiled bellows-hide three fingers thick, live the **Sump-Nits**. 

They are the size of a man’s forearm, though flattened so severely they can slip between the sliding steel leaves of a hip-cowl without breaking stride. Their bodies are cast of a brittle, phosphorus-rich bronze that gives them a greenish, wet cast in the violet daylight. They have no eyes; their vision is entirely kinematic, felt through four pairs of quartz-tipped stylus-legs that read the micro-torsions and heat blooms of their host’s stride. The Sump-Nits do not eat the host’s iron—to thin the armor would be to kill the kiln that keeps them from freezing solid—but rather scrape the tallow-like, graphite-heavy exudates that weep from the beast’s annular bearings. 

When a Sump-Nit is gravid, it does not lay eggs. It extrudes a single, wire-thin coil of spring-tempered spring-steel, pre-stressed under immense internal muscular tension. It anchors one end of this wire into a deep rivet-hole with a plug of self-curing resin, then drops away. As the giant host steps forward, the coil snaps straight, whipping the tiny larva at its tip through the freezing air like a projectile, aimed at the warm exhaust plume of a neighboring browser miles across the scree. Most strike raw shale and shatter into silver powder; the few that strike exhaust-casing burrow inward through the heat vents, their shells smoking against the 400-degree drafts, to take up their places in the next nursery.

***

Farther down, where the copper-filament moss forms a springy, conductive crust over the fault-lines, the predatory clades operate. Predation here is neither a chase nor an ambush, but a process of **mechanical fatigue**.

The most widespread of these hunters are the **Wedge-Ticks** or **Billet-Biters**. They are low, broad-beamed hemipterans the size of slag-pots, their outer shells formed of layered, cold-hammered titanium-silicate that is utterly indifferent to the diamond-glare above. They move on six wide, leaden skids rather than legs, dragging themselves across the copper felt by means of a high-torque internal capstan—a ratcheting bio-winch that spools a tendon of braided carbon fiber hooked into distant rock-fissures.

A Billet-Biter will shadow a six-legged browser for fifty years, trailing the animal's slow, two-hour footsteps. It waits for the inevitable: a seam-failure, a cracked pivot-pin, or the fatal crystallization of a bellows-hinge caused by extreme cold. When the giant’s cadence hitches—when a leg fails to clear the scree by half an inch—the Billet-Biter strikes not with fangs, but with an array of internal, chemical-fire wedges. 

Dragging itself beneath the faltering joint, it anchors its body to the underlying granite with hydraulic expansion bolts fired three feet deep into the rock. It then elevates its back-plate via an internal toggle-press capable of generating thousands of tons of static lift. It does not crush the browser; it levers it off its balance. When the multi-ton browser topples, its massive steel plates buckle under its own immense weight, splitting along the annealed seam-weld lines. 

The death of a bellows-beast takes weeks. The Billet-Biter does not lap blood—the giant contains only gallons of sulfurous, high-pressure transmission oil and a slurry of pulverized quartz. Instead, the predator feeds by driving long, hollow augers directly into the exposed gizzard-furnace, drawing off the red-hot, ore-rich slag to fuel its own internal smelting organs, casting new plates for its shell in a continuous, glowing moult.

***

In the vertical spaces, where basalt horsts have cleaved into shear mile-high faces of black glass, cling the **Loom-Arachnids**. 

These creatures are almost entirely span without mass. Their limbs are gossamer girders of hollow, air-hardened bone-iron, reaching forty feet from tip to tip, while the central carapace is no larger than a sea-chest. They suspend themselves across the canyons on thick, humming stay-cables spun from biogenic tungsten wire. 

The Loom-Arachnids do not spin webs to catch meat; there are no soft flies in this sky. They are **galvanic filter-feeders**. As the freezing winds whip over the plateau’s edge, carrying vast static electrical charges generated by the friction of ice-dust and flying iron-grit, the Loom-Arachnid’s web acts as an induction array. The cables hum in the wind—a deep, singing B-flat that vibrates through the rock-faces—channeling the atmospheric current directly into the beast’s thoracic coil. 

Inside their bodies, this electricity drives a slow, constant electrolysis of ambient steam, splitting the flash-condensing plumes of the lower geysers into pure hydrogen and oxygen. They vent the oxygen through glowing, blue-rimmed nozzles along their flanks, using the hydrogen to inflate thick, armored ballast-bladders of vulcanized hide. When the static season ends, these spiders unhook their stay-cables with a sound like pistol shots and rise slowly into the hard, violet zenith, floating for months in the thin air, their long, metallic legs dangling down like the ribs of unfinished cathedrals.

***

Deepest of all, beneath the living copper turf and below the frost-line where the rock is hot and soft with mantle-bleed, exist the **Bore-Singers**. 

These are not individual animals in any recognizable sense, but the distributed nodes of the piston-driven siphonophorae that govern the tableland’s hydraulics. A single Bore-Singer is a cylinder of ring-muscles three fathoms across, cased in fluted ivory-chitin and lined with millions of self-lubricating cilia made of raw, extruded nickel. 

They reside in natural volcanic conduits, acting as living check-valves and accumulator tanks. They have no brains, only a distributed ring of neural copper-nodes that communicate across miles through pressure pulses in the fluid they pump. Their thoughts are purely hydraulic: a rise in mantle-pressure ten miles to the west is felt as a headache in the east, answered by a rhythmic, mile-wide contraction that shunts twenty thousand gallons of superheated brine into the surface geysers.

To the things that crawl on the iron sheets above, the Bore-Singers are simply the pulse of the world. Their steady, mechanical thumping—*thung-clack, thung-clack*—travels up through the basalt pillars, through the copper moss, and into the iron hooves of the grazers. It is the metronome to which all life on the plateau is indexed. A browser does not step until the pressure-wave lifts its foot-cowl; a Billet-Biter does not turn its winch until the rock relaxes on the back-stroke of the piston.

***

And among the high crags, where the air is so cold that grease turns to yellow stone and the sun is an eye of white magnesium, there are the **Litho-Cenobites**. 

They are the oldest things on the plateau. Nearly indistinguishable from the iron-banded shale boulders they rest upon, they are sessile molluscoids that have abandoned all motion. Their shells are thick, pyramidal heaps of slag-crust, weathered only by the scouring of high-altitude ice. 

They live solely on the light. Their shells are studded with thousands of crude, naturally grown biconvex quartz lenses that focus the blinding, unfiltered solar radiation into internal focal-chambers lined with photosynthetic lead-salts. Inside these white-hot chambers, a slow, silent transmutation occurs: sunlight is converted directly into heavy, unstable organic isotopes that trickle through their petrified vascular systems like liquid gold.

They make no sound. They do not vent steam. They do not twitch. Only once every few centuries, when an alignment of shadows or a shift in the tableland's tilt cuts off their beam of violet light, will a Cenobite's shell yawn open with a dry, screaming groan of un-greased iron hinges, exposing its white-hot, crystalline heart to the sky for a single second, before snapping shut to begin another epoch of cold, blind combustion.
