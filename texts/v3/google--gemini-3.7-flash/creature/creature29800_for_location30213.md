At the lowest tier of the basin, life begins as a calcified, mineral-hungry crust. 

The concrete flumes and footing skirts are lined with a dense, slick carpet of cyanobacteria—not the bright, sun-fed green of pond water, but a deep, bruised shade of aubergine and tar-black. It feeds directly on the lime leaching from the fresh concrete and the film of light turbine oil floating on the surface flow. It is thick enough in places to peel off in wet, rubbery sheets, smelling intensely of iron filings and stagnant river bottom.

Moving across this pelt are the isopods. They are not the gray, sluggish woodlice of the surface world; in the perpetual 40-degree bath, their metabolism runs hot and frantic. Their shells are bleached to the color of scalded bone, semi-translucent around the margins so that the dark track of their digestive systems is visible through the chitin. Thousands of them move in coordinated, rustling waves along the waterline, scouring the concrete for mold spores and grease-fall. When a high-pressure relief valve vents overhead, sending a concussive shockwave through the water, the isopods do not scatter; they flatten themselves against the textured aggregate of the concrete, anchoring their hooked legs into the pores of the stone until the vibration passes.

In the wide sedimentation pans—where the superheated runoff pools into dark, sluggish lagoons before draining into the deep river pumps—the fish have adapted to a sunless, accelerated existence. Originally swept in through intake grates from the river miles away, these are mostly descendants of benthic carp and bullhead, though stripped of their natural pigments. 

They are pinkish-white, soft-bodied, and entirely blind. Their eyes have clouded into smooth, milky spheres, made useless by the permanent amber gloom. In their place, their barbels have grown long, thin, and intensely sensitive, trailing through the warm sludge like pale whiskers. They do not hunt by sight or even by scent; instead, they navigate by the turbine hum. The continuous 60-cycle vibration that thrums through the basin’s bedrock acts as their acoustic horizon. They orient themselves against the resonance of the steel baffles, feeding on the endless snowfall of bacterial flakes, drowned moths, and shed isopod carapaces drifting down through the water column. Because the water never chills, they spawn continuously, their fry darting in ghostly, shimmering schools through the shallows among the submerged conduit supports.

Higher up, where the air is thick with steam and the metal stays dry, the vault belongs to the moths and their hunters.

The thermal moths are fragile, chalk-white creatures with broad, powdery wings. They do not know a caterpillar stage that feeds on leaves; their larvae live inside the damp, warm fiberglass lagging that insulates the high-pressure steam conduits, chewing through fungal filaments and paper binding. The adults emerge into the warm draft, their wings heavy with condensation. They gather in quivering, vibrating halos around the amber glass of the sodium vapor lanterns, their fluttering wings kicking up a faint dusting of dry scales that drifts down like soot through the illuminated fog.

Waiting for them on the underside of the galvanized catwalks are the geckos. Flat-bodied, broad-headed, and pale as lard, they are the undisputed apex predators of the upper volume. Their skin is so thin and unpigmented that the silhouette of their ribs and the dark shapes of digested moths are visible through their bellies. Their toe pads have grown exceptionally broad and tacky, adapted to grip wet zinc, sweating iron, and grease-filmed paint without slipping. 

They do not make the clicking territorial calls of their outdoor ancestors; in the overwhelming roar of the cascading overflow, sound is useless. Instead, they communicate through physical vibrations, pressing their throats flat against the steel beams to drum out rhythmic pulses that travel through the trusses. They are completely indifferent to the industrial noise, resting for hours on the warmest iron joints where the steam pipes cross, their golden, slit-pupiled eyes tracking the slow drift of the vapor clouds until a moth flutters too close.

Between these visible layers, in the narrow gaps where the conduit hangers anchor into the bedrock ceiling, live smaller, quieter opportunists. Long-legged, blind sheet-web spiders spin webs so laden with clinging dew drops that the silk sags like wet lace between the bolts. They feed on the midges that hatch out of the sludge pans below, pulled upward on the rising thermal currents.

There are no seasons here, no winter die-offs, and no nocturnal lulls. Because the heat never abates and the food supply—mechanized, organic, and constant—never fails, life in the cooling vault operates in a permanent state of fast-forward. Generations turn over in weeks. Every creature is soft, pale, moisture-slicked, and fiercely specialized, woven into the eternal, mechanical heartbeat of the turbines overhead.
