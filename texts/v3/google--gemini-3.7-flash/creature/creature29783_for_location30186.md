To understand what lives in the Sump, one must begin not with individual beasts, but with the crust—the living rind that coats the ceiling and slowly consumes the brickwork.

The grease-beetles are the foundation of the vault’s economy, but they are rarely seen as solitary things. They live in slow, shifting pavements that follow the seams of lime-mortar. Each beetle is as broad as a man’s open palm, flattened like a flagstone, its shell a semi-translucent, tallowy amber that lets the shadow of its dark gut show through. They have no eyes; in their place are two fringed, fan-like maxillary palps that constantly stroke the wet tile, grazing the fur of sulfur-bacteria that drinks the mortar’s minerals. They are heavy, and the suction of their broad, pad-footed legs against the slick clay produces a wet, smacking drag as they creep. When two herds meet at a lime-seam, they do not fight with mandibles; they butt their rounded carapaces together in slow, silent leverage matches, scraping their claws down to the bare orange biscuit of the brick until one loses grip and falls into the dark below. 

Their falls are not always fatal, but they are loud: a hollow, heavy *clack* that triggers an instant frenzy in the floor-water.

Suspended between the herds on the ceiling and the predators below is the world of the plumb-lines. The adult vault-skaters that produce them are rarely seen; they are ghost-thin things, spindly as dry grass, with wings reduced to vestigial, milky stubs. They cling to the driest niches near the highest crown of the vault, never feeding, surviving only a few days to mate and drop their egg-sacs into the mortar crevices. 

It is their larvae that dominate the empty air. 

These larvae are blind, segmented maggots no longer than a little finger, pale as peeled almonds, suspended at the ends of mucus threads that can stretch forty feet without breaking. The larva secretes the thread from an abdominal gland, weighting the bottom with a sticky, droplet-shaped bead of digestive acid. The lines do not hang plumb in the still air; they drift and twist on the faint convective warmth rising from the water, tangling into glistening, gossamer webs that catch the mist. When a grease-beetle falls, or when a swarm of minute, blind gnats rises from the sluice-grates, they become fouled in these dangling lines. The larva feels the tension change through the silk rooted in its anchoring pad, grips the wet brick with its hook-like head-claspers, and begins to winch the thread upward, swallowing the silk back into its gut as it houls its catch into the ceiling gloom.

In the shallow water beneath this vertical forest of mucus, the mud-dogs rule the red brick. 

They are amphibians, but they have drifted far from the newts and salamanders of the upper world. A mature mud-dog is two to three feet of slick, pinkish-white muscle, its flesh so transparent that the black line of its spinal cord, the dark red pulse of its two-chambered heart, and the coiled pale tape of its gut are clearly visible under the bruises of blue daylight. They have no eyes—only two sensory pits on the snout that can taste the iron and sulfur balance of a single gallon of water. Their heads are broad, flat spades of soft cartilage, perfectly shaped to root between the curved bricks.

The mud-dogs live in a state of perpetually starved agitation. They spend hours lying motionless in the shallowest depressions, their bellies pressed into the submerged thumb-marks stamped into the seventeenth-century tiles, their ribbed, scarlet gills fanning the water like fronds of cedar. But the moment a drop of insect fat touches the water, or the splash of a falling beetle breaks the rhythm of the dome’s seepage, they detonate. They do not swim with their small, clawless limbs, which are kept tucked against their sides, but by violent, scythe-like strokes of their deep paddle tails. They strike the surface with an open, toothless maw lined with hard, cartilaginous serrations that can shear through a beetle’s greasy shell with a sharp, wet snap.

When the mud-dogs breed, they rely on the history of the room itself. The females seek out the deepest, clearest thumb-impressions left by the long-dead brickmakers—indentations where the water is quietest and least disturbed by the floor-currents. Into each thumb-print, she presses a single, jelly-wrapped egg, anchoring it with a smear of cloudy albumen. The male follows, his pale tail threshing the shallows, coating the tiles in a milky milt that smells of wet copper.

Beneath the mud-dogs, scavenging what they shred, are the ghost-crayfish. These are tiny, porcelain-white decapod crustaceans, no larger than a hazelnut, that have abandoned swimming entirely. They crawl in their thousands over the submerged brick courses, their long antennae sweeping the mortar joints. They are the quarrymen of the Sump: their calcified pincers are adapted specifically to pinch off tiny flakes of the river-shell mortar, which they grind in their gizzards to extract the lime and sulfur-slimes. Because they eat the very cement holding the floor together, they leave behind them thin, wandering trenches of clean brick, mapping their generations across the saucer of the floor in faint, geometric mazes.

Then, there is the life of the siphon.

When the great, hidden counterweight behind the southern wall tips and the hydraulic surge crashes into the hall, the entire chamber shifts into another mode. The sudden two-inch flood of cold, iron-heavy water is a dinner gong. 

Out from the four granite sluice-mouths come the water-striders—not the delicate insects of sunlit ponds, but pale, blind monsters the size of sparrows, with six long, hair-fringed stilt-legs that keep their bodies suspended an inch above the water-skin. The surge carries them across the chamber in a skittering cloud. They do not hunt the living mud-dogs; they skate across the violent surface of the wave to snatch the drowning beetles that have been washed from the lower tiers of the wall, impaling them with hollow, needle-like proboscises before the receding water can drag them toward the central drain.

And at the center of it all, in the deep, saucer-shaped depression where the floor-bricks dip into a drowned, silted throat, lives that which does not move.

In the lowest point of the Sump, where the discarded carapaces of three centuries have accumulated into a thick, gray sludge of rotten chitin and river-lime, lies the Sump-lung. It is not an animal in the common sense, but an ancient, sprawling colonial sponge, dull violet and spongy as boiled leather, that has grown to line the central exit-well. It does not chase; it breathes. As the flood-water rushes down into the drain every eight minutes, the Sump-lung expands, its thousands of pale oscula sucking in the churned muck, the beetle-husks, the shed skins of the mud-dogs, and the drifting gnat-corpses. When the siphon empties and the floor drains dry down to the slick brick, the colonial mass slowly contracts with an audible, sucking sigh, weeping the cleaned water down into whatever dark conduits lie deeper still, digesting the room’s waste in the silence between the rushes.

Here, nothing is wasted. The sulfur from the masonry feeds the scum; the scum feeds the beetles; the beetles feed the hangings; the hangings drop meat to the mud-dogs; the mud-dogs drop bone to the shell-eaters; and the water sweeps the dust of all of them into the sponge’s purple throat, which bleeds minerals back into the rising mist to coat the ceiling once again. It is a closed, mechanical eternity of damp clay and pale flesh, ticking like a clock under the blind dome.
