### The Sound-Bred and the Grain-Walkers

To live upon the heartwood is to exist within an instrument perpetually played. Everything that walks, crawls, or roots upon this continental loom has been shaped by two physical absolutes: the lack of soil, and the vibration that never ceases.

The **Drum-Beetles** (*Ostra-tympanon*) are not merely numerous; they are the clock and weather of the plateau. Their carapaces are not rounded like lowland scarabs, but flattened, shield-like hexagonal plates of bleached, bone-white keratin, reinforced by ridges that mimic the grain of the wood they grip. Their underbellies possess twin calcified hammers—organs of dense, crystal-fused chitin—that strike the hollow burls at precise mathematical intervals. 

They do not drum solely for mates; they drum to feed. 

The vibration loosens the dry, ancient lignin dust deep within the microscopic tracheids of the wood, causing a fine, pale flour of fossilized cellulose to spout from micro-fractures in the bark like boiling steam. The beetles’ feathery mouthparts sweep this airborne flour from the air. Where millions gather, the sound is a physical wall—a low, oscillating roar that will curdle blood in soft tissue and liquefy mammalian fat if one stands unprotected upon the burl. 

Living in the interstitial spaces between the beetles are the **Siphon-Flies**. With wings composed of glass-clear, rigid mica-like membranes, they do not flap so much as buzz like sheared wire in a hurricane. They possess no biting mouthparts, only hollow, needle-tipped proboscises eight inches long, formed of flexible amber-horn. They are the scavengers of the dusk-sweat. When the wood groans and expels its resin at twilight, the siphon-flies plunge by the hundreds into the boiling, copper-scented basins, drinking until their translucent abdomens swell into heavy, glowing droplets of ruby and gold. They are so light, and the air is so thin, that when they die, they do not fall; they drift upward on the heat currents rising from the vascular arches, carried miles into the cobalt sky like glowing embers.

### The Upper Canopy: The Suspended Hunters

Miles above the marrow-floor, clinging to the underside of the titanic arch-strata, exists an inverted ecology that never touches the ground.

Here live the **Loom-Striders**—predatory creatures of terrible economy. They are arachnoid in gait, but mammalian in blood, having bodies no larger than a hound’s ribcage, suspended between four whip-thin legs of fibrous, articulated cartilage that can stretch eighty feet across. Their feet end in dual-opposed grappling talons that bite into the ceiling-grain with pneumatic force. 

The Loom-Strider does not spin silk. Instead, it harvests the descending aerial tendrils that drop from the moist underbelly of the city-sized arches. It knots these green whips into vast, drifting cat’s-cradles that hang down into the open air like colossal vertical harp-strings. When swarms of siphon-flies or wandering young grazers strike these suspended nets, the Loom-Strider does not drop; it simply pulls the cords taut with its legs, crushing its prey against the high wood ceiling by leverage alone, then descending down its own leg-lines to drink the hot, tannin-rich blood before it can dry in the thin atmosphere.

Below them, skimming the thermal walls of the arches, are the **Cambium Gliders**. Blind, paper-thin reptiles whose ribs have extended into fixed, sail-like kites of dry skin, they never land. They ride the vertical updrafts generated by the heat of the breathing wood, tilting their long, rudder-like tail-spines to dive through the clouds of siphon-flies, catching them in gaping, bristle-lined mouths that snap shut with the sound of breaking twigs.

### The Colossi: The Stilt-Grazers (*Xylophages*)

The **Stilt-Grazers** are the undisputed masters of the open floor. Standing nearly seventy feet tall, they are marvels of biological distillation. 

Their four limbs are not jointed like mammalian legs; they are solid, tapered columns of solid osteo-dentine—dense, un-marrowed bone with the compressive strength of granite. At the base of each stilt is a broad, shock-absorbing pad composed of living, self-healing pitch, which cushions the bone against the seismic rattle of the floor.

Because their food is virtually devoid of nitrogen and water, their digestion is an internal furnace. Their bellies are massive, spherical fermentation vats lined with grinding gizzard-stones made of petrified burls they have swallowed whole. The sound of a stilt-grazer resting is not the sound of breathing, but a deep, churning *creak-crunch* that can be felt through the floor a mile away.

Their heads lack eyes, nostrils, or ears in the conventional sense. The entire frontal skull is a wedge-shaped rasp of self-sharpening, iron-hard horn. They move with glacial slowness, sweeping their skulls in broad, hypnotic semicircles across the fossilized grain, peeling away ribbons of petrified bark forty yards long to reach the pale, weeping cambium beneath. 

They are followed perpetually by clouds of scarlet moss spores and scavenging mites the size of shields. A grazer lives for centuries, its bone stilts slowly thickening until they resemble the very buttresses they feed upon. When one finally starves—its rasp worn flat against the stone-hard heartwood—it dies standing. It locks its stilt-joints in place, stiffening into a monument. Within three suns, the siphon-flies have emptied its soft tissues, the red moss has bound its ribs into a dry, blood-colored cage, and its hollow body cavity becomes a dry, wind-whistling cistern utilized by the few humans who know the paths.

### The Vascular Depths: The Pulse-Eels

Deep inside the colossal, city-state trunks—within the pressurized sap-veins where no light ever penetrates—lives a stranger fauna.

The sap does not run like water; it is a pressurized, boiling emulsion of sugar, volatile turpentine, and dissolved metals, moving through pipelines sixty feet in diameter under dozens of atmospheres of hydraulic force. Within this churning, dark river live the **Pulse-Eels**.

Lacking lungs, eyes, or skin, their bodies are armored in overlapping rings of dense, flexible xylem-plate that absorb nutrients directly through osmotic pressure. They have no fins; they ride the surge of the dawn-pulse and the dusk-ebb, moving dozens of miles through the internal plumbing of the shelf in a matter of minutes. 

Only at twilight, when the bark fissures weep, do the pulse-eels briefly touch the outside world. They thrust their blunt, valve-like heads out through the oozing cracks into the shallow drinking basins to release clouds of microscopic larvae into the cooling resin. To the humans waiting beside the pools with bowls, a glimpse of the pulse-eel’s glistening, amber-scaled head breaking the surface of the boiling sap is a holy omen—and a sign to draw water instantly before the channel snaps shut with the sound of a guillotine.

### The Knothole Folk: The Suspended Tribes

The humans who inhabit this sky-shelf do not consider themselves terrestrial beings; they call themselves the *Un-Grounded*, or simply *Those Who Hang*.

#### Physiology and Senses
Generations in the high thin air, surrounded by the mechanical howl of the plateaus, have altered them. 
*   **The Sight:** Their eyes have narrow, horizontal pupils and a second, milky nictitating membrane that shields the retina from the kiln-glaze sky and the constant, blinding amber-glare off the polished wood.
*   **The Stride:** They walk with an odd, crouching, toe-sprung gait, never letting their heels strike the heartwood firmly to avoid conducting the drum-beetles' tooth-rattling frequency directly into their skeletal systems.
*   **The Voice:** They rarely speak with their vocal cords; normal speech is drowned within twenty paces by the drum-beetles and the siphon-flies. Instead, they communicate via **Xylophony** and **Skin-Sign**. They wear rings of polished beetle-horn on their knuckles, tapping complex, rhythmic codes into the grain of the suspended pods or onto long, dry sounding-rods they carry. Across distances, they signal with broad, semaphore-like movements of their arms, using cloaks dyed with the bright, warning scarlet of the climbing moss.

#### The Architecture of the Air
The Knothole Folk never sleep upon the floor. To sleep upon the heartwood is to wake with one's joints inflamed, brain concussed by the vibration, or to be consumed by the wandering swarms of carcase-mites.

Instead, they construct **Cradle-Camps**. 
Using the descending aerial roots—which they split with beetle-jaw wedges and soften over sap-steam—they weave globular, tear-drop pods thirty to fifty feet in diameter. These pods are suspended fifty yards in the air, hanging from the undersides of the colossal arches like strange, fibrous fruit. 

Inside a pod, the world is remarkably still. The air is filtered through thick linings of dry, beaten lichen-cloth. The floor of the pod does not vibrate; it swings with a slow, hypnotic rhythm in the thermal winds, scented with the bitter, antiseptic steam of the sap-stoves.

#### The Tool-Smiths and the Resin-Arts
Having no metal, the people’s technology is an art of high-pressure organic chemistry:
*   **Chitin-Steel:** The great, serrated mandibles of the burrowing wood-beetles are their swords and adzes. These mandibles are treated by boiling them in the copper-rich dawn-sap, then quenching them in the cold, volatile pine-oil that pools in high-altitude hollows. The resulting material is harder than bronze, holds an edge like obsidian, and will not shatter when struck against the petrified grain.
*   **Pitch-Glass:** By skimming the clearest resin from the high-arch fissures and baking it in sealed lichen-clay crucibles, they produce a dark, amber-colored glass. From this, they fashion shallow drinking bowls, hunting goggles, and the heavy, smooth balance-weights used to stabilize their swinging homes.
*   **The Tendril-Bow:** Their weapons are recurve bows formed from three laminations of stilt-grazer rib-splints bound together with the braided neck-sinews of the siphon-flies. The arrows are light, needle-like splinters of petrified heartwood, fletched with the mica-wings of the flies, tipped with beetle-mandible barbs steeped in the caustic, blistering digestive enzymes of the Loom-Strider.

#### Philosophy and Death
The Knothole Folk view the Shelf not as a landscape, but as a sleeping god whose waking would mean their instant annihilation. They believe the constant drumming of the beetles is the god’s heartbeat, and that if the noise were ever to cease, the shelf would lose its tension and plunge sixty miles into the abyss below.

Their relationship with death is practical and reverent. When a hunter falls or an elder expires, there is no burial—for there is no earth to receive them. 

Instead, the body is laid upon a high, sunbaked burl just before dusk. The kin gather in the suspended pods above, tapping out the rhythmic *Song of the Release* with their sounding-rods. As the dusk-sweat begins, the siphon-flies descend in a glittering, whining cloud, followed by the rapid, spidering tendrils of the scarlet moss. 

Within a single turn of the light, the flesh is gone, the bones are integrated into the living fiber of the climbing vine, and the red cord that results is harvested three days later by the hunter’s children—braided into the suspension lines of the family pod, so that the dead literally hold the living above the singing floor forever.
