To survive the cirque, a body must first give up the habit of looking for dry land.

The people who work the tiers are broad-chested and short-limbed, their torsos widened by enormous lungs that must pull what little life exists out of the thin, freezing sky. They do not walk so much as adhere. Their feet are wide, calloused to the density of walrus hide, with splayed, muscular toes that can lock into the slick gutters of basalt or hook the slippery curvature of mastodon bone without breaking stride. The soles of their feet are pale, almost bloodless, cross-hatched with deep, self-sealing fissures pickled hard by the salt. 

Underneath their wrappings—which are layers of oiled diver-bird skin stitched together with gut-thread, feathers turned inward against their ribs—their own skin has taken on the slate-gray, waterproof quality of deep-sea mammals. They have little body hair; instead, their bodies maintain a dense, uniform layer of dense subcutaneous fat that smells faintly of tallow and cold copper. 

Their eyes are their most striking adaptation: they do not have round pupils. In the unshielded glare of the basin, their irises close down to horizontal, amber-tinted slits, protected by thickened, oily nictitating membranes that blink horizontally across the eye to clear the brine spray. When they look up into the sun, their faces do not squint; they merely look through these translucent inner lids, which give their gaze the strange, unblinking stillness of reptiles or raptors, even while their limbs move with desperate, insect-like speed.

Because speech is impossible against the deafening quiet and the drag of the water, their communication has moved entirely into touch, posture, and percussive rhythm. A master-weaver adjusting a sinew weir does not shout commands; she stamps a heel twice into the marrow-channel of a structural rib, sending a dull, hydraulic vibration through the scaffolding that a child three tiers down feels in his shins and understands instantly. Their fingers are never still. Even when resting in the dripping shelter of an earthen cell, their hands flick and twitch in continuous, micro-gestural dialects—a twitch of the knuckle for *rising surge*, a press of the thumb for *grease-rot*, a sweep of the wrist to indicate the sudden plunge of the divers overhead.

They do not sleep for prolonged periods. The basin does not permit it. To lie still for two hours is to have one’s furs saturated in mineral brine that will freeze into a stiff, suffocating armature the moment the current shifts. Instead, they drop into sudden, five-minute trances while crouched on their haunches in the dry sod-domes, their chins tucked against their breastbones, waking with a violent, whole-body shudder to immediately take up the antler picks and salt-shovels again.

Their children are born into the water. A newborn is plunged into the temperate, mineral-thick slurry of the deepest volcanic spring-heads near the diorite floor before it is ever wrapped in skin. If the child’s skin blisters or weeps, it does not survive the night; if the child’s hide thickens and takes the salt without cracking, it is bound to the back of a diver-scavenger with braided gut and carried through the spray until it can balance on its own splayed feet.

***

Between and beneath the hurried steps of the people, the basin’s smaller, non-human inhabitants maintain their own frantic lifecycles, completely indifferent to the human hives except where the bone-scaffolding creates eddies in the brine.

The ribbon-amphibians—the *churn*—are neither fish nor eel, but something older, preserved in the high, cold chemistry of these cracks. They have no skeleton, only an elastic, cartilage-like notochord that allows them to bend at impossible angles in the violent flushes. They are utterly blind, their heads terminating in a blunt, shovel-shaped snout equipped with thousands of electro-receptive pits that map the stone walls by the faint charges given off by the scraping of metal picks and the friction of the salt. 

These ribbons live in a state of perpetual migration within a four-hundred-pace circle. They are pulled upward from the deep, geothermal fissures beneath the diorite, carried by the explosive surge of supercooled brine, and then spend their brief surface hours desperately fighting against the drop, swimming against the sheets of water that cascade over the residential tiers. They feed on the translucent liverwort and the grease that weeps from the lashed bones, their small, circular mouths lined with rasps of horn that scour the masonry. As they swim, they exude a thick, clear slime that lowers the surface tension of the water around them; without this slime, the brine would freeze into solid crystalline sheets in seconds, crushing the city under thousands of tons of dead weight. The people do not overfish the ribbons for this reason; they harvest only what they can salt and store in the hollows of whale ribs, knowing that if the ribbon-population thins, the living ice will seize the basin and freeze them in place.

Above them, the four-winged diver-birds operate like dropped plumb-lines. They are roughly the size of cormorants, but infinitely denser, with solid, marrowless bones that allow them to plunge through the thick, viscous water without bobbing back to the surface. Their four wings are not identical: the forward pair are broad, stiff, and tipped with clawed knuckles for pulling themselves along the stone under water; the rear pair are small, narrow scythes used for steering through the violent mountain drafts above the cirque. 

These birds do not possess true feathers. What covers them is a coat of dense, interlocking scales that split into fine, oily filaments at the tips, entirely impervious to water and so slick that salt crystals cannot nucleate on their bodies. When they dive from the ink-blue sky into the narrow gutters of the city, they enter the water with a sound like a cracked whip, driving their needle-beaks into the mass of ribbon-amphibians, seizing two or three at once, and using their forward wing-claws to scramble up the vertical masonry walls, emerging back into the air already beating their wings to shed the spray. 

The people leave the birds’ nesting ledges along the upper crags undisturbed, harvesting only the dry, salt-whitened molt that falls from the cliffs. From this shed plumage, they weave their waterproof capes and the strainers through which the finest mineral salts are sifted.

***

Then there are the scrapers—the hard-shelled, spider-legged grazers that coat the shadowed undersides of the terraces like living shingles. 

They are flattened, plate-like arthropods, wide as a man’s chest but scarcely thicker than a thumb, their carapaces formed of layered, iron-heavy calcite that gives off a dull, metallic sheen in the glare. Their ten jointed limbs end in recurved, diamond-hard points that can punch small pits directly into the basalt. They are the basin's cleaners. They scuttle across the rock with a rapid, mechanical vibration, their legs moving so fast they appear as a blur of dark needles, stripping the black iron-lichen and the dead matter left behind by the divers and the humans.

The scrapers are blind to the sun, responding only to the pulse of the brine. When a sudden surge of water sweeps down a terrace, the scrapers do not flee; they flatten themselves against the stone, lock their leg-picks into the basalt, and let the heavy brine wash over their domed shells. The water’s force only pushes them tighter against the rock. 

The people use the carapaces of dead scrapers as roofing tiles, lashing them over the wet sod of their domed cells with sinew. In the colder hours, when the sun dips behind the high iron rim of the cirque and the light becomes a thin, violet blade, the living scrapers gather in dense, clattering clusters around the human chimneys, drawing up the faint, greasy warmth of the bone-fires below, their thousands of legs shivering in unison with a sound like dry seed-pods rattling in a gale.

***

In this basin, nothing ever rots in the usual sense. 

There is no decay of the damp, soft, moldering kind; there is only *wear*, *calcification*, and *dissolution*. The dead—whether bird, scraper, ribbon, or human—are not buried in the earth, for there is no earth to dig, only wet sod and stone. When an elder's limbs finally slow to the point where they can no longer clear their terrace before the surge arrives, they are laid out in the central sluice. The brine does not destroy them; it claims them with terrifying speed. Within three shifts of the sun, the supercooled water has leeched every drop of blood and oil from the flesh, replacing it with vitreous mineral salts until the body becomes a heavy, translucent sculpture of white-and-iron stone, smooth as the river-pebbles and hard as glass.

These petrified remnants are not worshiped; they are practical. The builders take them up with antler mallets and sinew ropes, dragging them into the foundations to serve as cornerstones for the next tier of sod-domes, or wedging them into the gaps between the mastodon ribs to divert the flow of brine away from the nursery galleries.

Everyone here is eventually built into the cliff. The city is made of its own ancestors, worn down by the perpetual friction of the water, smoothed until no individual face remains, functioning only as the dark, durable shelf upon which the living balance, gasping and scurrying, under the blinding, heatless furnace of the sky.
