To understand what lives here, one must first understand that life, in this place, had to divest itself of fluid long before the sap stopped flowing. Anything with wet blood, soft eyes, or humid breath would have shriveled, cracked, and burst into static dust within days. What remains is an ecology formed entirely of the same cured, screaming matter as the world around it: beings of dried sinew, hollow reed, polished pith, and tension.

They are not immediately distinguishable from the architecture. In the total blackness, if one were to run a hand along the suspension ropes, one might feel what seems to be a complex, heavy knot—a cluster of bound rush and whittled peg-work reinforcing a high-stress junction—only to feel that knot slowly, imperceptibly shift its weight under the palm.

These are the Cord-Keepers, or what the builders became when the moisture left them. 

They are tall, impossibly lean figures whose limbs are composed of long, split-bark laminations bound with dried tendrils. They have no faces in any human sense; where a countenance should be, their heads are smooth, hollow resonant bulbs of cured burl-wood, perforated by hundreds of hairline slits like the soundboards of lutes. They possess no eyes, for there has never been light to justify them, but their entire bodies are acoustic sensors. Their rib cages are open, cage-like frameworks of bent osier that vibrate in sympathy with the city’s bass drone. 

The Keepers do not walk; they lever and sway. Their long, five-jointed fingers end not in nails, but in curved, flint-hardened thorn-spurs that hook precisely into the weave of the bridges. They move across the undersides of the suspended streets, hanging upside down over the floorless void, their joints producing a rhythmic, dry *clack-tick-clack* that is entirely swallowed by the greater roar of the shrinking chamber.

Their metabolism is entirely mechanical and static. They do not eat; they are sustained by the friction and the immense static charge generated by millions of dry fibers grinding against one another in the dark. They draw life from the torque of the city itself. When a cable groans under hundreds of tons of lateral load, a Keeper clinging to that cable drinks the kinetic strain through its fibrous palms, its internal lattice of dry pith humming like a struck bell.

Their sole, eternal purpose is the maintenance of tension. They are living wedges and counterweights. When a massive suspension cable begins to slacken or fray toward a fatal snap, a Keeper will crawl into the joint, wedge its own ossified limbs into the braid, and pull itself taut, remaining locked in that agonizing, load-bearing posture for three hundred years without shifting an inch. Half of the rafters supporting the multi-tiered dwellings are not timber at all, but the petrified, locked bodies of Keepers who have taken the strain into their own marrow.

Yet they are not the only things inhabiting the hollow.

Down in the vast, floorless drop—suspended in the rising thermal drafts of freezing, bone-dry air—are the Drift-Drays. They resemble enormous, flattened discs of woven chaff and paper-thin xylem sheets, spanning thirty or forty paces across. They have no limbs, only broad, ribbed expanses of dried membrane like the seed-wings of ancient maple trees. They do not fly so much as hover upon the constant, deafening sonic pressure that rises from the chamber’s base. 

The acoustics here are so dense, the low-frequency vibrations so physical, that the sound itself provides a buoyant, thrumming cushion. The Drift-Drays tilt and glide on waves of pure resonance, angling their paper-light bodies toward the deep bass hums of the main towers, rising when the cavern groans, and sinking when the sound attenuates. 

Far below them, at the rounded base of the capsule where the dust of ages lies several fathoms deep, lives the lowest order of this dry world: the Chaff-Sifters. These are segmented, blind leviathans of hollow reed, resembling massive, jointed bellows. They plow endlessly through the powdery drift of ground bark and dead spores, their bodies expanding and contracting with a dry, rasping sigh. They ingest the talc-fine dust, extracting the infinitesimal particles of ancient resin and friction-heat, and exhale behind them plumes of static-charged chaff that rise back into the air like dry smoke.

Communication between these beings is purely harmonic. A Keeper wishing to signal another across a mile of lightless void does not call out; it reaches out with a hardened thorn-spur and *plucks* a primary suspension cable. 

The note it strikes is monumental—a clean, violent, metallic twang that sets ten thousand wicker dwellings trembling. Across the chasm, the soundboard-faces of other Keepers resonate in answer, buzzing softly like hornets trapped in a wooden box. By changing their grip, by dampening a rope with an elbow or leaning their weight against a sounding-spar, they modulate the city’s acoustic roar, carrying on conversations that take the form of subtle shifts in the cavern’s ambient drone.

To them, the city is not dark. It is blindingly, brilliantly illuminated by pitch. The snap of a snapped cord is a searing flash of white light; the deep, seismic groaning of the capsule walls is an amber horizon that never sets; and the quiet, dry rattle of their own limbs against the wicker is a soft, comforting luminescence by which they measure out eternity.
