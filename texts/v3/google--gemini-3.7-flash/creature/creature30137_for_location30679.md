They are called the Unmade, or simply the Hands, though they have no name for themselves that relies on sound. 

To look at one of the builders standing still is rare, almost unnatural; to see them is to see them in an arc of strain, shoulders knotting beneath pale, hairless skin that has the grey-white translucent quality of lard or skimmed milk. Because the glare from the selenite seams overhead never wavers, their skin has never developed pigment. It is so thin along the ribs and temples that the blue-black tracery of their veins shows through like ink in ice.

Their eyes are large, lidless, and shielded by a tough, glassy nictitating membrane that keeps the airborne gypsum grit from scouring the cornea. Behind this film, their irises are not dark, but a cloudy, opalescent grey that drinks the cold phosphorus light without squinting. They do not blink; they shed a slow, thick rheum from the corners of their sockets that carries away the dust, drying into chalky tracks down their cheeks.

They possess no vocal cords—or rather, the apparatus has withered into a dry, vestigial pouch behind the trachea. Communication among them does not travel through the deadened air; it travels through the stone. Their feet are broad, completely flat, and devoid of arches, with soles calloused to the thickness of horn. Through these pads, they feel the rhythmic, sub-audible thrum of the entire hollow: the shudder of a megalith shifting half a league away, the scuttling approach of a draft-herd in the sunken conduits, or the sudden, trembling pulse that signals an order to drop ropes and let a wall fall. 

Close-range communication is a frantic, beautiful language of touch and shadow. Their fingers are disproportionately long—nearly twice the length of a surface human’s—with double-jointed knuckles that allow them to rap, flutter, and grip with extraordinary leverage. When two work-gangs meet at the intersection of a terrace, they do not pause; they graze hands in passing, their long fingers fluttering across each other’s forearms and leather harnesses in a flurry of taps that convey structural balance, grain-weight, and the hour of the next demolition.

They do not sleep in beds or rooms, for a room left standing is an invitation for the weeping mineral fog to cement it into an unmovable tomb. Instead, they sleep in standing clusters, leaning against one another in the dry leeward hollows of the thermal vents, their leather harnesses linked by bone toggles to keep them from slumping onto the wet flags. They sleep in brief, feverish fits of twenty or thirty minutes, their eyes wide and glassy under the surgical light, their muscles twitching with the ghost-memory of the sledge and the lever.

When one of them dies, their passing is absorbed instantly into the metabolism of the city. A worker who collapses under the weight of a gypsum slab is not mourned with silence, for silence is already the condition of their world. Their fellows strip the leather harness within minutes. The marrow and fats are rendered down to replenish the slick, grey bone-grease that keeps the stone-rollers spinning smoothly over the flags. Their long thigh-bones and ribs, exceptionally dense from a lifetime of load-bearing, are chipped into wedge-pins and measuring pegs. Nothing is buried. To bury something in this floor would require breaking the shelf, and to let a body rest upon the stone would mean watching it turn, within weeks, into a smooth, calcified mound—a flaw in the geometry of the avenues.

Beneath the frantic pace of the Hands move the *Ounces*—the multi-legged draft beasts that inhabit the sunken gutters of the city. 

They are long-bodied, low-slung creatures, shaped like a cross between an amphipod and a river-smoothed boulder. Their backs are covered in overlapping plates of dark, wet slate-colored keratin that continually sloughs off in thin, mica-like flakes as they scrape against the narrow channel walls. They have twelve pairs of short, column-like limbs that end not in hooves or claws, but in wide, disc-shaped suction pads lined with dense velvet bristles. These pads distribute their immense weight so evenly that a fully laden beast carrying four tons of raw basalt passes over the wet flagstones with nothing more than a faint, rhythmic *shuck-shuck-shuck* of displaced moisture.

The Ounces are totally blind. In place of eyes, their blunt, wedge-shaped heads are crowned with a cluster of pale, fleshy antennae that trail along the stone channels, tasting the film of water for chemical markers left by the work-gangs. They feed on the soft, chalky mineral blooms that crust the lower tiers of the city and the thick cakes of compressed, sulfur-eating mold harvested from the lips of the deep steam vents. They are endlessly patient, massive engines of low-frequency labor, leaning into their hempen traces with a slow, hydraulic inevitability, their breath steaming into the chill air in thick, rhythmic puffs that smell of wet lime and iron.

High above both the builders and their beasts, inhabiting the middle void between the roofs and the weeping vault, live the *Cilia-Flocks*.

These are creatures of pure silence and surface tension. Broad-winged and paper-thin, their spans reach up to nine feet, though their bodies are no thicker than a man’s forearm. Their wings are not feathered, but covered in an ultra-fine, downy velvet—translucent white, like the selenite seams above—that absorbs every whisper of air displacement. They have no beaks; their mouths are wide, soft-rimmed funnels lined with filtering hairs.

The Flocks spend most of their lives riding the massive, invisible columns of cool air that rise where the deep floor-vents meet the icy drip of the ceiling. They spiral upward toward the light, bathing their pale membranes in the glare, then tuck their wings and plunge in long, sweeping glides across the expanse of the valley. 

They descend upon the city only when a terrace is newly dismantled. As the blocks are lifted, exposing the damp, dark undersides that have been sealed from the phosphorus light for days or weeks, a microscopic frost of pale, legless mites and subterranean crust-spores is momentarily bared. The Cilia-Flocks drop like falling sheets of parchment, skimming millimeters above the flagstones, their soft mouths vacuuming the stone clean before the masons have even set the next foundation rollers. 

The relationship between the three is not one of affection or mastery, but of a shared, frantic panic against petrifaction. 

The water falling from the ceiling is not life-giving in the way of surface rain; it is heavy, calcifying water, saturated with liquid gypsum, lime, and soluble flint. If a tool is dropped in a puddle and left for a lunar cycle, it is no longer a tool; it is a nodule of solid stone welded to the floor. If a building is allowed to stand for a season, the mortarless joints fill with weeping calcite, fusing the blocks into a single, monolithic tooth that can no longer be flaked or moved by hand. 

Thus, the entire population lives inside a race against the mineral world. The city does not change because its people are fickle, or because their culture values novelty; the city changes because **it must remain liquid.**

To be still is to be turned to stone. 

And so, under the light that never falters, the pale chains of hands pass the shale blocks without end; the slate-backed beasts drag the rollers until their pads wear smooth; and the moth-winged gliders sweep the dust from the seams, all of them moving in the white, freezing stillness of a world that is forever rebuilding itself to keep from dying into a monument.
