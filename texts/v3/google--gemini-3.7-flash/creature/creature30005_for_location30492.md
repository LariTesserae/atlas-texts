To exist in the rift is to be pared down to sinew, lung, and friction. 

The people who inhabit the scaffolding—the *Cord-Folk*, or simply the *Tensioners*—look less like high-altitude mountaineers and more like biological extensions of the rigging. Because the air is stripped of moisture and thin as a razor, their bodies have abandoned unnecessary bulk. They are long-limbed, dry-skinned, and narrow-shouldered, built to slip sideways through the sixteen-inch gaps between counterweight tracks and furnace flues without snagging their smocks. 

Their skin does not sweat in the traditional sense; the atmosphere would sublimate the moisture instantly, chapping the flesh to the bone. Instead, their pores secrete a thick, waxy sebum that smells faintly of cold lard and kerosene. This natural grease traps what little moisture their organs retain, giving their faces a dull, varnished sheen under the carbon-arc lamps. Their eyes are deeply hooded, shielded by double-layered, amber-tinted nictitating membranes or thick glass goggles strapped with hardened gut-string to keep the quartz dust from scouring their corneas. Their chests, by contrast, are disproportionately deep—barrel-ribbed cages housing massive, hyper-vascularized lungs that work with slow, rhythmic bellows-strokes, expanding sideways rather than forward to avoid throwing off their balance on narrow catwalks.

Because the chasm swallows resonance, they do not waste breath on spoken sentences. A rigger might go three weeks without vocalizing anything louder than a dry, clicking glottal stop. Instead, their language is tactile and percussive. They communicate across platforms through *tap-cant*—striking the steel tension cables with small brass rings worn on their knuckles. A two-stroke rap on an east-wall guy-wire can signal a jammed ore-bucket five hundred feet below; a sustained, tremulous scraping of iron on iron warns an entire tier that a micro-seismic shear is traveling up the diorite face. Up close, between the roar of the coke burners, they speak a fluid, one-handed sign language refined to tiny twitches of the knuckles, readable even in the dim silhouette of a drop-forge flare.

Their feet are rarely shod in stiff leather. They wear split-toed slippers stitched from the dried, vulcanized hides of the cleft’s larger vermin, soles dusted with crushed quartz and resin to bite directly into smooth iron trusses. To watch a gang of them move a two-ton smelting pot across a seventy-foot span of vibrating wire is to watch an act of collective nerve: they do not fight the chasm's sway; they lean into the harmonic frequency of the suspension cables, shifting their collective center of gravity with the synchronized ease of water sloshing in an iron trough.

Living alongside them, integrated into every tier of the hive, is an ecosystem that has abandoned the concepts of earth, soil, and sunlight.

The velvet arachnoids—which the workers call *Greasers* or *Mantle-crabs*—are not feared; they are cultivated. Broad as platters, with flat, multi-jointed legs tipped with horn-like claws, they spend their lives skittering along the underside of the main suspension cables. Their velvet-coated carapaces are natural reservoirs for the heavy animal fats and mineral greases used to lubricate the pulleys. As they groom themselves, they methodically scrape away the abrasive quartz grit that drifts down from the mountain's fractures, preventing the steel cables from chewing themselves apart within their sleeves. A rigger will often share the warmth of his quilted canvas vest with a dormant Greaser, letting the creature nest against his ribs during the dead shifts when the furnaces are banked.

Higher up, where the flues vent their sulfurous, bone-dry exhaust, roost the *Spine-birds*. They are squat, neckless creatures, almost entirely spherical, insulated by concentric layers of dense, hair-like down that does not burn even when exposed to stray furnace embers. Their beaks are heavy, blunted wedges of solid keratin, designed not for seeds or flesh, but for prying open the cold, hairline micro-fissures in the diorite to get at the *Lichen-ticks*—flat, dormant crusts of life that draw sustenance directly from the sulfur fumes and the trace minerals of the crushed quartz. The Spine-birds have no true song; they communicate in rapid, wooden clacks of their bills, a sound that mingles so perfectly with the dry *snick-tick* of the city's clockwork escapements that one can rarely tell machine from animal.

Lower down, in the permanent twilight where the sparks go out, lives a different class of inhabitant: the *Abyss-Gangers*. 

These are the salvage-divers and ballast-minders who live on the underslung cradles beneath the lowest foundries. Down here, thirty paces above the sheer, pitch-black nothingness of the bottomless fracture, the temperature drops further still, hovering at an unchanging, needle-sharp frost. The Abyss-Gangers wear heavy suits of double-quilted felt, stuffed with spine-bird down and lead-weighted at the ankles to prevent the updrafts from inverting them. Their job is the maintenance of the great dangling pendulum weights—massive blocks of rough-cast iron suspended on mile-long cables that swing in the darkness beneath the city, counteracting the mountain's tectonic torque and stabilizing the entire scaffolded lattice against catastrophic resonance.

The Abyss-Gangers are nearly blind in daylight, their pupils permanently dilated to drink in the faintest reflections of falling forge-sparks. They navigate their pitch-black, dangling world entirely by vibration, pressing their bare palms or foreheads against the icy iron cables to "read" the health of the city above. They can feel the failure of a single turnbuckle two hundred feet overhead as a discordant hum in the metal, identifying the precise tier and frame before the catastrophe can cascade.

Death in the rift has its own austere choreography. When an inhabitant dies—whether from lung-grit, an unexpected snap of a winch line, or the slow petrification of old age—there is no burial, for there is no earth to receive them, and no fuel to spare on cremation. The body is stripped of its fat for the machine greases, its bones carved into lightweight needles, turnbuckle pins, and tap-cant rings. What remains is dried until it is entirely light, desiccated by the altitude and the greedy, moistureless draft, then bound into tight, canvas-wrapped bundles and attached to the lowest pendulum weights. They become part of the city’s ballast, adding their small, dry mass to the counterweights that keep the rift from crushing shut.

In their off-shift hours, suspended in tiers of canvas hammocks that overlap like fish scales beneath the underbelly of the stamp-mills, the inhabitants rest with a shallow, bird-like stillness. They do not dream of open horizons or wide, sunlit plains; such concepts represent only exposure, instability, and structural failure. Their comfort is the rigid immutability of diorite, the taut, singing perfection of a cable under maximum load, and the knowledge that between the two crushing walls of the world, every single bolt, creature, and human knuckle is held in an absolute, necessary balance.
