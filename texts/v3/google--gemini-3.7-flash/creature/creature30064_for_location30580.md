The people who inhabit the Sump do not walk with the heel-first gait of plainsmen; they move with low, flat-footed strides, their toes splayed wide to grip the crisp, unrounded edges of the limestone. Their soles are thick, pale, and cross-hatched with small, bloodless cuts that heal into hard white ridges, like scars on raw rawhide. Because the rock is sharp enough to shear through soft leather within three days, nearly everyone goes barefoot or wears sandals carved from scrap cedar planks lashed with greased sinew. 

They are a people stained uniformly by stone-flour. A fine, cool paste of lime and sweat gathers in the creases of their elbows, around their nostrils, and in the deep folds of their eyelids, giving their faces a sculptural, unblinking quality. They rarely wash it off with water—water here is too heavy to haul and too precious to waste on vanity—but scrape their limbs clean at dusk with curved bone blades, collecting the scrapings into tallow-pots to be used as caulking for the timber frames.

Because the rock is constantly descending beneath them, their concept of home is entirely devoid of permanence. A household is not an address, but a set of portable markers: three cedar lintel-pegs, a heavy rolled mat of split cane, and a bronze wedge marked with the family notch. A couple might sleep for a month in a cubic alcove three tiers up, only for that alcove to be designated for the morning split; by nightfall, their bed-niche has been detached from the wall as a fifty-hundredweight block, rolled onto cedar skids, and sent down to the lower basin, while the family has carved a new hollow four feet lower and ten feet deeper into the damp face.

Their children are born with an extraordinary sense of balance and an acute sensitivity to pitch. They can tell, merely by pressing an ear to a dry pine wedge, how many strikes of the beetle remain before the seam lets go. They play games in the wet runnels, chasing the tiny, blind white amphipods that wash out of the stone’s deeper aquifers—creatures no larger than a grain of spelt that die the moment the sun dries their water-film. 

Old age among the quarry-dwellers is marked not by stooped backs, but by a peculiar deafness to human speech paired with an uncanny attunement to the groaning of the rock. The elders—the Seam-Listeners—spend whole shifts sitting motionless on cantilevered platforms above the active faces. They hold long, thin willow wands wedged lightly into hair-line fissures. By resting their fingertips against the trembling wood, they can feel the slow, hydraulic expansion of water trapped miles down, signaling hours in advance when a tier will fracture cleanly or when a blowout of cold mud might burst through a living-room wall.

Domestic life is quiet and tightly contained within the rock’s breath. Because freshly split limestone shatters dangerously if exposed to direct, intense heat, hearths are never built directly against the rock faces. Instead, cooking is done in suspended iron baskets hung from the cedar scaffolding, using low-smoke charcoal made from scrub-roots gathered on the plains above. The food is salty, dry, and preserved: pressed cakes of ground dried peas, dried mutton pounded with tallow, and bitter teas brewed from the pale, leathery lichens that are carefully scraped from the oldest, northernmost lintels. 

Beneath the human stratum, a small, opportunistic ecosystem thrives in the damp fractures. The ash-colored lizards are long-bodied and nearly translucent, their bellies so thin that the dark specks of the insects they swallow are visible through their skin. They have abandoned territorial fighting; instead, they cluster together by the dozens in the warm, greased grooves left behind by the cedar rollers, feeding on the "flour-flies"—minute, wingless midges that lay their eggs exclusively in the moist lime-paste of newly opened seams. 

In the lowest, coldest trenches, where the sunlight reaches for only twenty minutes at noon, there are colonies of a peculiar subterranean snail. Their shells are not spiral, but flat and broad, like iron scales, cemented firmly over the weep-holes where the bedrock sweats. They filter the mineral-heavy water directly as it oozes from the dark, excreting a dense, rubbery slime that the quarriers scrape up with their fingernails to soothe the lime-burns on their shins.

There are no graves in the Great Sump. When a worker dies, the body is not buried—for there is no earth to receive it—nor burned, for timber is needed for cranes and wedges. Instead, the dead are laid in the narrow gap behind a newly set timber brace, sealed behind the next slab of stone that is moved into the city's internal walls. Within weeks, the mineral sweat of the limestone seeps through the joints, soaking the shroud, calcifying the flesh, and slowly turning the ancestor into the very bedrock upon which their grandchildren will drive the next wedge.
