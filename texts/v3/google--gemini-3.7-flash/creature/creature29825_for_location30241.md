At the base of everything are the *scourers*—the broad, white-bodied millipedes that form the living pavement of the rift. They are not true arthropods, though their casing dries to the same brittle hardness; beneath their milky, calcified dorsal plates run twin rows of pneumatic lung-pouches that inflate and collapse with a wet, rhythmic pumping. They have no eyes, only fans of stiff, vibrating antennae that sweep the basalt for the slippery, yellow-white curd of chemotrophic grease. Their legs are tipped not with claws, but with spatulate, flinty spades that do not dull against the obsidian; as they march, three score to a beast, they rasp the rock clean down to the glassy grain. They live for perhaps eighteen days. When they die, their bodies do not soften or rot; their neighbors simply walk over them, shearing off the softer undercarriages and grinding the dry dorsal plates down into a fine, alkaline grit that mixes with the vent steam to form a warm, pale mud.

Wading through this living gravel are the *trench-crawlers*—the titans whose bleached ribs support the human scaffolds above. These creatures are built like low, segmented bridges, ten to fifteen paces in length, arched high in the center to let the swarm of smaller crawlers flow uninterrupted beneath their bellies. Their armor is composed of overlapping tiers of thick, slate-gray scutes, scarred with deep thermal scoring where they have scraped past active steam-vents. They are ponderous filter-feeders of the floor, dragging massive, horn-rimmed scoops along the shale to shovel up hundreds of pounds of molted casings, dying scourers, and sulfurous crust at a time. Within their cavernous gizzards, heavy basalt stones—swallowed intentionally as ballast—churn continuously, pulverizing chitin and shell into a gray slurry. When a trench-crawler pauses to breathe, it lifts its flank-plates, exposing great crimson, feathery gill-clusters that steam violently in the canyon draft, releasing a sour, baking-bread smell that draws clouds of smaller life into its shadow.

Higher up, threading the sun-shafts and tumbling through the rising vapor, are the *sail-skimmers*. Their bodies are nearly weightless, little more than an inch of translucent thorax suspended between four vast, glassy wings stretched over frames of flexible cartilage. They do not flap so much as tilt and plane on the fierce thermal currents boiling up from the floor. Their lower abdomens are swollen, bulbous sacks of clear membrane that act as living cisterns; diving down to the dripping underbellies of rock arches or skimming the surface of condensation pools, they gulp up water until they are heavy, then climb back into the blinding heat of the sun-pillars to let the warmth digest the airborne spores and bacterial dust trapped in their bellies. From below, looking up into the light, thousands of them appear as shimmering, golden motes, constantly colliding, copulating mid-fall, or being snapped out of the air by the whip-tongues of the *glass-runners*—long-legged, mantis-like hunters that cling upside-down to the canyon lip, camouflaged against the soot-streaked rock.

Nothing on the floor is soft. Even the parasites that ride the trench-crawlers—the *borers*—are like short iron screws, drilling into the thick connective tissue between armor plates with three-part rotating jaws. When a borer finishes its cycle, it drops into the mud, hardens entirely into a hollow, thorn-like husk, and waits to be stepped on, driven upward into the soft footpads of passing herds.

Fifty feet above this grinding engine of shell and slime live the scaffold-dwellers.

They are humans who have been pared down by the rift until they match its severity. Their skin is not brown or black, but a pale, mottled gray-ochre, permanently stained by the mineral mud they rub into their pores every morning to seal out the sulfur-sweat of the vents and deaden their scent to the hungry mantis-things climbing the rock. They have almost no body hair—it rots away quickly in the wet greenhouse air—and their chests are barrel-shaped, broad and deep, adapted to draw nourishment from the thick, oxygen-heavy soup of the basin floor.

Their eyes are narrow slits, shielded by heavy, hairless brow-ridges and long, leathery folds of skin that act as natural visors against the vertical fury of the sun-pillars. When they speak, they do not use full vowels; their language is a dry, percussive dialect of palate-clicks, tongue-snaps, and short, guttural grunts that blend completely into the endless clatter of mandibles and snapping slate below. To shout is to invite the interest of things that hunt by vibration.

They live in the balance of sinew-tension. Their scaffolds are masterworks of crude joinery: the long, hollow leg-bones of dead crawlers are driven like pitons into the cooling cracks of the basalt walls, then cross-braced with the curved dorsal plates of the same beasts. Everything is lashed together with the tough, cured gut-strings of the great burrowers—cordage that shrinks and tightens as it cures in the hot steam, binding the bone-frame until it rings like iron when struck.

The scaffold-dwellers do not sleep lying flat. They lash themselves by the waist and thighs to vertical bone-posts, resting upright in webbed cradles of woven insect-gut, their feet resting on smooth rungs of polished shell. In this position, should a midnight geyser erupt beneath them or a sudden structural cord snap under the weight of condensation, they wake already standing, anchored to the cliff face.

Their hunting is a patient, rhythmic angling. A fisher sits on the cantilevered edge of a bone-beam, naked save for a loin-apron of scraped chitin-scales, holding a weighted line of braided sinew. At the end of the line hangs a heavy, barbed sinker made from the serrated jaw of a glass-runner, baited with a fistful of fermenting bacterial grease. He drops it down into the dark, churning rivers of the floor, letting it bounce over the shells of the marching scourers. He does not pull on sight—he cannot see through the steam—but reads the line with the hardened calluses of his fingertips, feeling the distinct, thrumming strike of a heavy predator lunging at the bait.

When the line jerks, two or three hunters haul it up hand-over-hand, their corded back muscles knotting under the gray-smeared skin. The creature comes up thrashing—often an armored, multi-jawed prowler the size of a boar, snapping violently at the empty air. Before its claws can catch the scaffold framework, a hunter strikes it between the dorsal plates with a heavy, unhafted wedge of obsidian, driving the glass blade home with the heel of a stone-weighted club.

The kill is processed immediately, with an efficiency that wastes nothing. The thick, viscous hemolymph is funneled into hollow bone tubes before it can coagulate; it is rich in mineral salts and serves as their water. The white, fibrous muscle along the walking legs is stripped out and eaten raw or quickly blanched in the nearest steaming rock-vent that hisses out of the cliff. The carapace is scraped clean and set aside to be split into tools, armor-shingles, or fish-hooks; the tough internal gut is cleaned with handfuls of dry scree and set to dry on the outriggers, where it turns the color of amber and becomes as strong as wire.

These people have no concept of soil, wood, or stars. The world is a floor that eats itself, a wall that bleeds hot water, and a hole in the sky that burns with a white, blinding eye for six hours before fading into a sweltering, slate-gray twilight. They tell their children that the world above the rift is an empty desert of dry air where creatures without shells simply shrivel and turn to powder, and that the only true life is here, in the hot, crowded dark of the crack, where the stone is still young enough to bleed.
