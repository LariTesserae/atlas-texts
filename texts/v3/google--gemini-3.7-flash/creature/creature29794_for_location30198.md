At first, movement across the deck-plates is indistinguishable from the floor itself settling. 

Because the sound is absolute and the air is heavy with brine and petrol-fog, life here does not announce itself through noise or sudden darting. It possesses the same low, hydraulic momentum as the basin. It is broad, thick-walled, and mostly flat, built to endure the crushing percussion of the pistons and the sudden, sweeping weight of the half-hour flushes.

The most common of them do not have names that could be spoken with a tongue, but they belong to the troughs.

They are the *skids*—wide, oval sheets of dense, grey muscle and horn, roughly the size of hatchway doors, that lie suctioned directly to the pitted non-skid plating. Their dorsal sides are not shell, but a living, mineralized slag: layers of oxidized iron, paraffin, and calcified fungal grit bound together by a cold, tallow-like secretion. To an eye scanning the dark, a skid is simply a slightly higher swell in the iron floor, an irregular patch of rusted plate where water pools and parts.

Underneath, however, where they meet the metal, they are soft and hot. 

The skids live by an unyielding clock: the four-second stroke of the subsurface pistons. Every time the floor shudders with the hydraulic valve-slap, a skid breaks its seal, riding the micro-fracture of the vibration to slide half an inch forward along the film of cold grease. In the three seconds of stillness between strokes, it pulls itself down flat, drawing the greasy, metal-laden runoff under its skirt through thousands of hair-like cilia. It does not possess a mouth so much as an expansive, porous belly that sifts lead, sulfur, and grease-fed mold directly from the slurry. 

When the thirty-minute flush cycle begins—when the counterweights groan and the deck tilts—the skids do not flee. They cannot. Instead, they discharge a thick, foul-smelling resin from their margins that cures instantly in cold water, gluing their edges flush against the diamond-plate. The ten-thousand-gallon deluge of black condensate roaring down the culvert washes over their grooved backs without catching an edge; when the floor slams back down with its concussive boom, the impact shatters the brittle glue, and the skids resume their four-second, half-inch crawl.

Deeper in the shadows, where the accumulator towers meet the deck in wide, curving aprons of poured concrete, the life becomes more upright, though no less heavy.

Here live the *bellows-forms*. They are pale, barrel-shaped organisms anchored deep within the flooded conduit troughs, rising five or six feet into the mist like discarded lengths of rubberized ducting. They have no eyes; the violent monochrome of the electrical arcs above means nothing to them. Instead, their skins are ribbed with thick, fibrous cartilages that ring their bodies like barrel-hoops, each hoop studded with tiny, copper-colored pits sensitive to galvanic current.

The bellows-forms feed on the overhead discharges. When a high-voltage junction snaps in the dripping superstructure above, the sudden ionic shift in the air causes the creatures to contract violently, pulling gallons of mist and chemical steam into their hollow central cavities. Inside their wet, ribbed throats, hanging sheets of grease-fed fungal tissue strip the condensed vapors of nitrogen and unburned hydrocarbons. Seconds later, they exhale: a slow, wheezing sigh of scrubbed, tepid air that smells of wet chalk and scorched copper, the only breath in the valley that does not belong to the machinery.

They are slow-growing, taking centuries to build their thick, rubbery walls out of the sulfur crusts they process. When one dies, its cartilage rings do not rot; they simply oxidize, stiffen, and become another pipe among the millions of pipes, eventually crushed flat when a counterweight or an accumulator wall shifts.

Higher up, clinging to the bellies of the weeping intake trunks in the bruised gloom, are the *weft-hangers*. 

They are stringy, pale, and communal—resembling knotted curtains of wet rope more than distinct animals. A single weft-hanger colony may stretch for forty yards beneath a sweating pipe, suspending itself by thousands of tiny, barbed hooks driven directly into the rusted seams of the metal. They have evolved to take advantage of the continuous condensation. Their long, fibrous tendrils hang downward into the empty air like the threads of a loom, catching the fat, falling drops of grease-water before they can hit the floor.

The weft-hangers are the only things here that produce light of their own, though it is thin and sickly: a faint, greenish phosphorescence that pulses along their hanging threads in slow, rhythmic waves, completely out of phase with the machinery. It is not an attractant—there is nothing to attract. It is an excretory process, a chemical shedding of the excess zinc and phosphor they leach from the sweating galvanized pipes. When the steam vents shriek overhead, the sudden wash of heat causes the weft-hangers to writhe in place, a slow, tangled shivering of miles of pale cordage against the black iron.

And beneath all of this, in the deep, submerged conduits where the slow-stroke pistons churn the black water in perpetual darkness, there is something larger.

It does not come onto the floorplates, for it has no structure capable of supporting its own bulk outside the buoyancy of the flooded channels. It is known only by the way the floor-level intake grates occasionally buckle outward, bent by a tremendous, soft pressure from below, and by the thick, gray grease—richer and heavier than machine oil—that welters up through the seams in the deck after a piston-stroke.

It is a continuous, sprawling biomass that has filled the abandoned drainage channels and dead cooling jackets beneath the floor for miles. It is neither plant nor animal, but an immense, creeping mantle of cellular tallow, gray-white and semi-solid, that cushions the very machinery it inhabits. It lubricates the colossal shafts. Its blind, gelatinous lobes wrap around the hot steam-pipes, insulating them, drinking the radiant heat, and sloughing off dead, calcified crust into the intake grates to keep its own mass from suffocating the flow.

It has no brain, but it has a pulse. It has lived alongside the stroke of the four-second hydraulic valves for so long that its own interior fluids now surge in perfect, sympathetic unison with the engines. If the pistons were to stop, the creature's great, soft vessels would collapse, torn apart by the sudden cessation of the rhythm that drives its life. 

It does not know that there was ever an outside, or that the roof above the hanging pipes was once the open sky. To everything that creeps, clings, and wheezes in the iron valley, the world is precisely as large as the engine, and will last precisely as long as the cycle turns.
