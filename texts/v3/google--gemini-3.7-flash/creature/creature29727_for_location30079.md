Beneath the steady, fifteen-second thump of the rocker arm, the gravel apron does not remain still; it shifts under the traffic of things that have made the machine’s rhythm their own pulse.

The black water-voles are the most numerous of the floor-dwellers, though they bear little resemblance to their lean marshland cousins. Here, fed on a ceaseless diet of dropped tallow rind, softened packing-felt, and the swollen larvae of midges, they have grown blunt-nosed and barrel-ribbed. Their pelts are extraordinarily dense, permanently dressed in a sheen of animal fat and cylinder oil that sheds the cold drizzle in rolling pearls. They make their burrows not in soft mud, but in the crushed basalt directly beneath the massive oak stop-blocks. Where other creatures would be crushed or deafened by the concussion of the beam, the voles huddle into the vibration. The ground-tremor keeps their nesting chambers dry and drives away burrowing centipedes; when the stops strike wood with that heavy *thung-chunk*, a litter of young voles, blind and pink in a bed of shredded hemp-rope, merely twitch their whiskers in time.

They have learned the precise arc of the moving brass. A vole will sit unblinking on a warm bearing pedestal, waiting for the massive rocker to swing skyward, darting in during the seven-second window of clearance to lick the warm, yellow grease from the trunnion before the shadow falls and the iron descends again. They do not hurry; their sense of time is strictly mechanical, divided into quarter-minutes.

Above them, where the white condensing plumes shear against the raw shale walls, lives a peculiar caste of invertebrates. In the hairline cracks left by the blaster’s drills, pale, translucent spiders—scarcely larger than a fingernail—weave triangular webs across the bore-grooves. These webs do not rely on sticky silk to catch flying insects; instead, they act as condensed-moisture traps, catching the heavy steam droplets until they sag. The spiders drink the warm, mineral-heavy dew, which is rich in dissolved limestone and copper salts from the cylinder jackets, and prey upon the clouds of blind midges that rise at twilight from the drainage troughs. These midges never develop eyes; they navigate the vertical cut purely by the air currents pushed out by the piston strokes, drifting in synchronized swarms that rise when the exhaust vents and fall when the cold mountain air is sucked downward into the brick arches.

There are human presences, too, though they seem less like masters of the place than another variety of specialized fauna. 

Two Sump-wardens share the cut, working twelve-hour watches from a small, dry recess chiseled into the north wall—a niche barely deep enough for a zinc-topped table, a coke brazier, and a pair of oilskin coats. They are men with the pale, scoured complexions of those who work in clean steam, their skin perpetually scrubbed by cold mist. Because the stone walls amplify the engine’s displacement into a roar that defeats the human voice, they have abandoned speech entirely while down in the pocket. They communicate with short, sharp snaps of the fingers, or by striking a brass disc on their belts with a small lead rod. 

Their movements around the machinery are slow, rhythmic, and hypnotic. They do not fight the noise; their ears are plugged with sheep’s wool soaked in beeswax, leaving them attuned only to the low, bone-conducted thrum of the foundations. They can tell by the vibration through the soles of their clogs if a copper band on the southern condenser has stretched by a sixteenth of an inch, or if a willow root has worked its way into the cold-water intake.

The wardens wage a quiet, continuous negotiation with the green growth. Armed with curved billhooks and coarse salt, they spend their idle minutes paring back the coltsfoot and river-willows that attempt to throttle the machinery. The plants here grow with an unnatural, glistening speed, their sap thinned by the endless mist and their leaves spreading wider than wagon wheels to drink the unobstructed daylight. A sapling pruned to the stone on Monday will put out four inches of pale, succulent cane by Thursday, its root-tips seeking the warm grease-runnels between the flagstones. The men do not hate the weeds; they heap the cut dock and hart’s-tongue into iron hampers to rot, using the heat of the compost to warm their grease-tins and dry their sodden woolens.

Up in the iron rafters of the gantry cranes, seventy feet above the stone flags, the soot-tail martins have established a hanging city. Their nests are constructed not of ordinary river mud, but of grey shale flour mixed with oil-scum and machine-grease, creating a mortar that dries as hard as stove-iron. The birds fly with a manic, angular speed that contrasts sharply with the slow stroke of the beam engine below. They hunt the plume-lines, diving directly through the roaring columns of steam to snatch midges, emerging on the other side with their dark blue feathers slicked flat and steaming. 

The birds and the engines have reached an agreement of shelter. The heat radiating upward from the condensing cylinders keeps the gantry web ten degrees warmer than the street above, allowing the martins to stay deep into the winter, long after the wild flocks have migrated south. In the frost of January, the Sump becomes an oasis of steam and clamor: the shale walls rimed with ice at the rim, while at the bottom, among the glossy ferns and the hot iron, the martins wheel, the voles fatten on sheep’s tallow, and the two wardens move through the bright mist like priests of a cold, tireless, and wonderfully fertile god.
