### The Wood-Marrow and the Split-Feeders

To survive on beams that lengthen by miles in a matter of days, life must either move faster than the wood or live entirely within the tempo of its destruction. 

Deep inside the translucent cellulose chambers of the fresh struts—where warm, nutrient-thick sap surges under volcanic hydraulic pressure—lives the **Pith-Worm**. These are not soft grubs, but long, segmented tubes of pale cartilage, thick as a man’s torso and forty yards long. They have no eyes; their heads are blunt rings of spinning, self-sharpening teeth made of iron-rich mineral deposit. They swim upward *with* the sap-flow, chewing channels through the core of the timber as it vaults into the freezing air. When an arch overstrains and shatters, these worms are cast out into the cold void by the hundreds, freezing instantly into brittle, curling ribbons of white flesh before they can hit the mist leagues below.

Trailing the growth tips are the **Suture-Lice**. Small, flat, and broad as shields, their shells are formed of overlapping plates of calcified bark. They do not walk; they ratchet. Using four pairs of vice-jawed appendages tipped with diamond-hard mineral spurs, they clamp themselves across the fresh tension-cracks that open along the hulls of the pylons. They feed on the pressurized amber foam that bleeds from these fissures, drinking until their bellies glow with a faint, bioluminescent gold. In doing so, their bodies act as living staples, temporarily holding the outer rind of the wood together against the dry frost until the cold cements the wound with ice.

### The Graze of the Under-Arcs

Where the light cannot directly sear and the ground-fog wets the underside of the mega-struts, life grows slow, cold, and immensely wide.

The most common wanderers of the high scaffolding are the **Stilt-Striders**. They resemble immense, spindly crabs built of white horn and frozen sinew, some spanning thirty paces from leg-tip to leg-tip. Their bodies are small and disc-like, suspended high above the timber on eight needle-thin limbs that flex like archer’s bows. Each foot is an opposable claw that locks into the deep fissures of the bark with a mechanical, spring-loaded snap. 

The Striders graze upside down, hanging suspended over the five-mile drop. They move with an agonized, hydraulic patience, taking a single step every few minutes. Their mouths are delicate, fan-like scrapers that peel the frost-moss and the sweet, gelatinous condensation beads from the wood. Their hearts beat once every several minutes, their blood rich with heavy, sugary alcohols that prevent freezing even in the teeth of the downdrafts. When a beam begins to groan with fatal load, a Strider does not run—it cannot. Instead, it draws its long legs tight against its shell, clamps its spurs into the bark, and flattens itself into a low pyramid, gambling that the fracture will pass to the left or right of its grip.

Hunting them are the **Gale-Leeches**—solitary, boneless sheets of pale, muscular mantle that look like discarded sails. They do not crawl; they flatten themselves against the wood, sliding along the frost-glaze on a bed of secreted acid that melts a micro-thin layer of water beneath them. When they reach a Strider, they do not tear it; they drape themselves entirely over its joints, freezing their own lower tissue to weld the prey to the wood while their acidic underbelly dissolves the shell at leisure.

### The Mid-Air Drift

The vast gulfs of empty air between the pylons are not unpopulated; they are traversed by creatures that never touch the wood until they die.

The **Sieve-Kites** are animals made almost entirely of sailcloth and hollow reed. Their wingspans can reach fifty feet, but they weigh less than a child. Their skeletons are light cages of porous bone filled with warm, volcanic methane gas filtered from the lower mist-layers. They have no flapping musculature; they are pure gliders, hanging motionless on the violent thermal updrafts that roar out of the floor-cracks.

Their lower jaws are broad, dip-net scoops of fine, hair-like baleen. As they soar through the leagues of empty light, they trawl the falling glass-shot: the drops of frozen sap, condensation hail, and airborne pollen that drift down from the ceiling arches. They have large, mirror-surfaced eyes that take in the shadowless glare without burning, tracking the distant, slow-motion arcs of collapsing struts leagues away. 

To the Kites, a structural collapse is not a catastrophe, but a feast. When a mega-pylon snaps, the air is suddenly filled with millions of tons of shredded fiber, pulverized sap-dust, and stunned pith-worms flung loose by the shockwave. The Kites fold their wings into narrow darts, dropping thousands of feet in screaming, silent dives to skim the debris-cloud before it vanishes into the floor-fog.

### The Knuckle-Folk (The Listeners)

Humanity here has become something specialized, sparse, and quiet. They live in tiny clans—never more than forty—tethered to the **Knuckles**, the rare, swollen nodes where several immense structural timbers collide and fuse into a mass of wood as stable as a mountain range.

#### Physiology and Dress
The Listeners are tall, narrow-chested, and long-fingered. Their skin is milk-pale, patterned with pale blue veins that run close to the surface, and their eyes have double-lids to cut the surgical phosphor-glare. They do not wear woven cloth; they wear the greased, thick hides of the deep-vent worms that push up from below. These leathery tunics are lined with the downy, fibrous fluff harvested from the unexploded seed-pods of the high timbers.

Every human wears a **Harness of Tendon** from the day they learn to crawl. It is a harness woven with six independent anchor-lines, each tipped with a heavy bone peg and a hand-hammered bronze wedge. They do not walk along the tops of beams; to expose oneself to the upper curve of a strut is to invite the downdrafts to peel you off into the void. They live entirely beneath, hanging in the shelter of the wood’s own bulk.

#### The Basket-Camps
Their homes are **Weft-Nests**: pod-like cages of springy willow-fiber and worm-gut suspended by complex rigging beneath the knuckles. These nests swing gently in the howling drafts. They have no solid floors; the bottom of a nest is an open lattice through which one looks directly down into miles of empty air. To the Listeners, a solid floor is a dangerous trap that collects ice and catches the wind; a floor must let the storm pass *through* it.

#### Culture and the Art of the "Pitch"
The central figure in any camp is the **Ear-Man** or **Pitch-Tuner**. 

Because the mega-flora grows with volcanic speed, its internal stress is musical. The Listeners do not trust their eyes; the glare is deceitful. Instead, the Pitch-Tuner drives a long, hollow needle of mammoth-bone three feet into the heartwood of a supporting arch, pressing their ear to the bone’s open mouth for hours in total silence.

They can hear the sap rushing like a subterranean river; they can hear the microscopic tearing of cellulose fibers as an arch overextends; they know the dry, sharp *ping* that signals a frost-fracture miles away in the white emptiness. 
* A low, rhythmic *thrumming* means the strut is young and drinking from the vents; it is safe to anchor more nests. 
* A sound like a tightening bowstring—a high, singing whistle that rises in pitch over days—means the cantilever has overshot its structural limit. When the note climbs past a certain harmonic, the clan cuts their tethers, drops their nests into the lower wind-currents on long guide-ropes, and migrates along the cross-braces before the pylon violently shears itself out of the world.

#### Tools and Economy
They have no fire; wood is their ground and air is too dry, so fire is a suicidal obscenity. They eat their food cold, cured in the astringent, alcohol-heavy sap of the high timbers or dried in the freezing draft until it is as hard as slate. 

Their most precious material is not metal, but **Old-Heart**: the petrified, black wood found at the core of struts that have miraculously survived for decades without collapsing. This wood is denser than basalt, does not split in the frost, and takes an edge sharp enough to shave bone. From it, they carve their adzes, their wedge-pegs, and the heavy, flat balance-skis they use when they must cross the open, rime-slick spans between the safety of the knuckles.

#### The Long Drop
The Listeners have no word for "earth" or "ground." The void beneath them is simply *The Fall*. 

When a member of the clan dies, or when someone is thrown loose by a sudden split, there is no mourning for the body. To fall is not considered a descent into a grave, but an entry into the open air—the only truly permanent thing in a world that shatters itself every sunrise. They simply watch the body dwindle: a tiny, dark speck turning slowly against the silver-and-jade glare, falling through leagues of cold light, until the mists miles below swallow it into the deep heat that feeds the roots.
