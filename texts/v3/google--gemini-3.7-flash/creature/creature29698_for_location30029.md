At the ground level, where the cartilage plains meet the razor-cut footings of the monoliths, life begins in the film.

The glass-shelled segmented creatures that slip from the fern brakes are not scavengers of rot, for there is very little to rot yet; they are *litho-vores* of a strange sort, drawn to the sheer, frictionless faces by the mineral sweat that weeps from the composite. Their carapaces are brittle, formed of layered silicate so clear that their slow, lavender digestive pulses are visible beneath the plating. They do not have legs so much as rows of broad, pneumatic suckers that create acoustic seals against the pearlescent walls. As they climb—straight up, indifferent to height, following the beaded lines of water—their undercarriages rasp against the synthetic skin with the sound of a fingernail drawn over fine crystal. They leave behind them dry, silver ribbons of desalted slag that the creeping plum-colored vines immediately seize upon, anchoring their hair-roots into the residue before it can wash away.

Higher up, where the walls warm to the touch of blood and the angled gravity tilts the mist into diagonal sheets, the air belongs to the *veils*.

These are not birds, nor are they insects. They are wide, paper-thin organisms shaped like severed ribbons or broad kites of semi-translucent skin, without skeletons or recognizable faces. They steer through the twenty-degree mass-displacement fields with minimal effort, catching the rising thermal currents generated by the megaliths’ upper terraces. Their bodies are nearly weightless, buoyant from internal gas-pockets filled with the lighter-than-air fractions of the basin’s ozone-rich vapor. They hunt the drift: microscopic spore-capsules discharged by the jointed clubmosses, and tiny, filament-tailed motes that breed in the standing water of the high ledges. When two veils meet in the middle air between the towers, they do not fight; they overlap for a dozen slow seconds, their faint internal amber phosphors strobing in synchronized pulses that mimic the infrastructure’s own seam-bleed, exchanging moisture through surface contact before peeling apart and drifting out toward the dim lowlands.

In the invisible dry cylinders—where the standing hum strips the moisture out of the air and leaves the atmosphere crisp and hot—a wholly separate, frantic ecology has taken hold. Here live the *hum-striders*. Long-limbed, fragile, constructed of what looks like brittle black resin and dry tendon, they cannot survive in the damp gloom of the avenues; if they wander into the mist, their limbs waterlog and snap under their own weight. They spend their entire lives suspended within the resonant columns, clinging to the unseen perimeter of the energy field as if it were a solid cage. They move with jerky, electric speed, vibrating at the same frequency as the standing wave, harvesting the electrostatic static charges that arc between their needle-like antennae. From below, looking up through the mist, their dry corridors appear as tall, clear shafts through the purple fog, within which dozens of tiny, spindle-shaped silhouettes dart and tumble like dust motes caught in a sunbeam.

And then there are the ones who walk the vast, quiet boulevards.

They are few, spaced leagues apart across the cartilage floor, moving with the unhurried, measured cadence of things that know they cannot be pursued. They are tall—nearly twice the height of a man—and impossibly narrow, their silhouettes echoing the slender needle-spires that pierce the stratus overhead. Their skin is smooth, matt-white, and cool, sharing the exact light-swallowing texture of the megaliths’ lower walls, as though they were cast from the same mathematical mold that dropped the city into the rift. 

They have no eyes; across their smooth, unbroken brows runs a single horizontal slit that opens and closes in slow, rhythmic succession, revealing not an eyeball, but a moist, dark organ sensitive to the subtle shifts in local gravity and the acoustic hum in the teeth. They do not wear clothes, nor do they carry tools. Their long, four-jointed arms end in fingers that taper to points as fine as drafting pens.

These beings do not speak. Their communication is an internal, resonant shifting that sets the condensation on their shoulders quivering in concentric rings. They seem to exist as curators, or perhaps mere witnesses, to the ongoing calcification of the equation that made their home. 

One will walk for three days along a single black avenue, never deviating from the exact center line of the cartilage strip, until it reaches a ninety-degree trough where the water has pooled too deeply. There, it will pause. It will kneel—a slow, multi-jointed folding of white limbs—and press the tip of a single, needle-fine finger into the razor-sharp seam where floor meets wall. It stays there for hours, perfectly motionless, while the plum creepers slowly advance toward its feet and the mist collects on its back, feeling the sub-audible respiration of the non-baryonic foundations, checking the math of the foundation against the weight of the water, making sure the world is still holding its shape.

When it rises, it leaves no mark on the yielding black floor. It steps over the creeping, pale-veined timber, glances up with its blind, sensing brow toward the sea-green phosphor pulsing in the heights, and continues on into the warm, heavy gloom, entirely at peace with a city that has only just begun to exist.
