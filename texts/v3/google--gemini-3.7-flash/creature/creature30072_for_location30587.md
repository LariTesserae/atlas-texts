### The Scrapers and the Churn

At the lowest scale of motion, the ecosystem behaves like a living sandpaper. 

There are no soft worms here, no slugs; an exposed membrane would be flayed to powder in moments by the drifting silica needles. The base foragers—the **Flour-Ticks** and **Sieve-Beetles**—are flattened, disk-shaped creatures whose legs do not extend outward but are tucked under broad, overlapping skirts of slate-gray chitin. They do not walk so much as vibrate over the bark, their undercarriages fitted with rotating, rasping plates made of biogenic quartz. They graze the fine, hot frost of lipid crystals that condense on the bark, their bodies vibrating at frequencies that liquefy the waxes beneath them, sucking up the slurry through pinhole stylets before the surrounding heat can flash it into vapor.

Where the great boles split under the torque of their own expansion, exposing the pressurized interior pith, the **Tusked Borers** arrive within seconds of the crack. These are animals the length of a horse, segmented like armored bellows, whose front segments are dominated by a massive, outward-curving pair of shovel-horns forged from cross-grained structural horn and iron-doped lignin. They do not wait for the wood to die—death does not occur here in any static way—they charge into the bleeding fracture while the timber is still groaning, driving their wedge-heads into the rift to hold it open. They feed on the inner, tallowy heartwood, gorging until the inward surge of new growth threatens to trap them. Many are crushed; their shed tusks and calcified shells become permanent knots engulfed by the trunk, forming the hard irregularities that human settlers seek out as anchor points for their scaffolding.

Higher up, in the light of the phosphorescent vascular pods, move the **Grip-Cats**—though they are feline only in their coiled, predatory patience. In truth, they are heavy, six-limbed hexapods whose hide is not fur, but a dense, imbricated coat of stiff, straw-colored needles that interlock with the glass-moss like burrs. They have no eyes; in a world where sound is a continuous, concussive pressure, they hunt by the micro-vibrations of wood grain. Their feet terminate in opposable, inward-curving grapples that can punch three inches deep into resinous heartwood. A Grip-Cat does not stalk; it anchors itself to a high-tension bough, ratchets its tendons until its limbs are under mechanical strain like a drawn ballista, and waits for a frond-flyer to cross the amber glare beneath it. When it strikes, the sound of its talons ripping free of the bark is like a rifle shot. It eats swiftly, cracking open the dry, paper-like carapaces of its prey to lap at the thick, concentrated fats of the thoracic flight muscles before the air bakes the carcass into dry husk.

### The Upper Canopy: Frond-Gliders and the Thermal Skimmers

The flyers of the upper rift do not flap their wings in the manner of birds. The air is so thick with rising thermal columns—heated by the sheer friction of the forest’s growth and the metabolic furnaces of the vascular arteries—that flight is an act of perpetual, soaring suspension.

The **Mantis-Kites** possess wingspans that rival small skiffs, formed of gossamer-thin sheets of translucent silicate mica bound together with structural resin. Their bodies are long, hollow splints of dry marrow-wood, light enough to float on the weakest updraft. They steer using long, trailing rudder-filaments that cast off static sparks as they brush against the canopy. 

These creatures are aerial filter-feeders: their mouths are broad, cavernous scoops lined with combs of fine, dry baleen that sift the storm of starch dust, charred wood-meal, and floating wax-flakes churned up from below. They never land. Their mating is a violent, high-speed entanglement high in the tallow-noon of the vault, their hooked appendages locking together in a spinning descent that separates only yards above the crushing timber tiers. 

Their eggs are dropped directly into the thermal vents: hard, golf-ball-sized spheres of solid amber that tumble down through the branches until they wedge into a fissure, where the intense heat of an expanding node incubates them within days.

Beneath them circle the **Prowl-Wedges**—broad, delta-winged hunters whose underside is armored in slick, non-stick wax plates to prevent them from being glued fast if they misjudge a dive into an open lipid-artery. They hunt the Mantis-Kites from above, dropping with folded wings like falling stones, using their heavily thickened, pick-like forelimbs to shatter the brittle mica wings of their prey mid-air, following the tumbling kite down to feed upon the shattered remains before the swarm on the branches can strip it bare.

### The Chisel-Folk: The Inhabitants of the Living Grain

The people who cling to the surging flanks of the timber have long ceased to look like the soft, water-bearing beings of the upper world. 

They are small, wiry, and extraordinarily dense. Their skin is not moist; it is dry, cross-hatched like cured vellum, and perpetually coated in a gray paste made from powdered silicate moss and boiled tallow—a barrier that prevents their precious internal lipids from sweating out into the desiccating air. They have no body hair; where eyebrows and lashes would be, they have developed thickened, leathery ridges that channel the hot, floury dust away from their eyes. Their eyes themselves are small, protected by thick, amber-tinted nictitating membranes that give their gaze a flat, glassy stillness.

They do not wear woven cloth, which would shred against the razor-moss in hours; they dress in garments made from the tanned, oil-soaked hides of the Grip-Cat and the flexible, overlapping wing-scales of the Mantis-Kite, sewn together with sinew pulled from the deep-wood borers.

#### The Architecture of Perpetual Retreat
To live in the basin is to engage in an unceasing, rhythmic war against the geometry of the forest. 

A human settlement is never a static village; it is an apparatus of suspension, built like a ship's rigging around the slowest-moving megaliths of timber—the ancient, gray-barked "Anchor-Boles" that have begun to ossify into solid, mineralized heartwood. Even so, the wood crawls beneath them. The scaffolds are joined not with rigid nails, but with slip-knots of braided bast-fiber and sliding joints of polished grub-tusk that allow the platforms to expand, flex, and creak without splintering when the branch beneath them swells.

Every family possesses a set of **Hewing-Picks**—heavy, broad-bladed tools faced with flaked black glass or the serrated edges of borer mandibles. The work of the household is relentless: every morning and every evening, the walls of the rock-hard wooden cells must be gouged back. The chips fly in dry, fragrant showers; the timber beneath weeps a thick, aromatic paste that is instantly scraped into clay jars for fuel and food. 

If a dwelling is abandoned for a week, it does not decay; it *heals*. The room narrows, the doorways shrink to slits, the shelves are squeezed into solid grain, and within a month, the hearth, the tools, and the scaffolding are nothing more than fossilized inclusions deep inside twelve feet of pristine, resinous growth.

#### Sustenance and Culture
The Chisel-Folk do not understand thirst in the terrestrial sense. They do not drink; to put pure water into their bodies would cause their lipid-adapted metabolisms to fail. 

Instead, they eat the **Tallow-Marrow**: the dense, buttery sap tapped from the high-pressure pod-nodes, which they boil down in their suspended kilns with ground borer-meat and the starch-rich meal of the pulverized bark. This food is pressed into dense, dry cakes that smell of cedar, hot oil, and scorched flour. It burns with a slow, intensely hot flame if set alight, but in the mouth of a Chisel-dweller, it crumbles into a rich, lubricating paste that fuels their explosive, wiry strength.

Their language has evolved to survive the acoustic violence of the rift. Human vocal cords cannot compete with the deafening roar of snapping branches and the high-frequency screech of billions of stridulating carapaces. As a result, the Chisel-Folk speak through:

*   **Tympanic Clicks:** Using bone picks struck against the hard, hollow chambers of the scaffold-timbers, sending percussive codes vibrating through the wood that can be felt through the soles of the feet miles away.
*   **Whistle-Horns:** Long, curved tubes carved from borer tusks that emit shrieks at precise, shrill frequencies capable of cutting through the botanical roar like a knife through grease.
*   **Sign and Tension:** In close quarters, their communication is almost entirely silent—a rapid, fluid language of finger-taps against the taut structural ropes of their dwellings, reading the tension of the settlement through their palms.

They hold a deep, philosophical horror of stillness. To them, rock—the inert stone of the planetary shell far above—is an unholy, dead thing. Life, holiness, and reality are defined entirely by *the Grain*—the surging, violent, burning forward-motion of the wood that feeds them, tries endlessly to crush them, and guarantees that no day is ever lived on the same ground as the day before.
