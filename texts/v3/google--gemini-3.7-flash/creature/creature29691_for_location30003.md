To survive upon the plates, life has surrendered the concept of the fluid. There is no blood here, no sap, no soft interior that could withstand the vacuum’s boil or the sudden, crushing accelerations of the field-lines. What lives in this altitude has more in common with dry optics, mica sheets, and balanced clockwork than with flesh.

### The Adherents and the Scour-Feeders

Across the river-smooth, waxy basalt of the plate surfaces, where the wind-slip would shear away anything taller than a grain of salt, existence is measured in millimeters of thickness. 

Here dwell the *Foil-Lichen* and the creatures that graze them—the **Laminates**. To the eye, a Laminate resembles nothing more than a broad, iridescent leaf of pressed shale or flexible slate, scarcely three atoms thick at its edges, adhering to the parabolic rock via pure Van der Waals forces. It has no legs or cilia; it moves by generating microscopic thermal differentials along its underbelly, surfing the sun-warmed stone on a wave of its own dry friction. 

A Laminate spends decades following the hard-cut line of the sun’s shadow. As the plates spin and bank, the shadow-lines slide across the jade-like plains at hundreds of leagues an hour; the Laminate does not chase the line at this speed, but rather aligns its brittle nervous lattice to anticipate where the shade will pause. In the freezing dark behind the edge, it feeds: its pale, crystalline mouthparts unfurl like jeweler’s foils, lifting the microscopic silicate flour that drifts through the thin void before the next shear-wind blasts it away.

Where several plates intersect in their parabolic climbs, the turbulence creates brief, dead-air pockets—cavities between plunging walls of stone where the metric fields cancel each other out. In these momentary shelters live the **Needle-Strata**. 

They are colonial organisms composed of thousands of hollow, jointed needles made of sintered zinc and dry marrow. They have no permanent shape. When two plates sweep past each other within spitting distance, a Needle-Strata colony will fire itself across the gap as a single, coordinated burst of spines, anchoring into the microscopic fissures of the opposite slab. They do not bite or consume one another; they feed entirely on the kinetic difference between the masses. By bridging two plates that move in opposite directions, the needles are strained and squeezed, their dry piezoelectric joints generating tiny pulses of cold electrical current that sustain their crystalline metabolism. When the stress grows great enough to shatter them, they uncouple, collapsing back into a quiet, tangled mat until the next close approach.

### The Pelagic Kites and the Pylon Parasites

In the clear, endless blue between the tumbling continents, the ecology belongs to the **Vellum-Wards**—the sailing-ship-sized sheets of layered cartilage mentioned in the ancient air.

Close to them, one sees that their surfaces are not smooth. They are dry, corrugated, and semi-translucent, yellowed like centuries-old parchment kept under glass. Their dorsal sides are ridged with fine, non-conductive ribbing that catches the hard sunlight, turning unmitigated solar radiation directly into mechanical tension along their trailing edges. 

A Vellum-Ward possesses no eyes, for in a world without atmosphere, light does not scatter into an image until it strikes a surface. Instead, they sense the topology of the unseen: long, sensory whiskers of glassy silica stream behind them for hundreds of meters, twitching as they drag through the invisible gradients where one metric-expansion field grinds against another. By feeling the "grain" of the void, they tack against gravity itself, riding the steep cliffs of space where the floor drops away into the blue haze below.

They do not land; to touch the rock at these velocities would turn them to pale powder. They reproduce by fragmentation in the high sky. When a Vellum-Ward reaches a span of fifty fathoms, the lateral stresses of a high-speed bank will split it along a central, dry seam. There is no fluid loss, no trauma. The two halves merely stiffen their outer stays, curl their split edges to catch the shear, and bank away from one another in opposite directions, each already complete, each sailing on its own.

Beneath them, around the isolated twenty-mile ceramic pylons that hang motionless in the gulf, a stranger life gathers. These are the **Annulars**—thick, circular beings shaped like washers or quoits of porous, dull-white ceramic. They do not have wings. Instead, they encircle the pylons without ever touching them, held suspended by magnetic repulsion. 

For centuries, an Annular will slowly slide up and down the length of a single pylon, rising ten miles during the sunlit hours and falling ten miles through the freezing dark. They live by scraping the fine, diamond-hard meteoric dust that accumulates along the spar’s electrostatic skin. Because they never make physical contact with the pylon, their motion is entirely frictionless. A solitary pylon may host fifty such rings, all descending in absolute silence, spinning slowly on their axes like the beads of an abacus left behind by an indifferent god.

### The Inhabitants of the Citadel Hollows

Within the hollow interiors of the floating plates—in the tumbled, inverted halls of the non-Euclidean citadels that have been turning in this sky since before the air was stripped—something slower and more deliberate persists.

These are the **Litho-Celibates** or the **Hinged**. 

It is impossible to say whether they were once people, or if they are the machines that built the citadels, or merely minerals that have spent so long in the presence of metric-fields that they have learned to dream. They are upright forms, nine or ten feet tall, composed of segmented slats of slate-gray composite, loosely bound together by magnetic tension rather than sinew. They have no faces, only a vertical slit that runs the length of their torsos, through which an internal spindle of cold, blue phosphor faintly pulses.

The Hinged do not walk; they shift. When their citadel plate rolls through the upper apogee of its arc, turning the sky upside down, the Hinged adjust their center of mass with a soft, metallic *shudder* that travels through the stone. They gather in the dry, polished courtyards of the ruins, standing in geometric arrays that mirror the constellation of the moving plates above them.

They have no language of sound. When two of the Hinged communicate, they step toward one another until their magnetic fringes overlap. The slate slats of their bodies rattle in a sudden, complex sequence—a tactile code of vibration transferred through the waxy floor. 

They appear to have only one task, which they have pursued for an unrecorded number of millennia: they maintain the balance of the tumble. 

Whenever a drifting fragment of masonry or a chunk of worn basalt threatens to alter the center of gravity of their plate—which might cause it to collide destructively with another rather than execute its eternal, near-miss parabolic pass—a group of the Hinged will slowly drag the stone across the courtyard. They move with an agonizing, geologic patience, taking four hundred days to move a single block fifty paces, calculating the trajectory against the silent clockwork of the sky. Once the stone is positioned, they lock their slate limbs, settle their weight into the rock, and go still again.

Around their feet, the gray lichen-webs slowly grow, filling their joints with dry, metallic threads, until it is hard to tell where the inhabitant ends and the ancient, tumbling plateau begins. They wait for the next shadow to fall, perfectly content in the cold, bright vacuum, turning forever inside the machine that needs nothing more to be whole.
