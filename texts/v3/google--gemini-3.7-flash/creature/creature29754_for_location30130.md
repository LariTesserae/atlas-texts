The life of the Great Sunk is not an ecology of seasons, migrations, or green growth; it is an ecology of the *fall*. Everything that moves below exists because of the sheer, vertical cliff-faces above and the millions of warm, beating hearts that wedge themselves into the cold rock.

### The Swarm in the High Walls

The swiftlets that claim the upper fractures are not delicate birds. Generations in the unchanging, twilight air have bred a dense, short-winged strain, their feathers thick with waterproof, rancid tallow that repels the constant weeping of the basalt. They do not leave the trench; they hunt the thermal columns within the chasm itself, feeding on the midges and micro-moths that rise from the damp floor on plumes of rotting guano.

Their nests are calcified brackets, glued to the sheer rock with a thick, amber-colored saliva that dries as hard as dental enamel. Because space on the vertical planes is fiercely contested, the birds nest shoulder-to-shoulder, layered in shingled bands hundreds of feet deep. The mortality rate is high and continuous. Weakened adults, unhatched eggs, and fledglings nudged from their brackets fall in an endless, unceasing drizzle down the face of the drop. They strike the scree miles below with small, wet thuds, providing the foundation for everything that crawls beneath.

Riding the birds are flat, wingless louse-flies, degenerate parasites the color of raw pork that scuttle sideways across the wet rock faces, dropping down the stone walls like dropped coins whenever a roost is disturbed.

### The Shingle-Crawlers and the Chitin Floor

Where the talus flares out into the floor of the Sunk, the bird-rain is met by the recyclers. 

The pale crickets are first. Translucent, long-limbed as spiders, and soft-bodied, they move in fluid, ghost-like sheets over the wet scree. Their antennae are four times the length of their bodies, sweeping the cold air for the scent of fresh albumen or shattered bird-flesh. When disturbed, they do not leap in arcs—they flow like spilled water, pouring down through the gaps between gravel into the deep, dark interstices of the fractured bedrock.

Beneath the crickets move the *slate-biters*—the armored beetles. They are heavy, slow-moving tanks of black chitin, five to seven inches in length, with broad, spade-like front legs modified for pushing aside crushed shale. They are entirely blind; where their eyes should be, the carapace is fused into smooth, thick ridges. Their mandibles are massive, asymmetrical, and notched like bone-shears. They do not simply scavenge; they grind. A dead martin is reduced to gray pulp, bone meal, and bare quill-stems within forty minutes by a knot of three or four beetles, their heavy shells clattering against one another and the wet stone like tossed river pebbles. 

These beetles carry their own miniature world: their undersides are colonized by dense carpets of microscopic, pale-orange mites that feed on the oily grease that leaches from the beetles’ joints.

### The Breathing Carpets

Where the scree meets the flat shale pavements, the lizard carpets begin. 

These are large, flaccid, broad-headed skinks with skin the exact color and texture of wet basalt—mottled charcoal and dull, oily violet. They possess none of the quickness of sun-basking reptiles. With a body temperature locked permanently at cellar cold, their metabolism is a slow, rhythmic crawl. They do not run; they nudge, pile, and shift.

To conserve what little metabolic warmth they generate, they lie stacked four and five deep in long, undulating drifts along the bases of the house-sized boulders. A single "carpet" may contain thousands of individuals, a slow-heaving mass that stretches twenty yards across the rock. 

They feed with an economical lack of effort. They do not hunt the armored beetles, whose shells are too thick to crack, but rather wait for the waves of soft crickets to wash over them, or snap up the dead swiftlet chicks that tumble directly onto their backs. A skink will open its pale pink mouth, let a falling carcass drop straight into its maw, and spend three days slowly working its throat muscles to swallow, its eyelids remaining shut the entire time.

Underneath these lizard heaps, in the warm, foul micro-climate created by their packed bodies and decaying droppings, grows the Sunk’s only true fungus: a chalk-white, rubbery mold that forms thick ropes between the rocks, binding the gravel together like mortar.

### The Pavement Stalkers

At the top of the invertebrate pyramid are the *pale-whips*—giant, blind, multi-legged myriapods that live deep within the labyrinths of the fractured shelf and emerge only onto the flat pavements. Growing up to three feet in length, with chalk-white, flattened segments and pairs of thickened, pincer-like rear legs, they flow across the slick stone with eerie, silent grace. 

The pale-whip hunts the lizard heaps. It slides its flattened head beneath the edge of a sleeping skink carpet, selects a lizard by touch, drives hollow venom-spurs into its soft underbelly to liquify its insides, and drags it backward into the crevice without ever disturbing the lizards sleeping an inch above.

### The Inhabitants of the Dry Niches

And under the highest overhangs—where the stone stays dry and the floor is fine, powdery dust rather than wet scree—there is still a human shape moving in the violet twilight.

They are not visitors. They are the unbroken line of the hands that pressed the ochre against the basalt.

Shorter than surface people, barrel-chested and bow-legged from a lifetime of navigating loose rock, their skin has the dead-white cast of lard, oiled heavily with swiftlet-grease to turn the dampness. Their eyes are large, wet, and cloudy, reflecting the faint milk-gray sky overhead like pale mirrors; they do not see detail at distance, but their hearing is extraordinarily attuned to the acoustic bounce of the walls.

They are functional mutes to the upper world, for they do not speak with the breath and tongue in the normal way—the roar of the swiftlets and the clatter of the beetles would drown ordinary speech. Instead, they communicate in low, guttural clicks produced in the back of the throat, rhythmic tongue-slaps that carry through the acoustic chamber of the Sunk, and by drumming their palms against the resonant basalt slabs.

Their clothing is composed entirely of swiftlet skins cured in lizard fat, sewn together with beetle-gut using needles filed from bird wing-bones. They do not use fire often—the wood does not exist down here, and the dry liverwort burns too fast—but when they do, they burn dried guano cakes over the ancient, cracked river cobbles their ancestors brought down, using the smoky, sulfurous heat to roast the thick tail-meat of the giant skinks.

They live in the dry alcoves, sleeping curled together like the lizards to share warmth. They do not look up at the rim of the canyon; the upper world is an abstract, empty brightness that holds no food and no shelter. Their reality is the floor, the cold stone, the ceaseless white noise of millions of wings, and the slow, rich, mechanical harvest of the fallen world.
