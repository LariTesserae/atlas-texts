The people who live in the Sump-Ward do not walk with the long, loose strides of surface dwellers. They move with a low, unhurried glide, their feet sliding along the perforated catwalks with toes slightly turned out, maintaining three points of balance almost unconsciously. To step heavily or to rush is to waste water, and here, water is measured in what leaves the body.

They are known simply as the *Tenders*, or more locally, the *Calkers*, and they are the first generation to have been born or entirely broken into the Ward. Because the rock was sheared only decades ago, there are few elders among them; the population is predominantly young, narrow-chested, and spare. The heat and the high air pressure have shaped them: their ribs show plainly beneath taut, hairless skin that stays perpetually slicked in a faint, emulsion-like film of sweat, mineral condensation, and vaporized cylinder oil. 

They wear very little—mostly kilts or aprons made of stiff, boiled hide that shield their groins and thighs from pipe burns and hot oil splashes, paired with coarse hemp wraps bound around their shins to protect against the ragged zinc siding. Their feet are covered in thick, wood-pegged leather slippers without iron nails, so as not to spark against the mudstone or slip on the tallow-slick iron grating. 

Because the ambient noise is an unbroken weave of pneumatic groans, high-pressure whistles, and the muted *clack-clack* of the pyrite conveyors, their spoken language has largely degraded into short, clipped glottal sounds, delivered close to the ear. Over distance, they use a percussion vocabulary: tapping rhythmically on the copper conduits with bone-handled scrapers, sending sharp, metallic vibrations down the length of the trench to report a clogged hopper or a dropped accumulator piston.

Their homes—the suspended tenements swaying from ceiling chains—are marvels of claustrophobic efficiency. 

Inside these corrugated zinc boxes, the air is even hotter than in the vault below, trapped against the stone roof. The Tenders do not sleep on mattresses, which would rot into sodden, moldering lumps within weeks; instead, they sleep suspended in open-weave hemp hammocks hung between the structural corner-ribs of the huts. The corrugated walls are fitted with tilted lead troughs along their bottom seams, catching the steady trickle of condensation that sheets down the metal. This water is collected in porous clay jars, filtered through crushed charcoal and pyrites, and used for sponge-baths and boiling. 

Living in these boxes requires an intimate choreography. When one person shifts their weight in a hammock, the entire structure tilts slightly on its chains, chiming softly against the catwalks. The Tenders know each other by the specific pendulum-swing of their dwellings.

Their diet is built around the few things that can endure the heat. They do not cook with open flame—gas-flares are reserved for boiler ignitions and structural light—but with steam. Small, perforated brass tins packed with dried tubers, salted marrow, and pressed meal cakes are lowered directly into the exhaust streams of the thermal engines, emerging minutes later scalding hot and infused with the bitter, mineral tang of sulfur and steam-pipe scale. 

They also harvest what the rock offers. The pale, rubbery fungus that grows along the machine-tallow spillways is carefully scraped with horn knives. It is too tough to chew fresh, so they cure it in brine-pans tucked near the cooling towers, turning it into a salty, leathery chew that stave off the deep salt-cravings brought on by twelve-hour shifts of unceasing sweat. The copper-colored moss that drinks the periodic steam vents is dried and ground into a dry, abrasive pinch that they pack between lip and gum; it acts as a mild astringent, numbing the throat against the raw sting of the iron dust and sulfurous air.

There are no pets, no pests, no vermin. A dropped crumb of meal cake simply sits upon the iron grates until it dissolves under the drip of a lagging pipe or is tracked into the slurry by a boot. In place of animal life, they watch the non-living systems with the superstitious care one might grant a dangerous herd of cattle. 

They speak to the steam accumulators, giving names to the massive hydraulic pistons that sink and rise in their subterranean pits. A fitter will lay a palm against the lagging of an expansion loop, reading the shudder of the metal like a physician taking a pulse, knowing by the cadence of the vibration whether a pump three tiers down is choking on silt or if a coal feeder has bridged in its chute.

When a Tender dies—usually from steam-scald, oil-pneuomonia, or a slip into the deep sumps where the heavy pyrites collect—there is no soil to bury them in. Instead, their body is stripped of its hide wraps and bone tools, washed with water from the cooling troughs, and carried to the lowest shelf of the ward, where the iron mudstone meets the sump floor. There, in the cold, sulfurous drainage where the rock sweats continuously, they are placed in a narrow channel beneath the heaviest tallow runoff. 

Within days, the rubbery white fungus begins to colonize the skin, weaving a pale, thick, waterproof shroud over the form. Over months, the mineral-rich water and iron-dust leach into the tissue, hardening it, cementing it into the newly cut floor of the vault. In this way, the people become part of the shelf itself—another layer of dark, unyielding stone, buttressing the great weight of the works that continue to rattle and breathe above them.
