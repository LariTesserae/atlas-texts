To survive in a city that is simultaneously a cathedral, a hydraulic turbine, and an unclosing throat, an organism must be anchored, deaf, or hollow. 

Most who live here are all three.

Because the acoustic cavitation within the wood is violent enough to emulsify soft tissue, nearly nothing in the square possesses open ears or liquid-filled globes for eyes. Vision here is an expensive, vulnerable sense, baffled anyway by the warped angles and the light that bends through pressurized cellulose. Instead, the higher fauna perceive the square through *seismotaxis*—they feel the architecture’s stress-map through their bones.

Along the wide, impossible avenues, moving in the lull between sap-flushes, walk the **Stave-Men**. They are neither entirely animal nor grown timber, but tall, triple-jointed quadrupeds whose limbs end in dense, hammer-shaped clubs of solid keratin. They do not walk so much as sound the floor: each step is a deliberate, ringing strike against the lignum-vitae paving discs. By the timbre of the rebound—a dull thud over a clogged vascular duct, a high, singing ping over a dry xylem vault—they read the city's internal tensions for miles around.

Their hides are thick, dry, and split into rectangular, corky plates like the bark of a burned pine, weeping a sticky, yellow mastic that seals their joints against the stinging mist. They have no mouths on their heads; their heads are merely blunt, solid wedges of bone used to batter away calcified growths and stubborn over-buds. They feed through the soles of their feet, which are lined with microscopic, grasping villi that drink the thin film of cambial sweat from the pavements. 

When the Stave-Men reach an intersection where the perspective twists—where a street tilts sixty degrees upward without rising—they do not pause. They simply shift their weight into their low splay-stance, their center of mass drawn down by the city's local, skewed gravity, and hammer their way up the vertical facade as though pacing a flat courtyard. They are the city’s unhurried herd, drifting wherever the vascular wood beneath them is sweetest and most swollen with sugar.

Behind them, in the deep shadow of the five-story colonnades, are the **Balcony-Weavers**. 

If the square was grown to mimic an ancient capital, the Balcony-Weavers are its mocking citizenry. From a distance, resting in the arched niches of the upper tiers, they look like desiccated, long-armed sleepers wrapped in burial linen. But they are colonies, not individuals: masses of pale, fibrous silk-wasps that weave themselves into a single, collective garment shaped vaguely like an elongated human form. 

These mantles cling to the balustrades by thousands of hooked, microscopic tarsal claws. They do not move during the roar. They hang motionless, catching the shed dross of the ceiling in their folds—drifts of dried sap crystals, flake-bark, and the wing-cases of battered manta-locusts. When the sap-flush approaches, the mantles do not flee; they tighten. They draw themselves flat against the calcified heartwood, compressing until they are thin as sheets of parchment, offering no surface to the roaring foam that scours the tiers. Only when the valve clears and the hot mist settles do the mantles loosen, sighing with a collective rustle of wings, to digest the nutrient-silt trapped within their weave.

Higher still, suspended in the yawning void between the east and west districts, lives the life of the **Tension-Cables**.

These mast-thick braids of living vine vibrate with a continuous, low-E hum that makes the teeth of anything nearby crack. The creatures that live here must match that vibration or be shaken to liquid. 

They are the **Hollow-Bones**—creatures five fathoms long, slender as willow whips, with bodies consisting almost entirely of a ribcage formed from light, calcified wood-chitin. They have no lungs; the roar of the city acts as an external pump, forcing the hot, oxygen-dense air directly through the porous lattice of their sides. They do not drop from the cables; they have grown around them. Their claws are fused rings of horn that encircle the vines completely, sliding along the greased plant-ropes like curtain rings.

The Hollow-Bones move in frantic, rhythmic jerks along the underside of the cables. They graze upon the predatory centipedes, stripping the wax-armored hunters from the bark with serrated, plier-like beaks before the centipedes can strike. Because they are permanently encircled around the vines, they cannot leave them; their young are born as soft, corkscrew-shaped grubs that loop themselves around the cable behind their parents, feeding on the parents' shedding scales until their own horn rings fuse closed around the wood.

And in the very floor of the grand square, beneath the interlocking discs of lignum vitae, live the **Maw-Keepers**.

Where the violent, foaming flushes of pale sap drain down the spiraling holes in the avenues, there are valves. The city does not close these valves mechanically; it relies on these vast, sedentary, leather-skinned masses. A Maw-Keeper is little more than a muscular plug, three yards across, shaped like a ribbed acorn. Its flesh is tough as boiled rawhide, seasoned by centuries of chemical scouring from the mineral-heavy sap.

When the hydraulic pressure in the xylem channels peaks and the screaming whistle begins, the Maw-Keeper expands its body, wedging itself tightly into the throat of the drain to build the head of pressure. It holds the boiling, foaming tide back until the square is flooded a foot deep in white, froth-capped sap. It tastes the chemical composition of the entire district—detecting fungal rot five tiers up, calculating the nitrogen balance of the ceiling vaults, measuring the tension in the ironwood dome. 

Only when the city’s pulse demands it does the Maw-Keeper suddenly deflate with a sound like a ship's sail tearing in a storm. The sap drains in a violent, vortexing roar down into the deep, sub-urban taproots, pulling with it all the filth, drowned locusts, and shed bark of the last four hours, leaving the wood steaming, bleached, and bare.

Nothing here sleeps. There is no night, only the slow green dimming when a cloud passes over the cellulose ceiling far above, and the rhythmic, skull-splitting thunder of the cavitation cycles. The square does not need people to be inhabited; its flesh is too rich, its breath too hot, its pressure too magnificent to remain empty for an hour. Every cell, notch, and gutter has been claimed by things that have traded mind, sight, and silence for the single privilege of holding fast to the roaring timber.
