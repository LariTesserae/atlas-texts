They are called the Suture-Folk by no one, for there is no outside voice to name them, but the title fits the nature of their hands. 

Barely two knuckles in height, they have the proportions of tall, slender things compressed by an immense pressure they have long ceased to feel. Their skin is the pale, curdled grey of skimmed milk left in an unglazed dish, entirely hairless, dusted with an indelible flour of powdered limestone that gathers in the deep, clean creases of their joints. Because the cold does not bite them, they do not shiver; because the blinding glare never dims, they do not blink. Their eyes have no whites—only broad, flat discs of cloudy quartz-grey, shielded by double lids of translucent membrane that slide across horizontally, filtering the white-gold sky into a cool, readable grid of contours and tension-lines.

Their hands are tools of horrifying delicacy. The fingers are long, four to a palm, ending in square, flattened nails that they keep honed against slate to the edge of surgical chisels. There is no tremor in their grip. When a worker sets a bone needle through the hollow quill of a feather or splits a river-mica scale into three distinct sheets of cellophane thinness, the hand moves in single, unbroken trajectories. They do not sweat water; their pores exude a thin, viscous lipid that smells of tallow and cold river silt, coating their palms so that they leave no moisture on the dried moss or the tension-lines, preventing the dry air from sapping their inner reserves.

They dress in garments that are less clothing than tailored architecture. Shaved pelt from the underbellies of field-mice, degreased with lye and scraped down to the thickness of parchment, is sewn with spider-dragline into tight, seamless cowls and tunics. These are lacquered with distilled willow-resin, making them waterproof and stiff as horn, holding the body in an upright, energy-saving posture. Around their waists, bandoliers of twisted gut hold miniature burins, scrapers made from the enameled teeth of shrews, and tiny, tallow-dipped bone pegs used to wedge slate into the face of the cliff.

They have no language of breath. To open a mouth in this cleft is to let out precious moisture and invite the lime-dust to crust on the tongue. Their vocal cords are vestigial, withered into dry cords. Instead, their speech is tactile, optical, and seismic:

Across the plazas, two builders will converse through the tension of a shared hair-line held between their teeth, biting in rapid, micro-metered rhythms that translate along the cord as distinct vibrations against the jawbone. For long-distance communication—across the dizzying, four-foot drop from the upper cliff-dwellings to the floor—they use their reflectors. Fastened to their shoulders are small, faceted disks of cleaved quartz mounted on swivels of mouse-bone. With a flick of the thumb, a watcher flashes three silent pulses of the flat sky-light against the wall of a neighboring terrace: *cistern full*, or *line loose*, or *tallow cold*.

Living alongside them are the beasts of their closed world, none of which exist in any wild state:

The **Wool-Ticks** or *Down-Mites* are their herds. These creatures, the size of fat dried peas, are soft-shelled and spherical, covered in a dense, silvery pelt of plumose down so fine it behaves like liquid smoke. They are kept in shallow, scooped recesses in the limestone, lined with down and dry lichen. The mites possess no eyes, only sensitive, heat-seeking palps that cluster around the pale-skinned people whenever they approach. They are living stoves; their slow, fermenting metabolisms burn the bitter willow-paste fed to them and generate a constant, faint, greasy warmth. The people do not slaughter them wastefully; they comb the fleece with bone cards to weave their insulation, and through tiny, silver tubes inserted into dorsal pores, they tap an amber lipid that burns with a small, clear, sootless flame when ignited by a spark struck from flint.

Beneath the bridges, on the terraced ramps that contour the bedrock, crawl the **Carved Isopods**. These are great woodlice, long as a thumb-joint, whose slate-grey domed carapaces have been etched over generations with elaborate geometric cartouches—lineages of ownership, cargo marks, and prayers for structural stability. Their shells are polished with fat until they gleam like wet horn. Fitted with tiny harnesses of braided willow-fiber, they move with a heavy, tireless, many-legged trundle, dragging sledges of knapped flint, mortar-pots of chalk-slurry, and dried wafers of black fungus. Their drivers walk alongside them without whips, guiding them by gently tapping their sensitive antennae with needle-pointed wands of copper.

In the deepest pockets, where the tenements overhang the floor and the sky-glare is baffled into a milky twilight, live the **Line-Keepers**. These are the oldest among the folk. Their limbs have grown stiff and calcified from decades of breathing stone-flour, their joints swollen into knots. They sit motionless in the suspended watch-boxes made of ribbed bird-skulls, their fingers permanently curled around the master cables that hold the metropolis together. They feel the valley. If a frost-crack widens by a hair’s breadth a foot below them, or if a retaining wall of liverwort begins to slip under the weight of condensation, the vibration travels up the braided roots and into their withered palms. Without looking down, the Line-Keeper adjusts a suspended lead drop by a fraction of a millimeter, shifting the counterweight, restoring the balance of fifty dwellings at once.

There is no concept of waste, and no distinction between the dead and the city they inhabit. When a citizen ceases to move—their tallow-sweat dried, their quartz eyes dulled to chalk—there is no burial, for there is no earth to spare. The body is stripped of its resin-cloth. The flesh is rendered down in small limestone crucibles to yield oil for the lamps and lubricant for the suspension-pulleys. The long, slender leg-bones and the curved slats of the ribs are bleached in the blinding sky until they are brittle, then carved into roof-timbers, bridge-struts, and needles. 

A young child in the valley may live in a house whose lintel is made from their great-grandfather’s clavicle, roofed with slate shingles cleaved by their grandmother’s chisel, caulked with moss gathered from the cistern their sister tends. They sleep in tiers, packed side by side with the warm, purring down-mites between them, while overhead, through the open slit of the valley, the flat, gold-white heavens stare down forever into the silent, perfect machine of their existence.
