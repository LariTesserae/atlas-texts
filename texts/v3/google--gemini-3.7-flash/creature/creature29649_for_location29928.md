To live in the hollow is not to occupy it, but to have matched its speed until the difference between body and basin has worn away.

At the smallest measure, beneath the powdery skin of silt, the floor is threaded by an ancient, dry network of lichenous hair—not green, but the color of dried liver and rusted iron. It does not grow by reaching upward; it thickens downward into the clay, grain by grain, binding the red silt into a fragile, sponge-like crust that never rots because there is nothing wet enough to rot it. In the heat of the night, it exhales a faint, sour warmth, an imperceptible distillation of the afternoon’s sun stored in microscopic chambers of resin and salt.

Within the low, twisted shrubs, the inhabitants are sealed inside the wood itself. Inside the knuckles where the pale stems bend, tiny gall-wasps, blind and wingless for generations uncounted, live in chambers no larger than grains of millet. They do not chew through the bark. They exist in a state suspended somewhere between sleep and crystallization, bathed in a thick, clear sap that smells of turpentine and baked bone. Their life cycles are not governed by seasons—for seasons bring no rain here, only variations in the angle of the heat—but by the slow, century-long swelling and shrinking of the plant’s resin canals. When one dies, its shell calcifies into the heartwood, becoming a permanent node in the branch, while another slowly takes form in the drop of pitch beside it.

Beneath the faint hexagons of the ancient dried wash, deeper than the dust has settled, lives something that has surrendered motion entirely. It is a creature shaped by the geometry of the mud cracks—broad, flat as a piece of river slate, its back divided into tessellated plates of keratin and compacted clay that mirror the earth above it. It has no need for eyes; where eyes might have been are two smooth, convex shields of horn, opaque and cool as the bedrock. Its heart beats three or four times between the setting of the sun and its rising, a slow, hydraulic pulse that nudges thick, dark fluid through limbs that have folded themselves so tightly against its underbelly they cannot be pried loose without breaking.

This sleeper does not hunt. It possesses a set of delicate, feathery strainers along the margin of its underside—pallid, fringed gills that taste the dry air trapped in the subterranean fissures. It feeds upon the microscopic fall of dried pollen carried down through the cracks over centuries, and upon the mineral salts that bloom in the dark when the heat draws them out of the red siltstone. To the creature, the passage of a night is not an interval of time, but a single inhalation of iron-scented air; a century is merely the time it takes for a ridge of dust to shift an inch across its dorsal plate.

Near the three flaked river stones, in the pocket of dead space where the sandstone wall meets the flat dirt, there is another presence—slender, pale, and dry-jointed. A whip-scorpion or solifugid, but stripped of all haste, its limbs lengthened until they resemble the stiff spines of the shrubs. Its carapace is matte and pale, coated in a fine, powdery grease that prevents the air from drawing even a molecule of vapor from its tissues. It stands entirely motionless on six of its eight legs, its long, thread-like feelers held aloft, resting lightly upon the surface of the stones.

It does not move because movement here is a waste of eternity. It does not wait for prey, because there are no crickets or beetles to catch; instead, it lives upon the discarded molts of its own kind, preserved indefinitely in the dry still air, and upon the micro-mites that crawl among the resin beads of the bushes. Its feelers are so sensitive that they register the infinitesimal settling of the rock as it cools by fractions of a degree, reading the red stone’s history in the tiny, microscopic twitches of its grain. 

Nothing in the hollow possesses a voice. There are no lungs large enough to force air into a sound, no membranes constructed for clicking or scraping. The only sound of life is the faint, dry ticking that occurs when the shrubs contract as the deepest hours of night approach—a sound like fingernails lightly brushing flour. 

These dwellers have no fear of the dark or the stars above them, for they are the hollow’s own substance, shaped into legs and plates and shells, holding their breath in the warm, iron-colored silence until the world outside finds its own way back to dust.
