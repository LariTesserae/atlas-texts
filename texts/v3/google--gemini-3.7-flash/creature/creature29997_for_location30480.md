The herds do not have names, because there is no language here to hold them, but their presence is as thick and constant as the wet slate of the earth.

They are called by their weight. An adult is eight or nine hundred pounds of dense, water-logged tissue, packed inside a hide that feels like boiled wool soaked in tallow. Underneath the pelt, their fat is not white or yellow, but a dark, greasy gray, saturated with the heavy hydrocarbons they ingest from the engine runoff. They have no necks to speak of; their blunt, wedged skulls simply widen out into high-shouldered torsos. 

Where their eyes should be, there are only twin plates of calloused cartilage, smoothed by decades of rubbing against iron plate and one another’s flanks. They do not need sight. Their entire sensory life is concentrated in their lower jaws and the broad, spongy undersides of their four feet. The lower jaw is split, armed with two spade-like incisors of blue-black enamel that never stop growing, worn down constantly as they plow the gravel down to the bedrock to get at the mineral-slick lichen. 

Inside their thick chests, the lungs are vast, bellows-like bags designed to extract the thin oxygen from the frost-mist without losing internal moisture. When they exhale, they do not blow plumes of white steam; their breath is chemically cold, scrubbed of heat by a complex network of sinus chambers that retains every calorie of warmth before the air leaves the nostril-slits. 

Their social structure is purely thermodynamic. They move in a continuous, grinding circle around the crawling engine’s boiler hulls—a slow-motion whirlpool of living mass. The animals closest to the cast iron are baked to a dull, steaming fever, their pelts drying into brittle crusts; as they overheat, they are pushed outward by the pressure of the frozen, shivering beasts on the perimeter who are desperate for the radiating grease-heat. A single individual might take three weeks to cycle from the cold exterior of the herd, through the crushing press against the hot metal, and back out into the windward frost. 

Beneath them, in the deep ruts carved by the machine’s seven-foot-wide track links, a completely different tempo of life plays out. 

The pressure of the machines is immense—thousands of tons concentrated onto iron shoes—and it creates a temporary, localized thaw in the permafrost, forcing deep-strata water to the surface in narrow, oily canals. Before the water can freeze back into slate-gray ice, the *scuttlers* awaken.

These are things the size of a man’s hand, shaped like flat, segmented shovels, with shells of calcified shale and iron sulfide. They do not hatch; they are perpetually dormant in the crushed stone, frozen into suspended animation for decades or centuries until the heat of a passing tread melts the interstitial brine around them. Within ninety seconds of the iron track lifting from the stone, they are wriggling up through the gravel by the thousands, their translucent, fan-like legs churning the warm mud. 

They have no mouths. Instead, their bellies are lined with microscopic, porous filaments that absorb the dissolved petroleum and sulfur compounds boiling off the track pins. Their life cycle lasts less than four hours—the time it takes for the machine’s thermal shadow to pass and the frost to reclaim the trench. In that window, they gorge, copulate in frenzied, silent clusters, and drill their soft, rubbery egg-sacs three inches down into the wet stone slurry before the cold solidifies the gravel into iron-hard pavement once again.

Above this wet, low churn, the birds occupy the upper decks of the steel leviathans.

They are large, flat-chested things, almost entirely flightless in the dense sense of the word; they do not soar, but drop and glide on short, heavy wings stiffened with thick, grease-proof quills. Their plumage is not soft down, but a dense, overlapping coat of charcoal-colored scales that smell strongly of machine oil and wet soot. 

They spend their entire lives on the iron. They nest in the dead angles of the superstructure: inside the hollows of non-functioning steam whistles, behind the giant, unpainted gusset plates of the chassis, and along the warm tops of the exhaust manifolds. Their eggs are triangular, slate-gray, and cemented to the vibrating steel with a vomit of vulcanized lichen and engine grease so strong it cannot be chiseled off without taking the iron's rust with it.

These birds are the cleaners. While the herds sweep the earth, the birds scour the hulls. With short, parrot-like beaks lined with serrated bone, they chip away at the heavy, petroleum-rich crusts that build up around the lubrication wells. They gorge on the ancient, waxen grease that seeps from the trunnion bearings, processing the heavy lubricants through specialized, multi-chambered crops that convert petroleum into a rich, foul-smelling milk they regurgitate for their young. 

They are nearly blind, their small eyes clouded with the perpetual milky cataract common to high-altitude fauna, but their hearing is exquisitely tuned to the machinery. They can sense the failing of an internal brass bushing three bulkheads deep by the subtle shift in the vibration of the girder they are roosting on; when a valve is about to vent, they do not look for it—they feel the pressure-wave building in the metal beneath their talons and rise in an effortless, silent drift seconds before the scald hits.

In the long wake behind each engine, where the heat finally dissipates into the slate horizon, trail the scavengers. 

They are longer, thinner beasts, built on tall, spindly legs that look too frail for the wind, moving with a high-stepping, mechanical gait that keeps their bellies far above the frozen grit. They follow the crush-paths miles behind the engines. They have no teeth; their heads end in long, horn-sheathed probes which they drive into the refrozen trenches to pry out the scuttlers that failed to dig deep enough before the frost set in.

They are solitary. They do not approach the warm boilers; they cannot bear the press of the herds, and their thin hides would blister against the iron. Instead, they are creatures of the extreme cold, living on the exact boundary where the machine’s influence dies and the plateau’s natural sterility returns. When one of the great herd beasts grows old, loses its footing in the churning mass, and gets dragged under the track links, the machine leaves behind a long, dark smear of tallow and crushed bone pressed flat into the gravel. The high-steppers gather around these ribbons of grease in the twilight, standing shoulder to shoulder in absolute silence, dipping their horn probes into the stone until every trace of organic matter has been extracted from the frost.

Nothing here dies easily, and nothing is wasted. 

The lichen that coats the lower bulkheads acts as a living gasket, sealing hairline fractures in the cast-iron steam chests with its rubbery, lead-heavy growth, keeping the machines pressurized; the machines, in turn, provide the only heat in fifty thousand square miles of howling stillness. 

When one of the great crawlers finally encounters a mechanical failure it cannot bypass—when a drive-shaft thick as an oak snaps under torque, or an ancient fire-tube collapses within a boiler—the silence that follows is not a relief, but a catastrophe. 

The heat does not vanish instantly; it bleeds out over forty or fifty hours. As the iron goes cold, the herds press harder and harder against the dying flanks, crushing the ones in the center until the metal itself is buckled inward by the desperate weight of thousands of tons of living muscle. The birds remain on the manifold pipes long after they have cooled to the temperature of frost, their scale-feathers freezing to the steel, until the wind finally shears them from their perches like dry leaves.

And when the iron is completely cold, the herd turns away as one body, their blunt snouts lifting into the wet, quartz-scented wind, seeking the low, sub-audible thrum of the next nearest engine, twenty miles across the slate flats, crawling slowly through the gray mist toward a horizon that never comes.
