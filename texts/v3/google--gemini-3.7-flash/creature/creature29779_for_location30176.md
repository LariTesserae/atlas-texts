The life here does not struggle against the weight of the water or the mineral dark; it has had long enough to become made of them.

At the lowest tier of the ecology are the **tallow-grazers**, the segmented, finger-shaped creatures that crawl across the valve-wheels. Closer inspection reveals they are not insects or crustaceans, but something closer to armored, chitinous annelids whose segments are reinforced with plates of bonded gypsum and iron-slag. They have no eyes, no antennae, and no distinct heads—merely a tapered, sucker-like anterior ringed with concentric rows of microscopic, diamond-hard mineral teeth. They do not eat the iron directly; they feed upon the greasy, lipid-rich biofilm left behind by decades of dissolving machinery tallow, scraping the ancient whale-fat and paraffin from the deep crevices of threads and keyways. When the brine surge crashes down and tears them from their perches, they curl into tight, ribbed spheres like dropped machine nuts, rolling harmlessly along the stone gutters until their ventral pads find purchase again on a rusted baffle.

Deeper within the flooded hollows of the engines live the **manifold-polyps** and the **siphon-worms**. The worms are anchored within the copper piping and old lubrication lines, using the threaded fittings as burrows. Their bodies are long, milk-white tubes of hydrostatic tissue, semi-transparent enough that one can see the dark, iron-rich slurry churning through their central digestive tracts. They pulse in counterpoint to the drip of the ceiling, pumping water through their bellies not merely to extract oxygen, but to precipitate lime and copper salts out of the brine. Over generations, their discarded sheaths have petrified, forming delicate, branching filigrees of green-tinged malachite-stone that choke the exhaust ports of the rotary engines like pale, calcified coral.

Floating in the stagnant films between the engine carcasses are the **sheen-skimmers**. These are paper-thin, nearly invisible sheets of living tissue, often two or three handspans wide, that rest directly beneath the water's surface tension. They resemble discarded scraps of wet silk or translucent parchment. Their upper membranes are coated in specialized oleophilic cells that feed exclusively on the rainbow skims of oil and fuel-distillates that continuously leach up from buried reservoirs. When a drop falls from the vault overhead and pierces them, they do not tear; they ripple like liquid mercury, gather the disturbed petroleum droplets back into their center with minute ciliary currents, and slowly re-seal their surface.

In the darker alcoves where the gypsum curtains have grown thick enough to form hollow, resonating chambers, there are larger, slower forms.

Tucked into the clearance beneath the four-inch-thick flywheels are the **dull-shells**—massive, sluggish mollusks whose shells are indistinguishable from the river-smoothed iron around them. Over centuries, these creatures have incorporated the ferric fallout of the disintegrating ceiling into their mantles, growing broad, dish-shaped carapaces of pure bog-iron, heavy as anvils and rusted to a deep, velvety umber. They scarcely move an inch in a year. Instead, they sit directly over the floor-seams, their soft, grey, suet-like bellies pressed hard against the vibration of the subterranean strata. They live by absorption, drawing the electrical trickle generated by the galvanic rot between the dissimilar metals—the weeping copper manifolds meeting the cold cast-iron beds—directly into their simple, distributed nervous systems.

And in the deepest part of the central sump, where the iron flap lifts and *clonks* with the shifting drainage of the world above, lives something that has attuned its entire existence to that singular cadence. 

It is rarely seen in full, resting beneath three feet of turbid, milky brine, nestled between the sheared foundation-bolts of a dynamo bed. It is long-bodied and pale as tallow, its skin smooth, thick, and rubbery, like the boiled hide of a sea mammal, but completely devoid of fins, eyes, or limbs. It is essentially a single, enormous bellows of living flesh, sixty feet long, folded upon itself in the deep sediment. Its flanks are lined with broad, horizontal slits fringed with pale, muscular gill-drapes that look like rows of wet chamois leather.

When the subterranean flap lifts and the cool brine surges through the chamber, this creature does not flee. It yawns open. Its blunt, toothless maw—wide enough to swallow a flywheel whole—expands against the current, taking the entire volume of the surge into its cavernous, ribbed interior. For several minutes after the *clonk* has faded, it lies motionless, slowly compressing its massive torso to force the filtered brine out through its gill-drapes, catching every drifting grazer, every ribbon of mineral mold, and every trace of suspended chalk in the fine, velvet sieves of its throat.

As it exhales, it releases a deep, low-frequency rumble that harmonizes with the hum in the teeth of the vault. The siphon-worms extend toward the vibration; the sheen-skimmers drift toward its wake; and the thousands of finger-creatures on the valves crawl toward the fresh plume of iron-silt it leaves behind in the dark, keeping the slow, drowned mechanism of the chamber alive.
