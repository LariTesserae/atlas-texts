To a passing eye, there is nothing living here that does not have roots. The absence of fins, scales, and the soft wriggling of annelids gives the valleys a deceptive vacancy—as though the cordillera were an abandoned garden carved from slate. But life at four hundred fathoms cannot afford the fragility of meat, the vulnerability of gas-filled organs, or the hasty metabolism of things that swim. 

What lives here does not swim. It anchors, wedges, and endures.

Wedged into the lee of the unmortared breakwaters, half-settled in the gray lime drifts, are shapes that at first resemble nothing more than tumbled blocks of basalt or the swollen root-burls of the black siphon-weed. They are wide, flattened, and polygonal, their surfaces plated in thick, overlapping tiles of mineralized cellulose—the same dense, iron-threaded material that coats the kelp stalks. 

They are the tenders of the saddlebacks, though they have no name for themselves that could travel through air. They exist entirely within the medium of hydrostatic weight and percussive wave.

Their bodies possess neither bone nor blood as shallow waters know them. To survive the crushing hydraulic mass, they are built as living hydraulic engines. Their interiors are a labyrinth of micro-tubules and rigid, fluid-filled chambers, equalized precisely to the freezing, mineral-saturated brine of the deep range. They do not breathe oxygen; they draw the cold chemical broth through porous vents along their lower margins, filtering heavy metal salts and sulfuric compounds through dense, felted mats of internal fiber, venting the spent brine in slow, rhythmic pulses that leave faint swirls in the surrounding silt.

They move with geological slowness, matching the hours-long intervals of the underwater weather fronts. When the cold pulse shears across the high cols, they do not flee; they drop low, pressing their broad, keeled backs into the angles of the stone breakwaters. Their limbs are not articulated legs, but stout, hydraulic wedges—broad levers of fibrous gristle that can lever a two-ton granite block or lock themselves into the granite crevices with enough force to resist the tearing current. 

Their sensory world is almost purely vibrational. Having no eyes—where no light has ever penetrated—their entire shell is mapped with fine, pressure-sensitive pits. They "see" the cordillera as a landscape of chords and frictions. They hear the hollow snap of a seed-pod three valleys away as a sharp, crystalline strike; they feel the deep, groaning vibration of the granite ridges as a map of elevation and danger. 

The stone workings across the range are not the relics of a vanished people; they are the ongoing, generational labor of these beings.

Because the currents are fierce enough to strip the young siphon-weeds from their footholds, the dwellers build. They quarry the fracture lines of the summits using the natural leverage of the frost-heaves and their own pneumatic strength, splitting the stone along the grain and nudging the slabs into low, sweeping crescents. These walls are acoustic and hydraulic baffles, designed to trip the violent currents, creating small pockets of dead-water in their lee where the monumental seed-pods can settle, take root, and grow without being torn loose.

The notched river stones and the braided bark ropes found in the silt are their instruments of balance. Because their own bodies are naturally dense and slightly negative in buoyancy, they use these smooth ballast-weights as anchors when working the high, exposed saddles. They grip the twisted cords with fibrous clasps, lowering themselves down the sheer faces of the cols like heavy pendulums to clear debris from the siphon-groves.

In the small stone chambers, sheltered by massive basalt lintels, they congregate during the fiercest shears. Inside, where the violent clatter of the iron-kelp is muffled to a low, resonant drone, they rest together in the lime-rich drifts. They do not speak, but they communicate by cycling pressurized fluid through the clusters of hollow reed-tubes they hold between their limbs. By adjusting the flow of brine through these tuned, rigid pipes, they produce subtle, low-frequency hums that vibrate through the stone floor—sharing information about the silt-load of the current, the opening of distant fault-lines, and the ripening of the great ballooning ballast-bulbs above.

They are as old and as patient as the range itself. Their lifespans are measured not in seasons, but in the slow accretion of mineral layers upon their plated carapaces, until an individual can no longer be distinguished from the granite boulders it has spent centuries arranging. When one finally ceases to cycle the brine, its fibrous joints stiffen and lock, its outer plates become a permanent substrate for the drifting spores of the black weed, and it remains part of the breakwater—a silent buttress holding back the cold, unyielding weight of the sea.
