To live here, a body cannot have mass that catches the water. It cannot have loose fins, long antennae, or buoyant air sacs, for to possess any surface area that offers purchase to the current is to be ripped from the stone and smashed against the iron-riveted sluice gates in the fraction of a second.

The true inhabitants of this vault are creatures of hydraulic geometry, clinging to the boundaries where the water’s ferocious velocity shears down to zero against the face of the masonry.

In the dead-water pockets—the hair-thin margins immediately behind the thick bronze straps and in the microscopic lee of the chisel ridges—there are the *scutula*, or tile-plates. They are no larger than a lentil, flattened until they are scarcely thicker than a sheet of parchment, their carapaces formed of a clear, amber-tinted calcite that matches the glaze of the terracotta pavers precisely. Shaped like aerodynamic teardrops, they have no legs that extend beyond their rims; instead, their undersides are rings of muscular, vacuum-tight suction disks lined with microscopic hooks that bite into the porous grain of the fired clay. 

They do not swim; they skate. When a scutula moves, it does so by breaking the seal of only one margin at a time, sliding a fraction of a millimeter along the herringbone floor, using the immense down-force of the sunlit torrent above to pin itself to the bottom. In the blinding shafts of light that strike the floor, their transparent shells act as tiny prisms, casting pinpricks of refracted violet and gold against the red tile. They graze exclusively on the thin golden film of diatoms, their scraping mouthparts moving with rapid, mechanical rhythm, harvesting the sun-fed crust before the current can strip it away.

Higher up, along the vertical limestone seams where the lime mortar is still soft and washing out, lives another order of life: the thread-spinners. These are minute, blind annelids that rode into the chamber as microscopic cysts carried from the deep thermal springs. They inhabit the narrow chalky gaps between the white ashlar blocks, anchoring their lower bodies deep within the mortar itself. 

From their burrows, they extrude single, glass-clear filaments of a water-cured resin—tensile lines thinner than a spider’s silk, anchored to the opposite lip of the stone joint. In the howling rush of the water, these threads vibrate like tuned lyre strings. The current is so violent that the lines hum at a frequency that matches the deep, subterranean rumble of the sluices. The worms do not cast nets; they simply hold the base of these singing tethers, waiting for the current to smash drifting particles against them—bits of pulverized leaf litter from miles upriver, drowned midges that tumbled down the open light-wells, or flakes of silk-algae sheared from the upper walls. When an impact shudders down the string, the worm reels it in against the tremendous drag, feeding on whatever the kinetic fury of the vault has pulverized.

Behind the heavy oak sluice gates, where the timber swells in its bronze tracks, the friction of the wood against the stone creates narrow, dark seams of absolute shelter. Here, tucked away from the blinding noon radiance, clusters of stalked hydroids have colonized the rough grain of the wood. They are pale, almost ghostly white, their bodies made entirely of flexible, cartilage-like tubing that bends double in the draft of the outflow. Their tentacles are short, stiff spikes that do not sway; they are locked into rigid, conical strainers that siphon the microscopic broth of the thermal water.

Nothing in this chamber rests. There is no sleep in a place where the sound vibrates the marrow and the light never softens. The life here is tuned to the relentless pressure, the bath-heat, and the absolute cleanliness of an artificial river. It is an ecology born of construction—species that require the exact tolerances of squared stone, the chemical leaching of fresh slaked lime, and the merciless, purifying violence of water that never stops falling.
