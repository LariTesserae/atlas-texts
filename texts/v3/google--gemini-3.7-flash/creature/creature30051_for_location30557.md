The people who inhabit the cliff-city are shaped, bone and blood, by the compression of the heights. 

They are wide through the torso, their ribcages arched and deep to house enlarged lungs that work with a slow, mechanical rhythm. Their blood is thick, dark with iron, moving sluggishly through heavy veins. To conserve moisture in an atmosphere that drinks wetness instantly, they rarely open their mouths fully when they speak; their language is a dialect of low, hummed vowels, glottal stops, and soft tongue-clicks that carry across stone alleys without scattering warmth into the cold. 

Their skin is thick and dark, stained with a walnut cast from the unshielded sun, but it is rarely seen bare. From crown to sole, they are wrapped. Around their eyes, they smear a thick paste of charred juniper and rancid fat to swallow the blinding glare of the pale slate and the white sea of clouds below. Their hair is braided tightly against the scalp with tallow, tucked under heavy skull-caps of beaten wool. 

They move with a characteristic gait—the *slate-step*—a low-centered, sliding walk where the foot is never lifted high, feeling through the thick rawhide soles for the purchase of each stone flag before weight is shifted. They do not run. To stumble here is to break on the rock or vanish into the abyss; therefore, haste is seen not merely as dangerous, but as a kind of madness.

Family life takes place in the dark, warm guts of the cells. The dry-stone houses are divided horizontally: the lower level holds the livestock, whose massive metabolic heat rises through slotted timber planks into the living floor above. A family sleeps together on broad, low platforms layered with sheepskins and thick, needle-felted blankets dyed dull brick-red with mineral earths. 

Their diet is simple, dense, and oily, designed to fuel internal combustion. They eat little raw food. The staple is a coarse paste of roasted barley and ground dried peas, kneaded in wooden bowls with hot salted tea and huge lumps of aged, pungent butter churned from the milk of the great wool-beasts. Strips of mutton and goat flesh, dried in the dry freezing wind until hard as slate shingle, are shaved into boiling broths thickened with pulverized marrow and mountain salt chipped from dry seams in the rock.

Beside the people, sharing their narrow terraces and breathing their shared steam, are the animals that make the city possible.

The great ruminants—the *yakhs* or wool-oxen—are massive, low-slung creatures with shoulders like boulders and wide, shovel-shaped hooves capable of gripping brittle basalt. Their coats do not merely grow; they cascade down their flanks in dense, felted skirts that nearly sweep the ground, saturated with a heavy, waxy grease that repels the daily needle-frost. These beasts are the true engines of the city. They haul the great slate slabs on wooden sledges up the ramps, their breath billowing out in regular, piston-like clouds. They are remarkably placid, their nervous systems slowed by the cold, standing motionless for hours against the stone tie-posts while their four stomachs churn through dried scrub, their heavy jaws rotating in a hypnotic, circular grind.

Higher up, where the alleys narrow to ledges barely a forearm wide, the smaller caprids rule. These are lean, wedge-headed goats with coats of dense, silver-tipped hair and immense, laterally flattened horns that sweep back over their shoulders. Their hooves are miraculous mechanisms—hard outer rims of horn encasing soft, rubbery central pads that act like suction cups on sheer stone. They live half-wild, owned in loose commonality by entire terraces. Children herd them with light willow switches, driving them along the absolute brink of thousand-foot drops to graze on the yellow lichen that encrusts the outer facades of the lowest houses.

Within the thickness of the dry-stone walls themselves—in the dark, windless voids between the inner and outer courses of slate—lives a shadow fauna. 

Tiny, gray-furred rock-voles scurry through the chinks, living on grain spills, dried droppings, and the discarded rinds of fat. Hunting them through the labyrinth of masonry are the mountain weasels: long, ribbon-bodied predators, snow-white in the winter and slate-gray in the bright season, that can dislocate their shoulders to slide through a crevice no wider than a man’s thumb.

Above, the sky belongs to the birds. The swifts and choughs do not merely roost in the cliffs; they are woven into the economy of the city. The masonry niches are crafted intentionally as nesting boxes. Every spring, young climbers descend on plaited leather cords to harvest a calculated portion of the eggs and the nitrogen-rich guano, which is prized above gold for fertilizing the tiny, terraced plots of mountain chive and bitter greens tucked into south-facing fault clefts.

At dawn, the swifts leave the city in a sudden, roaring rush—hundreds of thousands of black crescents diving off the ledges into the void, plunging down through the freezing stillness to hunt the thick insect blooms rising off the warm cloud-forests thousands of fathoms below. At dusk, they return, arrowing upward out of the cloud-floor in dense, wheeling spirals, their crops full of lowland life, bringing the energy of the unseen world below back up into the high desert of stone.

High above even the swifts, hanging on the razor-sharp thermals that peel off the basalt towers, circle the great bone-breakers—immense, diamond-tailed lammergeiers with wingspans that blot out the pale sky. They are the undertakers of the city. In a world where the rock is too hard to dig and wood too precious to burn for pyres, the dead are brought to the *Laying-Plates*: vast, flat slabs of slate jutting out over the sheerest precipices on the city’s outer rim. There, stripped of their felt and wool, the departed are given to the birds and the sun. Within hours, the flesh is stripped by the choughs and ravens; then the great vultures carry the long bones high into the pale air, dropping them onto the fractured rock below to shatter them into manageable shards, swallowing the marrow-filled splinters until nothing remains but white dust, which the high, cold wind scatters across the roof of the world.

There is no sense of isolation among these people, for they know no other state than this stark elevation. They view the world below the clouds as a drowned, chaotic place, thick with dampness and decay. Up here, where the stone is clean, the air is bright and thin, and life is measured in the slow stacking of slate and the steady warmth of wool, they endure—not in defiance of the mountain, but as a patient, calcified part of its face.
