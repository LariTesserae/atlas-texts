### The Breath-Keepers (*The Thrum-Folk*)

To look upon the people of the drift from above is to mistake them for large, pale amphibians trailing dark ribbons. 

They are human, but their bodies have answered the demands of the basin for centuries. Their ribcages are wide, vaulted high and deep like barrels to house vast, slow-burning lungs. Under their skin lies a smooth, continuous sheath of pale insulating fat—not the soft blubber of sea mammals, but a dense, firm tissue that rounds their joints and gives their limbs a heavy, marble-like smoothness. Their hair is kept long only along the scalp’s crest, braided tight with tallow and bitter lily-wax to keep it from fouling transit ropes, while the rest of their skin is scraped bare of hair using sharp slate tools to eliminate drag.

Because the water is so clear and the sunlight so direct, their eyes have developed a second, milk-filmed eyelid—a translucent nictitating membrane that snaps shut against the glare of the upper surface or the sting of lime-dust kicked up by shifting anchors. 

They do not live entirely water-logged. The upper halves of the floating hab-spheres trap large, domed air-bells, refreshed continuously through hollow reed-snorkels that poke through the surface or fed by the photosynthetic exhalations of the dense bladderwort and sedge mats. Inside these inverted pockets, the air is thick, warm, and humid, smelling of peat, musk, and curing fish. Here, families gather in the dry, resting on platforms of woven alder suspended above the water-floor. But their work, their courts of law, and their rituals take place entirely in the green void between.

They communicate with a double voice:
* Within the air-domes, they speak in soft, breathy glottal whispers to prevent sound from carrying through the water to neighboring spheres.
* Out in the open drift, they speak through vibration. By clacking slate plates worn like knuckles on their fingers, or tapping rhythmic intervals on the dry, hollow bone-pegs fitted into their diving harnesses, they can converse across the full four hundred paces of the basin. A skilled diver can identify a cousin by the pitch and drag of their stroke against the water before they even emerge from the amber-green shadow of a communal tower.

Their relationship to time is dictated by buoyancy. A child begins life in the highest, greenest habs—sunlit, light-headed, weaving flexible shoots near the surface rafts. As a person ages, their personal hab-spheres accumulate mineral crusts; by the time an elder reaches their twilight years, their home has drifted down into the cooler, dimmer mid-depths. To grow old is to settle toward the shale.

### The Working Commensals

No creature in the basin exists as mere wildlife; every animal is bound into the structural maintenance of the city.

#### The Indigo Scissor-Crabs (*Astacus calderis*)
Living along the underside of every domestic tower are colonies of indigo-shelled crayfish, bred to immense size. These creatures are the city’s carpenters and janitors. 
* They possess an innate, obsessive habit of trimming: their heavy, serrated pincers continuously snip away dead, rotting reed-fiber, decaying moss, and parasite sponges from the structural knots before rot can weaken the lashings. 
* In exchange, the divers feed them the offal of the char-catch and provide them with terracotta-like nesting burrows fashioned from sun-baked mud dropped down to the lower anchor-lines.
* Children learn their first knots by tying colored reed-markers to the carapaces of juvenile crayfish, herding them across the guy-wires like slow, blue cattle.

#### The Silver Char (*Salvelinus vitreus*)
Massive, blunt-headed trout patrol the lanes in disciplined, shimmering ribbons. 
* Their skin is nearly colorless—a pale, translucent pearl through which their pink spines can be seen.
* They graze compulsively on the sun-loving green slime that would otherwise choke the buoyancy gourds.
* The Thrum-folk do not net them indiscriminately. Instead, they cull only the oldest, whose skin has begun to cloud with age, using long, three-tined gigs of fire-hardened elk bone. The rest of the school is entirely tame, often schooling around a swimmer’s body to feed on the microscopic water-mites dislodged by their passing fin-strokes.

### The Deep Floor: The Resonant Old

At the bottom of the sink, forty fathoms down where the green light dims into an icy, slate-gray dusk, live things that predate the woven city itself.

#### The Bull-Lungfish (*Titanoceratodus*)
Resting half-buried in the glacial silt of the basin floor are the lungfish. Some are sixteen feet in length, their bodies like waterlogged logs of petrified cedar, covered in thick, diamond-shaped scales encrusted with freshwater bryozoans. 
* They are nearly blind, navigating the basin’s deep currents with sensory pits lining their broad, blunt snouts that detect the faint electrical pulse of an anchor rope snapping or a diver’s heart beating.
* Their deep, booming thrums—produced by flexing specialized muscles against their massive swim bladders—are the bass foundation of the city’s soundscape. 
* The people treat these ancient fish with great reverence, believing them to be the anchor-stones of the mountain's soul. When a lungfish dies, its ribs are carefully harvested to serve as the structural keels for new hab-spheres, while its skull is set into the tufa reef as a permanent shrine.

### The Floating Gardens and Surface Life

At the boundary where water meets the freezing sky, life changes abruptly. 

The garden-rafts—thick mattresses of living peat, roots, and pumice—form a mosaic ceiling over parts of the basin. 
* Beneath them, in the root-tangle, hide swarms of pygmy water-shrews with water-repellent, velvet fur. They dive into the upper chambers to hunt snails, their small silver forms leaving trails of glowing bubbles.
* On top of the rafts, exposed to the raw mountain air and occasional snowfall, grow bitter cresses, stunted willow shrubs, and the great white caldera lilies. 
* The flowers of these lilies open only when the midday sun hits the water at a direct ninety-degree angle. Their petals secrete a thick, sweet wax that the Thrum-folk skim off with wooden spoons; it is their primary waterproof tallow, used to seal skin, tools, and the air-locks of the suspended towers.

Here, on the thin gravel shelves around the rocky brim, the people come up to salt their fish and look upon the sky. They do not linger long. Exposed to the air, they feel unstable, unbuoyed, and heavy—crushed by the weight of their own limbs and the vast, empty silence of the mountain peaks. They slide back into the green water as soon as their work is done, returning to the comforting creak of the timber, the rhythmic clack of the stones, and the weightless avenues of the drift.
