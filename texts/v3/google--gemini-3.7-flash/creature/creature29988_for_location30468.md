They move with the heavy, unhurried cadence of things that have outlived the need for haste. 

At first, they are difficult to distinguish from the geology itself. They sit or lie submerged to the clavicles in the upper terraces, where the water is freshest from the seep. Their skin has lost its ruddy undertones, bleached to the grey-white of soaked birch bark, yet it is neither soft nor decayed; the high dissolved lime has tanned it, turning the epidermis dense and slightly slick, like the oiled hide of a river mammal. Around their ankles, wrists, and the creases of their elbows, where the mineral film dries during long hours on the ledges, fine crystal rimes form, dusting their joints in a pale powder that cracks with a faint, dry whisper when they flex.

There are only a few of them—perhaps five or six who frequent the basin at any one time, though their exact numbers blur into the background hum of the stone. They are broad-shouldered and thick-ribbed, with lungs built for the thick, alkaline soup of the air. Their hair is worn short, sheared near the scalp with flaked chert to keep the calcifying water from matting it into heavy, stiff ropes, though what remains is bleached the color of dry reed-pith. 

Their eyes are wide, pale-ringed, and seldom blink. In the flat, shadowless light of the hollow, their pupils are permanently expanded to dark disks, absorbing the scattered leaden gleam off the terraces. They do not look directly at one another often; in this space, vision is secondary to the skin’s read of temperature, vibration, and the acoustic slap of falling drops. 

When they speak, it is not in the vocalized cadences of the upper world, but in low, humming glottal stops and clicks of the tongue—sounds pitched below the resonant frequency of the chamber, so as not to set the vault humming with confusing echoes. Mostly, they are silent. They communicate by the displacement of water: the gentle surge against a neighbor’s hip that signals a shift in position, or a slow tap on a weir-stone to indicate a blocked channel.

Their work is almost indistinguishable from rest. One will spend half a morning leaning over the lip of a middle pool, using a thumb to gently guide the drift of the blind amphipods toward a shallow catch-basin, or carefully turning over a submerged slab to scrape the parchment mollusks into a stone cup. They eat them raw, chewing slowly on the rubbery, salt-sweet meat, swallowing the mineral mucus that coats them. It is a slow diet for a slow metabolism; their heartbeats are rhythmic, spaced, and heavy, attuned to the steady heat that seeps from the floor.

Between the human bathers and the stone, a third tier of life thrives. Pale, blind olms—slender as fingers and pinkish-white like freshly peeled roots—inhabit the under-shelves where the travertine mushrooms out over deep pockets of water. They are entirely unafraid of the people. When a bather sits motionless in the hollowed seats, the olms will emerge from the dark gaps between pools, undulating with lazy, ribbon-like strokes to brush their frilled gills against the bather’s submerged legs, nibbling peacefully at the dead skin shed into the mineral broth.

On the dry fringes, where the ceiling slopes down to meet the dolomite floor, long-legged cave crickets and ghostly harvestmen cling to the ceiling vaults. They do not descend to the water, but feed on the dry skin flakes and lime-dander that drift upward on the thermal plumes rising from the pools.

Nothing here is hurried, because nothing decays in the ordinary sense. Wounds do not fester in this water; they are sealed by the minerals, healing into thick, porcelain-smooth keloids that match the terraces. Even the dead are not buried in earth, for there is none. When one of them ceases to breathe, they are laid in the lowest, slowest basin—the one furthest from the inflow, where the water is thickest with dissolved dolomite. 

Over months, the olms clean the flesh to the bone, and then the water takes over. It lays down its slow, crystalline enamel across the ribs and skull, welding them to the weir, until the skeleton becomes another rimstone dam, holding back the warm pool for those who remain to bathe in.
