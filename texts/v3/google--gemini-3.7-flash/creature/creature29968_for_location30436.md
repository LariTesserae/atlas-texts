To live in this place is to be entirely free of the logic of dirt, decay, and the hunt. Because there is no soil to burrow into, no dead wood to rot, and no carrion to spoil under the steady sun, the living things that occupy this expanse have taken on the peculiar, bloodless perfection of the architecture itself. They are not messy interruptions to the geometry; they are its moving parts, its pressure gauges, and its aerostatic drifters.

The most visible of them are the **Hydra-Kites**—the broader, fully matured forms of those pale, paddle-bodied creatures that cling to the weeping aqueducts. In their adult state, their bodies are wide, paper-thin pentagons of translucent, horn-like cartilage, spanning twelve to twenty feet across. They have no eyes; their dorsal surfaces are smooth and white as paraffin, while their ventral sides are mapped with fine, amber-colored capillary groves that match the starch-chemistry of the timber’s sap. 

When a kite feeds, it spreads itself completely flat against the fluted bark of a column, sealing its perimeter with a perimeter-skirt of sticky, clear mucus. It becomes a living patch over a sap-vent, letting the immense hydraulic pressure of the tree inflate its thin internal cavities until it hums like a tightened drum skin. When the turgor pressure within its body matches the pressure of the wood, it breaks its seal with a sharp, wet *thwack* that echoes across the chasm, peeling off into the warm updrafts. 

Because the air between the pylons is perpetually rising—heated by the immense thermal mass of the wet timber and charged with humid starch-vapor—these creatures rarely flap. They drift across the miles of empty sky like discarded kites of oiled parchment, tilting their paddles to catch the spray-curtains, letting the mist condense on their bellies and running the runoff down toward central, pulsating siphon-mouths.

Down on the broad causeways, where the rush-textured floors remain spotless and warm underfoot, movement is sparse but deliberate. Here live the **Groomers**, or **Laminates**. 

These are long, low-slung beings that resemble segmented planks of pale boxwood or polished horn, moving across the kiln-hard surfaces on hundreds of microscopic, hydraulic tube-feet. They do not leave tracks; rather, their bellies secrete a thin, enzyme-rich film that polishes the causeway as they glide, dissolving any errant grain of dried sap or stray cellular debris and drawing it back into their digestions. 

A Laminate possesses no face, only a forward-facing sensory ridge of pale, light-sensitive cilia that tremble constantly in the draft. They are profoundly deaf to airborne sound, but they are exquisitely attuned to the internal resonance of the wood. If a blockage or an imbalance of pressure occurs in an aqueduct miles away, the Laminates feel the harmonic shift through the floorboards. They will gather in small, silent convoys, sliding side-by-side along the vast causeways toward the troubled joint, where they clamp their bodies over the seam, vibrating in counter-resonance until the vascular tissue inside softens, dilates, and allows the stuck volume of water to surge freely again.

Far more numerous, though entirely hidden from view, are the inhabitants of the interior conduits—the high-pressure channels that bore through the heart of the thirty-story columns. 

In these dark, roaring tunnels, where water races under hundreds of pounds of pressure per square inch, lives a fauna of extreme hydrodynamics. These are the **Needle-Eels** and **Torpedo-Polyps**—creatures of solid, vitreous muscle with skins as slick and friction-free as wet ice. They possess no bones, only dense rings of flexible, cartilage-like fiber that can withstand the crushing forces of the biological plumbing. 

They do not swim against the torrent so much as they hold themselves in place using barbed, anchor-like claspers that hook into the microscopic fibrous pores of the xylem walls. Anchored there, their long, ribboned bodies trail out into the pitch-black, pressurized deluge like streamers, their feather-fine oral combs sifting the sweet, colloidal starches from the water. When they release their hold, they do not fight the current; they shoot down the aqueducts at terrifying speed, carried for miles through the vascular labyrinth in a matter of seconds, before firing their anchors again into the wood of a distant basin.

At night—or rather, during the subtle dimming cycle when the great sun-glow mellows and the arches cool by a fraction of a degree—the balance of life shifts outward. 

From the sunken catch-basins and the pulsating fibrous grates along the causeways, **Siphon-Nymphs** emerge. They are delicate, spindly beings composed almost entirely of clear water contained in thin, iridescent membranes. Standing on legs like drawn glass, they move across the causeways with the halting, rhythmic grace of mechanical toys. They are drawn to the circular sphincter-vents along the flanks of the pylons. There, they gather to drink the freshest, most concentrated exudates, their translucent bellies swelling into glowing, greenish spheres as they fill with starch-water.

There is no predation here in the conventional sense. No jaws tear flesh; no blood is spilled on the wax-like timbers. The creatures of the shelf are entirely sustained by the excess vitality of the structures themselves. When a creature reaches the end of its life, it does not rot or wither. Its water-content simply leaks away; its cartilage contracts, dries into a clear, resinous amber, and is eventually enveloped and seamlessly subsumed by the growing cambium of the column it rests upon—grafted forever into the living architecture, becoming just another microscopic layer in the endless, pale expanse.
