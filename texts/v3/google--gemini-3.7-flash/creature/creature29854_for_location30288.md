To live here is to be deaf in the ordinary sense and acutely sensitive in every other. The sound in Sub-Level 4 is not merely heard; it is a mechanical violence delivered straight into bone, shell, and swim bladder. The creatures that persist here have had their sensory apparatuses radically rewritten by the iron drum they inhabit.

On the bottom plates, beneath the drifts of orange flocculent and shredded neoprene, lives a breed of shore crab that has forgotten the shoreline. Their shells are not the mottled greens or mottled browns of tidal mud, but are heavily mineralized, lacquered in layered crusts of iron oxyhydroxide that make them look like dropped tools or discarded fittings. Many carry small colonies of tube-dwelling bryozoans across their carapaces, pale calcareous zigzags that cement the animal to the chamber’s industrial aesthetic. Their eyestalks are stunted, sunken into deep orbital sockets and glazed over with thick, milky corneal tissue; light means almost nothing to them, but their legs—tipped with dactyls worn blunt from walking on checkered steel plate—are covered in dense fields of tactile setae. Through these hairs, they read the deck. They know the approach of the five-minute hydraulic cycle by the microscopic shear stress it sends through the floor rivets long before the water actually surges.

They graze in deliberate, scraping passes. Their chelae do not tear flesh so often as they rasp at the thick, greasy biofilms that coat the aggregate walls and bronze valve seats. In the stagnant eddies behind the manifold, where the current cannot strip it away, this film is half an inch thick—a dense, sulfurous mat of iron-oxidizing bacteria and anaerobic slime that feeds directly on the slowly leaching lead solder, the sulfur in the vulcanized rubber gaskets, and the iron ions shed by the decaying I-beams. The crabs eat the rust itself to harvest the microbes within it; their intestinal tracts are packed with heavy, brick-red paste, and they leave behind dense, pelletized spoor that settles into the floor seams, cementing the iron plates together like mortar.

Where the power cables cross the floor in cast-iron conduits, the water is slightly warmer—scarcely two degrees above the canal water’s winter chill, but enough to create a distinct microclimate. Here, the crabs pile upon one another in dense, clambering heaps, their jointed legs interlocking until they resemble scrap metal swept into a corner. 

Tucked into the gaps between those warm conduits are the flatworms and leeches: ribbon-thin, colorless things, semi-transparent except for the reddish sludge in their guts. They do not swim; they flow like spilled oil over the lead sheathing of the cables, drawn by the faint, leaking electrical fields that hum at sixty cycles per second. If an animal dies—pinned against a trash screen by the intake draw or stunned by a sudden cavitation shock—these worms arrive not by sight or smell, but by following the disruption of the galvanic field around the carcass. Within hours, the dead creature is covered in a shifting, gelatinous pelt of them.

Higher up, where the bronze wheels and valve stems protrude from the bulkhead, the eels have claimed the hydraulic geometry. They are long-bodied river eels, but altered by lifetime confinement in dark, pressurized water. Their skin is unusually thick, covered in an excessive, ropey mucus that protects them from the abrasive rust-grit suspended in the water, giving them a dull, leaden sheen rather than the oily iridescence of their wild kin. 

These eels never truly leave the pipework. They wedge their tails deep into the valve guides, wrapping their muscular posteriors around threaded brass shafts, leaving only their blunt, wedge-shaped heads and upper torsos projecting into the hall. They hang in the current at rigid, forty-five-degree angles, mouths slightly parted. Their eyes are small, ringed with pale scar tissue from brushing against sharp rust flakes, but their lateral lines are immense—visible as deeply pitted, white-rimmed trenches running the full length of their dark flanks. Through these pits, they feel the hall breathe. They feel the exact volume of water being drawn through the northern sluice; they feel the fluttering displacement caused by a school of blunt-headed fish twenty yards away; they feel the metallic ring of the maintenance chain against the crane rail. When the surge comes, they do not retreat; they stiffen into the current, letting the high-velocity flow rush through their open jaws and across their gills, gleaning whatever drowned worms, insects, or plant fragments the canal has drawn down from the world above.

The open water between the gantry platforms belongs to the shoaling fish. They are small—rarely longer than a finger—descended from lake chubb or minnows sucked through the intake grates generations ago, but their bodies have become pale, almost waxen, their scales paper-thin and lacking all pigment. In the jaundiced cone of the low-voltage luminaires, they appear semi-translucent; you can see the dark silhouette of their spinal columns and the pulsing, silver sacs of their swim bladders. 

Unlike surface fish, which use schooling to evade visual predators, these shoals move according to the acoustic resonance of the room. They have learned the standing waves of the chamber. When the primary turbine a mile away changes load and its drone shifts pitch, the entire school shifts with it, dropping three feet in the water column to occupy a new acoustic dead zone between two structural pillars. They feed on the "snow"—the endless, drifting fallout of oxidized iron flakes, bacterial clumps, and canal silt that hangs in the water. They dart forward to mouth a falling fleck, tumbling it over their gill rakers to strip the edible slime before spitting out the hard, rust core. 

Above them all, patrolling the cold, dim space just beneath the arched iron ceiling, are the room's solitary giants. 

Every few years, a flathead catfish or a broad-backed carp manages to survive the passage through the intake conduit as a fingerling, only to find the egress screens too narrow to leave once grown. In this chamber, without seasons, without sunlight, and with an unceasing supply of organic matter sucked down from above, they grow massive, heavy-bellied, and grotesque. 

One such creature—a carp nearly four feet long—cruises the ironwork of the upper gantry. Its scales are not golden, but a bruised, slate-grey, heavily scarred where it has scraped against overhead rivet heads and angle irons in the dark. Its dorsal fin is tattered, worn down to thick, cartilage stubs from years of navigating confined clearances. It is nearly blind; cataracts have turned its eyes into spheres of dull marble, but its long, sensory barbels sweep the steel girders like wet ropes, tasting the rust, the snail-trails, and the greasy algae that clings to the rivet seams.

It moves with an agonizing, ancient slowness, sculling with wide pectoral fins that displace gallons of water with every sweep. It knows the architecture better than the men who drew the blueprints. It knows precisely how much clearance exists between the overhead I-beam and the crane carriage; it knows which junction box houses a nest of freshwater sponges whose sulfurous scent carries on the drift. It has survived the pressure waves, the roaring sluice gates, and the endless, mechanical twilight so long that it has become an extension of the machinery itself—a living piston, cold-blooded and silent, drifting through the rust-clouded water of a room that was never meant to hold life, yet holds it in terrifying, quiet abundance.
