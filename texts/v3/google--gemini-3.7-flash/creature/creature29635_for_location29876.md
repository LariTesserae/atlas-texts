To live on the anvil of the plateau is to have no concept of silence, of dry skin, or of earth that does not shudder. Everything that breathes, crawls, or drifts here has been shaped by the ceaseless, four-beat measure of the wood-machinery and the cold, amber grease that keeps it from catching fire.

### The Bushing-Leeches and Gasket-Meat
In the tolerances between the sliding iron-bark trunks and the petrified peat—spaces measured in fractions of an inch where the wood groans under millions of foot-pounds of lateral pressure—live the *packers*. 

They are annelids, but they have shed all segment-flexibility in favor of a body like dense, vulcanized rubber, slate-blue and slick with vegetable petroleum. Some are sixty feet long and no thicker than a man’s forearm; others are flattened, broad as rugs, wedges of living cartilage that slide willingly into the grinding seams. They do not feed on organic tissue. They feed on the shear stress itself, drinking the weeping vegetable tallow that boils off the hot bark and drawing chemical sustenance from the microscopic carbon dust worn away by friction. 

Their flesh is perpetually being crushed, ground down, and reconstituted; their blood is rich in suspended graphite and cold-resistant alcohols, circulating through high-pressure hydrostatic vascular systems that mirror the hydraulic roots around them. When a root-shaft completes its backward stroke, the packers exhale a thin, whistling scream of compressed air through rows of lateral spiracles, venting heat before the return stroke wedges them tight again. They are the living seals that keep the plateau’s joints from seizing.

### The Scrap-Scavengers (*Strobe-Crabs*)
Darting through the pressurized mist along the fluted sides of the piston-snouts are the creatures that tend the bark: things the size of mastiffs, with ten asymmetrical, needle-tipped legs forged of a dark, iron-rich chitin that rings like struck bell-metal against the wood.

They are entirely blind. Where eyes should be, they have heavy, forward-facing balance-organs—pendulous globes of bone suspended in oil-filled cavities—that allow them to read the vibration of every approaching stroke. They move in frantic, stuttering bursts of speed, synchronizing their scurrying with the rhythm of the engines. 

Their mouthparts are a set of opposed, counter-rotating scrapers that sheer sulfur crusts, parasitic lichens, and congealed kerosene from the seams of the cedar. They work with a frantic, industrial precision. When the green friction-sparks jump from the root-beds, the crabs freeze, their reflective shells catching the instantaneous emerald light before they plunge back into the grease-spray. If one mistimes a step and is caught by the descent of a pneumatic bough, there is no blood—only a dry, percussive *crack*, like an iron casting breaking under a drop-hammer, and its brethren immediately converge to scrape the splintered chitin off the cedar before it can score the wood.

### The Gutter-Eels of the Kerosene Runoff
In the deep, foaming trenches where the hydraulic sap and vegetable kerosene churn toward the plateau’s drop-off, the liquid is too volatile and too cold for ordinary gill-breathers. 

Here dwell the *ribbons*. They are ribbon-thin, nearly two-dimensional fish with skins made of translucent, oil-repellent scales that shimmer like gasoline on wet asphalt. They swim against currents violent enough to strip bone, using the intense hydraulic head to force fuel through their porous, comb-like bellies. These combs filter out the tiny, spinning lenses of heavy water that form in the low-gravity pockets, cracking them internally through pressure-catalysis to harvest oxygen and leaving behind tiny trails of inert, fizzy foam.

The ribbons never sleep; they cannot afford to drift. To be swept over the plateau's rim is to fall five miles into the dead, oxygen-starved lowlands where their cold-stabilized blood would instantly boil in the warmer, thicker air.

### The Sheet-Gliders (*Vane-Manta*)
Above the shuddering lawn, where the bruised fog is sliced by the whirring, saw-toothed leaves, drift the plateau’s only fliers. 

They do not flap. They are broad, kite-like organisms made of dry, leathery membranes stretched across an internal lattice of hollow, silica-hardened bone. They look less like birds than pieces of ship rigging torn free in a gale. Their underside is lined with rows of adjustable, horn-rimmed louvers. By opening and closing these vents with wet, snapping clicks, they maneuver through the vertical steam-blasts vented by the dry-dock seed-pods.

The Vane-Manta feed on the wing. As the iron-moss trees stamp out their rotary leaves, swarms of these gliders dive into the spray of razor-sharp vegetation. They possess thick, shovel-shaped beaks lined with stone-hard ridges; they catch the spinning timber-blades mid-air, snapping them in half with the sound of a guillotine dropping, then swallowing the splintered, sap-heavy pulp for the volatile resins inside. 

To rest, they do not land—the anvil-peat would break their light frames under the vibration. Instead, they fire long, mucus-coated tether-lines deep into the fibrous husk of the hanging seed-pods, mooring themselves in the gale like blimps lashed to a mast, swinging wildly in the freezing ammonia downdrafts.

### The Pod-Brood
Inside the immense, translucent seed-pods hanging from the cable-creepers, life does not develop by gestation; it is assembled.

Through the fibrous, semi-clear walls, one can see the *brood*—tangled aggregates of limbs, piston-ribs, and rubbery sacs churning in an amniotic bath of warm, amber hydraulic fluid. When the pod reaches its terminal pressure, the release of the steam valve is accompanied by a massive, outward spasm of the hull. 

What tumbles out onto the freezing moss is rarely a single, discreet beast. It is a cluster of modular life: several blind, tallow-slick quadrupeds linked by thick, pulsating umbilical conduits; sprawling, multi-jointed crawling frames that have no organs except massive, pumping hearts designed to siphon fuel from the runoff streams into the moss-beds; and heavy, barrel-shaped infants that immediately wedge themselves into the nearest drainage trench, their skins hardening in minutes into the same slick, black armor as the iron-bark trees.

They do not have mothers. They have an infrastructure.

### The Bell-Wanderers
Rarest of all are the solitary giants that walk the miles of open peat between the industrial groves. 

Standing forty feet tall, they are shaped like narrow, three-legged tripods made of gray, wet muscle and petrified fiber. They have no heads; their apex is a massive, downward-pointing hollow bell of horn that acts as an acoustic resonator. 

The Bell-Wanderers are the true natives of the rhythm. They move across the smooth, anvil floor with agonizing slowness, lifting one massive, suction-rimmed foot only when the subterranean drive-shafts reach the dead-point of their stroke. Their bell-cavities hum constantly in sympathy with the five-mile-deep vibrations of the peat, producing a low, sub-audible drone that liquefies the fog around them into falling sheets of ice.

Wherever a Bell-Wanderer stands, the gravity-fails are most severe. Discs of water and kerosene lift off the ground and spin around their legs like the rings of small, cold planets. Small blind crabs climb their limbs to escape the crush of the pistons, and sheet-gliders shelter beneath the rims of their vibrating acoustic bells, waiting out the worst of the ammonia storms.

They are walking islands of relative stability, lumbering through the perpetual half-night, their entire bodies tuned to the single, eternal purpose of the plateau: to keep moving, to keep bearing down, to ensure that the friction never dies, and that the engine of the world never grows cold.
