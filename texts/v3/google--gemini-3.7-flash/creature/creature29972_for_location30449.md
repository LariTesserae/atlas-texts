To understand what lives in the basin, one must first understand that life here does not struggle against the iron; it has simply ceased to distinguish between the geology of stone and the geology of machinery. To the organisms of this plain, a brass crosshead moving through its tallow-slick guide is no more artificial than the flow of a thermal spring or the fall of rain.

The most numerous inhabitants are the creatures that never leave the joints of the engines. 

Tucked into the narrow, sheltered recesses beneath the great walking-beams are colonies of what look like flattened, slate-colored barnacles, known only by their faint, rhythmic clicking as the iron tilts overhead. These are sessile detritivores whose shells are not lime, but a hard, lamellar composite of bonded iron dust and excreted silicates. They anchor themselves directly to the cold copper plates and weeping brasses where the journals cast off their warm aerosol of condensed water and heavy petroleum lubricants. Their feather-fine feeding limbs, dark as soot, sweep the air in precise synchronization with the downdraft of the pistons, catching the atomized oil-mist before it settles into the gravel. When a pivot-arm dips, they close their plates with a soft, wet snap; when it rises, they bloom outward again, feeding on the mechanical breath of the basin.

In the shallow, rust-tinted pools that cradle the counterweights live the *skimmers*—broad, paper-thin organisms shaped like spade bits or discarded gaskets. They possess no eyes, but their undersides are lined with rows of sensitive, copper-threaded cilia that steer them through the warm emulsion. They feed on the leathery bacterial mats that cling to the iron sumps, grazing on the sulfur-eating crusts with rasping, keratinous jaws. Because the water never cools and never boils, their metabolic rate is perfectly flat. They do not sleep; they drift across the oily meniscus in widening, perpetual circles, pushed along by the regular, rhythmic shockwaves generated each time a counterweight grazes the surface of the pool.

Further out on the wide, gravelly flats between the engines, the scale of life expands, though its pace remains tethered to the cadence of the ground.

Moving across the magnetite sand are the *striders*—tall, long-limbed creatures standing six or seven feet at the shoulder, with three-jointed legs as thin and rigid as wrought iron rods. Their carapaces are dull, segmented, and dark, resembling overlapping plates of planished tin, pitted with tiny, circular breathing spiracles that weep a clear, bitter grease. They possess no heads in any conventional sense; their bodies terminate in a heavy, downward-curving vault that houses a dense cluster of acoustic and seismic organs. 

A strider does not walk by sight. It navigates entirely by the harmonic lattice of the basin. Each massive engine cluster rings through the bedrock at a distinct frequency—a low B-flat from the western crank-pits, a dry, percussive click from the northern rockers—and the strider steers between them as a ship navigates between lighthouses. They move with an unhurried, measured gait, lifting their spindly limbs high above the graphitic dust and planting them with a dull, metallic *tink* upon the gravel. 

These striders are miners of a sort. They follow the exposed crankshafts to their bearing blocks, where the friction of the turning shafts wears away trace shavings of soft bronze and lead. With delicate, triple-jointed mouthparts tucked beneath their chest plates, they sweep up these metallic filings and the thick, oxidized slurry that collects in the catch-trays, digesting the organic fatty acids in the grease and incorporating the heavy metals directly into the mineral matrix of their shells. When two striders meet near a bearing, they do not fight or display; they merely stand flank-to-flank for hours, their carapaces pressed together, letting the deep, concussive *thung-shunk* of the nearest piston vibrate through both of their frames until their inner fluid chambers fall into phase with the machine.

Beneath the gravel, in the warm, porous earth woven through with fissure drains, lives a stranger, more solitary fauna: the *bellows-worms*. These are thick, segmented creatures, sometimes twenty feet in length, with hides like vulcanized rubber or wet coal. They inhabit the gravel drains where the warm, condensed steam trickles down from the cooling jackets. Blind and nearly motionless, they wedge themselves into the drainage fissures, their accordion-pleated bodies expanding and contracting with the cyclic changes in air pressure caused by the subterranean dynamos. They are passive filterers of the warm runoff, absorbing the zinc, copper salts, and sulfurous broth that leaches through the crushed slag. 

Reproduction among the basin’s fauna is devoid of season, governed instead by the wear and clearance of the iron. 

When a bronze bearing after several centuries finally wears a hairline gap against its journal, the striders sense the subtle change in the machine's acoustic pitch miles away. They converge upon that specific sump, depositing clusters of dark, spherical eggs directly into the warm grease reservoirs. The eggs are tough, coated in a petroleum-resistant varnish; they incubate in the steady, gentle heat of the working friction, hatching into tiny, scurrying larvae that feed on the lubricant before dispersing into the wide desert of magnetite sand.

Even death here is neat and mechanical. When a strider or a sump-skimmer reaches the end of its centuries-long life, its limbs stiffen, its internal fluids dry into a powdery, graphitic tallow, and its shell remains standing or floating where it died. The wind and the fine magnetite dust gradually abrade the dried husk, polishing the plates until they shine like burnished pewter before breaking them down into fine, mineral sand. There is no decay, no rot, no foulness; the dead simply become part of the lubricating dust that keeps the great journals turning smoothly in their bronze beds.

Under the pale, unchanging sun, these creatures wander or cling, perfectly matched to the tempo of the iron. They do not know a world that is quiet, nor one that is dark, nor one that accelerates. They are the living lubrication of the basin, sharing its immense, unhurried breath, content to exist within an eternity of steady work.
