To know the makers of the bridges, one must first discard every instinct born of a wet world. There is no blood in them that could spill, no wet red meat to tear, no moisture to weep into the thirsty mineral air. If a limb were sheared by a falling slab of anhydrite, the wound would show only dense, pale layers of laminated fat, yellowed cartilage, and dry, fibrous muscle like salted jerky, sealed instantly by a waxy, amber-colored lipid that hardens upon contact with the static heat.

They are called—if they could be named in the language of sound—the *Tappers*, or the *Groomers of the Edge*.

In form, they are low-slung, four-limbed, and built with the dense, squat architecture of deep-mine machinery. Their torsos are broad, flattened chests wrapped in thick, interlocking bands of leathery, hairless hide that feels to the touch like oiled boot-soles. They lack anything resembling an optical face; where eyes would be, their skulls slope into a heavy, wedge-shaped prow of solid bone, reinforced to take the blunt impact of stone. 

Their sensory apparatus is entirely tactile, thermal, and resonant. Beneath the thick ridge of the brow runs a horizontal slit lined with thousands of delicate, dry cilia—not hairs for catching dust, but thermoreceptors so acute they can feel the body-warmth of a passing scavenger six arm-lengths away, or detect the faint, cool draft of a dead-end fissure where the stone drops by a thousandth of a degree. On either side of their heavy jaws sit large, concave plates of paper-thin cartilage that serve as acoustic diaphragms, tuned precisely to the high, ringing frequencies of sheared quartz and the deep, seismic groans of the mantle.

Their hands and feet are marvels of dry adaptation. They have four digits on each limb: two opposing pairs of thick, square fingers tipped not with nails, but with heavy, spade-like claws of solid keratin, dense enough to score limestone. The undersides of their palms and pads are calloused into thick, rubbery cushions, grooved with deep tread patterns that grip the slick, mirror-surfaces of feldspar like dry suction cups. 

They do not walk as surface creatures do, with rhythmic strides across a horizontal plane; they climb, wedge, and lever. Their joints are double-hinged and capable of locking into place with a dry, internal *clack*, allowing an individual to hang suspended between two sheer vertical blades for days at a time, entirely motionless, without expending muscular energy.

### The Craft and the Hunt

Life for these beings is a slow, methodical curation of stone and tendon.

Because they cannot waste motion, their hunting is an exercise in immense patience. They do not pursue the swift, many-jointed scavengers across the chasms. Instead, they position themselves above the narrow flues where the pale grazers congregate to rasp the sulfur seams. Hanging upside down by their locked foot-claws, their skin matched perfectly to the ambient body-heat of the rock, they wait.

When a grazer moves beneath them, the hunter does not strike with frantic violence. A heavy, pointed punch—carved from the femur of an elder or the dense core of a dead carapaced beast—is driven downward with a single, massive blow of a hammerstone. The strike must be precise: it finds the exact seam where the grazer’s mica plates overlap, shattering the nerve ganglion beneath. 

Nothing of the kill is lost. The grazer’s dry, non-aqueous fat is rendered by friction into a heavy, waxy paste used to grease joints and coat rawhide straps against brittle fracture. Its diamond-hard rasping mouthparts become drill-bits and awls; its long, springy dorsal tendons are stripped, dried, and plaited into the thick, three-ply ropes that span the gulfs between crystal fins. The carapaces are peeled away in wide, translucent sheets to be used as shingled roofing for the wedge-shelters or ground into abrasive grit for shaping flint.

### Architecture of the Hollows

Their dwellings in the three-slab wedges are not merely shelters; they are acoustic and thermal resonators.

Inside a shelter, the walls are lined with meticulously sorted flakes of mica and thin sheets of split slate, layered to trap the collective warmth of the family group. Because there is no wind to sweep away scent or heat, a shelter that has been occupied for ten generations carries a faint, distinct signature: the rich, paraffin-like odor of their lipid waxes, the dry chalkiness of bone meal, and a subtle warmth that lingers in the stone long after the occupants have climbed into the lattice.

In the center of each shelter sits a *strike-plate*—a carefully chosen block of ringing phonolite or dense, resonant jadeite, suspended on dry leather straps above the floor. This is their hearth, though it holds no fire. 

When a family gathers, they communicate not through vocal cords, for they have no lungs designed for wet exhalation, but through a language of percussive rhythm. They use small, polished bone mallets to strike the plate. A single tap sends a sharp, crystal-clear ping vibrating through the framework of the shelter, out into the anchors, and down the length of the suspension bridges. 

To a listener miles away in the dark, touching a finger to a quartz rib, the entire chasm is a gossamer web of telegraphy. A sharp, syncopated *tock-tock-tock* tells of a loose slab in the northern flues; a long, sustained hum generated by rubbing a ridged bone against a slate wedge announces that a hunting line has been secured across the Great Anhydrite Shear.

### The Cycle of the Husk

Death among them is without horror, for it carries none of the indignity of rot. 

When an elder’s joints finally seize, when the waxy secretions of the lipid-glands dry to a chalky powder and the acoustic diaphragms stiffen, they do not bury them. There is no earth to dig, no soil to receive them.

Instead, the dying one climbs to a chosen alcove—often high up on a narrow, vertical fin overlooking the deepest drop of the rift—and wedges their limbs into the rock. They lock their joints for the last time. As the life-force ceases, the body undergoes a slow, clean mummification in the desiccating body-heat of the air. Over decades, the skin tightens into a hard, resonant drum over the ribs; the muscles cure into dry cordage; the bone absorbs trace minerals from the stone contact-points.

The living return to these ancestors not to mourn, but to consult them as fixtures of the landscape. An ancestor is a reliable handhold on a difficult traverse. An ancestor's rib-cage, perfectly preserved and dry as old boxwood, is sometimes struck lightly with a bone mallet by passing travelers—a respectful tap that sends a clean, musical chime through the dark, confirming that the bridge is sound, the stone is still, and the route remains open.
