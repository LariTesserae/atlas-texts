The things that live in the basin are not swimmers. Swimming belongs to open, anxious water; here, where every foot of liquid is dense with dissolved lime and warm machine-tallow, life does not dart. It climbs, it drifts, or it anchors itself with the patient, hydraulic certainty of the pumps themselves.

At the lowest order, occupying the silted doorframes and the black sludge of the tenement cellars, are the **tallow-crabs**. They are small, broad-backed crustaceans whose shells are not chitinous, but calcified into a thick, slag-like crust from decades of rolling in iron filings and lime-saturate. They have lost the capacity for scuttling; instead, their legs have evolved into blunt, peg-like levers that pry along the mortar joints with the rhythmic persistence of clockwork escapements. They do not hunt. They feed on the thick, rancid mineral grease that still seeps from the hubs of drowned tram-wheels, and on the soft, white sheets of bacterial bloom that slough off the steam-mains like wet parchment. When a walking beam makes its slow downward stroke, stirring a brown cloud of rust from the rooftops, dozens of tallow-crabs simply raise their heavy, squared pincer-spoons to catch the settling silt, licking it clean for the sulfur and dead yeast it carries.

Higher in the weed-forests, coiled around the balustrades and the wrought-iron lintels of old machine-shops, live the **flue-eels**. They are blind, thick as a teamster’s thigh, and entirely white, save for a delicate marbling of rust-brown along their dorsal ridges where mineral scale has grown into their skin. They have no scales to speak of; their hide is a thick, slick blubber, impervious to the caustic, soda-heavy water. These creatures spend days wedged into the warm chimneys and exhaust flues, their snouts pointing into the gentle upward draft of heated water. They breathe through broad, frilled gill-slits that shudder with the exact frequency of the distant boiler hum. They do not strike at the cave-carp that drift past their mouths; instead, they exist in a state of suspended torpor, absorbing nutrients directly through their spongy skin from the warm, chemical broth that rises through the brickwork.

And among them—threading the narrow, weed-matted spaces between the third-floor fire escapes—are the **Loomers**.

They were people once, or at least they began in the dry, loud centuries when the foundry still smoked into the sky. Now, they are something shaped entirely by warmth, pressure, and the absence of haste.

They do not have gills, nor have they grown fins. Instead, their adaptation is one of lung and skin, of habit and weight. Their bodies are broad, smoothed by thick layers of pale, lard-like fat that insulates them against the alkaline bite of the water. Their skin is neither scaled nor bare, but treated continuously with a rubbed-in balm of sheep-tallow, paraffin, and boiled linseed oil, which they harvest from the deep grease-traps of the engine vaults; it gives them the yellowish, waxy appearance of candles left in a cellar. Their hair is entirely gone, replaced by a fine, moss-like growth of harmless green algae that helps camouflage their shoulders against the brickwork.

A Loomer does not swim. Swimming requires quick, wasteful gestures that disturb the delicate thermal layers of the basin. Instead, they *haunt* the architecture. They move by pulling themselves along the cast-iron gantry rails and fire-escape chains with long, calloused fingers that have grown wide and spatulate, their nails thick as horn from prying lead caulking out of old seams. On their feet they wear no shoes, but their soles are tough, yellow pads of dead horn, two inches thick, impervious to the jagged edges of rusted boiler plate.

They live in the "Dry Pockets"—the upper half-stories and attic dormers of the highest tenements, where centuries of trapped steam, methane, and exhaled breath have formed pressurized, dome-shaped air-bells against the plastered ceilings. To enter a home, a Loomer rises through a submerged stairwell, their pale head breaking the oily, shimmering interface between the water and the air with a soft *chup*.

The air in these pockets is not fresh; it is thick as wool, ninety degrees, tasting permanently of wet masonry, coal-gas, and dried fish. In these stagnant attics, lit only by the green glimmer rising through the floor-holes, they sleep in hammocks made of woven eelgrass ribbons, suspended inches above the water's surface. 

Their social life is silent and tactile. In the water, human speech is useless—a muddled, booming distortion that deafens more than it informs. The Loomers have abandoned it entirely for a language of iron-tapping. Each of them carries a small, square-headed hammer made of case-hardened steel, hung from the waist by a greasy hemp cord. By tapping out short, rhythmic sequences against the iron water-mains, they can send messages across all four blocks in a matter of seconds. A double-rap on a third-floor downspout means a thermal current is cooling; three long rings against a walking-beam pedestal announces that a maintenance-chamber window has cracked and must be caulked before the lantern dies.

Their labor is neither desperate nor ambitious; it is purely conservative. They are the caretakers of a dead engine that refuses to stop turning. 

Every day, two or three Loomers will descend into the lower pits—weighted down with pigs of pig-iron tucked into the pouches of their tallow-aprons—to visit the great walking beams. They carry scrapers carved from carp-bone. With long, meditative strokes, working in the total dark by touch alone, they scrape the freshwater sponge from the massive gear teeth, ensuring the wood-and-iron cogs do not jam. If the gears were to jam, the pump would stop; if the pump stopped, the convective draw that pulls fresh, warm water up from the boiler-hells would cease; the basin would go cold, the sulfur-bacteria would starve, and the light would go out forever.

They do not remember the sun. They have no word for "sky" that does not also mean the vaulted, rivet-studded iron ceiling fifty feet above the highest chimney. They believe the entire universe consists of brick boxes immersed in warm broth, surrounded by an infinite wall of cold cast iron, and that beyond that iron is only the dry, dead vacuum where nothing can be lubricated, and where to breathe is to choke on thin, weightless dust.

When an elder Loomer dies, they do not bury them in the silt, where the tallow-crabs would take them too quickly. Instead, they carry the body down to the deepest boiler flue, where the heat is still fierce enough to shimmer through the water like melted glass. They lash the corpse to the great walking beam’s piston rod with copper wire. 

As the beam makes its slow, three-minute stroke, the dead are drawn down into the boiling darkness of the cylinder, then lifted back into the amber haze of the basin, over and over, until the water has dissolved the fat, the iron has claimed the bone, and nothing remains but a thin smear of black grease on the cylinder walls, helping the machine run smooth for another fifty years.
