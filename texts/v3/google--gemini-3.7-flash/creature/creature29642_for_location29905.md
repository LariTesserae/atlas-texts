To survive in a brine that does not cloud, under a sun that never softens, an animal cannot hide behind murk or sediment. It must be either perfectly seen or completely glass-clear, and it must possess an interior architecture capable of withstanding sound waves that can shatter unreinforced tissue.

Life here has abandoned the soft, damp fragility of typical marine biology. Its creatures are built like precision instruments—combinations of horn, crystal, vulcanized oils, and cold-cast cartilage.

### The Resonating Shoals (*The Comb-Ribs*)
The translucent chordates that drift in vast, coordinated ribbons through the open water are known for their absolute symmetry. Their bodies are long, compressed blades of stiff, gelatinous amber, completely transparent except for a narrow spine of solid horn and the fine, polished bronze of their mirror-scales. 

They possess no traditional hearing; instead, their ribcages are combed and graded like the tines of a music box. Each rib is calibrated to vibrate at a precise sub-harmonic of the distant turbine impellers. When an industrial station cycles thirty miles away, a shockwave passes through the shoal; the fish do not flinch, but their flanks flash instantly white as their mirror-scales pivot on microscopic ligaments, dumping the acoustic energy outward into the water rather than allowing it to rupture their internal organs.

They do not hunt in the conventional sense. The violent, continuous cavitation of the water shears apart microscopic organic matter—free-floating lipid chains shed by dying siphonophores and oily bacterial slimes stripped from the cooling jackets of the caissons. The comb-ribs drift through these invisible nutrient corridors with their broad, triangular mouths held open, their gill-combs lined with piezoelectric bristles that extract suspended fats by electrostatic charge. 

### The Cable-Creepers and Anode-Lice
Closer to the machines, the ecosystem becomes dense, heavy, and metallic. Clinging to the black iron rivets and the sagging, catenary power cables are colonies of blind, iron-shelled isopods the size of carriage wheels. 

Their carapaces are not made of chitin or calcium, but of biogenic magnetite and layered zinc flake, formed by drawing dissolved metal salts straight from the cathodic discharge of the machine hulls. They have no eyes; in their place are two thick, forward-swept horns of solid iron that press directly against the vibrating metal plating. Through these horns, they "read" the operational health of the turbine inside. They know where the water is warm, where the lubricating tallow leaks through high-pressure packing glands, and where the electric current is concentrated enough to foster thick crusts of sulfur-reducing bacteria.

Moving among them along the copper cables are long, centipede-like scavengers whose underbellies are grooved like pulleys. They hook themselves over the thick lines and ride the long sag of the catenary arcs between massifs, gliding frictionlessly through miles of blue emptiness, grazing on the bio-galvanic fur that grows along the high-voltage lines.

### The Skylight Chains
Directly beneath the flat, mercury-like ceiling where the water meets the vacuum-thin air, the great siphonophores hang in sheer, vertical curtains. Some stretch downward for two hundred fathoms, tethered to the surface by floating bells filled with heavy, unfreezable mineral spirits.

Because the solar light drives straight down without scattering, these colonies cast sharp, dark shadows that fall thousands of feet down the sheer granite walls of the massifs. The bells themselves are pale pink, tough as wet leather, and nearly impervious to ultraviolet radiation. 

Their trailing tentacles do not sting with venom. Instead, they are strung with razor-sharp, vitreous barbs made of pure biogenic silica. When schools of smaller fish are startled by a sudden, concussive pressure-venting from the deep stations, they bolt upward into these invisible, hanging grids of glass wire, where they are sheared into segments and drawn slowly upward into the digestive nodes of the colony.

### The Zinc-Grazers (*The Cathedral Scavengers*)
The greatest living presences in the plateau are the solitary scavengers that cruise the acoustic trenches. These leviathans have no calcified bones; a skeleton of true bone would snap under the continuous, concussive hammer of the turbines. Their skeletons are entirely made of dense, oily blue cartilage, thick as the timbers of a galleon and flexible enough to absorb high-yield shockwaves.

Their eyes are milky, white globes without irises or lids, large as washbasins. They do not look for prey; the endless daylight of the plateau would burn out a standard retina. Instead, their eyes are solid cataracts of crystalline fluid that diffuse the light into a soft, directional compass, allowing them to orient against the black profiles of the rising massifs.

They navigate entirely by the "feel" of the water. Their wide, shovel-nosed heads are honeycombed with oil-filled acoustic channels that map the sea's standing waves. They can sense a blind spot—a place where the sound of two competing caissons cancels out into a pocket of eerie, pressureless calm—from forty leagues away.

When a zinc-grazer finds an industrial caisson, it approaches with a slow, mechanical inevitability. It wraps its leathery, oil-slick body around the station's grounding pylons and uses a massive, rasping tongue—covered in hundreds of thousands of diamond-hard silicate teeth—to strip the thick, chalky rime of zinc oxide and mineral grease from the iron skin. The machines do not repel them; the grazing clears the galvanic sludge from the heat exchangers, cooling the turbines and allowing the engines to run a fraction of a percent faster. 

In this way, the animal and the engine exist in an unspoken, mindless symbiosis. The machine shakes the sea, providing the lightless current, the heat, and the mineral salt; the beast clears the rust, absorbs the vibration into its cathedral of oil and cartilage, and drifts back out into the bright, freezing blue, carrying the sound of the engine in its ribs for the rest of its centuries-long life.
