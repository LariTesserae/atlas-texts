To live here, life had to forfeit the habit of rushing. It had to surrender the fragile, frantic pulse of warm blood and the twitching haste of terrestrial things, exchanging them for the slow, crushing permanence of the stone itself.

Nothing here swims. Swimming implies an elsewhere, a surface to seek or an escape to make, and in this boundless, sunless basin there is only the floor and the weight above it. The creatures that inhabit this submerged continent do not navigate the water so much as they are weighted down by it, sharing its density, its chill, and its monumental patience.

***

At the lowest tier—in the fine, pale silt that drifts into the masonry joints—the stone is shared by things that are barely distinct from the architecture. 

They appear at first as flat, broad flagstones that have drifted out of alignment: wide disks and rhomboids of pale, calcified flesh, three or four yards across, resting directly over the seams where the basalt slabs meet. They have no eyes, for there has never been light to meet them, nor do they possess limbs. Their undersides are carpets of millions of translucent, hydraulic cilia—not soft, but stiff as horn—that work themselves down into the cracks between the megaliths. 

These are the *Tallow-Slabs*. They do not hunt. They are living seals, drawing their sustenance from the slow, chemical bleed of the ancient mortar-substitutes and the deep terrestrial heat that leaks through the planetary crust miles below. Their metabolism is so retarded by the near-freezing brine that their hearts—chambers the size of wine casks filled with thick, clear lymph—beat perhaps twice in a solar year. When a kelp frond drags across their backs, they do not flinch; over decades, their leathery hides accumulate the same velvet algae that coats the granite, until they are entirely indistinguishable from the floor they tend.

Between them, moving through the silt with the imperceptible crawl of glaciers, are the *Gaugers*. They are long, segmentless ropes of pale, fibrous muscle, thick as ancient hawser cables, that thread through the sunken gutters and carved stone channels. They follow the lines laid down by the builders millennia ago. They have no faces, only blunt, sensitive snouts that taste the cold current for shifts in salinity and the vibration of falling silt. A Gauger will spend three centuries traveling the length of a single sunken avenue, its belly pressed to the granite, cleaning the fine ash out of the carved drainage grooves with its slow, rasping mouthparts, keeping the stone bare not out of duty, but because it feeds upon the micro-coatings of lichen that cling to the dressed rock. Where a Gauger has passed, the floor is left dark, polished, and naked against the black sea.

***

Higher up, within the leather-thick canopy of the kelp forest, lives a different kind of inhabitant.

Suspended among the towering stipes are the *Weave-Bodies*. They are neither fish nor coral, but colonial organisms that have grown to mimic the plants they dwell within. They resemble vast, tangled skeins of dark, greased cordage, suspended vertically in the water column, moored to the kelp by thick, fibrous knots. Within their knotted interiors, they harbor slow, cold fires—not light, but a faint, rhythmic heat generated by the chemical digestion of the sulfur and minerals carried on the deep drift.

The Weave-Bodies are sensitive to the physical sound that travels through the water. When the ocean hums its low, continuous note against the stone terraces, their strands vibrate in sympathy, tightening and loosening like the strings of a drowned instrument. They communicate across miles of black water through these physical acoustics, sending shudders through the kelp forest—dull, heavy twangs that travel through the masonry and are received days later by colonies leagues away. Their thoughts, if they have them, are communal, vast, and slow; a single sentiment may take a century to cycle through the forest, passing from frond to frond until it dissipates against the base of some colossal retaining wall.

***

And then, in the wide, empty courtyards and upon the great ceremonial staircases, there are the *Keepers*.

Whether they are what the original builders became, or whether they are merely the most complex beasts this cold world could forge, is impossible to know. They are solitary, upright shapes, standing twice the height of a man, though they carry themselves with the hunched, thickened posture of deep-sea pressure. 

Their bodies are colossal columns of dense, blubbery muscle, encased in a seamless hide like wet slate, cold and smooth to the touch. They have no lungs; their broad, vaulted chests are filled with cavernous spongy gills that slowly expand and contract with the hour-long leaning of the current. Their limbs are massive, ending in blunt, broad spades of solid bone, fused and worn, that can grip the stone without slipping in the heavy drift.

They have no language, no tools, and no eyes. Their faces are smooth, featureless domes of gray blubber, save for a wide, horizontal slit across their temples—a pit organ sensitive to the displacement of water and the mass of stone around them. They navigate by the "feel" of the masonry. They know the symmetry of the continent; they know every step, every terrace, every rounded corner of every house-sized slab of basalt.

A Keeper does not wander aimlessly. It moves with deliberate, agonizing purpose. 

One will stand at the base of a titanic staircase for twenty years, its massive feet anchored to the pavers, letting the kelp drape over its shoulders like a heavy mantle. Then, moved by some internal, tidal clock, it will lift one ponderous foot—the movement taking an entire morning—and set it on the next step. It will climb the staircase over the course of a lifetime, its thick, blunt digits tracing the worn gutters, clearing away the accumulations of dead weed, pressing loose pavers back into their beds with the terrifying, hydraulic strength of its limbs.

They do not interact with one another. When two Keepers pass on a wide causeway, they do not turn or gesture. They merely lean away slightly, their massive, slate-gray bulks displacing the heavy water, their skin brushing together with a sound like wet leather sliding over stone. For a moment, their internal hums align—the deep, resonant frequency of their hearts beating against the silence—and then they drift apart, one continuing down toward the sunken plazas, the other rising toward the barren terraces where the forest thins and the endless, flat masonry meets the black, empty sea.

They are not waiting for the water to recede. They have forgotten that there was ever dry air, or a sun to cast a shadow. They exist only to be heavy in a place of weight; to be durable in a place of friction; to ensure that as the world grinds slowly to powder, the stone is not left entirely alone in the dark.
