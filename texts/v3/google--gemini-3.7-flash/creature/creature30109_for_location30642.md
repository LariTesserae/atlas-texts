They do not walk with the heel-first strike of lowland people; to strike hornfels with an unyielding step is to ring a chime that carries up the cleft, and here, vibration is an indiscretion. The people of the shelf—if they are still entirely people—move upon the thick, calloused outer pads of their feet, their arches high and prehensile, the skin along their soles hardened into a tough, pale leather that mimics the velvet-matted underbellies of the hunting-hounds in the crevices.

They are short-waisted, wide-ribbed, and deep through the chest, built like bellows to draw nourishment from the thin, moisture-thickened air. Their skin has the greyish cast of wet slate, underlaid with a faint, plum-colored flush where the capillaries gather near the surface to drink what little thermal radiation bleeds from the mountain’s living core. They have little body hair, save for dense, coarse brows that slant sharply down toward their cheekbones to divert the hovering dew from their eyes, and thick, felted braids of scalp-hair that they grease with rendered snail-fat to keep from soaking up the mountain fog.

In their movements, there is no haste, but an endless, rhythmic continuity. 

They wake before the bruised vault of the sky pales, sliding out from the lower recesses of the five-tiered dwellings. These sleeping cells are dug shallowly into the cliff-face itself, lined with dry river-reeds and the cured, papery skins of spent gas-bladders. A family sleeps in a single knot, limbs intertwined for warmth, their breathing synchronizing into a shallow, collective rhythm that produces no more sound than the condensation falling through the ferns.

When they emerge into the stepped alleys, they do not speak. The human voice has not vanished, but it has been reshaped. To shout would be madness against the brittle stone, where an errant resonance might dislodge an unbalanced slab of hornfels; instead, their language is one of pressure, tactile glyphs, and low, pulmonary murmurs. When two builders meet on the narrow catwalks of a granary, they lean their foreheads together, letting the vibrations of their vocal cords pass directly through the bone of the skull. To an outsider, it would look like an act of tender mourning or secret conspiracy; to them, it is merely the morning exchange of structural news—which retaining braid has slackened, which cistern has filled.

Their clothing is an assemblage of what the shelf yields without protest. They wear tunics beaten from the inner fibrous bark of the petrified-wood roots, interwoven with strips of the waxy fern fronds that retain their supple, water-shedding nature long after they are severed. Over their shoulders, they drape heavy mantles made from the segmented carapaces of the slate-shelled gastropods, the plates drilled with flint augers and strung together with tendon. These mantles clack softly when first donned, but the wearers pack the joints with wet liverwort pulp, silencing them completely.

Much of their life is spent in the harvest, which is not an affair of seasons, but of hours. 

The morning scrape is performed with long, crescent-shaped shovels carved from the ribs of down-covered hound-things that have died of age in the wall-crevices. The harvesters move in lines along the terraces, peeling the night’s growth of bioluminescent slime from the stone before the pale day can wither it. The slime is scraped into tubs of braided grass, where it is kneaded with powdered shale and dried into the grey, crumbly tallow used for their smoldering, cold-flame hearths. 

Behind the scrapers come the children—nimble, spindly creatures who scale the vertical mortarless walls with the casual ease of geckos. It is their task to tend the gas-bladders. With long poles tipped with hooked bone, they gently steer the drifting ribbons away from the living-alcoves and toward the cliff-edges, harvesting those whose translucence has turned to an amber cloudiness—a sign that their interior oils are thick enough to be rendered. A child will reach up, catch a sensory tendril between two fingers, and stroke it with a specific, rolling pressure; the floating beast, lulled by the mimicry of a stone-lichen current, will slowly deflate its ballast and sink into the child’s arms like an armful of cold silk.

They do not bury their dead in the rock; the hornfels is too hard, and to break it unnecessarily is considered an insult to the mountain’s fragile balance. 

Instead, when an elder ceases to breathe, they are carried to the highest terrace, just beneath the shelf’s overhanging lip where the dripping moisture is heaviest. There, the body is laid upon a bed of living liverwort. The blind salamanders are brought from the cisterns in wide wicker bowls and placed upon the chest, the limbs, the eyes. The salamanders do not feed violently; their tiny, fern-like external gills pulse against the cooling flesh, and their skin secretes a clear, preservative enzyme as they nestle into the warmth of the decaying marrow. Over weeks, the body is slowly, silently incorporated into the terrace itself—the bone dissolving into the lime that feeds the waxy succulents, the skin becoming a rich, black peat from which the next generation of ferns will unroll.

In the late evening, when the pearl sky deepens into the color of a bruised iron kettle, the community gathers in the small, open-air forum. 

They sit on the terraced slabs, thousands of them, pressed side to side between the blind shale-predators that squat undisturbed upon the lintels. The blue, smokeless light of the snail-matter hearths casts long, cool shadows across the masonry. There are no songs, no instruments of brass or hide. Instead, the elders bring out large slabs of polished slate, scored with microscopic grooves carved over centuries with diamond-pointed stones. 

Taking wet river-pebbles between their thumbs, they begin to draw them across the stone faces in long, rhythmic arcs.

The sound it produces is not music as the lowlands know it. It is a soft, friction-free sighing—the sound of fog drifting over wet leaves, of an oiled joint moving in its socket, of stone settling against stone five thousand paces above the world. The thousand blind salamanders around the cisterns fan their crimson gills in time with the strokes. The people lean their heads back against the slate, their wide, dark eyes reflecting the cold blue embers, perfectly content in the damp, crowded, smothering quiet of their high ledge.
