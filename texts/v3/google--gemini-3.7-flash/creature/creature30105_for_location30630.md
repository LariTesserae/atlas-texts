The life here does not merely occupy the hollow; it is wound tight like a watch spring, driven by the violent luxury of warmth and pressure in a world that offers neither.

### The Pulse-Born

At the base of the food web are the organisms that do not measure life in days, but in the rhythmic condensing of the mist. 

When the spires trigger a drop of heavy moisture, the slick silt beneath the jade liverworts erupts. They are called *drift-midges* only for lack of a better word—they are closer to swimming seeds or feathered flakes of mica. They possess no digestive tracts, no mouths, and no eyes; their entire anatomy is reduced to a pair of branched, iridescent paddles, a sack of high-potency lipids, and a reproductive filament. 

They emerge by the tens of thousands in the three minutes it takes for a water droplet to bead and fall from a fig leaf. They rise like a steam of living glitter, collide in mid-air to exchange genetic material with a faint, static crackle, and drop back into the silt as spent husks before the rock has fully dried. Their bodies decay so quickly under the spire-heated soil that they leave behind a sweet, yeasty mulch—the true soil of the basin, without which the heavy figs could never grip the granite.

### The Spire-Huskers

Clinging to the three seamless black needles are the hollow’s true anomalies: flat, plate-bodied creatures roughly the size of a human palm, known in behavior as *huskers*.

Their bodies are broad, segmented shields of translucent, milky amber. They have no eyes; instead, their dorsal plates are etched with micro-grooves that register the infrasonic hum of the spires with pinpoint precision. On their undersides are wide, pale pads of modified muscle that function as acoustic couplers. They do not feed on organic tissue. Instead, they press themselves flush against the unreflective black alloy, their bellies matching the machine’s impossible internal vibration.

As they absorb the kinetic and electromagnetic runoff, their organs—clearly visible through their carapaces—ignite with a cold, cerulean luminescence. This energy drives an internal chemo-synthesis, turning inhaled trace minerals into dense, glassy deposits that they periodically excrete as smooth, pearl-like beads along the needle’s base. They move with agonizing slowness compared to everything else in the hollow, shifting only inches an hour, their bodies serving as living heat-sinks that keep the machine's mirror finish clear of mold and silt. 

When a husker finally dies, its blue light does not fade; it fractures into a brittle, crystal shell that other climbers shatter and consume to harvest the accumulated charge.

### The Perimeter Skimmers

Above the rock floor, the dense, humid dome of air is dominated by the aerialists—creatures that look like an impossible cross between hummingbirds and flying fish, scarcely longer than a finger.

Their bones are pneumatic and filled with pressurized air that would burst in the thin atmosphere just ten feet above them. Their plumage is not made of soft barbules, but of rigid, oil-treated quills that snap and whistle as they bank at blinding speeds. They hunt the falling midges and the smaller beetles with an erratic, zig-zagging flight that mimics the thermal eddies rolling off the spires.

Most striking is their relationship with the invisible boundary of the basin. The transition from the warm, pressurized pocket to the freezing mountain void is absolute, a shear zone only inches thick. The skimmers use this boundary as a trampoline. They will dive toward the outer lip, flare their stiff tail-fans against the "wall" of cold, thin air, and bounce backward into the basin with a sharp *thwip* sound, using the shock of the pressure differential to catapult themselves into sudden, blinding turns. 

To cross the threshold is death: occasionally, an aerialist is clipped by a rival and tumbles out into the thin plateau air, its lungs instantly failing, its frozen body dropping onto the bald granite outside like a pebble.

### The Pale Claws

Patrolling the soapy mounds of the granite basin are the six-legged runners. 

Their bodies are narrow, pale-fleshed, and entirely hairless, with skin so thin that the violent, rapid churning of their two separate hearts is visible beneath their ribs. Their feet do not use claws to grip; they are tipped with broad, moist pads that exploit the persistent water sheen on the stone, creating a capillary vacuum that allows them to sprint vertically up the polished granite walls without losing traction.

They hunt by sound and seismic vibration. Because the spires fill the basin with a constant, low-frequency roar, the runners have developed specialized jawbones that unhinge to touch the rock, filtering out the machine’s steady chord so they can isolate the microscopic patter of beetle legs three yards away. 

When they strike, they do not bite; their jaws are fused into a sharp, piston-like beak that punches through chitin with a hydraulic snap audible over the ambient din. They are intensely territorial, their pale flanks flushing with bright, violent crimson when another hunter approaches their chosen patch of liverwort.

### The Deep Fig Weavers

Beneath the frantic surface noise, buried in the dark, pressurized hummocks where the mountain figs twist their roots into the rock fissures, lives the basin’s slowest and most vital resident.

They are heavy, soft-bodied worms as thick as a wrist, their skin the color of wet river clay. They spend their entire lives woven into the root mats, eating the rain of falling beetle shells, dead midges, and the pearl-like frass of the spire-huskers. Their digestive systems produce a thick, rubbery mucus that glues the wind-blown dust to the barren granite, creating artificial terraces of rich, black soil that hold the vegetation in place against the localized updrafts.

Without them, the basin’s frantic life would blow away into the sky in a single season. They are the anchor of the orchard—blind, quiet, and ancient, their bodies carrying the deep, iron-heavy scent of the earth that the needles have gathered and preserved at the top of a dead world.
