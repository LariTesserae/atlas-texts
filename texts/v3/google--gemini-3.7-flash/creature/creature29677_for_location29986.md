To live here is, first and foremost, to understand that gravity is not a law, but a local habit.

The creatures that populate the open lattices do not fight the glare; they incorporate it. The flat carapace-beetles that sheet the I-beams are so smooth they disappear into the surfaces they graze, vanishing into the dazzle of raw milled alloy until a gust of thin wind tilts their bodies, sending a blinding ripple of solar glare across forty yards of vertical trusswork like a signal lamp. Underneath their mirrored shells, their soft parts are colorless and translucent, circulating a pale, glycol-rich hemolymph that never freezes, even when the wind coming off the edge of the world drops below the point where human breath turns to falling needle-ice. 

They feed on the copper-wool flora, but they also feed on the grease. They are the continent’s custodians, though they know nothing of it; their rasping, ceramic-tipped mandibles scrape away the hardened residues of dead lubricants, leaving the cold steel bare and bright for the condensation to wash clean.

Where the beetles graze, the *hinge-striders* hunt.

These are spindly, six-legged predators the length of a man’s forearm, built out of the same pale, semi-rigid cartilage as the birds. Their limbs are jointed like surveyor’s calipers, tipped with hardened pads of fibrous horn that adhere to smooth metal by dry suction. They do not walk so much as snap from one geometry to the next. A hinge-strider can sit motionless for two sunlit days on the underside of a cantilever, its long, needle-like abdomen tucked into the shadow of a rivet head, waiting for a ribbon-wing bird to misjudge the draft around a flange. When it strikes, it does not lunge; its joints release stored mechanical tension with the dry, sharp *clack* of a spring-trap, snapping across three feet of empty space to pin the membranous bird against the steel before it can open its wings.

Higher up, where the architecture twists into recursive loops, the ecology becomes completely non-linear.

The ribbon-wings do not nest in single locations; they nest in intervals. Because a bird can fly into a vent-stack and emerge through the bottom of the same stack thirty feet *below* where it entered, their breeding colonies exist in stable spatial eddies. A pair of ribbon-wings will construct a nest out of stripped copper-wool and the discarded chitin of beetles, wedging it into a corner where three right-angled girders form a pocket. But because of the spatial fold, the nest is simultaneously situated inside four different structural bays across a three-mile radius. A single chick, blind and naked as an unboiled sausage, sits in what appears to be four separate holes at once; the parent bird can land in any of the four bays to drop an oily midge into the same open, translucent beak.

Because sound dies quickly in the vast dampness, the life here communicates almost entirely through touch and vibration.

Deep inside the hollow box-girders live the *tappers*. They are blind, pale, mammal-like things—perhaps descended from long-tailed viverrids or rodents brought here when the construction was first laid down, though they have lost all fur, their skin having turned into a tough, slick rind the color of wet lead. They move in small families through the dark, warm interiors of the pressurized conduits, following the hum of the hydraulic lines. To find one another across the chasms, they do not cry out; they strike the resonant walls of the girders with hardened, bony knuckles on their forepaws.

A single strike sends a clean, ringing tone down five leagues of continuous steel plate. Another family, twenty miles away across a silent void, feels the vibration through the foot-pads of their sleeping young and taps back: *Clear channel. High pressure. Safe.*

And there are people, or what people become when they stay above the earth too long.

They call themselves the *Truss-walkers*, though they rarely walk in the terrestrial sense. They live in the "dead zones"—the vast, sheltered spaces beneath the main transit decks where the air is still, the condensation forms stable drinking pools in the hollows of inverted channel-beams, and the sun strikes only at dawn and dusk. 

They are tall, lean, and pale-eyed, their retinas shielded by permanent amber-tinted lenses made of polished machine-grade resin, strapped tightly to their brows with oiled leather. Because the glare can blister the cornea in an hour, they treat bare vision as a weapon, looking at the sky only through narrow slits in their visors.

Their clothing is constructed entirely from the continent’s sparse harvest: vests woven from the tough, unyielding threads of the copper-wool moss, lined with the paper-thin, curing skins of ribbon-wings, and waterproofed with coats of amber-colored turbine grease. They smell of paraffin, zinc oxide, and dried blood.

They do not use speech for ordinary conversation; speaking aloud in this thin air is exhausting and carries no further than three paces. Instead, they talk with their iron-shod climbing staves. A walker standing on a crane boom can converse with a companion standing upside-down on the opposite gantry through a rhythmic, telegraphic clinking against the steel. The language is intricate, based not on words, but on pitches, structural harmonics, and the duration of the beam’s ringing decay. To a Truss-walker, every grade of steel has its own accent: cold-rolled plate is bright and fast; cast-iron counterweights speak in a low, sullen mumble; the high-tensile suspension cables scream like wire when struck with a brass hammer.

They build their shelters like hammocks, slung across the acute angles where massive diagonal bracing meets the horizontal struts. A family home is nothing more than a series of taught, grease-cured membranes suspended in the V of an eight-foot girder, rocking gently whenever a train of hoppers rumbles along some distant, inverted track miles above them.

They do not bury their dead. There is no dirt to receive them, and fire is precious, reserved only for the brazing of metal tools. When a walker dies, their kin carry the body out to one of the shear-folds—the places where the girders do not meet, where the stress of the continent hangs suspended across empty air on arches of glittering metal dust.

There, in the dead silence of the midday glare, they push the body into the gap. 

It does not fall. Caught in the static tension of the spatial fold, the body slowly pulls apart into a fine, crystalline mist of salt, water, and iron, its atoms aligning with the magnetic flux of the continent. For months afterward, travelers crossing that sector can look up into the gap between the trusses and see a faint, iridescent shimmer hanging motionless in the sun—not a ghost, but a small, shimmering piece of human mass helping to hold five thousand tons of cold steel together.
