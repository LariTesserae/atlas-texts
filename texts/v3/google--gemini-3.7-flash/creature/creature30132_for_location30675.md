To understand what lives here, one must understand that life in this room does not measure time in seasons, but in the slow depletion of alloys and the millimeter-by-millimeter descent of lime. Nothing here is warm. Nothing moves quickly enough to generate a wake.

At the lowest trophic tier are the *solder-eaters*—not true animals, but vast, skin-like consortia of chemolithotrophic fungi and iron-fixing bacteria that have made their homes directly upon the galvanic junctions. They do not merely tolerate the weak violet voltage leaking from the submerged copper-zinc sleeves; they depend on it. Where the current is strongest, the microbial mats grow three inches thick, swelling into liver-colored lobes that feel like cold fat. They smell faintly of wet slate and battery acid. When an electric pulse fluctuates—perhaps because a valve collar miles away shifts under shifting earth—these mats shudder, exuding microscopic clouds of zinc oxide into the dark water, feeding the mist above.

Moving over these mats are the grazers, creatures whose carapaces have forgotten how to make melanin. The crayfish are entirely vitreous, so translucent that the slow peristalsis of their indigo-stained guts is visible through their dorsal plates. Because the water is so still, their appendages have grown absurdly attenuated: legs like needle-fine wire, antennae three times the length of their bodies. They do not swim by snapping their tails—such an exertion would tear their paper-thin pleopods—but drift, feather-light, buoyed by the high mineral density of the brine. When they molt, their cast-off shells do not decay. Impregnated with calcite and iron salts from the water, the empty husks calcify where they fall, so that the sunken cart tracks are lined with thousands of rigid, ghostly replicas of crayfish, standing like crystal monuments along the submerged iron rails.

Higher on the walls, where the air is only mist and brick, live the *condenser spiders*. They are long-bodied, chalk-white tetragnathids that do not spin orb webs. Instead, they string single, perfectly horizontal threads between the brick vaulting and the iron flanges, precisely along the line where the rising warm vapor from the city collides with the freezing seepage of the masonry. The falling drops strike these threads, running along them to collect in heavy, hanging globules. The spiders drink from these suspended beads, which are rich in airborne soot and grease-spores, and hunt the blind, wingless crane flies that drift up from the lower water on thermal drafts. The spiders move with a mechanical, hydraulic jerkiness, pausing for hours with their legs pressed into the mortar lines, their bodies flattened so tightly against the brick that they become indistinguishable from lime efflorescence.

In the intermediate zone—the three feet of black water over the sunken machinery—the ribbon-salamanders are the absolute monarchs. 

They are not solitary by nature, but by density; the chamber’s slow metabolism can only support perhaps seven or eight mature individuals. They are *proteids* of an ancient, aberrant strain, possessing neither eyes nor lungs, but sporting four pairs of bushy, plume-like external gills dyed deep scarlet by hemocyanin. Their skin is smooth, slimy, and faintly opalescent, marbled with the faint, yellowish veins of subcutaneous fat. 

These salamanders have developed an acute galvanosensory system. Along the sides of their paddle-shaped heads run rows of electro-receptive pits that can detect the micro-current of a crayfish twitching forty yards away through a cast-iron valve housing. They do not hunt by pursuit. A salamander will drape its six-foot, ribbon-thin body through the spoked rim of a drive wheel, anchor its tail around an iron cotter-pin, and simply wait. It rests its chin on a bolt-head, its gills fanning once every ninety seconds, completely motionless until a grazer drifts within three inches of its jaw. The strike is not a bite, but a sudden, terrifying vacuum created by the expansion of its throat cavity—a single, hollow *thwump* that leaves the surrounding water rippling against the pillars for minutes afterward.

They are extraordinarily long-lived. A ribbon-salamander that wedges itself into the dark cavity of an intake pump may have curled there when steam still hissed through the flanges above. Their heartbeats drop to two per minute in the cold midwinter drainage; their bones do not show growth rings, but fine, metallic stain-lines where their marrow has sequestered excess lead and copper from the municipal runoff.

Deepest of all, down in the drowned transfer pits whose brass-edged rims yawn below the cart tracks, lives something that has ceased to be entirely animal or entirely mineral. 

In the total, anoxic stillness of those twelve-foot wells, where the water is so cold it is nearly dense enough to float wood, there are *siphon-sponges*. They have anchored themselves to the submerged brass intake grates. Huge, pale, and vascular, they have grown through and around the brass mesh, taking its exact geometric form—square, perforated, and heavy. They pump the lower water through themselves using millions of microscopic cilia, their pale bodies trembling with the effort of circulating gallons of dead, lightless brine. 

They are the lungs of the basement. They draw in the microscopic detritus of the upper vault—the shed scales of the mollusks, the spider silk, the bacterial flakes—and exhale water that is chemically pure, completely clear, and utterly dead. Around their bases, where their fleshy skirts meet the corroded brass, colonies of tiny, blind flatworms cluster in trembling rosettes, basking in the minute warmth generated by the sponge's cellular friction.

Nothing here ever sees the sky, nor do any of these things have a concept of the city whose weight presses down upon the brick vault above them. To the creatures of the chamber, the world is a cold, echoing box of water and rust, lit by the violet whisper of dying batteries, governed by the heavy, rhythmic condensation of iron, and measured out in the slow, eternal falling of drops that turn everything to stone.
