In the high, warm air, the drift-medusae do not ever fully descend. They are born in the moist upper canopy of vapor, budded off from the trailing tendrils of their elders in clutches of fifty or a hundred at once. Barely larger than the seed of an apple at detachment, they swell within an hour on the ambient mist, absorbing water directly through their outer mantles until they are the size of tea-saucers, flat and disc-like, buoyant as blown soap. 

They have no nervous center that could be called a brain, only an azure ring of light-sensitive nodes around the margin of the bell that detects where the sky is most radiant. They track the invisible thermals rising from the warm stone floor. When they graze, they lower their gossamer oral arms into the clouds of yellow aerial micro-moss, sifting the spores through microscopic combs of clear cartilage. When two medusae touch in mid-drift, there is no collision; they simply slide over one another on cushions of displaced vapor, swapping small, glowing packages of genetic material through their sticky fringes before parting again without a sound. When they die—usually within three days of their budding—their mantles do not rot. They liquefy instantly into a warm, faintly sweet droplet that joins the weeping condensation on the leaves below, leaving behind only a pair of microscopic, crystalline balance-stones that tumble into the shallows and dissolve like salt.

Down on the water’s meniscus, life is ruled by the *striders*—translucent, ten-legged arthropods whose carapaces are formed not of chitin, but of a biological silicate identical to flexible quartz. Their joints are lubricated by a clear, natural oil so slippery that their limbs never puncture the water’s surface tension. 

They move in strict geometric figures: triangles, heptagons, sudden concentric circles that expand and vanish before the eye can trace the shift. They are not chasing prey in the desperate sense of ancient predators; they are harvesting the oily film of dead-current micro-bacteria that blooms wherever the submerged vitrified lattices leak their faint, subatomic warmth. A strider will pause over a seam of tallow-colored stone, lower its delicate, three-part proboscis into the water film, and drink for thirty seconds, its internal organs—pale lavender, coiled like watchsprings—flushing briefly with crimson light as it metabolizes the mineral soup. Its lifespan is scarcely longer than a single cycle of the basin’s air-currents. Within an afternoon, it has hatched from a pearlescent cyst on the underside of a liverwort leaf, grown, traced its million glittering angles across the glare, laid a cluster of clear eggs directly onto the wet stone, and curled its limbs inward, drying into a brittle husk of sea-glass that the next walker gently shatters underfoot.

On the islands of liverwort and rubbery succulent, the *velvets* lie in heaps. 

They are lizards only by ancestral memory. Their limbs have shortened into soft, suction-tipped cushions that cling to the waxy surfaces of the vegetation without tearing the tissue. Their skin is not scaled, but covered in a dense, mossy pelt of microscopic cilia, gray-green and permanently wet, which drinks in oxygen directly from the saturated air. They have no nostrils, no lungs to expand and contract, no ribs; they are flat, supple pancakes of muscle and fat, scarcely thicker than a finger, resting layered three or four deep in the crotches of the broad fronds. 

Their compound eyes, faceted like dark hematite, do not blink. They track the aerial bells with slow, meditative patience. When a floater drifts within reach, the velvet’s mouth does not open with a snap; its lower jaw unfolds downward like the petal of an orchid, releasing a tongue that is not muscular flesh, but a jet of pressurized, sticky mucus. The tongue extends two feet in a fraction of a heartbeat, snatches the jellyfish from the air, and retracts, folding the gelatinous mass smoothly into a multi-chambered gullet where it is reduced to pure energy within minutes. 

The velvets speak to one another not with sound, but with heat. Across their velvet backs run faint, branching channels that flush with localized blood-warmth, creating fleeting, infrared mosaics that can be seen by their kin: announcements of satiety, the readiness to mate, the subtle shifts in the basin’s atmospheric pressure. When a pair mates, they simply press their bellies together on the broad face of a leaf, their cilia interlocking like felt, and remain motionless for a quarter of an hour while their body temperatures rise in unison until a faint wisp of steam rises from their flanks.

Running between these stationary clusters are the *tallow-weasels*, the warmest things in the basin. They are no larger than field mice, long-bodied and low-slung, with pelts of blinding, silver-white fur that is utterly impervious to water; they can plunge beneath the surface film and emerge completely dry, the moisture sliding off them in perfectly spherical beads. 

Their feet are paddle-shaped, tipped with soft, clawless pads that make that faint, rhythmic *snick* against the vitrified stone. They are foragers of astonishing speed and delicacy. Their snouts are equipped with whiskers that do not feel movement, but sense the electromagnetic decay of the dormant machines buried below. They follow the invisible lines of ancient power etched into the basin's floor, digging with their snouts into the tiny crevices where the stone lattices meet the basalt, extracting microscopic tubers and the dormant cysts of water-worms. 

The tallow-weasels possess hearts that beat at several hundred strokes per minute, yet they exhibit none of the frantic twitching of ancient rodents. Their movements are fluid, circular, and serene. They live in communal burrows hollowed out beneath the root-mats of the giant liverworts, where a dozen of them will sleep in a single, breathing knot, their long tails wrapped around one another’s faces, radiating a steady, comforting heat that incubates the plant above them.

Even the sessile life possesses a quiet, half-animal vitality. The indigo mosses do not merely wait for spores to land; their fan-shaped fronds pulse with a slow, peristaltic wave every few minutes, fanning the warm water across their undersides to bathe their microscopic gill-tufts. Around the bases of the melted, knee-high pedestals, where the dormant field-generators still leak their faint, rhythmic hum, the moss grows thick and dark as lapis lazuli, forming thick, sponge-like rings that drink the stray voltage and emit, in return, a steady exhalation of tiny, phosphorescent bubbles that rise through the water-skin and pop with a faint, imperceptible sigh.

There are no carrion-feeders here, because there is no carrion. The system is too ancient, too refined; decomposition has been replaced by immediate, cooperative absorption. When a velvet reaches the end of its few days of life, it does not stiffen; its tissues soften into a pale, nutrient-rich jelly that the liverwort beneath it drinks within hours, turning the fronds a brighter, deeper green. The small, silver-furred quadrupeds lick the remnants from the leaf surfaces, their tongues velvet-soft, cleaning the slate for the next generation that is already hatching from the warm mud beneath.

Everything is translucent or reflective. The life here seems made of the same substance as the light and the weeping water—condensed, given a momentary form of incredible complexity, set into motion for a day or an hour, and then allowed to melt back into the warm, planetary glaze of the floor. It is a world without winter, without night, and without hunger, where every living thing is a fleeting, exquisite eddy in a calm and endless pool.
