In the narrow gaps between the towers, life does not walk; it wedges itself. 

The things that leave those heel- and palm-prints in the floor are not visitors to this basin, nor are they its masters. They are its yield—beings whose anatomy has been pressed, scraped, and hardened into conformity with the terracotta world they extend.

They are known, if they could be named at all, as the **Stampers** or the **Setters**, though among themselves there is no spoken language to carry a name. They are tall, laterally flattened hominids, their ribcages compressed from birth so that their sternums project like the keels of skiffs. This allows them to slip smoothly through the two-hand-span fissures between the flue-clusters without turning their shoulders. Their skin is entirely hairless, uniformly stained the dull, ferrous rust of damp brick, and perpetually crusted along the flanks with dried lime-slurry and tallow grease. 

They possess eyes, but the lids are thick, fleshy folds that remain drawn shut, sealed against the lime dust and kiln grit; they have no use for sight in a place where light is only an accidental byproduct of heat. Instead, their faces are dominated by broad, flat nasal clefts that sample the draft with exquisite sensitivity, tasting the moisture gradient, the sulfurous tang of peat smoke, and the cold, metallic ozone of untouched voids. Their ears are large, shell-like apertures set flat against their skulls, tuned precisely to the percussive acoustics of the masonry. By resting their temples against a pillar, they can hear the cracking of a green tile six tiers below or gauge the tension of a hemp counterweight three hundred yards up in the scaffolding.

Their limbs are tools of direct contact. The skin on their palms and the balls of their heels has thickened into dense, insensible pads like boiled leather, ridged not with human whorls, but with cross-hatched calluses scored by the grit of coarse temper. Their fingers are long, blunt-ended, and possess an extra interphalangeal joint, allowing them to fold back completely or spread into wide, spade-like wooden floats to spread wet clay across the grooved floors.

They do not sleep lying down—there is no floor space broad enough that is not actively being worked or drained. Instead, they roost inside the unglazed chimneys, wedging their knees and elbows against the internal flues where the warm, ascending draft keeps their joints from stiffening in the chill basin air. Dozens will stack themselves vertically inside a single conduit, asleep like bats in a hollow tree, their rhythmic breathing contributing to the soft, whistling drone that echoes through the brick pipes.

### The Commensals of the Mortar

Below and around them moves an ecology that lives off the chemistry of the construction:

*   **The Lick-Mites:** Pale, blind, and soft-bodied, these creatures are the size of sheep ticks, though far flatter, with carapaces the color and texture of raw dough. They congregate in the hairline fissures where the tallow-and-shell grout remains soft. Thousands of them line the seam-work, their rasping mouthparts gently scraping at the animal fat. As they feed, they excrete a milky, calcareous fluid that sets upon contact with the draft, effectively sealing the joints more tightly than the builders left them. They are neither pests nor pets; they are the living finish of the masonry.
*   **The Dowel-Spiders:** Moving high across the lashed saplings, these leathery, eight-limbed scavengers have legs that taper into hard, cylindrical keratin pegs. As they crawl, their limbs seat themselves with dry, mechanical *taps* into the round peg-holes drilled into the struts. They feed on the moldering rawhide cordage and the carcasses of fallen Lick-Mites, spinning thick, water-repellent webs of coarse, fibrous gut across the open tops of unused chimneys to trap the moisture-beads rising on the warm air.
*   **Sump-Eels:** In the drainage braids and the skin-deep condensation pools dwell translucent, ribbon-like organisms. Having no eyes and only vestigial fins, they pull themselves along the shallow ceramic grooves using small, hooked ventral scales. They feed on the drift of ash and pulverized bone-temper washed down from the mixing troughs, filtering the water before it plunges into the circular floor-drains.

### The Life Cycle of the Edge

The Setters do not reproduce in beds or dens. In the lower, hotter tiers, near the smoldering vent-holes where the peat cakes bake the foundation blocks, there are wide, shallow vats filled with a thick slip of fat, river silt, and crushed freshwater shell. Here, the young are brought forth—small, rubbery, and remarkably flexible, their bones little more than dense cartilage. 

From their first days, they are coated in slip to protect their skin from the drying drafts. They learn to gauge the world not by image, but by resistance: the firmness of mud, the heat of a draft-hole, the sound of a timber groaning under load. They do not learn to speak; their communication is a grammar of percussive taps delivered with stone mauls against the floors, an ongoing, rhythmic telegraphy that coordinates the movement of counterweights and the delivery of fresh clay across miles of darkened basin.

They do not bury their dead, nor do they burn them entirely. A Setter whose joints have calcified to immobility, or whose flattened heels have worn down to the bone, is brought to the mortar-troughs. Their flesh is rendered down in the lower kiln-pots to yield the tallow that binds the deeper courses; their bones are crushed with stone pestles into temper to strengthen the wet mud; and their leathered palm-skin is cut away to serve as backing for the wooden trowels used by the next generation. 

Nothing in their existence is wasteful, because nothing here is separate from the work.

### The Front

At the margins of the world, out where the draft is wild and ice-cold, the work reaches its true ferocity. 

Here, in the absolute black, the Setters labor in continuous shifts, driven by an innate, mechanical panic. They work in chains: one group haul heavy, sloshing baskets of raw, heavy clay dragged up from deep, hidden fissures; another kneels in the slop, beating the air bubbles out with heavy slate paddles (*slap-clack, slap-clack*); a third group, the Stampers, step forward with heavy, rhythmic lunges into the wet floor-blocks, dropping their full weight onto their heels, then leaning down to press their palms into the center, sealing the maker's mark into the earth before the tallow-grout has time to skin over.

Behind them, the draft-tenders are already lashing the next rank of saplings, boring the vent-holes, and lighting the peat cakes. 

They do not know what lies at the end of the basin. They do not know if there is an end. If you stood before them, they would not attack you, nor would they welcome you; they would simply brush against you with rough, lime-dusted shoulders, reach past you with long, spatulate hands, and smooth a lump of wet clay over the spot where your feet had just been standing.
