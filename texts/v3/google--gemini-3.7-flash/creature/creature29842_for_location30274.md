At the lowest functional register of the city are not individuals, but the *crust-weavers*: trillions of thumb-nail-sized, calcifying hemichordates whose soft bodies never leave their self-secreted chambers. They are the masonry itself. Eyeless, jawless, and lacking any centralized brain, they respond only to the twin stimuli of sulfur-bearing heat and current velocity. When a spire narrows and the flow of mineral-rich water accelerates past a flank, the weavers on that side are triggered by fluid shear to exude a viscous, calcium-saturated mucous that hardens upon contact with seawater. Where the current dies, they starve and withdraw, allowing the passage to scour wider. They do not know they are carving boulevards or trueing the walls of flues; they are simply feeding, their calcified husks remaining behind long after they die, fused by the pressure and nacreous drift into an unyielding, seamless limestone marrow.

Living across this substrate are the grazers, the *felt-gleaners*. These are broad, flattened arthropods the size of carriage wheels, their carapaces coated in a thick, sulfurous fuzz of their own. They move on dozens of articulation-free, soft-tipped stylets that do not chip the stone, creeping across the velvet biofilms with the slow, deliberate glide of tectonic plates. Their mouthparts are quadruple rasps of hardened magnetite, scraping the sulfur-eating mats down to the raw white nacre. As they graze, they leave intricate, spiraling negative-spaces in the slime—chemical signatures that other gleaners read with long, feathered antennae. These trails serve as the city’s ledgers, warning of exhausted pastures, localized gas-vent choke-offs, or structural fractures leaking cold, unbreathable abyss-water into the lower galleries.

In the mid-level aqueducts and the high, hollow colonnades dwell the *thrall-worms*—the pale, ribbon-like entities whose continuous undulation provides the city’s secondary circulation. Pale as curdled milk, some stretching forty feet from blunt, sensory-pitted snouts to tapered, finned tails, they anchor their midsections to the interior grooves of the flutes with rows of fleshy, vacuum-pad claspers. They do not travel so much as they pump. By rippling their muscular lateral fringes in synchronized waves, they drive millions of gallons of nutrient-dense brine upward through the vertical avenues into the high nursery chambers. 

The thrall-worms are deaf and blind, but their translucent hides are mapped with dense clusters of electro-receptors and barometric pits. They feel the approach of an obstruction miles away as a faint back-pressure against their skin. When one worm shifts its rhythm to compensate for a blocked conduit, the change cascades down the entire colonnade, a hundred ribboned bodies altering their stroke in unison, sighing against the slick stone walls with a wet, rhythmic rustle that can be felt in the floorboards of the tenements above.

Deeper within the labyrinthine tenement clusters, where the water is thickest with iron and warm as fresh blood, reside the *pallid sessiles*. These are colonial siphon-dwellers, half-mollusk, half-tunicate, occupying the petrified honeycomb cells like tenants in an eternal lodging. They possess long, muscular cloacae that reach out into the communal hallways, sifting the water for the fecal snow of the higher spires and the shed cuticles of the gleaners. 

The sessiles have developed an intricate somatic communication: they strike their heavy, enamel-capped siphon rims against the stone cell mouths. A single sharp *tock* resonates through the porous architecture, picked up by the resonance of neighbor chambers, traveling for miles through the structural skeleton of the city. To a listener pressed against the nacre, the city speaks in a perpetual, polyrhythmic clatter—a decentralized telegraph reporting thermal spikes, the calving of exterior spires into the deep silt, or the downward drift of a carcass from the pelagic realm above.

Above the spires, drifting in the turbulent thermal plumes, are the floating classes. Enormous, colonial siphonophores—some stretching like luminescent, beaded ribbons over a hundred feet into the darkness—act as living, drifting ceilings over the open plazas. They are nearly completely translucent, save for the dark, oil-filled nodules they use to regulate their buoyancy in the upwelling heat. Beneath them drift the *pteropod swarms*: winged snails with gossamer shells as thin as onion skin, beating their parapodia in frantic, continuous prayer to remain suspended in the warm chimney-drafts. They feed on the bacterial plumes, and in doing so, their dense clouds cast flickering, shifting acoustic shadows that the hunters below use to triangulate their movements.

And in the shadows of the lintels, tucked into the dry-dock recesses where the water stagnates, wait the ambushers—the *gorgons of the cornices*.

These are heavy, radially symmetrical predators, resembling an unholy convergence of an octopus and a deep-sea ray, their bodies composed of dark, dense muscle with no internal skeleton. They wedge themselves into the masonry using powerful, backward-facing hooks along their arms, letting their eight main appendages trail out into the thoroughfare like decorative pelmets. These limbs are covered in hyper-sensitive tactile hairs that drift lazily in the passing current, registering the microscopic eddies left by passing thrall-worms or careless scavengers.

When an eddy breaks against their hairs with the right frequency, the strike is instantaneous—a silent, hydraulic eruption of arms that drags the prey backward into the dark recess. The gorgon’s beak does not crush with sound; it dissolves with concentrated acid secreted from salivary glands, breaking down flesh and shell alike into a rich, mineral paste that is absorbed slowly over weeks. The empty, acid-pitted shells are then pushed out of the niche to fall into the street below, where the gleaners and crust-weavers quickly incorporate them into the paving, cementing the victim's remains into the ever-thickening floor of the avenue.

Nothing is imported; nothing is discarded. Even the dead are civic materials. When an old thrall-worm finally exhausts its muscles and unclasps from its conduit, the current carries its soft, heavy body down into the deepest, unventilated foundations—the *sumps*. 

There, in the sulfurous mire where the water boils straight from the earth's basalt mantle, wait the *carrion-anemones*. Broad as threshing floors and black as pitch, they engulf the dead in undulating folds of caustic gut-tissue, stripping the flesh to fuel their own slow, vegetative division, while their foot-discs excrete a thick, stony paste that reinforces the city's sinking foundations against the crushing weight of the ocean above.

In this place, identity is dissolved into architecture. To live here is not merely to inhabit the city; it is to be an organ, a valve, a filter, or a grain of mortar within a single, continuous, dreaming anatomy that has never seen the sun.
