To walk among the square-milled trunks is to be watched not by eyes, but by thousands of calibrated clearances. 

Nothing here breathes; there is no moist expansion of lungs, no rhythmic intake of chilled air to fog a cold plate. The things that inhabit this floor do not possess meat. They are entities of dry tolerance and joinery, born of the same laminated grain and cold-drawn wire that forms the undergrowth, differing from the forest only in their freedom from the fastener bolts.

### The Foliar Gleaners and Punch-Tape Fauna

In the mid-tiers of the canopy, where the paper-thin cellulose leaves click and shuffle in the drafts, live the smallest of the moving forms. These are the *shear-wasps* and *planers*—flat, multi-segmented organisms no thicker than a playing card, composed of thin slices of amber-colored mica bound with shellac. They slide between the leaves with the frictionless glide of well-oiled feeler gauges, their edges so keen they do not disturb the static balance of the branch they traverse.

They do not feed on organic tissue. Instead, they graze upon the dry electrostatic crust that blooms along the edges of the silver foil fronds, scraping it away with jaws shaped like hardened carbide scrapers. In doing so, they clear the pathways for the leaf’s direct-conversion lattices. When startled, they do not fly; they release their grip and plane downward through the dense air in dead-straight, diagonal trajectories, vanishing into the sub-floor intake grates with the soft, metallic *snick* of coins dropping into a slot.

Farther down, clinging to the vertical boxwood trunks, are the *indexers*. Shaped like articulated wooden vernier calipers, they spend days perfectly motionless, their pegged feet locked into the concentric growth-rings of the square trees. Inside their hollow, boxwood carapaces, a tiny brass hairspring—wound tight during some forgotten seismic compression of the floor—uncoils against a deadbeat escapement. A faint, dry *tick... tick... tick* emanates from them. They measure the tree’s microscopic expansion against the cold iron of the floor vents. Should a trunk tilt by even a fraction of a degree, an indexer will drive a dry hornbeam wedge from its own abdomen into the base-seam, shimming the forest into plumb before freezing into stillness once more.

### The Browsers of the Seams

The mantis-forms mentioned in passing are only the most visible of a broad caste of large, dry browsers: the *striders* and *cam-hounds*. 

A strider stands eight feet tall at the shoulder, though it weighs remarkably little. Its limbs are long, lathe-turned dowels of lignum vitae, cross-braced with fine piano wire that hums at a low, glassy pitch as it steps across the zinc floor. Its head is a solid cube of bleached maple, housing no brain, but rather an intricate, tumbling array of polished quartz prisms that focus the ceiling’s cold glare down into a bundle of synthetic fiber-optic cables running along its spine.

These creatures follow the conduits. Where two copper-bramble thickets cross an ungrounded busbar, an arc of cold, blue electricity will occasionally jump the gap, leaving behind a crust of fused silica and oxidized lead. The strider approaches these nodes with halting, stop-motion steps. Its mandibles are hardened steel punches that shear off the bead of cold solder with a sharp *clack*. 

Inside the creature’s chest, there is no stomach. There is only a heavy, balanced flywheel of compressed graphite, suspended in vacuum-sealed mica bushings. The consumed metal is dropped into the housing, where it is worn down by dry friction against the spinning stone, generating a localized electrostatic field that recharges the coiled bimetallic ribbons that serve as the creature’s muscles. 

They have no sexes, nor do they mate. When a strider’s graphite flywheel finally wears its journals smooth and wobbles to a halt, the creature simply locks its joints and becomes a dead trellis. In time, the surrounding brambles recognize the stationary iron and wood, sending runners through its ribcage, sleeving their copper tendrils around its limbs, and turning the carcass into a permanent junction tower.

### The Sub-Floor Threaders

Beneath the boiler-plate crust, in the miles of pressurized pneumatic ductwork through which the dielectric fluid is pumped, lives something continuous and undulating: the *gasket-serpents*.

These are long, flexible ribbons of woven asbestos cloth and spring-steel shim stock, dozens of yards in length, that ride the currents of the clear, ether-thin fluid. They have no limbs and no sensory organs other than a continuous tactile awareness of the pipe walls. They move with an erratic, whipping motion, propelled by the systematic thrum of the giant bamboo pistons.

The gasket-serpents serve as the scavengers of the hydraulic system. As the dielectric fluid circulates copper salts and fine silicates through the millwork of the world, tiny particles fall out of suspension, threatening to foul the tight clearances of the submerged valves. The serpents collect these particulates on their textured bellies, compacting the dust with their own body-friction until it forms flat, hard wafers of compressed talc and copper-leaf. 

Periodically, a hatch will cycle open on the forest floor, and the head of a serpent will emerge—a blank, spade-shaped plate of rolled zinc. It will deposit a stack of these dry, circular wafers neatly onto the boiler plate, like a bank teller setting down a column of tokens, before sinking back down into the rushing fluid. 

### The Planar Intelligences: The Draftsmen

Deep within the groves of thirty-foot honeycomb baffles, where the optical depth of the forest folds and miles collapse into inches, there are entities that cannot be called fauna, yet possess a cold, methodical volition. The floor-dwellers call them, if they could name them at all, the *Draftsmen*.

A Draftsman appears as an upright pillar of stacked, laminated veneers—perhaps ten feet high and three feet square—standing upright without legs, gliding across the seamless zinc by means of hundreds of tiny, flush-set ball bearings embedded in its lower plinth. Its surface is carved with microscopic, labyrinthine grooves that whistle faintly as ambient air is drawn into its core by internal convection.

The Draftsmen do not browse, nor do they maintain the mechanical trees. Their concern is the geometry of the shell itself. They are drawn to the perspective anomalies—the places where a dropped leaf crosses leagues of sightline, or where two parallel beams of structural iron appear to diverge and converge simultaneously.

When a Draftsman finds such a warp, it halts. Its body unfolds like an architect’s folding rule, spreading out into a series of rigid, interlocking wooden frames that span the distorted aisle. From within its joints, it extends slender dry calipers tipped with lead points, scribing razor-thin alignment marks directly into the steel plates of the floor.

It will remain unfolded for weeks, entirely rigid, acting as a structural jig. It forces the warped sightlines back into parallel by the sheer, unyielding rigidity of its seasoned timber. Once the perspective has settled—once the optical horizon has snapped back into flat, cold trueness—the Draftsman folds itself back into a single, silent pillar, its joints clicking shut like the lid of a fine instrument case, and rolls smoothly away into the glare of the phosphor ceiling.

### The Absolute Silence of Thought

There is no memory in this place, at least not in the sense of history. There are only the punch-cards: long, continuous scrolls of perforated parchment that are continuously extruded by the roots of the largest boxwood towers, passed through the jaws of the striders, and gathered by the undergrowth to be drawn down into the sub-floor channels. 

These cards carry no language. They record only the mechanical tolerances of the vast floor—the ambient voltage, the tension on the canopy’s counterweights, the micro-inch wear on the zinc plates. 

Whatever lives here is not an inhabitant of the machine; it is the machine’s own wear-and-tear given form and agency, an ecology of dry shims, self-lubricating graphite, and spring-steel linkages, perpetually repairing a structure that was never built, under a ceiling that will never dim, upon a floor that will never know the weight of mud.
