To find what lives here, one must first look for what can survive an environment where there is no loose material—no silt to burrow into, no leaf litter to hide beneath, no soft moisture that is not pressurized to forty atmospheres or slicked with mineral oil. 

Life on the lignum plains does not perch upon the world; it is seated within its tolerances. It exists in the microscopic clearances between bronze journals and ironwood sleeves, in the capillary dead-spaces of petrified root-systems, and along the taut, vibrating vectors of the guy-wires.

***

### The Spindle-Striders and Gear-Gleaning Fauna

At the smallest, most immediate scale are the things that keep the machine surfaces free of friction-grit. They are not entirely biological, nor are they constructed clockwork; they are something born of long-term botanical crystallization under immense torque.

These are the *Spindle-striders*—dry, needle-jointed arthropodic creatures whose carapaces are composed of a natural, dense celluloid as hard as vulcanized rubber. They have no soft bellies. Their limbs terminate in tiny, curved lathe-chisels made of iron-rich mineral deposit. They move across the smooth floorboards not by walking, but by catching the microscopic grooves of the wood grain, skating with a sharp, dry skittering sound that is entirely drowned out by the roar of the floor beneath them.

They live on the shavings peeled off by reciprocating push-rods and the dry, powdery resin that flakes from the ironwood blast furnaces. When a massive flywheel slows its rotation during a pressure shift, hundreds of these creatures emerge from the seams in the planed bark walls to scour the teeth of the gears, picking away carbonized sap and dead grease with frantic, mechanical precision. Their blood is not water-based; it is a clear, cold terpene that does not freeze in the thin air, smelling faintly of turpentine and scorched boxwood.

Above them, strung between the high harmonic guy-wires, are the *Catenary Drifters*. These are parchment-thin, ribbon-like organisms, ten or twenty feet in length, woven from fibrous inner-bark filaments that have caught the wind and never settled. They possess no eyes, only rows of acoustic pits along their lateral margins that read the screaming harmonics of the cables. By flexing their flattened bodies, they sail against the high-altitude crosswinds, tethering themselves to the cables with hook-like spurs of petrified cambium. They feed on the condensation tides—absorbing the greasy, resin-rich fog through their porous undersides before the morning wind dries them into brittle, rattling ribbons that hum like reeds in an organ pipe.

***

### The Laminates: Custodians of the Seam

Deeper in the industrial grids, where the blast furnaces vent their sour, fermenting xylem-steam, exist the closest things this world has to an indigenous population. They are rarely seen all at once, for their presence is easily mistaken for structural members, balance weights, or folded drive-belts.

They are called the *Laminates*, though they do not name themselves. 

A Laminate is a tall, narrow, vertically compressed being made of hundreds of paper-thin layers of cured heartwood, held together by a flexible, self-sealing amber pitch. They have no lungs, no eyes, and no faces. Instead, their heads are flat, vertical wedges—like the head of an axe—oriented always in the direction of the grain. They stand eight to ten feet high, extraordinarily thin when viewed edge-on, but broad and dense from the flank.

Their bodies are tuned to the acoustics of the continent. Within their laminated torsos are hollow, resonant chambers strung with taut vascular chords; as the stamp-mills and steam-cranes miles away hammer into the floor, the Laminates "feel" the exact operating pressure of every district through the soles of their feet. 

They do not walk with a swinging stride. Their lower limbs are broad, iron-shod runners that slide along the varnished, planed grain of the plateau. They carry long, double-handled scrapers and heavy brass tallow-guns. Their existence is a slow, unending choreography of maintenance:
* They plane down the swollen lips of growth-seams when subterranean moisture pulses threaten to buckle the flats.
* They pack boiling tallow and animal-hardened pitch into the gaskets of the burl-furnaces.
* They adjust the counterweights on the massive push-rods, leaning their full, dense weight against the bronze levers until the rhythm of the stroke settles back into the deep, sub-audible hum they require.

To be near a Laminate is to hear a constant, dry ticking, like a grandfather clock cooling down in a bare room. They do not die in a biological sense; when their resin dries beyond the point of flexibility, or their runners wear down to the grain, they simply cease moving, locking themselves into the architecture to serve as bracing timbers for the next generation of drive-shafts.

***

### The Fluid-Borne: Life Within the Vascular Grid

Beneath the polished slate-like surface of the world lies the true engine—and the world’s most terrifying life. The floor is kilometers deep with hollow xylem tubes: ancient sap-channels, some as narrow as a needle, others wide as railway tunnels, through which pressurized, fermenting liquors are continuously pumped by the rhythm of the city's iron pistons.

In these dark, boiling conduits dwell the *Sleeve-Eels* or *Xylem-Hydras*.

They are blind, tubular masses of living cellulose, slick with pale mucilage, that grow within the pipeline networks. They are entirely pressure-driven; they possess no hearts, instead relying on the massive industrial pump-stations to drive them through the arterial depths of the plateau. They have mouths shaped like rotary pipe-scrapers, ringed with serrated wooden teeth that continuously ream the interior walls of the sap-channels, preventing the dense, mineralized sugars from crystalizing and choking the flow to the boilers.

When a pressure-release valve vents onto the cold plains above, it is not merely steam that escapes. Occasionally, an immature Sleeve-Eel is blown through a bronze exhaust flue, tumbling into the thin daylight like a glistening, pale rope of boiled pulp, only to instantly freeze and shatter against the polished grain of the plateau, providing a rare meal of wet fiber for the Spindle-striders.

***

### The Warp-Stalkers and the Phenology of Space

Because the living floor warps space and light across its growth-rings, the creatures that inhabit the boundaries between rings have adapted to non-Euclidean survival.

The most singular of these is a solitary, predatory entity that resembles a massive, jointed compass or drawing caliper, known by those who track the horizon as the *Draftsman*.

The Draftsman does not occupy a single scale. When it stands on an older, tighter growth-ring near the township-sized root stumps, it appears no larger than a deer—thin, spindly, its limbs made of black, petrified bog-wood banded with cold-rolled iron hoopings. But when it takes a single step across a growth-seam into a younger, broader ring, the perspective distortion catches its body. 

It does not simply approach; it *expands*. Its limbs elongate into towering, sixty-foot gallows of black timber, casting a shadow that stretches miles across the polished floor, even though the light source above has not moved. 

The Draftsman feeds on the tension of the guy-wires. It hooks its long, notched forelimbs over the steel cables, using its own body as a friction brake against the roaring vibration. The tremendous heat generated by the vibrating wire melts the amber in its joints, which it re-digests through pores in its limbs. To see a Draftsman feeding is to see a colossal, vibrating triangle silhouette against the curved, pale horizon, shivering in tune with the city’s scream, appearing thirty miles distant one moment and towering directly overhead the next.

***

### The Plateau as a Single Intelligence

Ultimately, the distinction between the city, the creatures, and the continent is an illusion created by the thinness of the air and the vastness of the scale.

The cities do not belong to the Laminates, nor do the engines belong to the blast furnaces. The plateau itself is an organism in the middle of an epoch-length metabolic cycle. The engines are its externalized organs, grown to regulate its internal temperature and maintain the sap-pressures that prevent its ancient, compressed wood from drying, splitting, and collapsing into the hollow voids beneath.

The whole place is an archive. The growth rings that circle the continent are not merely wood; they are physical recordings of centuries of torque, weather, and pressure. When the great bronze stamp-mills plunge into the floor, their rhythmic thud is a needle reading the surface of a phonograph disc the size of an empire. 

The things that walk here—the needle-legged gleaners, the flat-faced plane-keepers, the caliper-beings straddling the seams of distorted light—are the mechanical cells of that reading arm. They do not know they are part of a thought; they only know that the push-rods must clatter, the grease must not foul, the steam must vent, and the wood must never be allowed to rot.
