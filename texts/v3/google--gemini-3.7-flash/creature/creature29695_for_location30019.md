To endure here, life must renounce the luxury of soft flesh and the illusion of buoyancy. In this chamber, buoyancy is an executioner; anything that drifts is instantly hurled upward into the sunlit mouth of the fissure and shattered against the cloud-level spillways. Survival is strictly a matter of purchase—of friction, anchor, and tensile strength.

The pale, armor-plated finger-things that graze the granite are *split-fleas*, though they share nothing with true insects save for their jointed rigidity. Their bodies are flattened plates of translucent calcite, layered like mica, almost as hard as the quartz they walk upon. They have no eyes; in a world illuminated by blinding, razor-cut shafts of sun, light is useless to them—they see through the vibrations in their limbs, reading the acoustic stress of the rock. Their legs end in curved, needle-sharp hooks made of pure bio-mineralized iron, which they drive directly into the microscopic fissures left by the hydraulic fracturing. When a split-flea feeds, it does not browse; it rasps the cold, ozonated mineral skin from the stone with a radula like an emery wheel, vibrating its mouthparts at the exact frequency of the rock’s own fracture planes to shake loose the silicate flour. When the torrent surges—when the deep vents below heave with a fresh pulse of sub-glacial steam—they clamp their laminar shells flat against the wall, hydrodynamically neutral, presenting only a zero-drag edge to the tearing water. If a leg snaps, they do not bleed; a thick, crystalline resin instantly plugs the fracture, setting in the freezing current faster than ice.

Deeper in the vertical rifts, where the stone is too smooth even for iron hooks, live the *tallow-threads*. They are not true algae, but colonies of chemotrophic colonial hydroids that feed on the ionized sulfur and crushed-rock gases bubbling up from the vent roots. They exist in a state of eternal tension: their anchors are biological rivets that bore chemically into the slate, while their long, white fibers—tough as boiled sinew—whip in the current with such violence that they hum. To touch a patch of them with a bare hand would be like touching the spinning blade of a carding wheel; the threads would strip the skin to the periosteum in a single beat.

And then there are the men who come down into the nave.

They call themselves the *Splitters*, or the *Cold-Bitten*. They are not mountain folk by birth, for the high valleys breed men who fear the deep waters, nor are they sailors, for the sea is too wide and soft compared to this stone cannon. They are a strange, insular breed of quarry-divers whose chests have grown barrel-thick and dense from working against the hydrostatic hammer. 

They do not dive naked, nor do they dive in wool, which the current would unravel in seconds. They dress in suits of heavy, hairless pig-leather, cured in train-oil and stitched with brass wire. Over their chests and groins they lace curved plates of split ash-wood to protect their internal organs from the concussive shock of the micro-detonations. Their ears are filled with beeswax mixed with spruce pitch and hammered in with wooden plugs; even so, every veteran Splitter is entirely deaf, their eardrums long since turned to scarred gristle by the hydraulic thunder. They communicate along the stays through hard, rhythmic jerks of the tallow-cordage, a language of heavy tugs that travels through the vibration of their knuckles.

A diver’s shift on the suspended pine platform is measured not by minutes, but by the burning in the lungs and the endurance of his grip. They descend in pairs, strapped to descending-stones—river-boulders wrapped in iron bands—that drag them down against the violent upward shear of the vent. 

Once on the platform, their work is brutal and precise. They carry heavy bronze-headed mallets and short, wedge-shaped chisels of cold-forged alloy, tethered to their wrists by braided horsehair. They do not fight the current; they let it pin them to the logs of the scaffold. A diver bites down on a shaped block of boxwood held between his back teeth to keep the pressure from forcing his jaw open and tearing his cheeks, his eyes shielded behind thick, green-tinted spectacles of poured glass sealed to his face with mutton tallow.

They seek the *sky-spar*—the virgin quartz and selenite plates growing in the newly cracked seams of the western wall. In these high, pressure-cooked rifts, the crystals form with unnatural speed, fed by the mineral-saturated torrent, growing so flawless and clear that they are entirely invisible when submerged. The Splitters find them by feel, running calloused, gloved thumbs along the razor-sharp fracture lines until they catch the edge of a plate.

The harvesting is a desperate race against the mountain itself. If left too long, the current's cavitation will pit the crystal face, or a sudden thermal fracture will shatter it into worthless sand. When a Splitter finds a clean leaf of spar, he hammers his copper wedges behind it with methodical, dead-weight strikes. The bronze mallet makes a high, dull *clink* that can be felt in the divers' teeth above the roar of the water.

When the plate breaks free—often a sheet of crystal three feet across and scarcely thicker than a fingernail—the diver must catch it against his ash-plated chest before the upward torrent can seize it and spin it into a guillotine. Holding the razor-edged glass tight to his ribs, he pulls the release on his counterweight. 

The upward current, which strives endlessly to empty the mountain into the sky, does the rest. It catches the diver like a leaf in a chimney, rocketing him toward the sun-drenched overhead slit in a blind, foaming rush of pale light and freezing ozone, his boots skimming the freshly cleaved granite by inches, praying that the tallow-tethers hold and the copper wedges driven into the ribs of the world do not pull free behind him.
