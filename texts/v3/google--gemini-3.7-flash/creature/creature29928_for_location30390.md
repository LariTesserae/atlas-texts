To understand what lives in the sun-well, one must understand that life here does not struggle against the light; it has surrendered to it, trading color, speed, and hunger for permanence.

The disc-insects that ride the thermals are neither flies nor moths, though they share the dry fragility of both. Up close, their bodies possess no wet tissue, no soft belly or dark fluid. Their shells are sheer wafers of laminated chitin, translucent as split mica, so thin that the excessive sun passes straight through them, casting no shadow on the floor below. They do not feed in any predatory sense. Their mouthparts have fused into microscopic sieves that strain the rising convection currents for fine, wind-borne chalk dust and the invisible spores shed by the tendon-succulents. They mate without descending: two discs brush together in the updraft, adhere briefly along a serrated margin like two dry leaves caught in an eddy, and drift apart. When their cycle ends, they do not rot. Their hollow frames lose their buoyancy, wobble out of the air column, and drift down to the floor, where they shatter upon the stone into a powdery, glittering talc that enriches the silt.

On the ledges, the blind lizards—the milk-skinks—are the masters of stillness. Their bodies are flattened as if ironed against the rock, barely thicker than a finger, their ribs spread wide to maximize contact with the heated limestone. Their skin is not made of traditional scales, but of a pebbled, chalk-like integument that sweats a microscopic wax, preventing what little moisture they possess from boiling away into the dry air. 

If one watches a skink for hours, its life resolves into infinitesimal increments. It does not possess eyelids; a clear, calcified scale covers the vestigial eye-sockets, giving its face the smooth, uncarved look of an unfinished marble figurine. They do not hunt the disc-insects while the insects are aloft. Instead, their sustenance is drawn through the belly. The skin along their undercarriages is porous and vascular, acting like a dry sponge. At dawn, when the dew point briefly touches the stone before the sun enters the aperture, the skinks press themselves into the microscopic slick of mineral condensation, drinking through their ribs. Only in the late afternoon, when an exhausted insect drifts down within reach, will a skink casually tilt its pale jaw and take the brittle wafer into its mouth, crushing it with a sound like stepping on a dry eggshell.

Inside the shallow stone cups on the floor—the ones drilled by the thirty-foot drop of water—lives the hollow’s most secret inhabitant. 

Each thimble of mineral-heavy water contains a single pair of minute, blind water-fleas, no larger than a grain of coarse salt. They are entirely clear, lacking even the black eye-spot of their surface relatives. They spend their lives in perpetual suspension within that single ounce of water, swimming in gentle, looped orbits through the mineral broth. When the heavy drop falls from the roof—a catastrophe that would destroy larger creatures—its impact merely displaces them into the vortex. They ride the shockwave down to the smooth basin wall and rise again with the rebound, feeding on the film of blue-green lithotrophic bacteria that coats the submerged limestone. Because the cups never dry and never overflow, generations of these fleas live and die within a bowl the size of an acorn, their world bounded entirely by the circular tension of the surface film and the rhythmic percussion of the falling drop.

Even the creeping tendon-succulents behave like animals in slow retreat. They do not turn green; their skin is a tough, pearlescent ivory, shielding their tissues from ultraviolet burn with high concentrations of chalky carbonates absorbed from the floor. Underneath the flat straps of their stems, thousands of microscopic root-hairs secrete a mild acid that slowly pits the limestone, knitting the plant so firmly to the substrate that it cannot be peeled away without breaking the rock itself. Where a stem dies, it leaves behind a pale, raised scar—a petrified relief of its passage that will outlast the hollow itself.

High up in the shadows beneath the northern lip, where the stone is perpetually cold and damp, the grey moss harbors small, wingless mites that move with the sluggish drift of clockwork. They are the only creatures in the well that know darkness. They graze on the fungal hyphae that web through the damp fissures, never venturing down into the glare, existing in an inverted world of damp limestone and dripping condensation.

Long ago, when people used the river-cobble footholds to climb down into the well, they were not hunting. The bone awls left by the grinding block were not weapons; their tips are blunted and polished from lifting the living sheets of tendon-succulent from the floor, likely to extract the thick, clear gel that fills their segments—a balm that smells of wet stone and protects skin against the cruel scrub-plain sun above. The people came down the ramp, ground their minerals in the shade, collected the slow water from the dripping wall, and climbed back into the world.

Now, without them, the community of the well remains complete. It is an ecology balanced on a needle: sustained by the heat of an open sky, fed by the dust of its own walls, and metered out by the single, clear drops that fall, one by one, through the warm, empty light.
