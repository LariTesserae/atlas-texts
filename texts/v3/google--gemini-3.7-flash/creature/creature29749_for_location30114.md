To understand what lives here, one must understand that in the Flue-Well, life does not occur as an interruption to the iron. It is an extrusion of it—a slow, wet calcification that has had centuries to learn the geometry of flanges and the precise thermodynamic rhythm of the pipes.

In the upper reaches, where the heat is driest and the lead cables sag in heavy catenaries, the inhabitants are nearly weightless. Suspended between the loops of cable hang the *line-weavers*—not true arachnids, though they share their deliberate, eight-jointed economy of motion. They are pale as lard, their bodies no larger than a thimble, but their legs are impossibly long, fine as drawn copper wire and tipped with hardened, hook-like tarsi that click faintly against the lead. They do not spin webs to catch prey, for there is nothing in the dry air to fly into them. Instead, they string horizontal balance-lines between the hottest steam-risers and the coldest dead bulkheads, anchoring themselves across the thermal gradient. Their bodies act as living peltier junctions, drawing a microscopic, metabolic charge from the difference in temperature between their left legs and their right. When they move, it is with a dry, stuttering gait, feeling the tension of the cables for the faint harmonic shudder that precedes a steam-drop falling forty feet below.

Lower down, where the rotted hemp lagging has slumped into wet, fibrous collars around the pipe elbows, the community becomes dense, soft, and subterranean. 

Inside the mulch of the decaying insulation dwell the *tallow-grubs*. They are segmented, blind, and as thick as a man’s thumb, their translucent skins revealing a digestive tract blackened by pulverized magnetite and ancient petroleum jelly. Over uncounted generations, their gut flora has evolved to crack the long-chain hydrocarbons of the antique tallow lubricants that still impregnate the joints. They do not bite; their mouthparts are soft, rasping discs that slowly buff the rust until it shines like pewter, leaving behind them a trail of oily, sterile slime that prevents the metal beneath from corroding any further. Where the grubs congregate, the pipework remains pristine, preserved in a coat of living grease.

Following the grubs through the lagging are the *shear-spiders*—flattened, broad-bodied hunters that have abandoned venom entirely. Their front appendages have fused into heavy, horn-tipped pincer blades shaped precisely like pipe-wrenches. They move laterally through the narrow space between conduit and iron wall, pressing their flat carapaces against the warm steel to stay heated. They hunt by listening through their bellies. When a tallow-grub rasps too vigorously against a valve seat, the shear-spider slides forward, slips its pincer under the grub’s soft flank, and neatly snips it against the curve of the pipe. They waste nothing; the blood of these grubs is thick, pale, and smells of engine oil and tallow, and it is drunk directly from the cut before it can evaporate into the dry air.

At the floor of the well, where the condensation gathers in the drowned accumulator pans, the ecology changes from the fibrous and dry to the slurring and amphibious.

In the black, iron-saturated pools, beneath the slow, rhythmic *tock* of the forty-foot drip, sits the *sludge-lung*. It is difficult to distinguish from a discarded mass of vulcanized gasket rubber. It lies flattened in the corners of the sills, four or five feet across, anchored to the diamond grating by hundreds of tiny, muscular pedicels that grip the perforations. It has no eyes, no face, and no limbs. Its upper surface is a thick, leathery mantle encrusted with orange iron-crust and sulfur crystals; its underside is a pale, pulsing lung that slowly pumps the hot, oxygen-poor water through a labyrinth of internal gills. 

The sludge-lung feeds on the constant, gentle rain of organic debris: the shed carapaces of the isopods, the mold spores falling from the upper risers, and the mineral flakes that detach from the pipework. It is the slowest engine in the well. Its heart beats perhaps once every ten minutes, sending a dull, wet hydraulic thud through the water of the pan. A mature sludge-lung may not move from its chosen plate for forty years, gradually incorporating the iron silt into its own flesh until its margins become as rigid and brittle as cast iron itself.

Between these sedentary layers moves the largest creature the Flue-Well sustains: a heavy, blind wanderer known only through the changes it works upon the dark.

It has no native name, but its shape is dictated entirely by the gaps between the conduits. It is long-bodied and low-slung, roughly the mass of a large hound, but flattened horizontally to squeeze through gaps no wider than a man’s hand. Its skin is thick, hairless, and deeply wrinkled, slate-grey and perpetually slick with condensation and pipe-grease. It moves upon six splayed limbs that end not in claws, but in broad, callous-padded clamps that can span the curve of a four-inch high-pressure pipe without slipping.

This creature possesses a sensory apparatus entirely foreign to the world above. Where its eyes should be, its skull broadens into a wide, bony spade covered in a honeycomb of pit-organs that read the thermal topography of the room. To this creature, the dark is not black; it is a complex, luminous landscape of infrared intensities. The superheated steam-risers are blinding, white-hot pillars; the dead, cold bulkheads are vast, sheltering expanses of deep blue; the dripping condensation lines are bright, falling sparks.

It traverses the tiers with an agonized, hydraulic patience. It climbs the vertical riser-forest not by leaping or scurrying, but by wedging its back against one pipe and its feet against another, levering its heavy bulk upward inch by inch. Its ribs are hinged to collapse inward, allowing it to worm its way into the narrow clearances behind boiler plates where the heat is most suffocating and the isopod colonies are thickest.

It feeds by grazing. It presses its wide, soft snout against the magnetite crust, hoovering up the isopods, the fibrous mold, the chemotrophic lichens, and the soft-bodied grubs with a wet, vacuuming sound. Its stomach is a grinding mill of swallowed iron scale and heavy gravel; it digests the bacteria and the tallow, excreting a dense, mineral-rich paste that sets in the air like mortar, slowly filling in the structural cracks of the lower foundations.

When it rests, it curls around the warmest junctions, hooking its limbs over the cross-braces, its breath coming as a low, wet wheeze that blends indistinguishably into the sound of escaping steam. Its body temperature is so close to that of the iron it embraces that if one were to reach out in the absolute blackness, one would not know where the pipe ended and the animal began—until the warm, oil-slick hide gave way beneath the hand with a slow, heavy pulse, shifting slightly to accommodate the cooling of the metal, settled deep into the long, dark, mechanical afternoon.
