To survive here, flesh must understand torque, shear, and turgor. Nothing with rigid, brittle bone lasts long; the landscape treats anything that cannot bend or completely flatten as grit to be ground into the sawdust drifts. The things that live in this high, cold foundry are defined by elasticity, hydraulic patience, and a total absence of voice.

***

### The Gasket-Fauna
In the narrow tolerances between sliding sheets of bark—where thousands of tons of mineral-crusted fiber grind past each other on films of cold mucus—live the things that have abandoned thickness altogether. 

They are wider than a man’s outstretched arms but scarcely thicker than a wet leaf. Their bodies are composed of stacked, oil-saturated leather plates that overlap like roof shingles, slicked with an amber secretion that repels the freezing mist. They have no faces, only a margin of sensory bristles along their perimeter that taste the pH of the sap and measure the micro-vibrations of approaching grain-stress.

They are scavengers of the friction zones. As the plates heave, these creatures act as living shims, wedging themselves into the microscopic clearance gaps to lap up the slurry of pulverized cambium and bacterial foam crushed out by the movement. When a plate clamps shut with sudden, hydraulic violence, they do not crush; they simply extrude their internal fluids into specialized perimeter sacs, flatten to a fraction of a millimeter, and wait. The pressure does not kill them—it holds them fast, like a pressed flower in a heavy book, until the valve reopens an hour or a week later.

***

### The Tension-Stalkers
Above the flats, where the petrified vines arch across the chasms under immense, quivering loads, move the larger, spindle-limbed wanderers. 

They resemble gaunt, four-legged frames of dark horn, covered entirely in a dense, sound-swallowing pelt that looks like grey lichen. Their limbs are not articulated with ball-and-socket joints, but with fibrous, braided ligaments that can twist a full three hundred and sixty degrees without tearing. Their feet are split into wide, soft pads lined with thousands of microscopic, hooked burrs that latch instantly onto the velvet lining of the wood.

These creatures have no hearing organs—sound here is a dead medium, instantly smothered by the porous heartwood. Instead, they "hear" through the pads of their feet and long, whip-like whiskers that trail against the wood behind them. They can read the strain-gauge of a wooden girder through the soles of their limbs:
* A high-frequency, sub-audible hum means the vine is drying and about to snap;
* A slow, deep, fluid pulse means a sap-surge is rising through the hollow core, and the pylon will remain stable for hours.

They follow these stress lines with uncanny certainty, rarely stepping on wood that is within thirty minutes of a catastrophic fracture. They feed on the cold galls—cutting into the swollen, atomizing vents with heavy, chisel-shaped incisors of hardened resin, drinking the supercooled, sugary moisture before it can freeze into frost on the surface.

***

### The Well-Lurkers of the Reservoirs
In the high saddles and deep crooks of the branch-systems, where dark, enzyme-heavy water collects in terraced pools, life takes on a heavier, slower geometry.

Suspended in these cold, black baths are the bladders—creatures that look like swollen, ribbed water-skins, the color of wet walnut. They have no eyes and no limbs. They float half-submerged, anchored to the submerged wooden ribs by long, prehensile taproots that burrow directly into the living bark, siphoning trace minerals and the heavy starch-liquor that leaks from the vascular valves.

Around them, in the dark water, move small, blind swimmers resembling translucent, many-jointed centipedes. These swimmers graze on the thick carpet of yeast that grows along the bladder’s hide, while the bladder itself gently expands and contracts, acting as an auxiliary pump that keeps the reservoir from freezing solid. 

When the branch-basin suddenly buckles—as it inevitably will when the mountain reconfigures—the water spills down the defiles in a violent, silent torrent. The bladders simply detach, pull their soft tissue entirely inside their tough, fibrous rinds, and tumble down the wooden slopes like giant, shock-absorbing seed pods, rolling until they wedge into a new crevice, where they slowly put down fresh anchors.

***

### The Seamers
Deep in the labyrinth of dry flues and hollowed-out counterweight chambers, there are beings that look, from a distance, almost like men wrapped in moldering felt, though they are nothing of the kind.

They are bipedal only because the narrow, vertical seams of the wood demand that posture. Their skin is dry and grey, thick as shoe-sole leather, and their long, four-jointed fingers are tipped with broad, flat nails that can be driven into the grain like wedges. They are communal, living in small bands of five or six in the "dead pockets"—hollows within the heartwood where the growth rings have fossilized and ceased to move.

A Seamer’s entire life is a calculation of structural rhythm. They carry no tools of metal or bone, only tapered pegs of ironwood which they drive into widening stress-cracks to keep their temporary dwellings from collapsing during the hourly shifts. 

They communicate entirely through touch and physical impact, tapping rhythmic sequences onto the wood with their knuckles—messages that travel through the dense heartwood for miles, bypassed through the sound-deadening velvet by direct conduction.

They do not hunt; there is little to hunt. Instead, they are master harvesters of the system's waste:
* They scrape the dry, peat-like sawdust from the drifts to weave into their insulating cloaks;
* They collect the dense, tallow-like grease that weeps from the pistons to use as fuel for small, wickless stones that smolder without smoke;
* They harvest the dormant embryos inside the locomotive-sized seed pods, carving away cubic feet of bitter, nutrient-dense albumen before the pod is drawn into the subterranean churn.

The Seamers have no concept of permanence. A settlement lasts as long as a particular vascular cycle remains open—sometimes three days, sometimes three generations. When the heartwood beneath their feet begins to shudder with the unmistakable, rising tension of an impending cambium-upheaval, they do not panic. They pack their felt rolls, hammer free their ironwood pegs, and step out into the pale, glaring daylight, walking soundlessly across the shifting plates to find the next quiet pocket in the turning wood.
