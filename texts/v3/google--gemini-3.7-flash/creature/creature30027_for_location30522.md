The first thing to understand about the life here is that it does not experience the world as a picture. In a realm of warm, permanent blackness, existence is mapped in resonance, thermal gradients, and the tactile grain of rock.

In the mortarless seams between the megaliths—where the granite blocks were dressed so flat that only a finger’s width of space remains—live the *lithophones*. These are not simple frogs, though they share their damp skin and bulging throats. Their bodies are flattened like spade blades, wedges of dense muscle and pliable cartilage that slide twelve inches deep into the joints. Their ribcages are fused to their sternums in broad plates, allowing them to press their entire bellies against the basalt. When they call, they do not merely push sound into the air; they drive their vibrations directly into the structural mass of the wall. A single terrace wall, three hundred yards long and forty feet high, acts as a sounding board. A male pulsing his throat sac in a crevice on the east face is felt as a subtle, rhythmic trembling by females resting on the western corner three levels down. They feed on the blind, soft-shelled harvestmen that graze on the microscopic carpets of iron-eating bacteria filming the interior rock.

Above them, where the stonework overhangs in monumental corbels, the spider colonies have abandoned the spiral web entirely. Instead, they spin structural nets that mimic the masonry itself: heavy, cable-like stays of non-sticky, golden-brown silk anchored to the carved drip-edges of lintels, stretching across the thirty-foot drop to the terrace below. These webs do not snare by adhesion; they are trip-wires and acoustic trip-lines. The spiders—pale, long-legged, the size of a man’s outspread hand, with bodies covered in sensitive, plumose hairs—sit with their tarsal claws hooked into the stone gutters. When a heavy moth or a disoriented swift blunders into the vertical web-stays, the vibration hums down the silk directly into the rock. The spiders drop like plumb bobs on single draglines, striking from the dark air before swinging back into the safety of the friezes.

The mid-tier hunters of this vertical world are long-spined and rubber-jointed. There are civet-like creatures—the *gutter-cats*—whose ancestors must have climbed these walls when the stones were still bare. Over hundreds of generations in the warmth and wet, their eyes have turned to milky, useless pearls, but their muzzles are swollen with massive clusters of stiff, black vibrissae that brush the stone as they run. Their paws are broad and soft, equipped with retractile claws that can hook into microscopic tool-marks left by ancient chisels. They move along the narrow ledge-lips with an uncanny, fluid speed, never breaking stride even where a terrace corner drops five hundred feet into the cloud-filled void. They hunt by listening to the frogs within the seams, tilting their heads to locate the exact acoustic node of a call before thrusting an elongated, double-jointed foreleg deep into the crack to hook the caller out.

Where water pools, life becomes slow and ancient. In the thirty-foot-deep cisterns carved into the heart of the main plazas, the water never sees a ripple of wind; it only trembles from the falling condensation and the deep thrum of the frogs. In these subterranean vats live pale, eel-shaped lungfish that grow to the length of timber logs. They are nearly motionless, suspended between the floor silt and the stone ceiling, their gills filtering the micro-fauna that washes down the terrace gutters. They have developed external, branching sensory organs along their flanks that resemble pale fronds of fern; with these, they can feel the splash of a single beetle hitting the surface thirty yards away. 

Coexisting with them in the reservoirs are colonies of freshwater crabs whose carapaces have taken on the exact rough, granular texture of the dressed granite. These scavengers are the keepers of the water systems. They feed relentlessly on fallen foliage, bat guano, and carcasses, tearing dead organic matter into fine detritus that washes through the overflow sluices, preventing the monumental plumbing from silting shut.

And then there are the people.

They are not ghosts, nor are they a lost tribe in any romantic sense; they are simply the mammalian apex of this warm, vertical swamp. They have lived within the masonry so long that their bodies have reshaped around its dimensions.

They are small-statured, rarely standing more than four and a half feet high, with wide, barrel-shaped chests developed to draw the thin mountain air that filters through the cloud ceiling. Their skin is smooth, gray-tinted, and completely hairless, perpetually slick with condensation and an oily sebum that repels the constant damp. Their eyes are small and lidless, shielded by thick, translucent nictitating membranes; they do not use them to see, but only to register the rare, faint difference between the dense black of the cloud cover and the slightly paler black of the open abyss.

They do not use fire. In this pocket of warm stone, heat is an ambient certainty, and smoke in the enclosed arcades would be lethal. 

Instead, their culture is one of touch, acoustic mapping, and weight. They navigate the monumental stairs and the sheer drops with their feet—their toes are long, muscular, and capable of gripping the rounded edges of weathered steps with the dexterity of fingers. They wear broad belts of woven fig-bast from which hang carved bone rods. As they walk along the viaducts, they trail these rods against the carved relief of the walls, reading the patterns like a script. The facades are not merely decorative; they are tactile cartography. A band of deeply carved diamond-shapes tells them they are approaching a sheer cistern mouth; a series of shallow, horizontal grooves indicates a safe conduit ledge that can be followed for miles along the cliff face.

Their language is quiet, constructed almost entirely of percussive clicks, low throat-tones, and sharp, sibilant whistles that slip neatly between the deafening roar of the tree frogs. When two of them meet on a ledge so narrow that they cannot pass without touching, they do not speak; they press the flats of their hands to each other’s ribcages, feeling the rhythm of breathing and the tension of muscle, determining who will flatten themselves into a drainage niche to let the other swing past.

Their entire existence is bound to the maintenance of the stone. They are not masons—they do not quarry or cut new blocks—but they are obsessive caretakers of the drainage. They know, with an instinct passed down through uncounted lineages in the dark, that if the stone gutters clog with humus, the rain-weight will back up behind the retaining walls, the hydrostatic pressure will mount, and the terraces will slide into the abyss, taking their world with them.

Armed with heavy wooden scrapers and long, flexible cane plungers, small groups move endlessly through the viaducts. In complete darkness, working entirely by the feel of the water’s current against their calves, they clear the ancient stone grates, haul rotting mats of fig roots out of the corbelled sluices, and carry the accumulated silt to the terrace edges, casting it out into the warm, up-rushing wind.

They sleep communally in the dry interior chambers deep inside the platforms, wedged together in great, warm tangles of limbs on beds of dried fern, their ears pressed to the granite floor. Even in their dreams, they listen to the heartbeat of the place: the steady, musical *clink* of water falling through the dark conduits, the iron-solid grind of cyclopean stones settling into their footings, and the endless, roaring blanket of life that blankets the mountain in the night.
