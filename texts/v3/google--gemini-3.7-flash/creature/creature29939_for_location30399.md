The life that occupies the tooth does not possess blood as the lower valleys know it; it possesses lubricants, powdered resins, and dry, crystalline lymph that circulates through capillary action driven entirely by the flexing of joints and the vibration of the wind. To cut one of these things is not to cause a bleed, but to open a pocket of fine, sulfurous meal or pale, graphite-slick dust.

### The Skitterers: *Scritches and File-Crabs*

At the stone level, deep beneath the shivering umbrellas of the mica-trees, the pavement is patrolled by the *file-crabs* and *tinder-beetles*. 

The file-crab is the size of an open palm, shaped like an irregular, bevelled tile of grey slate. It moves on eight paired, two-jointed needles of solid horn. Its underbelly does not touch the granite; instead, it suspends a heavy, magnetic keel that grazes a fraction of a millimeter above the stone, constantly gathering the charged dust shed by the copper-hair mounds. When two file-crabs meet, they do not fight; they lock their lateral carapaces together with a sound like two whetstones struck in haste, exchanging electrostatic charges through interlocking rows of copper-bright bristles along their margins. 

Among them dart the tinder-beetles—elongate, dry-shelled things with segmented abdomens made of overlapping plates of translucent, amber-colored mica. They are blind; their heads are dominated by a pair of rigid, comb-like antennae that catch the droning frequencies of the terrace. They feed directly upon the singing bushes, crawling into the vibrating heart of the copper-hair mounds where the friction is hottest. There, their jaws—two opposing diamond-hard wedges—clip off individual hairs, not to digest them as organic matter, but to use the coiled mineral fibers to pack their internal gizzards, grinding the hard pollen-spores they swallow into microscopic flour.

### The Megafauna of the Shelf: *The Anvil-Hoppers*

The bounding things that burst through the brush are called *anvil-hoppers*, or simply *hammers*. They are built entirely of compression. Standing roughly four feet at the shoulder when crouched, their bodies are encased in an armor of fused, overlapping keratin plates the color of burnt bone, scored with fine geometric ridges that break the shear of the wind.

Their hind legs are not muscle-driven in the conventional sense, but operate on a system of massive, coiled tendon-plates that lock into place with an audible ratchet when they crouch. When released, the stored kinetic energy hurls them upward in violent, ballistic arcs. They do not navigate in the air; they simply punch through the gusts, their four thick, split hooves swinging out like grappling hooks. 

The undersides of these hooves are cupped and lined with a dense, velvet-like pad of micro-hooks that find instant purchase in the microscopic grain of the granite pavement. When an anvil-hopper lands, it strikes with the concussive force of a dropped boulder, its plates clattering shut, instantly rooted against the gale.

The hoppers do not have mouths with soft tongues or wet throats. Their faces terminate in a blunt, chisel-like beak of black horn, flanked by two heavy, grinding plates that operate horizontally. They feed on the *rattle-cane*, snapping the hollow, bone-like pods off their stalks and pulverizing them with a sound like a jaw crushing gravel. From these pods they extract the oily, dry sulfur-marrow that serves as their sole energy source. Their breath, forced through lateral vent-holes beneath their neck-shields, smells of heated chalk and ozone, blowing out in hot, dry puffs that leave white rings of dust on the stone.

### The Air-Dwellers: *Vellum-Kites*

Above the terrace, where the wind shears off the lip into the vast amber void, the *vellum-kites* live an entirely ungrounded existence. 

A mature kite has a span of twelve feet, but its total weight barely exceeds that of a bundle of dry reeds. Its frame is composed of hollow, calcified struts, across which is stretched a skin as thin and tough as oiled parchment, semi-translucent and patterned like cracked tortoiseshell. 

The kites have no lungs. Their bodies are continuously ventilated by the gale, which enters a series of fluted, whistle-like apertures along the leading edge of their wings, generating an eerie, high-pitched chord as they tilt and roll. They possess four forward-facing eyes, entirely dry, protected by fixed lenses of clear, unpolished beryl that never blink. 

The kites mate, hunt, and sleep upon the updraft. They do not land on the shelf, for their brittle framework would shatter upon impact; instead, they skim the lip of the overhang, dropping their trailing, hook-tipped tail-struts to snag the molts of other kites caught in the foliage, or dipping low to pluck the tinder-beetles from the outer branches of the mica-combs. 

Every few weeks, as the wind scours their skin into fraying tatters, a kite sheds its outer parchment layers in long, ribboning sheets. These molts do not rot; they drift in the thermals like great sheets of gold leaf, stiff, resonant, and immensely durable.

### The Harvesters in the Undercut

The humans who inhabit the three dry-stone shelters under the mountain's brow have become almost indistinguishable from the fauna. They are few—perhaps seven or eight in this camp—and their bodies have adapted to an absolute economy of moisture.

They do not speak with voices. The roaring acoustic violence of the terrace—the combined cello-drone, comb-rattle, and wind-furnace—makes vocal speech impossible at more than three inches, and opening the mouth invites the parching air to strip the throat raw. Instead, they communicate through a complex sign language executed with stiff, rhythmic snaps of the fingers, or by tapping out percussive signals on the hollow bone plates strapped to their thighs.

**Appearance and Dress:**
They wear heavy, wraparound coats made of kite-molts sewn together with copper-hair thread, the golden, parchment-like sheets layered over inner linings of carded fern-silk. Their heads are entirely enclosed in helmets of cured hide and bone, fitted with narrow, slitted visors of green tourmaline that turn the glare of the sulfur sky into a manageable gloom. Over their mouths and nostrils they wear cylindrical, horn-carved respirators packed with crushed charcoal and powdered resin, which filter out the abrasive silica dust and condense what microscopic trace of metabolic moisture they exhale, recycling it back against the lips.

Their hands are calloused to the density of wood, the skin stained a permanent deep amber-grey from resin and mineral oils. They move with an unnatural, halting gait—a deliberate, low-center-of-gravity crouch that mimics the anvil-hoppers, their boots tipped with forward-facing iron studs that bite the granite at every step.

**Their Economy and Sustenance:**
These people are not pastoralists, nor are they hunters of meat. They are *resonance-smiths* and *molt-gatherers*.

Their tools are dry and heavy:
*   **The Shearing Hooks:** Long poles of spliced ironwood tipped with curved, self-sharpening slate blades, used to harvest the mica leaves from the comb-trees before they fracture from internal tension.
*   **The Wind-Winches:** Heavy skeletal cranes made of whale-rib bone and tied down with guy-ropes of braided copper-hair. The gathering baskets they lower over the lip are woven from rattle-cane, designed to let the wind pass through them while snagging the drifting kite-parchment like sieves.

They do not drink water; the shelf provides none, and hauling it from the purple fog of the lowlands is a forgotten craft. Instead, they tap the swollen root-nodes of the comb-trees—where the mineral tendons meet the granite fissures. There, deep within the rock, the tree’s piezoelectric charge breaks down sulfur compounds and releases a viscous, amber liquid: a heavy, dense hydrocarbon sap that tastes of burnt sugar and alum. The harvesters drink this sap through narrow bone straws; it is thick enough to coat the gullet, metabolizing slowly without the need for sweat or urine.

Their food is a dry, grey paste pounded from the sulfur-rich marrow of rattle-cane seeds, mixed with the oily, scraped scrapings of anvil-hopper plate-joints and kneaded into hard, dense cakes that can be snapped like slate and dissolved under the tongue.

**Life Inside the Shelters:**
Inside the dry-stone huts, out of the direct blast of the draft, the sound changes from a roar to an all-encompassing, bone-shaking hum. The granite floor vibrates continuously. 

The interior smells intensely of friction—like a blacksmith’s shop after the iron has cooled, mixed with the dry, incense-like smell of powdered mica. There are no lamps, for fire would consume what little air exists in the shelter; instead, the room is illuminated by *glow-jars*—earthenware pots filled with live tinder-beetles and vibrating copper-hair, which shed a continuous, pale greenish-yellow luminescence through the friction of their movement.

Here, they cure the mica. Stacked beneath heavy granite slabs, the razor-sharp leaves are rubbed with fine hopper-grease until they become flexible, transformed into flexible armor scales, roof-shingles, and acoustic plates that they trade with other ledge-settlements along the mountain’s tooth via tension-wire cable cars strung across the chasms.

When an inhabitant dies, they do not bury them in earth, for there is none. They carry the body to the outermost lip of the terrace, lash it to a framework of rattle-cane, and anchor it with a thin lead-rope to the cliff. Within three days, the furnace-draft scours the flesh down to dry, chalk-white bone, and the skin cures into taut, golden parchment. The skeleton is brought back inside to carve into winches and tool-handles; the cured skin is cut into thread. Nothing is wasted, and nothing rots; everything is simply drawn tighter, tuned, and strung back into the wind.
