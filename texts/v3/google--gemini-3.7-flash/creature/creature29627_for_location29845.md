In this ocean, life has no memory of mud.

The silver pelagics that run the five-knot channels are not wild creatures in any historical sense, though their anatomy is ancient. They are the descendants of hatchery batches bred in their billions to balance the chemical loading of the intake filters—sleek, heavy-shouldered clupeids and carangids, identical down to the count of their lateral-line scales. Because there are no shallows, no reeds, and no coastlines to break them into regional variants, they have consolidated into singular, basin-wide super-shoals that measure forty miles from vanguard to tail. 

Their nervous systems do not answer to the moon or the turn of a planetary season; their calendar is the maintenance rhythm of the circulation banks. When the western impeller banks ramp down for cathodic inspection, the drop in water velocity is felt instantly as a softening of pressure against their flanks, and the entire mass pivots with the precision of a shutter closing. They move along the sodium light-shafts like grain pouring down a chute, feeding on the uniform mist of engineered green flagellates that drift from the chlorination scrubbers. In the brilliant, unscattered light of the mercury-halide domes, their bodies do not appear organic; they look like millions of freshly stamped alloy blanks thrown into the wash.

Around the structural margins of this open water, life becomes opportunistic, territorial, and sharply mechanical.

The pylons—those immense grain-elevator towers of riveted plate—have become vertical reefs, though nothing like the coral systems of a warm earth. In the upper three hundred feet, where the water is warmed by the skin friction of the current and the radiated heat of submerged power cables, the iron is colonized by a dense, monocultural pelt of rubbery, pale hydroids and cold-water barnacles whose shells grow flat and low against the metal, streamlined to resist the tearing sheer of the turbine wash. 

Living among these barnacles are the cephalopods: great, inkless, bone-white octopuses and broad-mantled cuttlefish that were introduced to thin the filter-clogging crustacean drift. With no natural rocks or crevices to claim, they have learned the anatomy of the megastructure. They wedge their soft, hydrostatic bodies into the negative space behind gusset plates, inside the relief ports of pressure-equalizing valves, and between the cooling fins of high-voltage transformers. 

Their camouflage is remarkable not for its mimicry of stone, but for its mimicry of industrial maintenance. A cuttlefish resting on an I-beam does not mottle into sand-patterns; its chromatophores contract into the dead, matte expanse of naval gray, scored here and there by thin, perfectly straight lines of white and yellow that match the safety striping on the gantry edges. Some have even learned to display the rhythmic, sweeping caustics of the work-lights across their backs to make themselves invisible to prey looking up from the darker floors. They hunt by ambush, their arms shooting out from behind standard structural flange clearances to snatch silver fish that stray too close to the steel.

Lower down, where the water is coldest and the hum of the intake turbines deepens into a physical vibration that can loosen teeth, the sharks hold the dark.

They are mostly deep-water squaloids—heavy, blunt-headed sleeper sharks and long-tailed thresher variants with eyes the size of tea saucers, clouded with thick protective tapeta that turn the sodium glare into a pale, workable twilight. The megastructure is an overwhelming sensory landscape for them. Their ampullae of Lorenzini—the electro-receptive pores across their snouts—are perpetually flooded by the massive stray fields leaking from three-phase subsea conduits and sacrificial zinc anodes. To a shark, every structural beam hums like a live wire; every welded seam has a distinct galvanic taste.

They do not patrol randomly. They follow the electromagnetic grid lines embedded in the zinc-coated floor, navigating from one cathodic node to the next across tens of thousands of square miles of flat, gray deck plate. They have learned that the violent percussive *thwack* of pneumatic ballast valves cycling miles away means dead or stunned fish will soon be tumbling out of the vortex eddies downstream. They gather in the lee of the structural ribs, hovering inches above the pristine floor plating where the welding scale still lies undisturbed, waiting for the current to deliver the casualties of the intake screens.

And then there are the inhabitants of the ceiling.

Miles above the floor, in the structural lattice where the high gantries clear the water by only a few feet, an entirely separate ecology thrives in the warm, dripping, high-pressure gloom. Here, the water churns against the under-decking in a continuous, froth-choked chop. Air pockets trapped between the cross-girders are rich with ozone from the lamp ballasts and the oily tang of compressor exhaust.

In this half-drowned zone live the maintainers—or what has become of them.

Whether they are human divers on saturation shifts that measure in decades, or purpose-built biological sub-contractors, they belong to the metal. They move through the catwalk network in heavy, grease-impregnated drysuits, their helmets fitted with wide, multi-spectral viewports that compensate for the blinding glare of the halides. They do not swim through the open basins; they travel along the catenary cable runs in open-framed brass cages, or walk the narrow lower flanges of the girders with magnetic boots that produce a rhythmic, hollow *clack-clack-clack* that rings down through five miles of water.

Their settlements are suspended under the major junction decks: clusters of pressurized diving bells, weld-shacks, and dry habitat pods bolted directly into the structural web. They eat the gray clupeids, salted and dried over the heat vents of the primary circulation motors, and drink condensation scraped from the cold steel decking overhead. They are profoundly deaf to the frequencies of human speech, their hearing permanently calibrated to the five-hundred-cycle drone of the turbine halls and the sudden, dangerous acoustic spikes that signal a cavitation pocket or a sheared rivet.

Between the divers and the animals, an uneasy working truce exists. When a diver drops into the water to clear a fouled trash rack at an intake mouth, the white octopuses retreat into the valve pockets, watching with pale, horizontal pupils. The sharks drift at the perimeter of the torchlight, kept at bay by the electric arc of the maintenance welder and the heavy, thumping rhythm of the diver’s rebreather.

Nothing here dies of old age in a bed of silt. When an organism fails—whether a fish caught in the shear of an impeller, an octopus that misjudged the cycle of a gate valve, or a tool dropped from a ceiling gangway—it falls. 

The descent through the pale, illuminated miles takes hours. It passes through the sodium shafts, down past the silent, vertical towers, through the empty spans where the water is so clear it seems not to exist at all, until it hits the zinc-coated bottom with a tiny, metallic tap. 

There, on the vast, cross-ribbed floor that looks as clean and sharp as the day the flood gates were opened, small white isopods—introduced to clean the weld lines—scuttle out from under the flanges. They do their work quickly, scouring the gray paint until it shines again, leaving not a bone, not a scale, and not a speck of rust behind.
