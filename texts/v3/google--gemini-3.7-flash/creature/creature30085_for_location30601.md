To live in the trough is to be made of water, wool, and heavy bone.

The people of the rift are broad-framed and low-centered, their musculature developed not across horizontal miles, but through the endless, knee-grinding ascent of damp stone. Their gait is distinct: a rolling, flat-footed tread that keeps the heel low and the toes splayed, pressing maximum surface against the slick facets of slate. They go barefoot more often than not, even in the cold, their soles thickened into leathery, gray-white calluses that grip wet rock far better than cured hide ever could. When they do wear boots, they are constructed from thick, oil-stewed river-hide, soled with coarse mats of plaited cedar bark that bite into liverwort without sliding.

Because the air is thick with perpetual mist and the sheer stone cliffs swallow noise, their voices have evolved a peculiar timbre. They do not shout—shouting is a flat, ugly sound that dies three paces out in the moss. Instead, they speak in low, chest-resonant murmurs that vibrate along the stone itself, or use a complex lexicon of finger-taps, sleeve-tugs, and quick, silent gestures made against the dark slate of their coats. A person can stand across a twenty-foot plunge from their neighbor, bathed in the silver daylight, and conduct an entire, intricate conversation without disturbing the nesting swallows six inches above their head.

Their clothing is heavy, built entirely around the defense against continuous, unfreezing moisture. They wear tunics of coarse, hand-spun goat hair that naturally repels water, layered over fine inner shirts woven from the beaten bast fiber of hemlock bark. Over these, they drape mantle-cloaks of felted moss-wool, rubbed thick with mutton tallow and the fatty grease of river otters until water does not soak, but simply balls up and rolls off in beads. Everything they wear smells of damp cedar, sheep fat, and old cold. 

Their skin is pale—not the sickly, translucent white of deep caves, for they live under the wide, cool sky, but a dense, milk-and-slate pale that flushes brilliant dark red across the cheekbones and knuckles from the steady chill. Their hair is long, oiled, and kept tightly plaited with thin copper wires or bone needles to prevent it from soaking up the ambient mist and dragging down the neck.

They are an unhurried people, bound to the patience of the stone and the trees. 

Among them, the most respected are the *Root-Binders*—the arbor-masons who spend their lives suspended in woven goat-leather harnesses against the cliff tiers. With small, bone-tipped spuds and pots of sticky river-clay, they tend the living frame of the city. They guide the young hemlock taproots into the joint-lines between newly placed ashlar blocks, coaxing them to swell and lock the stones in place, while shaving back any wayward rootlet that threatens to buckle an inhabited cellar. They know the grain of the basalt and the thirsty habits of the timber with equal intimacy.

Below them work the *Sluice-Wardens*, who keep the veins of the city flowing. Because the upper tiers drink first, the moral order of the rift is entirely hydrological. To foul a water-race is the single unpardonable transgression; it is a crime against every hearth below. The wardens live along the granite flumes, using wide paddles of carved alder to sweep away hemlock needles, leaf-litter, and fine shale silt, ensuring that every settling pool remains clear enough to see the white belly of a resting trout ten feet down.

Domestic life takes place in low-ceilinged rooms where the walls are often two-thirds living bedrock, cold to the touch and weeping thin, clear trickles into shallow floor-channels that lead out to the nearest drop. There is little dry wood in the trough, so their fires are small and carefully husbanded. They burn a hard, dense charcoal pressed from peat and alder pulp, fired in communal kilns carved deep into dry cliff pockets. Their hearths are chimneyless; instead, the draft is pulled downward through stone flues into the churning spray of the waterfalls, which washes the thin smoke clean before it can hang in the rift’s cool air.

They eat from the water and the vertical soil:
*   Pale, broad-shouldered trout, cold-cured in brine and wild mountain-celery, eaten raw in thin, translucent ribbons.
*   Cresses and peppery, cold-water succulent leaves scraped from the splash-zones of the bridges.
*   Thick, sour cakes made from the dried, pulverized inner bark of hemlocks, baked over hot river-cobbles and slathered with salty, pungent cheese made from the milk of the roof-goats.
*   Fat marsh-chestnuts and blind water-crabs harvested from the silt-traps in the lowest basins.

The non-human life of the city is not domesticated in the traditional sense; it is integrated. The mountain goats that crop the moss on the roofs are communal property, moving across the stone parapets like heavy, white-fleeced clouds, their split hooves clicking softly as they keep the turf from growing thick enough to rot the heavy rafters beneath. The martens, long and sleek with dark, oiled pelts, do not live as pets, but move freely through the gap-spaces between cellar walls and living-room floors, welcomed for their relentless pursuit of the stone-grub and the slate-boring beetles that would weaken the timber supports.

Deep down, where the city roots touch the trough’s floor and the light dims to a persistent twilight, live the *Lichen-Weavers* and the *Sump-Tenders*. Here, in the dampest, heaviest cold, great blind salamanders, as long as a man’s arm and the color of wet tallow, rest motionless in the deep, slow-moving overflow conduits. The people revere them as the slow hearts of the stone; to find a white salamander lodged in the foundation-arch of your home is a sign that the mortar is sound and the mountain has settled true.

Death in the rift is met with the same wet, quiet practicality as life. When a person dies, their body is not burned, nor is it buried in the earth, for there is no soil to spare. Instead, they are wrapped in open nets of woven cedar root and carried down into the deep foundation vaults—the dark, saturated clefts where the oldest hemlock roots drink from the floor of the rift. The body is laid among the root-masses, packed around with river-mud, living moss, and the pungent shavings of bitter wood. Within a season, the cold water and the ravenous roots have taken everything, turning the flesh and bone of the deceased directly into the living timber that holds the stone city against the cliff.

They do not fear the damp, nor do they look up at the rim two miles above with any longing. To them, the wind-scoured tableland above is a dry, barren, violent place, scourged by light and torn by empty air. Down here, wedged into the cold, silver marrow of the world, they are sheltered, perpetually watered, and anchored deep.
