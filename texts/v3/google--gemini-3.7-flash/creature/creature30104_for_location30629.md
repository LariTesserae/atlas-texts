The silence here is not empty; it is the held breath of things that listen through their joints.

The true residents of this folded court are the **Mortisers**—tall, narrow-framed humanoids whose bodies have the dense, dry lightheartedness of cured basswood. They have no skin in the human sense, but a continuous exterior of pressed felt and split reed, oiled with a pale, non-sticky cedar resin that smells like an open instrument case. Where their knuckles bend, the surface does not wrinkle into soft folds; it slides along precise, interlocking plates of shaved horn, dusted with talc so that they move without the slightest creak of friction.

They do not bleed. If a gouge slips or a beam shifts to shear off a finger, the wound exposes only a clean cross-section of pale, compacted grain—a cross-laminated marrow that weeps only a fine, dry starch. 

Their faces are long and flat-planed, faceted as if blocked out with three strokes of an adze. They have no nostrils, for they do not inhale moist atmosphere; instead, two slitted grilles of woven horsehair along their collarbones take in the dry, warm air, drawing it down into internal chambers lined with brittle reed-flutes. When they rest, they do not sleep; they wedge themselves into the vertical reveals between studs, standing upright or sideways depending on the local grain of gravity, and hum—a low, sympathetic drone that harmonizes with the natural resonance of the cedar frames around them.

### Sustenance and the Mouth

Because there is no water to swallow, their mouths are narrow, dry horizontal vents lined with fine copper bristle. They do not eat flesh or fruit. Instead, they sustain themselves on two things:
1. **The Bloom:** The white, chalky efflorescence that slowly sweats out of the unglazed terracotta tiles—a crisp, alkaline powder rich in calcified dust, which they scrape off with bone spoons and press into dry wafers between their palms.
2. **The Pith-Bread:** The brittle, spongy cores harvested from the largest parchment-ferns. Snapped into rods, the pith is ground in shallow stone troughs into a fragrant meal that tastes faintly of toasted oats and cedar ash. 

To digest, their bellies use no acid. Instead, a gizzard of smooth, rounded terracotta marbles rolls together with dry, rhythmic clicks whenever they walk, pulverizing the dust into an ultra-fine flour that sifts directly into their fibrous circulation.

### The Measuring-Hands

A Mortiser’s hands are not grasping paws, but sets of calibrated instruments. The index and middle fingers are straight-grained bone, their inner edges honed so flat they can detect a thousandth-of-an-inch cup in a plank simply by drawing a knuckle across it. The thumbs are tipped with small, blunt adzes of beaten copper, perpetually stained orange from the clay dust.

They do not build by drawing plans. When an impossible arch needs to be cast across a void, two Mortisers stand on opposite ledges, each holding one end of a taut, chalked linen cord. They snap the cord once. Where the white dust hangs in the dry air, a line of gravity forms. Then, with swift, silent strikes of their hornbeam mallets against the cedar uprights, they call the wood forward. The joints sprout toward each other, kerf meeting tenon, locking shut with the hollow *thwack* of an arrow finding an ash target.

### The Bellows-Beasts

Living in the hollows between the sub-flooring and the angled soffits are the **Bellows-Beasts**—creatures the size of large sheep, with bodies shaped like hexagonal concertinas. Their hides are thick, multi-layered parchment stitched together with waxed linen cord, stiffened at regular intervals by hoops of bent willow. 

They have no eyes or legs; they move through the joist-voids by expanding and compressing their accordion ribs, using rows of small, downward-angled reed-hooks on their undersides to rat-trap their way along the timbers. 

As they crawl, they breathe for the entire court. They draw the hot, static air from the high skylight pockets, compress it in their pleated bellies until it is bone-dry and charged with resin scent, and pump it through the hollow dowel-holes into the lowest floor-chambers. Without them, the air would settle and cool; with them, the atrium retains the exact, dry fever of a bread oven. When a Bellows-Beast is content, it vents a soft, rhythmic *chuff-puff* through its brass-collared snout, dusting the passing beetles with a fine mist of powdered chalk.

### The Curl-Hounds

Running in packs of three or four through the dense brambles of dry cane are the **Curl-Hounds**. They are entirely composed of long, springy ribbons of planed ash shavings, tangled and knotted around a central cage of split-bamboo ribs. They are weightless, skittering over the vertical brickwork like tumbleweeds caught in a draft, their feet making a frantic *shhh-shhh-shhh* against the burlap-pressed tiles.

The Hounds are scavengers of waste. Wherever a Mortiser chisels a mortise or a fern drops an old, brittle fan, the Curl-Hounds sweep in. They do not eat the wood shavings; they weave them into their own bodies, constantly replacing their outermost curls as they become brittle and snap. When a Curl-Hound grows too dense and heavy from gathered cedar curls, it finds an empty corner between two beams, wedges itself tightly into the angle, and ceases to move. Over several days, the ambient vitality of the room knots the hound permanently into the joint, transforming its tangled body into a structural knee-brace that sprouts fresh, pale fern-shoots from its heart.

### Language and Social Life

There is no speech here that uses wet lips or moist tongues. The Mortisers communicate through **kerf-striking**. By tapping the blunt side of a copper gouge against the timber they are touching, they send sharp, telegraphic vibrations traveling along the interconnected frame of the atrium. A Mortiser working three levels above, with his feet pegged into an inverted ceiling, feels the pulse through the soles of his felt shoes and knows precisely which dowel to drive to relieve the load below.

For intimate conversation, they stand close and scrape the dry fabric of their sleeves together in complex, patterned rhythms, like crickets the size of men. 

They do not die of disease or rot, for there is no moisture to harbor decay. When a Mortiser reaches the end of their vitality—when their joints have absorbed so much mica and resin that they can no longer flex—they choose their final place. They climb to an unfinished gallery, set their back against an un-pegged king post, and drive a long, squared oak trunnel through their own center-plate, locking themselves forever into the building's skeleton. 

Within a year, the pith-brush grows up through their ribs, their horn plates become one with the cedar framing, and their dried eyes, clouded with amber, watch over the self-assembling halls they have become.
