To survive on the submerged plateau, life has had to abandon the softness of earth and the shelter of rot. There is no mud to burrow into, no weed to weave into nests; there is only the stone, the ice-clear velocity of the water, and the eternal bruised dusk. Consequently, the things that live here are defined by three necessities: ballast, grip, and the geometry of the flow.

At the lowest tier—in the hair-thin tolerances where the massive basalt blocks meet—exist the *grout-leeches* and *suture-crawlers*. They are not true annelids, but flat, armor-plated ribbons of living slate, scarcely thicker than a fingernail. Their carapaces are infused with iron and heavy barium salts scavenged from the mineral snow, giving them an unnatural, leaden weight. They do not swim; to lose contact with the rock is to be instantly flung into the howling cross-currents and pulverized against a distant arch. Instead, they crawl through the subterranean seams on thousands of microscopic, diamond-hard stylets that bite directly into the micro-pores of the stone. Over centuries, their dead shells, crushed and compressed within the cracks, form the only mortar the plateau has ever known—a brittle, chalky cement of compacted carapaces that keeps the shifting megaliths from grinding each other to dust.

Where the seams open slightly wider, in the curved lee of the rounded domes, the brittle-star colonies have turned themselves into civil engineering. These are not frail, creeping things, but *gale-weavers*—echinoderms with limbs of thick, vulcanized tendon and jointed plates of calcified shell. They do not exist as individuals; they interlock their arms by the thousands, clicking together in intricate, hexagonal lattices that span the sheltered depressions between megaliths like living suspension bridges. As the down-drafts roar over them, the lattice vibrates at a resonant, low pitch, straining the violent water for the pale flakes of organic detritus that cascade from the peaks. When a loose block slips miles up-current, sending a hydraulic shockwave through the water, the weaver-lattices tense in unison, flattening themselves against the rock face until the pressure-wave passes.

Deeper in the avenues, where the water is channeled into roaring flumes between sunken colonnades, life becomes hydrodynamic to the point of abstraction. The shoals of rib-fish move less like animals and more like shards of mica caught in a gale. Their bodies are completely transparent, save for a spine like a needle of milk glass and two lateral stabilizing keels of stiff, horn-like cartilage. They have no eyes; their entire skin is a map of pressure-sensitive pit organs that read the turbulent shear of the water with mathematical precision. They don't swim against the freezing rivers—they surf the pressure-fronts, banking off the invisible walls of vertical down-drafts and banking through megalithic archways in sudden, synchronized flashes of blinding white belly-plates. They feed on the wing, snatching mineral-crusted copepods and free-floating larvae from the vortex edges before the current can pull them into the abyss.

And then there are the anchors—the great, solitary things that have made the architecture their skeletons.

Tucked into the deep, scoured cavities beneath the megalithic arches dwell the *pale mantles*. These are cephalopods of immense mass, but devoid of all grace or fluid motion. Their flesh is not soft, but dense, fibrous, and cold as blubber, pigmented the color of wet gypsum. They have lost the ability to change color, for there are no hues here to mimic; instead, their skin is heavily tuberculated, textured with raised knobs and ridges that match the exact roughness of water-scoured basalt, shedding the tearing currents around them like an aerodynamic hull.

A pale mantle does not hunt by pursuit. It wedges its anvil-shaped body into the groove between two fifty-ton blocks, driving four of its eight limbs deep into the masonry seam. The undersides of these anchoring arms have no soft suckers; they bear hard, hooked rings of keratinized bone that lock into the stone with the permanence of pitons. The remaining four arms—longer, thinner, tipped with sensory frills that taste the cold drift—trail out into the flume like frayed cables. When a tumbling fish, stunned by a vortex, or a drifting crab is driven past the archway by the current, the trailing arms snap shut with hydraulic violence, hauling the meal back into the dark recess beneath the megalith, where a beak like a black stone shear reduces it to paste. 

These creatures live for centuries in the same joint. Their bodies slowly deform to fit the specific curvature of the stone they inhabit, their skin wearing away where the water hammers it most fiercely, developing thick, calloused pads that buff the basalt beneath them to a mirror sheen. When an old mantle finally dies, its anchor-limbs do not immediately relax; the frozen, calcified muscle remains locked in the seam for years while the water gradually shears the softer flesh away, leaving behind only the ivory-colored rings of its hooks embedded in the stone like ancient ironwork.

Above all of this, in the wide, blue-gray expanses where the megaliths rise toward the twilight like the spine of a drowned mountain, roam the *cleavers*. 

They are vast, jawless, and almost entirely cartilaginous—basking leviathans whose forms are flattened vertically into massive, upright blades thirty paces from snout to tail. They do not possess fins in the traditional sense; their bodies are shaped like asymmetrical hydrofoils. By adjusting the tilt of their broad, stone-gray flanks against the screaming mountain currents, they generate lift without ever moving their tails, soaring sideways through the drowned ruins like gliders riding a thermal. 

Their snouts are broad shovels of bare bone, used to scrape the weaver-lattices and gout-leeches from the high, exposed shoulders of the pillars. The cleavers are blind, their tiny, vestigial eyes clouded over with cataracts of thick fat, but they navigate the endless avenues of stone by listening. They emit low, percussive clicks from their cavernous throats—deep, sub-bass groans that bounce off the basalt facades leagues away. To the cleaver, the drowned plateau is not a silent void of stone, but a ringing hall of acoustic reflections: the hollow ring of an archway, the dense silence of an intact foundation, the sharp, cracking reverberation of a block sliding down a terrace.

They follow the avenues as if they were roads laid out for them, banking between the bone-smooth pillars with inches to spare, sliding through the ice-cold twilight in an environment that has neither beginning nor end, where life has long since forgotten the sun, and the only gods are the current, the stone, and the weight of the water above.
