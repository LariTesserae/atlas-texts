At the level of the stone itself, life begins as a suction. 

Because the basalt and sintered silicates have no fissures, pores, or granular rot, the lichens do not wedge themselves in; they adhere through molecular contact, secreting a faint, alkaline mucus that hardens into a glassy seal against the planar shears. These crusts are cold to the touch and pale as milk-glass, tinted faint cobalt by the mineral salts they leach from the raw terrace floor. Among them grow the velvet mosses, which form perfectly circular pads—cushions six inches across, unanchored by taproots, holding themselves down purely through the weight of the water they trap. 

Living between these cushions are the limn-midges: wingless in their larval instars, nearly transparent, their intestines visible as dark silver threads of pulverized lichen paste. When they pupate and take to the air, they emerge in silent, drifting smoke-plumes above the drainage channels. They have no mouthparts as adults and live only four or five hours in the cold, bright mist, their entire purpose to mate in the buoyant updrafts around the warm pylon skirts and drop their gel-sleeved eggs into the swift, distilled rills before the water falls over the terrace rim.

Following the midges are the *plinth-skimmers*. They are birds built entirely for laminar airflow, with wingspans exceeding three meters but bodies no heavier than a handful of dry sedge. Their bones are pneumatic, filled with the thin high-altitude air, and their plumage is dense and waxy, utterly waterproof, shedding the constant mist in rolling mercury-like drops. They have double-hinged, needle-fine beaks that snap shut with an audible click, cutting through midge swarms at sixty kilometers per hour without breaking their glide. 

The skimmers do not land on the horizontal stone, where their long primary feathers would drag in the runoff; instead, they roost vertically on the sixty-story transit spires. Clinging to the sheer, engineered composite with needle-tipped rear halluxes, they tuck their heads into their breast down and absorb the faint, kinetic warmth bleeding from the towers. Their nesting sites are clustered along the south-facing seams of the conduits—thousands of round, felted pockets made of regurgitated sedge fiber and midge silk, cemented directly to the architectural struts. The warmth of the city foundations incubates their single, slate-grey eggs, freeing the parents to ride the abyssal thermals over the drop-offs for days at a time.

Down on the broad township slabs, the heavy ungulates—the *stridemen* or *six-hooved dray*—move in vast, slow ribbons that trace the invisible thermal veins beneath the rock. 

Anatomically, they are built like living ballast. Their six legs are short, thick columns of dense tendon and marrowless bone, arranged in three pairs that move in a synchronized, rolling wave (front-left and rear-left, then middle-right, and so on) that guarantees four points of ground contact at all times on the wet, slippery plane. Their hooves are not split horn, but broad, circular disks of fibrous, self-renewing cartilage, ribbed on the underside like the tread of an oceanic boot. When a dray steps, its weight forces water out from beneath the pad through microscopic channels, creating an instantaneous, non-slip vacuum against the glass-smooth silicate.

Their shaggy, oiled coats hang to their knees in heavy, dreadlocked skirts that channel the condensing mist straight down to the rock, keeping their thick hide dry. Their heads are massive and wedge-shaped, carried low to the ground; their lower jaws are armed not with teeth, but with broad, shovel-like horn plates that chisel the crustose lichens from the bedrock with an unhurried, rhythmic scraping sound—*shck, shck, shck*—that carries through the mist across immense distances.

The herds do not scatter. They move in tight, rectangular formations, an instinct born of the terraced geography: if an animal drifts over an edge, the fall is a kilometer straight down into the cloud layer. Calves are kept strictly in the center of the phalanxes, trotting on soft, oversized pads that make a wet, sucking slap on the wet stone. When the herd rests, they kneel together in dense, steaming squares, folding their legs beneath their bodies and pressing their bellies against the foundation seams where the deep stabilizers pulse their deep-mantle hum. The vibration lulls them; they chew their cud with half-closed, horizontal pupils, their slate-wool coats matting together into a single, collective blanket that traps a layer of warm air over hundreds of square meters.

Hunting them are the *shear-stalkers*, though "stalking" in this open, coverless landscape is an exercise in pure geometry.

These predators are long-bodied, six-jointed, and nearly flat, standing barely higher than a human knee but stretching nearly three meters in length. Their fur is dense, short, and the exact matte-white of the mist that hangs over the terrace rims. They have no visible ears—their auditory canals are protected beneath thick flaps of skin—and their footpads are covered in millions of microscopic, spatulate setae that allow them to sprint across wet, near-frictionless basalt at terrifying speeds without a sound.

Because there are no boulders, trees, or gullies to hide behind, the stalkers hunt using the architectural steps and the blind spots of the dray herds. A pack of three or four will lie completely flat in the shallow, geometric drainage troughs, submerged up to their nostrils in freezing, crystal-clear runoff, perfectly still for hours. They wait until a herd grazes toward the lip of a terrace step. When the lead animals turn to trace a corner, the stalkers rise in a synchronized, fluid sprint, cutting off the flank. 

They do not try to pull a dray down by throat or limb—the prey is too heavy and anchored too firmly to the stone. Instead, they use the sheer drops of the plateau. Working in silent coordination, snapping at the rear hocks with shears of serrated bone, they maneuver an isolated bull or elderly cow toward the millimeter-precise edge until the animal missteps, loses the grip of its cartilage pads, and slips into the silent abyss below. The stalkers do not follow; they know the geography. They descend hours later via the massive conduit service-stairs, navigating the vertical drops with effortless, spider-like agility to feed on the cloud-swallowed floor below.

And finally, there are those who maintain the silent contract between the stone and the flesh.

Within the slender transit spires, behind composite panels that show neither seam nor weld, live the attendants—whether human, post-human, or the slow, deliberate iterations of maintenance intelligence that outlived their creators' specific forms. They are rarely seen on the horizontal plazas, but occasionally, during the change of deep-tier weather systems, a solitary figure will step out onto an open maintenance gantry sixty stories above the herd.

They are tall, attenuated, dressed in seamless, unadorned robes of grey membrane that do not flap in the wind, but cling close, whispering against their limbs. They walk the spidersilk conduits between spires without harnesses, carrying long, slender calibration rods that touch the cables to test their harmonic tension. 

The animals below know them, or at least accommodate them as a feature of the terrain. When an attendant descends via the base lifts to inspect an anchor slab, the dray herds do not run; they merely part, their heavy, low-slung heads lifting briefly, breath steaming into the pale daylight, to watch the tall, slow-moving shape pass through their ranks. The attendant might reach down, running a gloved hand across the oily, wet fleece of a grazing bull, feeling the resonant vibration of the stabilizer humming up through the creature’s dense skeleton.

There is no conflict here, because there is no scarcity yet. The terrace is clean, the moisture is infinite, and the mineral bed is vast. Life and the impossible infrastructure exist in a state of cool, unhurried equilibrium, quietly colonizing the cold edge of the sky while they wait for the rest of the world to catch up.
