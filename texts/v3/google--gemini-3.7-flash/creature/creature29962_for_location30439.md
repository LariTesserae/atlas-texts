To understand what lives in the cistern, one must understand that nothing has entered or left since the adze-blows ceased and the water rose to the ceiling thousands of years ago. There are no visitors, no strays, no seasonal migrants. What lives here is not merely an assemblage of creatures, but a single, closed, slow-burning machine of flesh, lime, and warm broth.

At the base of everything is the silt. It does not consist of dust or soil, for there is none in this sky-vault, but of five fathoms of accumulated slough: the moulted carapaces of ten thousand generations of crabs, the hair-fine ribs of long-dissolved eels, the powdery chalk of crushed shells, and the dense, gray precipitate of mineral salts vomited up day and night by the floor-flue. 

In this silt live the blind worms—not the tube-dwellers of the pillars, but immense, skinless annelids as thick as pine logs, pale as tallow. They have neither heads nor tails in any conventional sense, only blunt, muscular sphincters at either end that churn continuously through the bone-flour. They are the true digesters of the cistern. They eat the dead falling from above, grind the lime against their own internal stones, and excrete a warm, alkaline grease that rises in oily ribbons toward the vaulted ceiling, feeding the pelagic drift.

Above them, the terraced steps are patrolled by the *ticking crabs*. They are completely flat, like plates of unglazed bone china, their carapaces etched with the faint, hexagonal impressions of the terracotta bricks they have pressed against since birth. Their limbs are absurdly long—spindle-thin legs that end not in claws, but in sensitive, three-jointed quills. Because they cannot see, they navigate by percussion. A crab will pause on the edge of an amphitheater tier, lift a single quill, and tap: *tock, tock, tock-tock*. The stone carries the tap; three hundred other crabs across the amphitheater freeze, receiving the vibration through their bellies. In this way, they maintain an exact, shifting grid across the masonry, spacing themselves like orchard trees so that no two crabs ever scrape the same square foot of mortar for micro-fauna on the same day. When one dies, its neighbors do not rush it; they simply wait for the tapping to cease, and by morning the silent space is neatly, methodically divided between them.

Clinging to the soaring columns are the shield-mollusks, known to the stone only as weight and friction. Some of these creatures have not moved more than three yards in a human lifetime. Their shells are not spiraled, but broad, flat domes of dense aragonite, overgrown with thickets of scarlet tube-worms until each snail resembles an altar piled with bloody feathers. Beneath the shell, the mantle is a slab of translucent, liver-colored muscle that breathes directly through the water, expanding with such force that it can wedge apart cracked masonry. When a shield-mollusk feeds, it extends a black, ribbon-like radula studded with thousands of microscopic opal teeth. The sound of twelve of these beasts grazing together on the lower tiers is a low, rhythmic rasp—like a mason dressing stone in a distant cellar. They grind away the brick itself, absorbing the iron and clay into their own shells, so that their upper carapaces are ringed in concentric bands of terracotta orange, basalt gray, and bone white, recording the cistern's structural erosion year by year.

In the middle water, where the thermal plume from the flue curls outward into the darkness, dwell the *veil-eels*. 

They are the only things in the cistern that move with urgency, though their urgency is like that of smoke in a draftless room. Their skin is not slimy, but dry and velvety, velvet-white, almost entirely transparent in the rare larval flares; one can watch the dark, churning pulse of their three hearts, the slow peristalsis of their stomachs, and the golden, unfertilized eggs stacked like beads along their spines. They possess no eyes, but their snouts are covered in thousands of tiny, vibrating pits that map the world in heat. To a veil-eel, the cistern is not stone and water, but a vast, hollow sphere of cold blue slate surrounding a towering, vertical pillar of throbbing crimson warmth. 

They sleep braided together in the hundreds, hung like heavy white curtains from the capital of each column. A single braid may weigh as much as an ox. When the heat from the floor surges—a sudden pulse of boiling brine from the deep earth—the braids dissolve in unison. The eels unravel into the gloom, drifting in wide, undulating ribbons through the colonnades, opening their soft, round mouths to swallow the rising thermal grease excreted by the silt-worms below. 

Higher still, jammed into the square crypt-niches that line the highest gallery near the ceiling, are the *old sleepers*—the heavy, blunt-nosed fish that have forgotten how to swim. 

They are descendants of some ancient river-carp or lungfish sealed in during the cistern's filling, but they have lost their scales, their swim bladders, and their fins, which have shrunk into fleshy, finger-like stumps. They do not forage. They wedge their broad, fat bodies so tightly into the stone niches that their skin has taken on the texture of the chisel-marks. They live in a state of near-permanent torpor, their gills moving once every few minutes with a wet, heavy sigh. They are entirely dependent on the eels, which, during their thermal ascents, brush against the niches, sloughing off bits of skin and nutrient-rich mucus that drift into the open, fleshy mouths of the sleepers. Some of these fish have grown so large within their cells that they can no longer be removed; the stone has become their skeleton, and should the water ever drop, they would be found petrified in the brickwork like ancient grain in a silo.

And at the very center of the floor, over the roaring, silent mouth of the heat-flue, sits the *Filter*.

It is not an animal that can be named, but a colony—a single, pulsating mound of glass-sponge and hydroid flesh fifty feet across and thirty feet high. It has built itself over the vent like an organic chimney, anchoring its base into the terracotta tiers with root-like cables of pure silica. The water that shoots from the earth must pass through its vast, porous body before it can circulate through the cistern. The Filter glows with a continuous, deep-seated, violet phosphorescence that never reaches the walls, illuminating only its own convoluted interior chambers, where millions of microscopic, transparent polyps dance in the scalding upwelling. 

Within the chambers of the Filter, the water is cleaned of its heavy metals, sweetened with enzymes, and breathed full of dissolved lime. It is the heart of the cistern, the furnace that keeps the blood-warmth from fading, the engine that eats the mountain's volcanic breath and turns it into living slime.

Here, in the absolute dark, there is no cruelty and no waste. Every drop of moisture has been drunk ten thousand times; every grain of lime has been a shell, then a tooth, then a stone, then a shell again. The cistern does not know that the world outside has ended, that the air is gone from the plateau, or that the stars are wheeling over its twenty-foot basalt skull. It knows only the steady, velvet thrum of its own circulation—a single, eternal body of warm water, gently turning its own flesh over and over in the dark.
