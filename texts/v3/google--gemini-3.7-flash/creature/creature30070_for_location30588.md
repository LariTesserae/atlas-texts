To survive here, a body cannot be soft, and it cannot depend on silence. The noise alone—that vast, rib-cage-crushing brass stroke and the hiss of live steam—would rupture delicate tympana; the chemical bite of the water would scour soft gills to ribbons in an afternoon. Whatever makes a home in the Sump has either calcified itself into something like living masonry or stripped away everything fragile until only rubbery muscle, horn, and grease remain.

The first true tenants are the **scale-plates** that coat the undersides of the flooded catwalks and the brick lips of the sluice gates. To an untrained eye, they look like nodules of boiler-scale or rusty iron washers fused to the steel. In truth, they are broad, disc-shaped gastropods whose shells are not made of ordinary calcium carbonate, but a dense, slag-like composite of iron oxide, lime, and polymerized machine oils. They do not crawl in any visible sense; they creep by microns per hour, anchoring their leathery, suction-rimmed skirts over the seams where warm grease and acidic effluent seep from the pipe joints. When the fifteen-minute draw-down begins and the water falls away with that great, hollow groan, hundreds of these plates clamp shut against the hot metal with a dull, wet *clack*, sealing their moist mantles in airtight domes until the white foam surges back over them.

Above the water line, in the dense, dripping canopies formed by the ten-foot aroid leaves and the sagging bundles of copper conduit, moves a different order of creature: the **lagging-crawlers**.

These are pale, flattened arthropods the length of a human forearm, resembling giant, segmented woodlice or eyeless mantids, though their shells are soft and chalky, dusted with an encrustation of dried lime. They do not possess eyes—light in the Sump is meaningless, fractured by steam and yellow halos—but are instead mapped entirely by thermal and vibrational receptors along their fringed leg-joints. They cling to the undersides of the boiling-water pipes where the jute and horsehair insulation has rotted through. There they graze, not on the metal itself, but on the dense, gelatinous bacterial mats and fibrous root-veils that drink from the pipe seams.

The lagging-crawlers have learned the geography of the heat. Their pale bodies match the fever of the bronze conduits exactly; to touch one would be like touching a hot, wet leather pouch. When an overhead pressure-relief valve shrieks, purging a cloud of scalding vapor, they do not flee; they turn toward the jet, extending delicate, feather-like antennae into the billow to comb grease-droplets and airborne sulfur crystals straight out of the steam.

Lower down, in the flooded streets and the drowned basements beneath the diamond-plate sidewalks, the water is too turbid and chemically violent for fish, but it is rich with sunken vegetable matter and industrial tallow. Here live the **keel-leeches** and **sludge-salamanders**.

The salamanders are heavy, neotenic beasts, some stretching four or five feet from snout to paddle-tail. Their skins are completely pigmentless—the color of boiled pork—and covered in a thick, self-renewing layer of clear slime that neutralizes the shifting acid of the runoff before it can reach their hide. They are entirely blind, their heads rounded into smooth, blunt spades suited for nosing into the tangled root-mats that choke the floorplates. 

Their lives are governed down to the second by the intake cycles of the subterranean pumps:
* When the water level is high, they drift lazily through the drowned avenues, sweeping their sensitive, whiskered snouts over the diamond-plate, feeding on the fallen scale-plates that have lost their grip, or tearing away mouthfuls of the thick, fibrous vine-flesh that dangles into the current.
* The moment the water begins its sucking, resonant drop, their behavior changes instantly. They feel the drop in hydraulic pressure across their lateral lines and scuttle downward, wedging their supple, bone-dense bodies deep into the exposed brick sluice-channels and the dead cavities behind the pump foundations. There they stack together by the dozen, coiled like damp fire-hoses in the gloom, their ribs locked against the masonry to keep from being drawn down into the massive intake impellers.
* When the pneumatic rams fire with that concussive thump and the backwash returns in a boiling wall of froth, the salamanders spill out again into the wide, steaming plazas, rolling through the turbulence, completely indifferent to the heat.

Higher still, in the narrow gaps between the corrugated baffling plates and the iron-truss skyways where the rain begins its fall, hang the **grease-spiders**. They are not true arachnids, but colonial, silk-spinning hexapods that do not build webs to catch flying prey—for there are no flies here—but to catch the air itself. 

Their webs are dense, greasy funnels strung across the structural ironwork, acting as passive strainers for the enormous volume of humid air forced upward by the displacement pumps. The fine, hydrophobic silk collects the aerosolized lubricating oils, soot, and chemical dust tossed up by the machinery. Twice a day, the colony gathers at the narrow neck of the funnel to harvest the accumulated grey wax, chewing it into nutrient-rich pellets that feed their larvae, which squirm within hollowed-out sections of decaying cork insulation.

Nothing here sings, and nothing calls. The air is too loud for voices; an animal calling for a mate would be drowned out instantly by the *thud-clack* of the valving and the roar of the weirs. Instead, reproduction and movement are synchronized entirely to the pulse of the terrace itself. Mating occurs only during the dead-stall between the intake and the backwash—those three brief seconds of absolute hydraulic stillness when the water hangs suspended, neither rising nor falling. 

In that sudden, breathless pause, thousands of pale spores, drifting larvae, and glistening pheromonal slicks are released simultaneously into the warm, mineral broth. Then the rams crash down, the foam surges white and furious over the steps, and the life of the Sump is swept violently across the three city blocks of iron and brick, churning into every cracked seam and hot valve, settling into the perpetual, sweating rhythm of the dark.
