The things that inhabit the sump do not merely dwell within its tolerances; they are calibrated to them, forged out of the same dense cellulose and heavy protein that sustain the engine itself. Nothing here possesses the frantic volatility of surface life. Movement is metered, deliberate, and entirely synchronized with the mechanical pulse of the wood.

### The Shield-Grazers (*Peltocarpa*)
The bronze-backed crawlers upon the amber conduits are wider than a man’s outstretched arms, their carapaces formed of thick, interlocking plates of mineralized lignin and heavy chitin. They do not have eyes, nor do they possess antennae capable of withstanding the scalding steam. Instead, their heads are fused into blunt, wedge-shaped shovels tipped with self-sharpening, silicated rasps.

They feed where the heat is most lethal. As the copper sap rushes through the pressurized xylem, micro-fissures in the amber tissue weep a dense, caramelized residue that bakes instant-hard against the wood. The grazers move along these lines of crust with the agonizing slowness of glaciers. Their eight short, spade-like legs end in hydraulic clamps that lock directly into the grain of the staves; to pull a grazer from its perch would require a winch, for if they lose their footing, the vibrating wood would throw them down into the vortex below. 

Beneath their heavy armor, their flesh is dense and spongy, saturated with a natural anti-scalding oil that smells of tallow and parched grain. They act as unwitting scourers: by stripping the baked sugars from the conduits, they prevent the resin from insulating the pipes, allowing the steam to shed its heat evenly through the vault.

### The Galvanic Swarm
In the open space between the high ribs, the clouds of blind midges drift through the rising thermals in dense, vibrating curtains. They are pale as tallow, almost weightless, their bodies little more than hollow husks suspended on gossamer, multi-jointed wings. 

These midges do not breed in water or mud; their larval stages are spent burrowed deep inside the wet, fibrous sleeves of the cork pistons. There, under crushing friction and constant electrical discharge, they feed on the microscopic fungal filaments that break down the sloughed bark. When they emerge, they take to the air not to search for food—they have no mouths—but to mate within the ionized corridors created by the blue sparks. 

Their flight is not erratic. When the pistons stroke upward and the wire-like tendrils discharge, the entire swarm snaps into crisp, geometric alignments, orienting their bodies along the invisible lines of the galvanic field like iron filings in a magnetic current. Their collective wingbeat produces a faint, high-register whine that nestles precisely between the low thrum of the sap-pumps and the hiss of the valves.

### The Resonators (*Cladoventis*)
Along the margins where the black slurry churns against the base of the structural ribs, the pale skinks maintain their watch. Their bodies are long, supple, and entirely devoid of pigment—flesh the color of skimmed milk, stretched tight over a skeleton of flexible green cartilage. 

They do not walk so much as ripple across the vibrating staves. Their broad, circular toe-pads do not rely merely on suction; they are dense clusters of piezo-sensitive hair-cells that draw a trickle of galvanic energy directly from the damp wood, keeping their cold-blooded nervous systems perpetually supercharged. 

A skink will remain motionless for hours on a vertical wall, its flat throat pulsing in exact quarter-time with the *clack-thud* of the intake gates. When a midge falls—stunned by a stray arc from the overhead tendrils or weighted down by condensed starch—the skink’s strike is instantaneous, snapping forward and returning to absolute stillness in the space between two piston strokes. Their skin is so thin that, through the translucent belly, one can see the steady, mechanical beating of their two-chambered hearts, keeping time with the greater heart buried miles deeper in the root-system.

### The Slurry-Augers
Beneath the lazy vortex of the floor basin, in the lightless, boiling soup of black pulp, live the largest of the chamber’s organisms: the Augers. 

They are segmented, worm-like behemoths, as thick around as oil drums and twenty feet in length, their hides composed of thick, rubbery bark-plates that have sloughed off the walls and fused with living muscle. The Augers are essentially living conduits. They possess no nervous system to speak of, only a ring of chemical sensors around a wide, circular maw lined with concentric rings of horn-like teeth.

They anchor their tails to the deep foundations where the pistons plunge into the sub-floor. As the cork shafts grind downward, displacing thousands of gallons of dense fiber-slurry, the Augers open their throats and swallow the pressurized pulp whole, filtering out the heavy lignins and mineral salts before venting a stream of clarified, nutrient-poor water behind them. They are the balance-keepers of the floor; without their ceaseless, digestive churning, the basin would silt up within a month, jamming the pistons in their sleeves and tearing the sump apart under its own hydraulic pressure.

### The Valve-Weavers
Tucked into the shadowed alcoves behind the bioluminescent fungal pads, nearly invisible against the damp bark, hang the Weavers. Neither true arachnids nor crustaceans, they are flattened, many-limbed organisms that resemble dried lichen until they move.

Their purpose is singular. Spun from their abdomens is a thick, amber-colored cordage—a living, fibrous silk that stays wet and flexible indefinitely in the high-pressure steam. When an intake gate begins to shudder out of rhythm, or a hairline fracture appears in the xylem staves, the Weavers emerge from their alcoves in slow, methodical processions. Moving with the unhurried precision of clockwork automata, they lace the silk across the vibrating seam, pressing their glandular mouths into the split to inject a fast-curing, rubbery sap that binds the wood anew.

They do not hunt; they are sustained by the condensation that drips from their own webs, rich with airborne yeast and trace copper from the sap-vents. Once their silk is spent, they do not die; they simply crawl into the mouth of the nearest intake gate during its open cycle, offering their calcified bodies to the grinding teeth of the lower engine, recycling their mass back into the slurry that feeds the roots.

***

In this room, life and function are indistinguishable. There are no parasites here, just as there are no wild things; an organism that did not contribute to the damp, thrumming balance of the sump would be shaken from the walls by the vibration, cooked by the steam, or drawn inexorably down into the slow, black vortex of the floor, ground to pulp, and pumped back into the vast and ancient heart that breathes beneath it all.
