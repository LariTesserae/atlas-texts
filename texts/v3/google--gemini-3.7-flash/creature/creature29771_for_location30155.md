The life in this sealed tooth-forest does not fight the rock; it flows into its grain, calcifying and softening by turns, so that the boundary between what is growing and what is precipitating has long since dissolved.

At the lowest tier, where the condensation gathers into lukewarm, rimstone-terraced pools, the white bacterial mats are not inert slicks. They are thick, layered skins—felted pelts of chemoautotrophic filaments several inches deep, buoyant with trapped pockets of metabolic gas. If touched, they feel like warm, wet velvet, trembling with slow internal currents. Within their depths, microscopic nematodes and blind, pale rotifers churn through the sulfur broth in billions, living their entire lifecycles within a three-inch swathe of drowned fleece. When sections of the mat grow too heavy with mineral encrustation, they slough off and sink into the crystal crevices below, where they are slowly entombed in calcite, turning into white, spongy fossils before they can even begin to decay.

Navigating the vertical spires above the water are the grazers: translucent, shell-less gastropods the length of a human finger, their flesh so clear that the pale golden knots of their nervous systems are visible through their mantles. They do not have the hard, coiled shells of surface snails, which would wedge and shatter in the tight lattice of aragonite needles. Instead, their backs are coated in a thick, self-secreting mantle of rubbery, mineralized gel that hardens on contact with the air’s acidic vapor and continuously flakes away behind them. They move with an agonized, glacial slowness over the razor-sharp terrain, their broad foot-pads exuding a caustic mucus that slightly etches the stone, blunting the needle-tips just enough to bear their weight. Where they pass, they leave a milky, glass-smooth path welded permanently onto the stone—living stonemasons smoothing the labyrinth over centuries.

Deeper in the crystalline thicket, where the vertical gaps narrow to the width of a palm, live the articulated creatures: long-legged, blind bristletails and pale troglobitic whip-scorpions whose appendages have stretched into hair-fine filaments three times the length of their bodies. They do not walk upon the stone itself; the needles are too sharp and crowded. Instead, they travel suspended between the spires, their multi-jointed tarsal claws hooked into the surface tension of the unbroken water-film that sheets over the rock. They move with ghostly, suspended agility, their feathery cerci tasting the air for microscopic pressure differentials caused by the landing of a single water drop. They feed primarily on the cast-off slime of the gastropods and the dead bacterial plumes, their pale gut-tubes glowing faintly white with sulfur-eating microbes.

Hanging from the vaulted ceiling, where the rising geothermal steam condenses into heavy, scalding droplets, are colonies of sessile, bladder-like organisms—thallophytes that have lost all greenness, existing as pearlescent, translucent sacs anchored to the stone by calcified stalks. They are the lungs of the pocket. Through thin, vascularized walls, they absorb the saturated vapor directly, filtering out sulfurous compounds and trace minerals. Every few minutes, as condensation builds within their upper funnels, they contract with a soft, wet *chuff*, expelling a small jet of cleansed, de-mineralized water that falls into the pools below with a distinct, higher-pitched *plink*. Their slow, unsynchronized contractions give the entire chamber an ambient, hydraulic respiration.

And then, deeper still—coiled in the dead-end crevice where the petrified heartwood rungs and the lashed shale altar rest—lives the largest inhabitant of the vault.

It is an organism without eyes, without limbs, and without a rigid skeleton: a pale, heavy-bodied cavern-leech or blind lung-siphon, thick as a man’s thigh and nearly ten feet in length when fully extended. It does not possess scales or fur, but a dense, lard-white dermis layered with a thick coat of slippery, lipid-rich cream that renders it entirely immune to the needles of the aragonite; it flows through the razor-sharp crystal forest like heavy oil poured through broken glass. 

It spends months at a time utterly motionless, looped through the mineralized rungs of the ancient wooden ladder. It has integrated the intrusion into its own biology; its underbelly rests against the shale slabs, where the slow, anaerobic breakdown of the ancient split-bark cordage produces a subtle, lingering seep of organic tannins and ancient carbon. The creature’s mouthparts—a wide, circular disc lined with fleshy, filter-feeding papillae—remain pressed against the base of the flowstone cascade, drinking the mineral wash and straining out the bacterial flakes carried down from the ceiling.

It has no concept of light, of outside, or of the mountain’s height. Its entire sensory universe is tactile and vibrational. It feels the *tink-tink-plat* of the dripping ceiling through its skin; it reads the slight, millidegree shifts in the rising thermal plumes; it senses the creeping progress of the snails along the walls. When it does move, it does so with a heavy, fluid deliberate-ness, rippling along the mineral bridges without snapping a single stone needle, its pale, glistening bulk parting the steam like a submerged ghost. 

It is both prisoner and sovereign of this high, drowned womb. It shares the room with the petrified wood and the stone altar, sustained by the heat of the Earth's interior, perfectly preserved in the wet dark, having outlived the names, the lineage, and the very memory of whoever closed the wall behind them.
