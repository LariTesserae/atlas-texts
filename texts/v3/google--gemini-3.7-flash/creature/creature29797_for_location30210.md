At the center of this drowned foundry are the **Bellow-Beasts**—the colossal, split-carapace molluscoids that anchor themselves into the magnetite strata. They are less like living clams and more like drop-forged hydraulic presses cast in manganese iron and dulled bronze. Their shells do not grow in organic spirals; they expand in stepped, angular terraces along the natural cleavage lines of the split basalt, locking directly into the rift walls via deep, calcified root-spurs that fuse on a molecular level with the stone. 

Inside their iron-heavy valves, there is no pale or glistening mantle. Their interior tissue is fibrous and black, dense as oiled hemp, strung with thick cords of vulcanized muscle laced with conductive copper filament. Instead of soft gills, their respiratory apparatus consists of closely racked arrays of razor-thin, perforated titanium-rich plates. When the valve levers open on its cold-welded hinge, the hyper-oxygenated water rushes across these plates at crushing speed, scouring away free ions and dissolved metals. The beast’s heart is an asymmetrical, three-chambered pressure pump wrapped in bands of dense tendon; it beats only once per duty cycle, coinciding precisely with the drop-weight impact of the shell slamming shut. The force of this closure does not merely seal the animal; it physically expels the spent, de-mineralized water through upper siphon-ports in whistling, hyperbaric jets, driving a dense, metallic sludge down into its digestive furnace.

Crawling between these massive forge-engines are the **Needle-Echinoderms**, or *Ticking Stars*. Their central disks are small, flattened pentagons of black ironstone, bearing a cluster of simple, faceted quartz lenses on their dorsal surfaces that catch the hard, unyielding mountain sunlight like dead glass. Their limbs—ten to twelve rigid, articulated needles of native nickel-iron—operate via sealed ball-and-socket joints filled with a viscous, bio-mineral lubricant. They do not creep with tube feet; they walk on hardened, diamond-faceted points that punch minute pits into the basalt. 

The stars are the maintenance cadre of the rift. They move with the jerky, stop-start cadence of high-torque clockwork, their joints clicking through ratcheted increments. They pick systematically through the interlockings of the larger beasts, shearing off wild crystalline growths of pyritic bloom and manganese crust before these deposits can fuse the great valves shut. Their mouthparts, set on the underside, are counter-rotating coring bits that grind continuously, reducing the rock-shards and mineral leavings into a fine, digestible powder. When the galvanic static arc rips through the rift, the needle-stars lock their limbs rigid in a single, simultaneous snap, anchoring themselves flush against the stone so the concussive hydraulic backwash does not fling them down the abyss.

Deeper in the shadows of the floor, wedged directly into the razor-edged fractures where the rift narrows to a hairline, live the **Brace-Worms**. Encased in segmented, tubular jackets of slag-crust that can measure three fathoms in length, their bodies are heavy conduits of pressurized, iron-fixing tissue. They do not feed from the water column. Instead, they drive their hardened, drill-like anterior tips directly into the sub-shelf fissures, tapping the pressurized mineral veins running through the mountain’s root. 

Their outer casings are banded with ridges of raw hematite, sweating a caustic, self-hardening cement that glues neighboring tubes together into rigid, triangular trusses. Over decades, their communal casings have formed an abiotic-looking scaffolding that physically braces the basalt rift, holding the canyon walls apart against the immense tectonic weight of the mountain shelf. Inside these iron conduits, the worms’ internal peristalsis is a heavy, rhythmic shudder that can be felt through the rock long before it is heard: a slow, pneumatic drag of semi-molten mineral pulp moving through gut-valves made of dense, keratinized plate.

Suspended in the vibrating water above the forge-floor, holding their stations against the laminar exhaust plumes, are the **Chime-Chits**. These are pelagic creatures, long and flat as iron strapping, no thicker than a pane of glass. They have no swim bladders; the violent, cavitation-heavy pressure pulses of the shelf would rupture any soft gas pocket in an instant. Instead, they remain neutrally buoyant through skeletons composed of porous, foam-like aluminum silicate. 

The chits have no eyes and no soft fins. Their locomotion is entirely piezoelectric: running along the dorsal edge of their blade-thin bodies is a series of crystal-bearing flex-plates. When the 30-second galvanic arc passes through the chasm, the chits’ bodies absorb the charge, their crystal spines snapping laterally in a sudden, whipping spasm that shoots them forward through the clear melt. In the quiet intervals between pulses, they drift like polished scrap-metal shavings, orienting their bodies along the invisible magnetic flux lines that bleed out of the rift’s banded magnetite walls. They feed by skimming the upward-venting plumes, straining the microscopic spatter of refined heavy metals cast off by the larger valves through narrow, comb-like baleen plates of spun copper wire.

Even the reproduction of this place is mechanical and severe. There is no soft broadcast of drifting larvae. When the tectonic shelf experiences a deep harmonic tremor, the larger valve-beasts undergo synchronized mass-spalling: their heavy mantles eject dense, pea-sized nodules of magnetic spat. These heavy metallic pellets do not float; they sink like lead shot, clattering down through the water column to snap violently against the basalt, held fast by the stone’s own natural magnetism. 

Those that strike open rock immediately drive a tiny, spring-loaded anchor-spur into the first micro-fracture they touch, biting into the stone and beginning the slow, patient extraction of iron from the melt. Those that land on the shells of their elders are ground to powder on the next stroke of the drop-forge, their pulverized minerals sucked into the siphons to be cast again into the living mechanism of the trench.
