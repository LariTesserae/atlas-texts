They are creatures born not of soil or season, but of geometry, pressure, and the unceasing aerosol of mechanical exhaust. To survive here is not a matter of foraging; it is a matter of anchorage.

At the base of this kingdom, flat against the gunmetal-blue mill scale, lies the **solder-pelt**. It appears at first to be nothing more than a thick, pearlescent slime, but under the amber glow of the relays, it reveals itself as a dense, communal felt of single-celled flagellates and oil-eating mold. It does not grow out from spores; it creeps along the grease-pencil lines, its microscopic filaments feeding on the tallow and zinc stearates left behind by the pressing dies. Where the pelt is thickest, it forms a rubbery, self-healing skin that prevents the razor-sharp sheared edges of the beams from slicing the larger bodies that press against them. It smells faintly of almond meal and battery acid, sweating clear beads of water that are instantly whisked away by the intake draft.

Directly upon this pelt graze the **iron-mites**. Seen closely, they have discarded the brittle, segmented elegance of terrestrial arthropods. Their bodies are rounded ingots of translucent, curd-white fat encased in a flexible, silicone-like cuticular shield. They have no antennae; in their place are two broad, spade-like sensory horns that remain flat against the steel, reading the mechanical hum of the turbines like a compass. 

Their sixty-four legs do not end in claws, but in microscopic, grooved pads that utilize the surface tension of the non-freezing water film to form an unbreakable hydraulic seal. When the slipstream spikes—when the louver doors outside adjust and the intake screaming climbs by half an octave—a chemical spasm shoots through the colony. In a fraction of a second, millions of mites expel their internal water through ventral siphon-pores, dropping their profiles flat against the zinc plates like suction cups. They do not walk so much as crawl with the glacial, rhythmic shearing of a tectonic plate, their belly-plates grinding the cold tallow into an edible paste. When an iron-mite dies, it does not release its grip; its hydraulic pads seize in place, and its siblings simply graze over its softening shell, incorporating its lipids into their own carapaces until only a faint, glassy ring remains on the galvanized floor.

Suspended above them, hanging from the pneumatic runs and the braided copper grounding straps, are the **lung-leeches**. 

They are creatures of pure tensile strength. Pale, tubular, and ribbed like vacuum hoses, their flesh is dense and rubbery, marbled with threads of deep, oxygen-starved violet. Their anterior ends have fused permanently into the vulcanized neoprene gaskets of the pipe flanges. Their circular mouths, ringed with microscopic, calcified drills, do not draw blood, for there is none; they tap the weeping, micro-fractured seals of the ship’s hydraulic veins. They drink the pressurized, bittersweet glycols, filtering out the petroleum bases through a row of fluttering, external gills that trail in the mist like strips of damp gauze. 

Because they cannot drop without being shredded by the lower intake grates, the leeches have evolved a secondary, social anatomy: their tails are hooked into the tails of their neighbors, weaving a suspended, hammock-like meshwork that cradles the structural voids. When the aircraft banking induces heavy G-forces, the entire leech-net sways as a single, groaning curtain of fat and sinew, absorbing the shock without tearing a single mouth loose from its gasket.

Woven through the spaces between this living netting are the **pipe-swifts**. 

They are not birds, nor truly fish, though they share the smooth, boneless flexibility of deep-sea eels. Blind and pigmentless, their flesh is so translucent that the slow, rhythmic peristalsis of their two parallel hearts can be seen through their ribs. Their skin is slick with an oily mucus that instantly emulsifies the cold water cascading down the vertical steel, allowing them to glide across raw, vertical bulkheads without losing momentum. 

They navigate entirely by the acoustic map in the iron. The deafening, unbroken scream of the turbines is their illumination: they press the broad, cartilaginous shields of their snouts against the vibrating I-beams, sensing the exact density of the steel, the proximity of a drain-port, or the approach of a rival knot of swifts through bone conduction. They breed in the deep, triangular gussets behind the relay housings—dark, windless niches where dozens of them entwine into tight, writhing spheres the size of a human head. Within these balls of cold meat, the temperature rises just enough—perhaps two degrees above the freezing fog—to incubate their leathery, marble-sized eggs, which adhere to the zinc plating with an adhesive so tenacious it outlasts the parent that laid it.

In the deepest gutters, where the non-freezing runoff pools before being sucked into the humming drain-valves, live the **siphon-blobs**. 

These are the quietest of the pocket’s inhabitants. Shapeless, pale yellow masses of mesenteric fat, some as large as a man's boot, they wedge themselves immovable into the 90-degree corners where the floor-plates meet the main spars. They have no limbs, no eyes, and no mouths. Instead, their entire dorsal surface is a porous, pulsating sponge that sips the chemical runoff—the shed mucus of the swifts, the dead flagellates of the pelt, the micro-droplets of turbine oil, and the bitter trace-salts. They act as the biological drains of the chamber, processing the toxic chemistry of the ship’s breath and excreting small, hard pellets of inert, wax-like resin that roll into the scupper holes and are shot out into the 60,000-foot void.

None of these things know that there is an outside. They do not know that the steel they cling to is soaring through the upper stratosphere at near-sonic speeds, nor that several inches of pickled iron away is a blue, empty vacuum that would boil their blood in seconds. 

To them, the world is flat, perpendicular, deafening, and eternal. It is a universe constructed entirely of ninety-degree angles, bathed in cold chemical dew, held together by the unyielding grip of millions of blind mouths, all vibrating in perfect, terrifying resonance with the machine that carries them.
