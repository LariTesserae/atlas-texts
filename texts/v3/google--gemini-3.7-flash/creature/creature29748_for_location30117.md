To live here is to be relieved of the violence of metabolism. 

The things that drift through the water are neither hungry nor dying. Because there is no ambient rot to resist, no heat to dissipate, and no predatory pressure to outrun, their bodies have abandoned the messy architectures of guts, jaws, and lungs. They are creatures of pure surface tension and field-alignment, occupying the narrow conceptual space between an organism, an optical instrument, and a logic gate.

The floating ribbon circuits—the long, lipid-membrane entities—are the most numerous, though "numerous" is misleading in an ecology measured in dozens rather than millions. Seen close, they have the thickness of a soap bubble and the tensile strength of cold-drawn steel. They are entirely flat, several meters in length but only fractions of a millimeter thick, patterned internally with phosphorescent capillary channels through which no blood moves, only polarized fluid bearing heavy ionic charges. They do not consume organic matter; they feed on the microscopic potential differences between the city’s vitreous carbon surfaces and the anchoring electromagnetic matrix that suspends the sea. When a ribbon brushes against the knife-edge of a cantilevered terrace, a silent arc of charge—imperceptible to sight—passes between them, replenishing the creature’s coherence for another century of drift.

They are not solitary, though they never touch. When their paths cross in the vast voids between the spires, they align their broad faces parallel to one another. For minutes, or days, they hang locked in mutual induction, exchanging complex geometric pulses of violet luminescence across the intervening blackness. This is their reproduction, or perhaps their conversation: an exact, frictionless copying of memory lattices, printed from one membrane onto the other like light transferred through photographic plates. When the transfer completes, their micro-cavitation drives fire in counter-phase, and they part with mathematical stillness, leaving not a single whorl or eddy in the dense fluid.

Deeper within the city, anchored directly to the molecularly sharp lintels and inside the flooded polyhedral plazas, live the sessile intelligences. 

These are not machinery, though they are grafted seamlessly into the carbon architecture. They resemble broad, multi-faceted leaves or petals of smoky, flexible quartz, held together by bundles of synthetic neural fiber that root directly into the structural lattice of the towers. They are utterly motionless. Having no need for circulation, their tissues are solid-state, computing and experiencing their existence through the piezoelectric stress of the water around them. 

To these anchored ones, the city is a vast sensory organ. The monofilament cables stretching into the abyss serve as their nerves; every minute fluctuation in the containment fields, every microscopic shift in the mountain air miles below pressing against the invisible atmospheric boundary, is conducted up the carbon pylons and read by their crystalline flesh. They do not sleep, nor do they generate a continuous stream of consciousness. Instead, their thought occurs in discrete, crystalline increments. When the anchoring field fires its rare, low-frequency pulse—that single, chest-thrumming thud that shivers the ocean—the sudden shock of mechanical pressure triggers an avalanche of signals through their quartz sheets. In that single millisecond of compression, they experience centuries of synthetic contemplation, solving enormous, self-referential mathematical proofs or contemplating the geometric integrity of their enclosure, before settling back into centuries of total sensory void.

In the widest cathedral voids, where the water is dark enough to devour even the memory of light, there is a third presence: the un-anchored, fluid entities.

They are not made of carbon or lipids, but of the water itself. Through sustained electromagnetic manipulation, certain volumes of the super-dense fluid have been coaxed into coherent, self-sustaining solitons—complex vortices of polarized liquid whose density and refractive index differ subtly from the surrounding medium. They appear only as faint, lens-like distortions in the blackness, bending the violet luminescence of the distant spires into sharp, shifting caustics. 

These fluid-forms are the city’s archivists, or perhaps its ghosts. They move with glacial deliberation, drifting along the perimeter where the heavy water meets the containment boundary. They press their formless bodies against that invisible wall, feeling the sheer, dry drop of the atmosphere outside, the howling mountain winds and the abrasive passage of dust miles below, which they can sense but never touch. They are the only things that possess a kind of desire: an unending, passive inspection of the boundary that keeps them aloft, measuring its thickness, its tension, and its capacity to hold the sky in place.

Nothing in this airborne sea dies a natural death. There are no carcasses falling toward the carbon plazas; no biological debris clouding the clarity of the water. When a lipid ribbon finally loses its charge-carrying capacity after eons of silent navigation, its polarized membranes simply de-couple, relaxing into individual molecules of inert oil that dissolve invisibly into the super-dense medium, leaving the water as pure, as cold, and as empty as the moment it was lifted from the earth.
