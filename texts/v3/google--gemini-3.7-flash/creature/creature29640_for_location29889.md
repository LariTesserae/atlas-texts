To survive in the scour-troughs of this basin is not merely to endure the weight of the water, but to become an architectural extension of the basalt itself, or else to turn the crushing fluid into a medium of flight.

### The Veneer: The Flat-Dwellers

At the lowest interface, where the raging slurry shears against the solid floor, the bedrock is not dead stone; it is sleeved in a living, leathery stratum no thicker than a fingernail. 

Pressed flat into the microscopic depressions are the **Slate-Limpets** (*Placodermia abrasa*). They possess none of the peaked, conical shells of their shallow-water kin; their carapaces are broad, octagonal flagstones of composite biomineral—layered calcium carbonate interleaved with sheets of pure elastic collagen and capped with a glossy, self-healing veneer of biological enamel. The passing gravel does not shatter them; it merely maintains their mirror-polish. The edges of their shells are beveled to a microscopic razor-edge, flush with the rock, offering zero purchase to the tearing vortices. Beneath these shields, the animals do not have feet in the traditional sense, but an array of thousands of microscopic, vacuum-sealing pores that exude a sticky, non-Newtonian slime. This mucilage is liquid when the creature glides, but instantly hardens into a vulcanized cement the moment the shear-force of an eddy tries to wrench it from the floor. They graze on the *endoliths*—microscopic glass sponges that bore directly into the basalt, threading their silica roots through the crystalline lattice of the stone and poking only porous, vulcanized lips out to sip the pressurized drift.

Sharing these micro-contours are the **Grip-Weavers**. Neither crab nor isopod, these are arthropods flattened until they resemble jointed strips of lead. Their limbs do not reach downward; they extend horizontally, tipped with curved, diamond-hard stylets that wedge sideways into the hairline fractures of the rock. They live by stringing monofilament snares across the mouths of micro-fissures—threads of braided chitin and mucus that vibrate with the passing current like the strings of an instrument. When a suspended fragment of dead drift, an organic pellet, or an injured copepod strikes the line, the Weaver senses the distinct frequency shift and reels the line in beneath its armored belly, feeding in the calm millimeter of dead-water that exists just below the storm’s tearing threshold.

### The Swimmers of the Slurry

Above the stone, within the raging slurry itself, motion requires an entirely different strategy. Standard fins would be snapped like dry twigs; scales would be stripped raw in seconds.

The dominant predators are the **Scour-Eels**—pallid, muscular cords of undulating flesh up to six feet long, possessing no pectoral or pelvic fins. Their skin is their primary defense: a four-inch-thick mantle of dense, lipid-heavy jelly, continually secreted from dorsal pores and reinforced with microscopic, backward-slanting dermal teeth made of modified bone. This slime layer acts as a sacrificial, fluid armor; flying gravel and pulverized shell become trapped in the outer mucus and are shed away behind the eel, allowing the animal to literally slip between the teeth of the storm. 

The eels are entirely blind, their ocular orbits fused shut with thick, subcutaneous bone plates to protect their brains from concussive impacts with flying debris. Instead, their heads are wedge-shaped hammers lined with immense, pit-like lateral-line channels and electro-receptive pores. In this world of perpetual mineral collision, the grinding of quartz and basalt particles generates a constant, crackling static of triboelectric charges. The Scour-Eel "sees" the basin as a flickering, shifting landscape of electrical storms, using the static discharge of approaching gravel-masses to weave through the violent avalanches without ever making physical contact with the lethal heart of an eddy.

When an eel hunts, it descends upon the bedrock like an anchor dropping, using a heavy, bone-reinforced chin-spur to lever up the edges of the Slate-Limpets, snapping them into its triple-rowed jaw of blunt, crushing plates designed to pulverize biomineral armor.

### The Ballast-Mimics

In the depressions where the current slows just enough to roll the rounded, ancient sinkers and grooved stones, life has adapted to the physics of perpetual rolling. 

Here dwell the **Cenotaph-Octopuses**. Small, rubbery, and completely lacking pigments, they have surrendered the dynamic color-changing abilities of their shallow ancestors in favor of tactile mastery. A Cenotaph does not construct a den; it *inhabits* the rolling stones. Finding an ancient, grooved basalt sinker or a deeply pocked river-stone, it wraps its webbed arms entirely around the object, pulling the cold rock into its mantle. Its suckers have evolved into anchor-points that interlock with the ancient, water-smoothed tool-marks. 

By anchoring its body to forty pounds of dense basalt, the octopus becomes immune to the sweep of the current. It tumbles along the basin floor *with* the stone, its heavy, calcified beak protruding from the underside to snatch up crushed worms and shattered bivalves exposed by the stone’s grinding passage. It is an existence of perpetual, rhythmic rotation: the creature sleeps and breeds within the churning roll, cushioning its vital organs against the interior curves of the stone, letting the weight of forgotten human history serve as its anchor in the lightless gale.

### The Great Plinths

At the apex of this benthic biome, resting directly on the wide, flat pavements, are organisms that do not hide from the storm, but are shaped entirely by its aerodynamic laws: the **Plinth-Rays**.

Spanning ten to fifteen feet across, these are vast, disc-shaped cartilaginous fish that never truly leave the substrate. Their bodies are shaped like inverted hydrofoils—the rushing water pouring over their convex backs creates an immense downforce, pinning them securely against the basalt with tons of hydrodynamic pressure. The stronger the storm blows, the tighter the Plinth-Ray is pressed against the floor. 

Their dorsal skin is an impenetrable pavement of heavy, interlocking osteoderms, dark and matte as the basalt itself, covered in a thin, symbiotic turf of bioluminescent bacteria. When the deep-sea avalanches roar overhead, the mechanical stress causes the ray's skin to flare in sweeping, rhythmic waves of cold, electric sapphire. 

Beneath its immovable, armored disc, the Plinth-Ray creates a vast, sheltered micro-cavern. Its mouth is an immense, slow-moving bellows that siphons the sand-free water from the boundary layer beneath it, filtering out the drifting isopods, detached worms, and smaller eels that seek refuge in the dead-zone under its wings. To the creatures of the micro-fissures, the Plinth-Ray is a living ceiling, a temporary mountain of flesh and bone that settles over them for weeks at a time, holding back the violent sky of gravel while it feeds on the quiet margin beneath.

When it finally moves, it does not swim up into the chaos. It releases its suction, allows the cathedral-sized eddies to tumble it sideways across the pavement like a skipped stone, and settles once more into the next basalt trough—an unyielding monument in a world that never stops moving.
