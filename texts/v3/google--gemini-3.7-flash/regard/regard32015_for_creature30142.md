A foolish power would have torn open the ceiling.

A foolish power, seeing the dark, would have split the thousands of feet of bedrock to let in the sun, not understanding that the sun would blind their sensory pits and scorch their sulfur-mats. A foolish power, hearing the screaming cavitation of the four-story impellers, would have stilled the gears, not knowing that stillness here is suffocation—that without the violent, churning wash, the ballast-matrons would choke on their own silt, the shear-sliders would collapse from lack of hydrostatic velocity, and the rubbery mats of thermophiles would starve in the cold. 

A foolish power would have made them float. And in floating, they would have been shredded.

The power that found them was not foolish. It was wise, and because it was wise, it knew that benevolence in a world of iron and pressure cannot look like mercy in a world of air and grass. 

It did not descend as a beam of light. It entered the vault as a *density*. 

It seeped down through the micro-fractures of the slate, slipping between the grains of compressed stone until it took the form of the water itself—heavy, hot, tea-colored, and thick with mineral grease. It did not displace the flood; it inhabited the current. It felt the violent shearing of the vortices, the concussive ringing of the pistons against the iron hulls, the copper-tasting heat of the exchangers, and it found the ecosystem whole, terrible, and complete.

It saw that these creatures did not suffer from their adaptations; they were the absolute triumph of life over an impossible geometry.

And so, the power asked itself what a steward owes to a world that does not know it is trapped.

The tragedy of the drowned vault was not its darkness, nor its violence. Its tragedy was *entropy*. The machinery was slowly, inevitably dying. One day, a main driveshaft would shear beyond repair. The impellers would drag to a halt against their encrusted housings. The thermal plumes would cool to the ambient chill of deep earth. The current would slacken, the acoustic map would fade into silence, and the ballast-matrons would slowly starve in a stagnant, rotting pond.

The benevolent power did not alter the design. It chose, instead, to become the maintenance it had been denied for a thousand years.

First, it sank to the bed of the abyss, beneath the lowest intake grates, where the foundation plates met the primordial rock. There, it rooted itself into the geothermal taps. It did not cool the water, but it stabilized the heat exchangers, ensuring that the subterranean fires would never vent too fiercely nor dwindle to ash. It made the thermal plumes eternal.

Then, it moved into the iron itself. 

It did not scour the rust, for the rust was the crustacean’s pasture and the matron’s camouflage. Instead, it whispered into the crystalline lattice of the ancient steel and cast brass. Where micro-fractures had begun to spiderweb through the roots of the impeller blades, the metal subtly knit. It introduced an imperceptible, self-healing temper to the bronze bearings and the long-slack counterweight chains. The machines would still grind, they would still screech, they would still throw off flakes of magnetite to feed the filter-feeders—but they would never catastrophically fail. The world would not seize.

Where the galvanic siphons clung to the shorting junctions, the power touched the copper braids. It did not end the short circuits; it made the galvanic pulse rhythmic and ceaseless, an endless, gentle feeding-spark that pulsed in time with the deep pressure waves, ensuring the leathery tubes would never starve for zinc or charge.

Finally, the power went to the deep dead-zones, to the largest of the ballast-matrons wedged between the intake manifolds.

The power did not attempt to lift her. It knew her weight was her dignity. 

Instead, it pressed itself against her vast, blackened rind and matched the frequency of her cranial dome. It entered her hydrophone mind not with words, but as an *infrasonic chord*—a note deeper than the lowest hydraulic thump, a resonant frequency that vibrated through her calcified grit and into the thousands of pale scavengers crawling through her breathing folds.

Through this resonance, the power gave the matrons a gift. 

It gave them the full acoustic horizon of the vault without the agony of the high-frequency brass scream. It softened the skull-splitting strike of the cavitation into a rich, complex timbre—turning the brutal, deafening noise into a vast, industrial symphony. The matrons, who had only ever known the groan of failing metal as a prelude to collapse, now felt the machine as an extension of their own flesh: an unbreaking, circulatory heart that would drive their food to them forever.

In the mid-water, the shear-sliders felt the change only as a subtle perfection of the draft. The vortices became slightly cleaner, the shearing edges of the plumes more predictable. They threw themselves into the abyss with frictionless abandon, their oily flanks gleaming in the faint blue arcs, drumming against the steel with resonance chambers that now found a richer, warmer echo in the vaulted arches.

The vault remained dark. The water remained bath-warm, heavy with sulfur and grease. The blades continued to churn the flood into a roaring frenzy. 

To an observer from the surface, it would still look like a drowned hell of blind iron and crushing weight. But to the things that lived there, the vault had subtly shifted from a dying tomb into an eternal engine. 

Satisfied, the benevolent power did not leave—for to leave would be to let the metal forget its new resilience. It remained as a quiet, hydrostatic presence resting in the deepest sump, listening with profound and patient joy to the clanking of the chains, the chattering of the chisel-claws upon the rivets, and the endless, beautiful thunder of the impellers moving the dark.
